## What the surgeon is doing and why

Liver transplantation replaces a failed or cancer-bearing liver with a whole (or part) donor liver in the same position. The recipient usually has cirrhosis with portal hypertension, so the surgeon is taking out an organ that is stuck down, surrounded by high-pressure collateral veins, in a patient with too few platelets and an unstable coagulation system. The new liver has been flushed with cold preservation solution and has been ischaemic for hours.

The operation has three phases, and each has its own physiology. Hold the sequence in mind as the map for the whole anaesthetic:

```
 Phase            Surgeon                      What you are managing
 ---------------  ---------------------------  ---------------------------
 1 Dissection     mobilise liver, divide       bleeding, ascites loss,
   (pre-          ligaments, skeletonise       coagulation, low filling
   anhepatic)     hilum                        pressure
 2 Anhepatic      clamp portal vein and        preload falls; no citrate,
                  cava, remove liver, sew      lactate or potassium
                  in caval and portal veins    clearance; cooling
 3 Reperfusion    release portal vein, then    reperfusion syndrome,
   and            hepatic artery, then bile    fibrinolysis, then signs
   neohepatic     duct                         of graft function
```

How the vena cava is handled decides the haemodynamics of phase 2. In the conventional technique the retrohepatic cava is removed with the liver, so it is clamped above and below and venous return from the lower body stops. Venovenous bypass (cannulae in the femoral and portal veins returning blood to the axillary or internal jugular vein through a pump) was devised to carry that return past the clamps. In the piggyback technique the recipient's cava is left in place, the liver is peeled off it, and the donor cava is sewn to a cuff of the hepatic veins or side-to-side, with only a partial clamp, so some caval flow continues. A temporary portocaval shunt can be added to decompress the gut. Cochrane reviews of both bypass and piggyback found too few trials to recommend or refute either; surgeons and units vary, and you need to know which is planned before induction.

The pain logic: the wound is the bilateral subcostal incision of upper abdominal surgery, which in any other patient would score 4 or 5. Recipients consistently need less opioid than patients having liver resection through the same incision. Proposed reasons include a denervated graft, no raw liver surface, steroid, and altered endogenous opioid handling in liver failure; none is proven. With the recommended plan this behaves as a 3, in a patient who spends the first hours sedated in intensive care.

## Steps

### 1. The call and the assessment
**Why:** An organ has been accepted; cold ischaemic time is running, so the recipient is admitted and prepared while the donor operation is completed.

**Anaesthesia:** These patients have been assessed for listing, sometimes months earlier: re-examine what changes. Encephalopathy grade, ascites, sodium, creatinine, potassium, haemoglobin, platelets, fibrinogen and a viscoelastic trace today. From the listing work-up: ventricular function and stress testing (the hyperdynamic circulation of cirrhosis hides a cardiomyopathy that reperfusion exposes), and the right heart. Portopulmonary hypertension is the lesion that makes transplantation dangerous: a mean pulmonary artery pressure above about 35 mmHg despite treatment carries a high perioperative mortality, and many units decline above 45-50 mmHg. Hepatopulmonary syndrome causes hypoxaemia that worsens upright and resolves slowly after transplantation. Ask the surgeon three things: caval technique, bypass or not, and the graft (brain-dead or circulatory-death donor, steatosis, cold ischaemic time, machine perfusion), because a marginal graft predicts a worse reperfusion.

### 2. Induction, lines and set-up
**Why:** Nothing surgical yet; the surgeon is preparing the graft on the back table.

**Anaesthesia:** Ascites and delayed gastric emptying justify a rapid sequence induction. Vasodilated patients drop their pressure with any induction agent: have noradrenaline running. Lines: arterial (radial, with a femoral line added in many units because radial pressure under-reads central pressure after reperfusion), a large-bore sheath for the rapid infuser, central access for vasopressors, and either a pulmonary artery catheter or transoesophageal echocardiography (varices are a relative, not absolute, contraindication to a probe passed gently). Cell salvage, forced-air and fluid warming, and a baseline gas and viscoelastic test. Do not correct an abnormal INR or platelet count before incision: haemostasis in cirrhosis is rebalanced, not simply deficient, and prophylactic plasma adds volume and portal pressure without reducing bleeding.

### 3. Incision and dissection
**Why:** Bilateral subcostal incision with midline extension, costal retractor, drainage of ascites, division of the ligaments and adhesions, and mobilisation of a stiff liver surrounded by collaterals.

**Anaesthesia:** Litres of ascites leave at once and the circulation follows over the next hour; replace with albumin, not crystalloid, and by response, not by formula. Bleeding in this phase is surgical and venous, driven by portal pressure. A restrictive approach helps: keep the central venous pressure low until the liver is out, avoid plasma unless there is uncontrolled bleeding, and support pressure with noradrenaline (vasopressin or terlipressin are added in some units to lower portal flow). One centre using this approach, with selective phlebotomy, reported no blood products at all in 79 of 100 consecutive transplants; that is a single-centre series, not a trial, but the direction is consistent with the liver resection literature (see [liver resection](liver-resection.html)). Treat bleeding by the trace: fibrinogen first, platelets for a low clot amplitude, plasma for a prolonged clotting time with ongoing loss. Viscoelastic guidance reduced plasma use in the one small randomised trial and red cell and plasma use in meta-analysis. Check gas, potassium, calcium and glucose hourly from now on.

### 4. Hilar dissection and test clamp
**Why:** The hepatic artery and bile duct are divided and the portal vein is cleaned; before committing, the surgeon may test-clamp the cava (conventional technique) to see whether the circulation tolerates it.

**Anaesthesia:** Before the clamps: 100% blood volume status checked on echo or by filling pressures, a modest volume load (250-500 mL), vasopressor running, calcium normal. A test clamp that drops mean arterial pressure by more than about a third, or cardiac output by half, despite these measures is the indication for venovenous bypass or a change to piggyback. The patients who tolerate clamping best are those with well-developed collaterals; acute liver failure and non-cirrhotic recipients tolerate it worst.

### 5. Clamps on: the anhepatic phase begins
**Why:** Portal vein clamped and divided, cava clamped (fully, or partially for piggyback), the diseased liver removed. The clock on warm implantation starts when the cold graft is placed in the abdomen.

**Anaesthesia:** With full caval and portal clamping, venous return and cardiac output fall by up to half; systemic resistance rises and pressure is usually held with noradrenaline and a small fluid load. Resist chasing a low filling pressure with litres: that volume is still in the patient when the clamps come off and will overload the right ventricle and congest the new liver. Renal venous pressure is high and renal perfusion is low for as long as the cava is clamped, which is the main intraoperative insult to the kidney and the rationale for caval-sparing surgery or bypass. On venovenous bypass, maintain flows above about 1.5-2 L/min, watch for air and thrombus in the circuit, and expect faster cooling.

### 6. Anhepatic metabolism
**Why:** For 45-90 minutes there is no liver while the surgeon sews the upper caval or hepatic vein anastomosis and then the portal vein.

**Anaesthesia:** Nothing clears citrate, lactate or tissue plasminogen activator. So: ionised calcium falls with every unit of blood product and needs calcium chloride by infusion or bolus; lactate and base deficit rise steadily; fibrinolysis begins to appear on the trace; temperature drifts down. Glucose usually holds in cirrhosis and falls in acute liver failure. Use this time to prepare for reperfusion. Send a gas 15 minutes and 5 minutes before unclamping. Aim for potassium below 4.5 mmol/L (insulin with glucose, salbutamol, washed or fresh red cells, and in the patient with renal failure intraoperative filtration), ionised calcium normal, pH around 7.3 (bicarbonate in measured doses, counting its sodium in the hyponatraemic patient), temperature above 35.5 degrees C. Give the methylprednisolone the protocol specifies. Draw up adrenaline 10 micrograms/mL, calcium chloride 1 g and atropine.

### 7. Flushing the graft
**Why:** The graft is full of cold, potassium-rich preservation solution. Before or as the portal anastomosis is completed, the surgeon flushes it with albumin or crystalloid through the portal vein, or vents the first few hundred millilitres of portal blood out through the lower caval anastomosis, to wash that solution out before it reaches the heart.

**Anaesthesia:** Know which flush is being used: a blood vent is a sudden loss of 300-500 mL that you replace as it happens. Confirm with the surgeon the order of unclamping (cava first, then portal vein is usual) and ask for a countdown. Increase inspired oxygen to 100%, lighten the volatile if the pressure is marginal, and stop any vasodilator.

### 8. Reperfusion
**Why:** The caval clamps come off, then the portal clamp: portal blood flows through the graft into the right atrium. The liver should pink up within a minute.

**Anaesthesia:** The reperfusion syndrome is conventionally defined as a fall in mean arterial pressure of more than 30% from the anhepatic value, lasting at least one minute, within five minutes of reperfusion. The causes arrive together: cold, acid, potassium, and vasoactive and inflammatory mediators from the graft and from the congested gut; sometimes air or thrombus. The right ventricle sees a preload surge and a rise in pulmonary vascular resistance at the same moment as its contractility is depressed, and systemic resistance collapses. Watch the ECG for peaked T waves and bradycardia and the echo or pulmonary artery pressure for right heart distension. Treat early and in small doses: adrenaline 10-20 micrograms IV repeated, calcium chloride 500 mg-1 g IV for hyperkalaemia or a wide complex, noradrenaline or vasopressin for vasoplegia that persists, and ask the surgeon to reduce portal inflow by partial clamping if the heart is struggling. In a series of 338 recipients, significant reperfusion syndrome was associated with more transfusion, more fibrinolysis, longer ventilation and more retransplantation, so its severity is also an early signal about the graft. Intracardiac thrombus and pulmonary embolism present here as sudden right heart failure; echocardiography makes the diagnosis.

### 9. Hepatic artery anastomosis and the coagulation trough
**Why:** With the liver perfused by portal blood, the surgeon joins the donor hepatic artery to the recipient's, the anastomosis on which the bile ducts depend.

**Anaesthesia:** Coagulation is at its worst in the 30-60 minutes after reperfusion: tissue plasminogen activator released from the graft endothelium causes hyperfibrinolysis, heparin-like substances are released, platelets are trapped in the graft, and the patient is cold and acidotic. Run a viscoelastic test 5-10 minutes after reperfusion. Lysis on the trace with diffuse oozing is the indication for tranexamic acid 1 g IV; a meta-analysis of 1,407 patients found antifibrinolytics reduced transfusion without a detectable rise in hepatic artery thrombosis. Lysis without bleeding often corrects by itself as the graft starts to work. Replace fibrinogen and platelets to targets on the trace and stop there: a new 3 mm arterial anastomosis in a patient who has been over-corrected with plasma, platelets and antifibrinolytic is at risk of thrombosis, which costs the graft. This judgement, to treat bleeding and not numbers, is the core skill of the case.

### 10. Reading the new liver
**Why:** The surgeon looks for a soft, evenly perfused graft that begins to make bile on the table.

**Anaesthesia:** You have the earlier evidence. A working graft clears citrate (the calcium requirement stops), clears lactate (the base deficit improves without bicarbonate), warms the patient, raises the glucose, and improves the viscoelastic trace over one to two hours; vasopressor needs fall. A graft that is not working does the opposite: rising lactate, persistent acidosis, hypoglycaemia, continuing coagulopathy and escalating vasopressor. Say this to the surgeon in plain terms while the abdomen is open, because a Doppler check of the artery and portal vein is quick now and a re-exploration later is not.

### 11. Bile duct, haemostasis and closure
**Why:** The donor and recipient ducts are joined end to end, or the donor duct to a Roux loop of jejunum where the recipient duct is diseased; drains are placed; the wall is closed, or left open with a temporary closure if the graft is large or the bowel oedematous.

**Anaesthesia:** One to two hours of steady work, and the time to protect the kidney: restore a mean arterial pressure at the patient's baseline with as little vasopressor as possible, avoid a high central venous pressure that congests both kidney and graft, and avoid nephrotoxins. Urine output often picks up after reperfusion; failure to do so in a patient with pre-existing kidney injury predicts postoperative renal replacement. Check the sodium against the starting value. A tight closure raises airway pressure and compresses the graft: tell the surgeon.

### 12. Emergence or transfer
**Why:** The surgeon wants a perfused graft, a dry abdomen and a patient who can be assessed neurologically.

**Anaesthesia:** A recipient who is warm, haemodynamically stable on little or no vasopressor, with a functioning graft, modest transfusion and no encephalopathy can be extubated in theatre or within a few hours; enhanced recovery consensus statements for liver transplantation now exist and units have moved in that direction. Everyone else is transferred sedated. Handover covers the clamp and ischaemia times, reperfusion course, products given, last trace, potassium, sodium change, urine output, and the lines.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a large subcostal abdominal-wall incision in a patient with an uncertain coagulation state, a kidney at risk, and a new liver whose drug clearance is not yet known. Opioid requirement is lower than the incision predicts: in the one direct comparison, recipients used less morphine than liver resection patients at every interval to 72 hours.

The defensible default is systemic. Intraoperatively: fentanyl or sufentanil in moderate doses, or remifentanil where early extubation is planned. Postoperatively: fentanyl or oxycodone by patient-controlled analgesia once awake, with paracetamol at a reduced dose (for example 1 g IV eight-hourly, by unit protocol, held if the graft is failing). NSAIDs are not used: the kidney is at risk from the operation and from calcineurin inhibitors. Morphine is avoided in renal impairment. Gabapentinoids are avoided in the encephalopathic. Low-dose ketamine helps the opioid-tolerant recipient.

Neuraxial techniques are not used: the coagulopathy is unpredictable for days. Subcostal transversus abdominis plane blocks or surgeon-placed wound catheters are reasonable once haemostasis is secure and are used in some units as part of enhanced recovery; the evidence is from small series and the dose of amide local anaesthetic should allow for reduced early clearance. This is author's judgement, and there is no PROSPECT guideline for liver transplantation.

Rebound is low because the plan does not rely on a block. Escalation triggers: severe or rising pain with a falling haemoglobin and a tense abdomen is bleeding; pain with fever, rising transaminases and a failing trace is hepatic artery thrombosis until a Doppler says otherwise; drowsiness on small opioid doses is graft dysfunction, hypoglycaemia or a sodium that has moved too fast.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Acute liver failure. A different patient: young, non-cirrhotic, coagulopathic without rebalancing, hypoglycaemic, with cerebral oedema. Manage intracranial pressure through the case (head up, normocapnia, sodium at the upper end of normal, avoid volatile at high concentration) and expect poor tolerance of caval clamping.
- Circulatory-death and marginal grafts. More severe reperfusion and more early dysfunction. Normothermic machine preservation reduced graft injury by half in the randomised trial and is changing which grafts are used.
- Hyperkalaemia at reperfusion. The commonest cause of arrest on the table. Risk rises with renal failure, large transfusion of stored blood, a long cold ischaemic time and a circulatory-death graft. Treat the anhepatic potassium aggressively.
- Venovenous bypass. Maintains preload and renal perfusion at the cost of cannulation injury, air embolism, thrombosis and heat loss; routine in few units, selective in many. Three small trials do not settle it.
- Piggyback. Better haemodynamics and a shorter warm ischaemic time, with a risk of outflow obstruction if the cuff kinks; a partial clamp still reduces return by a variable amount.
- Portopulmonary hypertension. If first found at pulmonary artery catheter insertion after induction, the case may be cancelled; this is why the catheter goes in before incision.
- Sodium. A recipient who starts at 122 mmol/L and receives bicarbonate, saline and 20 units of products can rise by 15 mmol/L in a day and develop osmotic demyelination. Track it and use low-sodium fluids.
- Massive transfusion. Managed as in [major haemorrhage](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html), with the difference that citrate is not metabolised until the graft works.
- Retransplantation and previous upper abdominal surgery. The dissection phase is long and bloody; plan products and a second anaesthetist.
- Living donor and split grafts. A smaller graft with shorter vessels, more arterial and biliary complications, and a risk of small-for-size syndrome from portal hyperperfusion. The donor operation is a hepatectomy and follows the [liver resection](liver-resection.html) card. Combined liver and kidney transplantation adds the considerations in [renal transplantation](renal-transplantation-and-donor-nephrectomy.html).

## Key sources

Liver transplant anaesthesia is practised largely on physiology, observational series and consensus. The surgical technique questions (bypass, piggyback) have Cochrane reviews that found only a handful of small, biased trials and no answer. Viscoelastic-guided transfusion has one 28-patient randomised trial and a meta-analysis dominated by observational studies; the concept of rebalanced haemostasis and the avoidance of prophylactic plasma are supported by laboratory work and by cohort data. The low central venous pressure strategy comes from a single-centre before-and-after series. The reperfusion syndrome definition is the conventional one derived from the 1987 description, whose abstract could not be viewed during drafting; its association with outcome is from one retrospective cohort. The pre-reperfusion targets for potassium, pH, calcium and temperature, the pulmonary artery pressure thresholds for portopulmonary hypertension, the sodium correction limit, the bypass flow figure and drug doses are standard teaching stated without a specific source. The pain comparison rests on one retrospective study of 25 patients.
