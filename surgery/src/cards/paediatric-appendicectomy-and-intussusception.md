## What the surgeon is doing and why

These are the two common causes of an acute abdomen in childhood, at opposite ends of the age range, and they reach you by different doors: the appendix through the emergency theatre, the intussusception usually through radiology.

Appendicitis is obstruction and infection of a blind-ended tube. The lumen blocks, pressure rises, the wall becomes ischaemic and bacteria cross it; untreated, it perforates within a day or two, faster in young children, who also have a short omentum that cannot wall the infection off. The surgeon removes the appendix before or after that point. Laparoscopically: an umbilical port, two working ports, the mesoappendix divided with diathermy or a sealing device, the base secured with loops or a stapler, and the appendix delivered through the umbilicus, in a bag if it is friable. If it has perforated, pus is aspirated from the pelvis and paracolic gutters and the field irrigated. The open alternative is a muscle-splitting incision over McBurney's point. Either way the operation is short; the illness that preceded it sets the difficulty. A child with a perforated appendix has been vomiting and febrile for days, with ileus, a fluid deficit of 5-10% of body weight, and sometimes sepsis.

Intussusception is the telescoping of one segment of bowel into the next, almost always terminal ileum into colon, in a child of three months to three years. The mesentery is dragged in with the bowel, its veins are compressed first, the wall swells and bleeds (the late "redcurrant jelly" stool), and arterial ischaemia and necrosis follow if it is left. The first-line treatment is not surgical: a radiologist passes a catheter into the rectum, seals the buttocks, and pushes the intussusceptum back with air under fluoroscopy or with saline under ultrasound, at a controlled pressure usually capped at about 120 mmHg. In a multinational series of 3,203 children this succeeded in 84.8% and perforated the bowel in 0.4%. The surgeon stands by for failure and for perforation. At operation the bowel is reduced by squeezing the apex back from distal to proximal (pushing, never pulling), and resected if it is dead, if it will not reduce, or if a lead point such as a Meckel diverticulum is found.

The pain logic: appendicectomy is a laparoscopic operation on an inflamed peritoneum and scores 3, with the same warning as in adults that it is routinely under-treated; in the 179-procedure cohort appendicectomy ranked among the most painful operations on day 1. Wall blocks treat the ports and not the peritonitis. Intussusception hurts intensely before reduction (colic every few minutes with pallor and drawing up of the legs) and hardly at all after a successful enema; the child who needs a laparotomy has an abdominal-wall wound and is managed as one.

## Steps

### 1. Assessing the child with appendicitis
**Why:** The surgeon has made a clinical or ultrasound diagnosis and wants theatre within hours; for perforation with sepsis, sooner.

**Anaesthesia:** Grade the deficit by heart rate, capillary refill, mucous membranes, urine output and the story (days of vomiting, fever, not drinking). Give balanced isotonic crystalloid 10-20 mL/kg IV and reassess; the perforated child commonly needs 20-40 mL/kg before induction and should have had antibiotics already. Check sodium and glucose in the young, the septic and the long-starved. Persistent tachycardia after fluid, in a child whose pain has been treated, is sepsis or under-resuscitation and not anxiety. Analgesia does not obscure the diagnosis: give intravenous paracetamol and morphine 0.05-0.1 mg/kg IV while waiting.

### 2. Induction with a full stomach
**Why:** Nothing surgical yet.

**Anaesthesia:** Peritonitis stops gastric emptying, so fasting time is irrelevant. A child's oxygen store lasts well under a minute of apnoea once anaesthetised, and the classic apnoeic rapid sequence produces hypoxaemia and a hurried, traumatic intubation. The paediatric answer is a controlled sequence: preoxygenate as far as the child allows, propofol 3-4 mg/kg IV (ketamine 1-2 mg/kg IV if septic), rocuronium 1 mg/kg IV, gentle mask ventilation under 12 cmH2O while the relaxant works, then intubation with a cuffed tube. In 1,001 children managed this way there was one regurgitation, no aspiration, and hypoxaemia in under 1%. Cricoid pressure is omitted by most paediatric anaesthetists. An inhalational induction is acceptable only where a cannula has not been achievable after a proper attempt. Pass an orogastric tube after intubation, empty the stomach and remove it at the end.

### 3. Ports and pneumoperitoneum
**Why:** Open access at the umbilicus, insufflation to 8-12 mmHg according to size, then two working ports in the left iliac fossa and suprapubic region placed under vision; the bladder must be empty.

**Anaesthesia:** Ask whether the child has voided; otherwise the surgeon may request a catheter or express the bladder. Peritoneal stretch at insufflation can cause a vagal bradycardia in a child: tell the surgeon to release the gas, and give atropine 20 micrograms/kg IV if it does not recover. Head-down with left tilt moves the small bowel away and moves the carina towards the tube tip: listen again once tilted. Raise minute ventilation by 20-30% for absorbed carbon dioxide. The mechanics in infants and small children are covered in [paediatric laparoscopic and thoracoscopic surgery](paediatric-laparoscopic-and-thoracoscopic-surgery.html).

### 4. Dividing the mesoappendix and base
**Why:** The appendix is lifted, its mesentery and artery sealed and cut, and the base ligated with two loops or stapled flush with the caecum.

**Anaesthesia:** Give dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV and, unless there is renal impairment or the child is still under-resuscitated, an NSAID (ibuprofen 10 mg/kg orally beforehand, or ketorolac 0.5 mg/kg IV up to 15 mg where available). Fentanyl 1-2 micrograms/kg IV at induction covers this phase. Bleeding is rare and comes from the appendicular artery or the inferior epigastric vessels at a port.

### 5. Washout for perforation
**Why:** Pus and faecolith are sucked out of the pelvis and from between loops, and the area is irrigated; some surgeons leave a drain.

**Anaesthesia:** Handling infected peritoneum releases bacteria and mediators: a rise in heart rate and temperature and a fall in blood pressure here is a septic shower. Treat with fluid boluses, then a vasopressor if 40-60 mL/kg in total has not restored perfusion, and tell the surgeon and the ward that the child may need a higher level of care. Warmed irrigation only. This is the child who needs longer-acting opioid before waking: morphine 0.1 mg/kg IV, and a nurse- or patient-controlled analgesia prescription.

### 6. Specimen delivery, closure and emergence
**Why:** The appendix comes out through the umbilical port, which is the only wound with a fascial closure.

**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon infiltrates all ports with levobupivacaine 0.25% to a total of 0.8 mL/kg (2 mg/kg). In the randomised trial of 87 children, adding transversus abdominis plane blocks to port infiltration made no difference to morphine use and lengthened the anaesthetic by 14 minutes, so infiltration is the default. Give ondansetron 0.15 mg/kg IV, reverse the relaxant against a monitor, and extubate awake in the left lateral position: the stomach is still not reliably empty.

### 7. Open appendicectomy (variant)
**Why:** A transverse right iliac fossa incision, the three flat muscles split in the line of their fibres, the caecum delivered and the appendix removed outside the abdomen.

**Anaesthesia:** One unilateral wall wound, and here a block does help: an ipsilateral transversus abdominis plane block roughly halved 48-hour morphine in a randomised trial of open appendicectomy. No pneumoperitoneum and no tilt. The induction is unchanged.

### 8. Intussusception: resuscitate before radiology
**Why:** The radiologist and surgeon want to attempt reduction promptly, because success falls and necrosis rises with time.

**Anaesthesia:** These children lose fluid into oedematous bowel and by vomiting, and the classical triad (colic, vomiting, bloody stool) is often incomplete. Lethargy, pallor and floppiness between spasms are signs of shock and of the encephalopathy peculiar to this condition, and are regularly misread as a child who has settled. A cannula, 20 mL/kg of balanced crystalloid (repeated as needed), a glucose, antibiotics if the surgeon asks, and a nasogastric tube if there is obstruction all come before the fluoroscopy room. Peritonitis, free gas or refractory shock are contraindications to enema reduction and the child goes to theatre.

### 9. Air enema reduction
**Why:** Air is insufflated through a rectal catheter to a set pressure for a few minutes at a time, usually up to three attempts, until gas floods into the small bowel on the screen.

**Anaesthesia:** Whether you are involved at all varies. In the multinational series more than two-thirds of children had neither opioid nor sedation, and neither was independently associated with failure or perforation. Units that use anaesthetist-delivered deep sedation or general anaesthesia report high success (92% with propofol in one series of 131 attempts) and argue that a still, relaxed child helps; a small cohort found weak evidence for better success with esketamine than morphine. The evidence does not settle it. If you are asked, the considerations are these. The stomach is not empty and the abdomen is about to be inflated from below, which raises regurgitation risk and splints the diaphragm: the author's practice is a secured airway with a tracheal tube if anaesthesia is requested, and titrated ketamine or intranasal fentanyl with full monitoring if the request is for analgesia and sedation. Straining against the enema (a Valsalva) is thought to protect against perforation and is lost under relaxant; do not paralyse for the reduction itself. Monitoring and equipment in radiology must be to theatre standard (see [remote site anaesthesia](../a/a12-remote-site-sedation.html)).

### 10. Perforation during reduction
**Why:** The colon wall, already ischaemic, gives way under pressure and air fills the peritoneum.

**Anaesthesia:** A rare event (0.4%) with a specific emergency: tension pneumoperitoneum. The abdomen becomes drum-tight within seconds, the diaphragm cannot descend, venous return is cut off, and the child becomes hypoxic and pulseless. The treatment is immediate needle decompression of the abdomen with a wide-bore cannula in the midline above the umbilicus, by whoever is nearest; the radiologist should have one on the trolley. Then intubate if not already done, give fluid, and go to theatre.

### 11. Failed reduction: laparoscopy or laparotomy
**Why:** After a failed enema (sometimes after a delayed repeat attempt a few hours later), the surgeon reduces the intussusception at laparoscopy or through a right transverse incision, squeezing the apex back along the colon, then inspects the ileum for viability and a lead point and resects with primary anastomosis if necessary.

**Anaesthesia:** A small child, now obstructed for longer, part-resuscitated and distended with insufflated air. Decompress the stomach, use the controlled sequence from step 2, and expect to need further fluid at induction. Avoid nitrous oxide. Glucose-containing isotonic fluid as background (1-2.5% glucose) with boluses of glucose-free balanced solution for the circulation. Reduction of ischaemic bowel can be followed by a fall in blood pressure and a rise in lactate as it reperfuses. For open surgery, a caudal (1 mL/kg of levobupivacaine 0.25%, less reliable to this height above about two years) or a transversus abdominis plane block after induction covers the transverse incision; avoid a caudal in the septic child.

### 12. After the operation or the enema
**Why:** The surgeon watches for recurrence after reduction, and for abscess and ileus after perforated appendicitis.

**Anaesthesia:** Maintenance fluid is isotonic with glucose and potassium, as the paediatric guideline recommends, because hypotonic fluid in a child with pain, nausea and high antidiuretic hormone produces hyponatraemia. Recurrence after air enema is 8.5% overall and under 3% in the first 48 hours, so a returning colic on the ward is a recurrence until imaged. After appendicectomy, regular paracetamol and ibuprofen with oral opioid rescue for simple disease; a morphine infusion or patient-controlled analgesia for 24-48 hours after perforation.

## Pain plan

For appendicectomy the mechanism is laparoscopic ports over an inflamed peritoneum, which is why a 3 is given for a 40-minute operation. The plan is multimodal and systemic: intravenous paracetamol 15 mg/kg, an NSAID once the circulation is restored, dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV, port-site infiltration to a weight-based ceiling, and opioid titrated in recovery. For laparoscopic surgery a transversus abdominis plane block adds nothing measurable to good port infiltration in the one paediatric trial; for an open muscle-splitting incision it reduces morphine over 48 hours and is worth doing. No PROSPECT guideline exists for appendicectomy in children, so the choice of adjuncts is by extension from the adult card ([laparoscopic and open appendicectomy](laparoscopic-and-open-appendicectomy.html)) and author's judgement.

Severity tracks the disease more than the wounds. Simple appendicitis: oral paracetamol and ibuprofen regularly for three to five days, with one or two doses of oral opioid on the first night. Perforated appendicitis with peritonitis: morphine by nurse- or patient-controlled analgesia for 24-48 hours, regular paracetamol, an NSAID if renal function and perfusion allow, and antiemetics, with step-down as the ileus resolves.

For intussusception, analgesia before reduction is the neglected part: most children in the multinational series received none. Intranasal fentanyl 1.5 micrograms/kg or intravenous morphine 0.05-0.1 mg/kg while the cannula and fluid go in is humane and was not associated with failure or perforation. After a successful enema, paracetamol is enough. After laparotomy the plan is that of an infant abdominal-wall incision: a block, paracetamol, and a nurse-controlled opioid infusion.

Rebound is low: there is no dense block to wear off. Escalation triggers after appendicectomy are pain rising after day 2 with fever (pelvic collection), or bilious vomiting and distension (ileus or early adhesive obstruction).

## Pitfalls and variants

- The under-resuscitated child. Children maintain blood pressure until late; induction then removes the compensation. Tachycardia and prolonged capillary refill are the signs to act on before theatre.
- Classic rapid sequence induction. Hypoxaemia and bradycardia during an apnoeic wait are commoner and more dangerous in children than aspiration during gentle ventilation.
- The obese adolescent. Adult doses by ideal body weight, adult pressures, and a thromboprophylaxis assessment.
- Appendix mass or abscess. Often treated with antibiotics and percutaneous drainage, with or without interval appendicectomy; the drainage may need sedation or anaesthesia in radiology.
- Negative laparoscopy. Mesenteric adenitis, Meckel diverticulitis, ovarian torsion in girls: the last changes the urgency and the operation.
- Hyponatraemia. Hypotonic maintenance fluid in a vomiting, hurting child; avoid by using isotonic fluid and measuring sodium daily while on drip fluid.
- Sedation for enema outside a monitored environment. A shocked toddler given opioid and a sedative in a dark fluoroscopy room, with an abdomen being inflated, needs the same monitoring and staff as an anaesthetic.
- Delayed repeat enema. Some units re-attempt after a few hours if the first attempt moved the intussusceptum part-way and the child is stable; keep the child resuscitated and fasted in between.
- The older child with intussusception. Outside the usual age range a pathological lead point (lymphoma, polyp, Meckel diverticulum) is more likely, enema is less likely to succeed, and resection is more likely.
- Doses, tube sizes and fluid tables are in [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html). The neonatal causes of bilious vomiting are in [neonatal emergencies](neonatal-emergencies.html).

## Key sources

Paediatric appendicectomy has two small randomised trials of the transversus abdominis plane block pointing in opposite directions for open and laparoscopic surgery, which is consistent with the block treating wall pain only. The induction technique rests on a large single-centre cohort and an opinion review; no trial compares classic and controlled rapid sequence induction in children. For intussusception the evidence on sedation is observational: one large multinational cross-sectional study showing that most children receive nothing and that neither opioid nor sedation predicts harm, and small series supporting anaesthetist-delivered sedation. The 120 mmHg pressure cap, needle decompression for tension pneumoperitoneum, the protective role of straining, and the encephalopathic presentation are standard radiological and paediatric surgical teaching, stated here without a source. Antibiotic and analgesic doses are standard paediatric doses. The fluid recommendations are from an American guideline and a German guideline that agree with each other.
