## What the surgeon is doing and why

A transplanted kidney is not put where the old ones are. The native kidneys are left alone, and the graft is placed in the iliac fossa, outside the peritoneum, because that is where three things it needs lie within a hand's breadth of each other: the external iliac artery, the external iliac vein and the bladder. The surgeon makes a curved incision from above the pubis towards the anterior superior iliac spine, divides the oblique muscles and transversus, sweeps the peritoneum medially and exposes the iliac vessels, ligating the lymphatics over them so that they do not leak into a lymphocele.

The kidney arrives cold. From a deceased donor it has been flushed and stored on ice or a perfusion machine for hours (cold ischaemia); from a living donor it has come from the theatre next door minutes ago. It is prepared on a back table in ice slush, then brought into the wound. From that moment until blood flows it is rewarming without perfusion (the anastomosis time, a second warm ischaemia), so the surgeon sews quickly: renal vein end-to-side to the external iliac vein, then renal artery to the external iliac artery, each behind vascular clamps. The clamps come off, the kidney turns pink and firm, and with a good living donor graft urine appears within minutes. Finally the ureter is tunnelled into the bladder dome over a stent, and the wound is closed without compressing the graft.

The donor operation is a [laparoscopic nephrectomy](nephrectomy-and-partial-nephrectomy.html) with two differences that change everything about its conduct. The patient is healthy and gains nothing, so the tolerance for complications is nil. And the kidney must come out with the longest possible vessels, undamaged, perfused until the last second and then removed and flushed within a couple of minutes, so the hilum is divided last, not first.

For you the recipient operation is defined by the patient and by one moment. The patient has end-stage kidney disease: hypertension, coronary disease that is often silent, diabetes in many, anaemia, a potassium and a volume state that depend on when dialysis last happened, platelets that do not work well, and a fistula that is a lifeline. The moment is reperfusion. A transplanted kidney is denervated and has lost autoregulation; its flow follows arterial pressure. Cold, potassium-rich, acidic preservation fluid and blood pooled in the leg return to the circulation as the clamps open. Everything before that point is preparation for it.

The pain logic for the recipient is abdominal wall: a long unilateral muscle-cutting incision low in the abdomen, moderate in intensity, worst on day 1 with sitting and coughing, and gone in a few days. It is unilateral and within T10-L1, which is why a single-sided truncal block fits. The constraint is pharmacological: no NSAIDs, and opioids chosen for a kidney that may not work yet.

## Steps

### 1. Recipient assessment and the clock
**Why:** Living donor transplants are elective and scheduled. Deceased donor transplants are urgent: every hour of cold ischaemia raises the chance that the graft will not work immediately, so the recipient is called in, cross-matched and operated on within hours, often at night.
**Anaesthesia:** Establish when the last dialysis was and what came off; the potassium now; the dry weight against today's weight; and whether the patient still passes urine. A potassium above 6.0 mmol/L or frank overload needs dialysis first and the nephrologist decides, with the cold ischaemia time in view. Treat every long-term dialysis patient as having coronary disease. Note the fistula side, anticoagulants, and the immunosuppression protocol with its timings. A patient called in for a deceased donor kidney is not reliably fasted.

### 2. Induction, access and monitoring
**Why:** The surgeon needs relaxation for a deep iliac fossa exposure and a bladder catheter through which the bladder can be filled later.
**Anaesthesia:** Protect the fistula arm before anything else: no cuff, no cannula, wrapped and visible. Induce with whatever keeps the pressure stable; avoid suxamethonium if potassium is above 5.5 mmol/L. Cisatracurium does not depend on the kidney and is the choice that needs no qualification. Rocuronium with sugammadex is widely used in end-stage disease, but its status should be stated accurately: the product information does not recommend sugammadex when creatinine clearance is below 30 mL/min, including in patients on dialysis, because the drug and its complex with rocuronium are cleared by the kidney and persist far longer when it does not work. Using it here is therefore off-label and a matter of judgement and local policy. The data that exist are reassuring: pooled studies of 655 patients show recovery about a minute slower than with normal kidneys, reduced clearance of sugammadex, and no excess of recurrent block or adverse events, provided the response is measured with a quantitative monitor and the patient is watched in recovery. Avoid long-acting drugs that depend on the kidney. For opioid, fentanyl is the first choice because it has no active metabolites; oxycodone and hydromorphone are better than morphine, whose active metabolite accumulates, but are not its equal: both have renally cleared metabolites (hydromorphone-3-glucuronide is neuroexcitatory), so they are given in reduced doses at longer intervals until the graft is working. An arterial line (not in the fistula arm, and with thought for future fistula sites) is worth having for the reperfusion period; a central line is traditional for central venous pressure, which has not been shown to guide fluid usefully, and is justified mainly for vasopressor and induction antibody. Warm the patient.

### 3. Incision and exposure of the iliac vessels
**Why:** The extraperitoneal iliac fossa is opened and the external iliac artery and vein are dissected free.
**Anaesthesia:** A quiet 30-45 minutes that should be used to get the patient into shape for reperfusion. Give balanced crystalloid, not saline: in the Australasian trial of 808 deceased donor recipients, delayed graft function fell from 40% with saline to 30% with a balanced solution. How much is less clear. The traditional recipe of filling to a central venous pressure of 12-14 mmHg risks pulmonary oedema in patients with stiff ventricles and no urine output, and a meta-analysis of four small trials found no effect of goal-directed fluid on graft function. The author's practice is 20-30 mL/kg over the case in a patient at dry weight, less in one who is above it, guided by pressure, pulse pressure variation and the look of the neck veins. Immunosuppression is given now to the unit's timing.

### 4. Venous and arterial anastomoses
**Why:** The iliac vein and then artery are clamped and the graft vessels sewn on. The kidney is out of ice and rewarming; surgeons aim to finish both in 30-45 minutes.
**Anaesthesia:** Clamping the external iliac vessels has little haemodynamic effect. Use the time: mean arterial pressure brought up to the patient's normal level, a low-dose noradrenaline infusion running if needed to hold it, a blood gas with potassium checked, and calcium and insulin-glucose available. Some units give mannitol or furosemide before reperfusion; the practice rests on old and small studies, surgeons vary, and it is their call. Tell the surgeon the pressure before the clamps come off.

### 5. Reperfusion
**Why:** The venous clamp is released, then the arterial. Cold preservation fluid, potassium, acid and vasodilators wash out of the graft and the leg, the graft fills, and any gap in the anastomoses or untied hilar branch bleeds.
**Anaesthesia:** Expect a fall in pressure and be ahead of it: volume in, vasopressor bolus in hand. A mean arterial pressure at or above the patient's usual value (80-90 mmHg is a common target, higher in the chronically hypertensive) is the single thing you contribute to early graft function. Peaked T waves or arrhythmia in the first minutes is potassium, more likely with a long cold ischaemia time, and is treated with calcium. The surgeon reports the graft's colour and turgor; a soft, mottled or blue kidney with a good pressure is a surgical problem (a kinked vein or artery), and one with a poor pressure is yours.

### 6. Ureteric implantation
**Why:** The bladder is distended through the catheter so the surgeon can find it, and the ureter is sewn into the dome over a stent with an anti-reflux tunnel.
**Anaesthesia:** You or the nurse fill the bladder with 150-300 mL of saline through the catheter on request and unclamp it afterwards. Keep the pressure at target: this is when attention drifts and the anaesthetic is lightened into hypotension. Record the time urine first appears.

### 7. Closure and block
**Why:** The muscle layers are closed over the graft without tension, usually with a drain; a tight closure compresses the renal vein.
**Anaesthesia:** A sudden fall in urine output or rise in airway pressure at closure should be mentioned, because the graft can be compressed in a small pelvis. The TAP block is placed now on the graft side with ultrasound (20 mL of ropivacaine 0.375%), or the surgeon infiltrates the muscle layers under vision. Reverse the neuromuscular block against a quantitative monitor.

### 8. Emergence and the first hours
**Why:** The surgeon and nephrologist want a perfused graft, a measured urine output and a potassium.
**Anaesthesia:** Avoid a hypertensive, coughing emergence on fresh vascular suture lines, and avoid the opposite. In recovery the orders are specific: hourly urine output replaced by a formula (the previous hour's output plus a fixed amount, in balanced crystalloid), a potassium and gas within the hour, and a pressure target written down. A living donor graft may produce a litre an hour; replace it or the patient becomes hypovolaemic within hours. A graft that makes no urine may be delayed function (common with deceased donors and not an emergency), a blocked catheter, or vascular thrombosis; sudden anuria after good early output goes for an urgent Doppler.

### 9. The donor: before the kidney comes out
**Why:** The donor's kidney, usually the left for its longer vein, is mobilised laparoscopically with the ureter and gonadal vein, leaving the artery and vein intact until everything else is free.
**Anaesthesia:** A fit patient, lateral, flexed, with pneumoperitoneum, which reduces renal blood flow. Run fluid more generously than for a cancer nephrectomy, aiming for a brisk urine output before the vessels are divided, and keep the pressure normal. Mannitol and heparin before clamping are used in some units and not others. Neuromuscular block must be reliable at the hilum.

### 10. The donor: division of the hilum and extraction
**Why:** The artery then vein are stapled or clipped flush, and the kidney is delivered at once through a Pfannenstiel or hand-port incision and flushed with cold preservation solution on the back table.
**Anaesthesia:** Seconds matter and the theatre goes quiet. Be ready for the same vascular catastrophe as any nephrectomy, in a patient who volunteered. If heparin was given, protamine follows on request. Afterwards the case is routine closure. Donor analgesia is planned without NSAIDs by most units, on the reasoning that a single remaining kidney should not be exposed to them, though practice varies.

### 11. Donor recovery
**Why:** The donor's recovery is watched as closely as the recipient's, and their experience matters to the programme.
**Anaesthesia:** Donors report more pain than expected for a laparoscopic operation; treat it as a score 3 operation and not as a favour. Nausea, shoulder-tip pain and the first walk are the day 1 issues. Creatinine rises by a third to a half and settles at a new baseline.

## Pain plan

Recipient. The mechanism is a unilateral lower abdominal muscle-cutting incision, so the plan is a unilateral truncal block, paracetamol, and a patient-controlled opioid for 24-48 hours. A meta-analysis of ten trials of TAP block in recipients found a reduction in 24-hour opioid of about 14.6 mg of morphine, lower pain scores and less nausea, with high heterogeneity and probable publication bias; the block is low-risk and worth doing, and should not be oversold. Quadratus lumborum and erector spinae blocks are alternatives without transplant-specific advantages. The opioid matters more here than elsewhere: fentanyl is the first choice for the pump because it has no active metabolites; oxycodone and hydromorphone are acceptable and better than morphine, but their metabolites are cleared by the kidney, so until the graft is clearing creatinine the bolus is small, there is no background infusion, and sedation or myoclonus in a patient with delayed graft function is accumulation until shown otherwise. NSAIDs are contraindicated. Gabapentinoids accumulate in renal failure and are avoided or dose-reduced. Epidural analgesia is effective but is not the author's practice: platelet dysfunction, heparin, and the risk that epidural hypotension is treated with fluid in an anuric patient or lowers graft perfusion make the trade poor for an incision this size. Bladder spasm from the catheter and stent is treated with a low dose of an antimuscarinic.

Donor. The plan is that of laparoscopic nephrectomy: intrathecal morphine before induction is the best-supported technique there, and a fascial plane block the alternative. Intrathecal morphine carries the same condition as on that card: sedation score and respiratory rate recorded hourly for about 24 hours under the local protocol, with no long-acting opioid or sedative alongside, which matters more than usual in a volunteer. In donors specifically, an erector spinae plane block more than halved 24-hour morphine against wound infiltration in a blinded trial of 69 patients. Paracetamol, dexamethasone and oral opioid complete it; most units withhold NSAIDs.

Rebound is moderate for both when the block or spinal opioid fades on the first night. Escalation triggers in the recipient: increasing pain over the graft with swelling or falling haemoglobin is a perigraft haematoma; pain with sudden anuria is vascular thrombosis; pain with fever and wound drainage later in the week is a urine leak or lymphocele. There is no PROSPECT guideline for transplantation.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Hyperkalaemia. Pre-operative from missed dialysis, intra-operative from reperfusion and acidosis, post-operative from delayed graft function. Check before induction and after reperfusion.
- Volume. Both directions harm: an underfilled patient gives a hypoperfused graft, and an overfilled anuric patient with a stiff ventricle gets pulmonary oedema with no way to excrete it but dialysis.
- The fistula. A hypotensive episode or a misplaced cuff can thrombose it. Check the thrill before and after.
- Saline. The old argument that balanced solutions are dangerous because they contain potassium is answered by the trial data: saline caused more delayed graft function.
- Deceased donor transplants after hours. Unfasted, undialysed or just dialysed, cardiac work-up of variable age. This is the urgent variant, and the decision to proceed is shared with the nephrologist.
- Re-exploration. Bleeding or graft thrombosis in the first 24 hours brings back a patient who may be hypovolaemic, hyperkalaemic and recently heparinised; arterial line, blood, and a tracheal tube.
- Induction antibody reactions. Anti-thymocyte globulin can cause fever, hypotension and cytokine release during the case; it is given slowly through a central line after premedication.
- Diabetes. Gastroparesis, autonomic neuropathy with labile pressure, and steroid hyperglycaemia needing an insulin infusion.
- Paediatric and small adult recipients of adult kidneys. Reperfusion takes a large fraction of cardiac output; volume and pressure targets are higher relative to size.
- Simultaneous pancreas-kidney transplant. A laparotomy, longer, with glucose swings; not this card.
- Donor right nephrectomy, vascular anomalies and hand-assisted or open donor surgery. Longer hilar dissection and a larger incision; the pain plan moves towards the open nephrectomy plan.

## Key sources

The fluid choice is settled by a large, blinded, registry-embedded Australasian trial, the strongest evidence on this card. How much fluid to give and what to target is not settled: the goal-directed trials are few and small, and the traditional central venous pressure target has no outcome evidence. The reperfusion pressure target is physiological reasoning and consensus. The TAP block meta-analysis is positive but heterogeneous. Sugammadex data in end-stage disease are reassuring and mostly observational, and do not change the fact that the product information advises against its use below a creatinine clearance of 30 mL/min. The donor block trial is a single centre. Statements on mannitol, furosemide and heparin describe variation in practice, not recommendations.
