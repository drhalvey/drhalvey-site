## What the surgeon is doing and why

A deep burn is dead skin attached to a living patient. The dead layer (eschar) cannot heal, is a culture medium, and drives the inflammatory and hypermetabolic response for as long as it stays on. Burn surgery removes it early, usually within the first few days, and replaces it with something that closes the wound. Early excision and grafting shortened hospital stay and reduced sepsis compared with waiting for eschar to separate, and it is the reason you meet these patients in theatre while they are still physiologically unstable.

Removal is done in one of two ways. Tangential excision shaves the burn in thin layers with a guarded knife or a hydrosurgical tool until the surgeon reaches tissue that is alive, and the sign of living dermis or fat is that it bleeds from many points at once. The end point of the operation is therefore brisk diffuse bleeding over the whole excised area. Fascial excision takes skin and fat together down to the muscle fascia with diathermy; it bleeds much less and takes a graft reliably, at the price of a contour defect, and is used for very deep or very large burns.

Cover is the patient's own split-thickness skin when there are donor sites to spare: harvested with a dermatome, usually meshed so that it expands to cover one and a half to four times its area, and stapled or glued on. When the burn is larger than the available donor skin, the excised wound is covered temporarily with cadaveric allograft or a synthetic dermal substitute, and autograft follows in stages as donor sites heal and can be re-cropped.

```
 burn  ->  excise to bleeding tissue  ->  cover
            (tangential or fascial)       autograft (meshed)
            blood loss, heat loss         allograft or dermal template
                                          then return in 2-4 days
 donor site = a new partial-thickness wound
```

A major burn is not one operation but a campaign: theatre every two to four days, each session limited by area, time or blood loss, with baths and dressing changes in between that are operations in everything but name.

The pain logic is the most complex in the book. Background pain comes from the burn and its sensitised margins. Each operation swaps some burn for a graft (less painful) and creates a donor site (more painful). Procedural pain from dressings and therapy is re-provoked daily. Tolerance to opioids develops within the first week, and neuropathic features and itch follow as wounds heal. This is skin and soft tissue pain made severe and prolonged by area, repetition and sensitisation, which is why the plan is systemic first, with regional techniques aimed at the donor site.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative review: which phase and how much
**Why:** The surgeon states the area to be excised, the donor sites, the positions and whether a tourniquet or infiltration will be used. That is the estimate of blood loss.
**Anaesthesia:** Establish the day since injury. In the first 48 hours the patient is in the resuscitation phase with low cardiac output, high resistance and capillary leak; formula-based fluid is only a starting point and is titrated to urine output and perfusion. From day 2-5 the hyperdynamic, hypermetabolic phase begins: tachycardia, low resistance, a core temperature reset to about 38.5 degrees, high oxygen consumption and faster clearance of many drugs. Check for inhalation injury, the ventilator settings, the haemoglobin, potassium, coagulation and albumin, the current opioid and sedative doses, and where the lines can go. Electrical burns carry more muscle damage than the skin suggests: look for myoglobinuria and compartment problems. Keep the fast short: enteral feeding supports a metabolic rate that can be twice normal, and in an intubated patient with a post-pyloric tube many units feed through surgery.

### 2. Induction and the airway
**Why:** Access to the face, neck and chest may be compromised by the burn itself, by oedema that peaks at 24-48 hours, or later by contracture.
**Anaesthesia:** A small limb burn in a well patient is a routine anaesthetic. A recent facial or neck burn or an inhalation injury can swell unpredictably: if the patient is already intubated, guard that tube through every move, and if not, assess as a potentially difficult airway with a videolaryngoscope and a smaller tube ready. Old burns bring microstomia and a fixed, flexed neck. Secure the tube with tape ties, a wire to a tooth or a suture. Suxamethonium is unsafe from about 48 hours after injury until the wounds have healed, because up-regulated acetylcholine receptors spread across the muscle membrane and release potassium on depolarisation. Non-depolarising relaxants are resisted for the same reason: use rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg for a rapid sequence, expect a slower onset and shorter duration, and monitor.

### 3. Access and monitoring through damaged skin
**Why:** Excision and harvest remove or cover the usual sites for cannulae, electrodes and cuffs.
**Anaesthesia:** Lines can go through burned skin when there is no alternative, and should be sutured. For excisions above about 10% have two large cannulae or a multi-lumen central line with a rapid infuser, and an arterial line for pressure and sampling. Electrodes will not stick to burned or greasy skin: use needle electrodes or attach to surgical staples. A cuff over a dressing reads acceptably; an oximeter goes on an ear, the nose or the tongue. A urinary catheter with temperature sensing serves two purposes.

### 4. Temperature and positioning
**Why:** The surgeon needs wide exposure of burn and donor sites, often on opposite surfaces of the body.
**Anaesthesia:** A patient without skin loses heat by evaporation and radiation at several times the normal rate, and the hypermetabolic patient defends a higher set point, so 36 degrees is already a deficit. Raise the theatre to 28-32 degrees before the patient arrives, warm every fluid, cover everything not being operated on, and ask for areas to be exposed in sequence. Hypothermia worsens coagulopathy during an operation whose end point is bleeding. Turns are done as a team with the tube held; re-check lines and monitoring after each.

### 5. Infiltration and tourniquets
**Why:** To reduce bleeding the surgeon may inject dilute adrenaline solution under the burn and the donor site, apply adrenaline-soaked swabs after each pass, and operate on limbs under tourniquet.
**Anaesthesia:** Litres of infiltrate count as fluid given and, if local anaesthetic is added, as a dose to be totalled. Absorbed adrenaline gives tachycardia and hypertension that mask hypovolaemia: a normal pressure during excision is not reassurance. With a tourniquet the loss is deferred to deflation, when it arrives together with the reperfusion load.

### 6. Tangential excision
**Why:** The burn is shaved until the bed bleeds uniformly, which defines viable tissue.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the haemorrhagic step and it is fast. In small burns the median loss was about 50 mL per percent of body surface excised, and loss per percent rises with larger, later and infected excisions. Start transfusing early in any excision above 10%, on the surgeon's progress and not on the blood pressure. Track haemoglobin, base deficit, ionised calcium and temperature every 20-30 minutes in large cases, and move to balanced plasma and platelet replacement once red cell transfusion passes about four units. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV before the start reduced blood loss and transfusion in a meta-analysis of four small trials. Agree a stopping rule with the surgeon in advance (area, time, units transfused or temperature) and call it when it is reached. Manipulating colonised eschar releases bacteria and mediators: a fall in pressure with a rise in temperature during excision is bacteraemia as often as bleeding.

### 7. Haemostasis
**Why:** A graft will not take on a bleeding bed, so the surgeon stops the ooze with adrenaline swabs, pressure, diathermy and sometimes fibrin sealant before applying skin.
**Anaesthesia:** A pause that lets you catch up. Use it to correct volume, calcium, temperature and coagulation, and to reassess the vasopressor: these patients are vasodilated at baseline and a low-dose noradrenaline infusion is often already running.

### 8. Donor harvest
**Why:** Split-thickness skin is taken from unburned areas (thigh, buttock, back, scalp in children) with a powered dermatome and meshed.
**Anaesthesia:** A further bleeding surface and the origin of the worst postoperative pain. If the thigh is the donor, a fascia iliaca or lateral femoral cutaneous nerve block is the technique with trial support: in a small randomised study, median donor pain in the postoperative phase was 0 with a fascia iliaca block and 6 without. Place it before harvest through intact skin, single shot or as a catheter if the patient is due back in theatre. Where no block is possible, ask for tumescent infiltration of the donor site with dilute long-acting local anaesthetic and adrenaline.

### 9. Graft application, dressings and splints
**Why:** Autograft, allograft or dermal substitute is laid on and fixed with staples, glue or sutures, then covered with a bulky or negative pressure dressing and splinted across joints so that it cannot shear.
**Anaesthesia:** Dressing a major burn takes 30-45 minutes of lifting limbs and rolling the trunk. Keep the patient anaesthetised and warm until it is finished; stimulation is intermittent and the temptation is to wake too early. Give the planned analgesic loading now: ketamine and opioid infusions adjusted, paracetamol, and the block topped up.

### 10. Emergence and transfer
**Why:** Fresh grafts are lost to shear, so the surgeon wants a calm patient who does not thrash against splints.
**Anaesthesia:** Ventilated intensive care patients return sedated on their existing infusions, at doses that reflect tolerance. For those waking in theatre, aim for comfort before consciousness: these patients need opioid doses well above standard and wake in severe pain if given a naive regimen. Transfer under warm covers, and hand over blood given, last haemoglobin and potassium, temperature and the analgesic plan.

### 11. Between operations: dressings, baths and the return
**Why:** Grafts are inspected at about five days; dressings are changed every one to three days; the next excision is already planned.
**Anaesthesia:** Dressing changes are where pain control succeeds or fails. They are predictable and repeated, so they can be planned: extra opioid beforehand, ketamine, inhaled analgesia, and for large changes a formal sedation or general anaesthetic in theatre or the bath room. The same patient comes back every few days, which is a reason to minimise fasting each time, to keep one line of documentation on what airway technique and doses worked, and to place catheters that will serve several sessions.

## Pain plan

The mechanisms are background burn pain, donor site pain and procedural pain, layered on rapid opioid tolerance and central sensitisation. No single block reaches all of that, so the strategy is systemic and layered, and the "block" is an adjunct for the part that has a nerve supply you can reach.

Base layer: regular paracetamol; opioid by infusion or patient-controlled analgesia in the acute phase, converted to long-acting oral opioid with breakthrough doses, at whatever dose is effective, since requirements are well above normal; ketamine by low-dose infusion for its opioid-sparing and anti-hyperalgesic effect; an alpha-2 agonist (clonidine or dexmedetomidine) for sedation, analgesia and the sympathetic drive. Gabapentinoids are widely used once neuropathic features and itch appear. NSAIDs are used selectively: renal function, the stress ulcer risk and graft bed bleeding all count against them in a major burn, and they are reasonable in small burns. There is no PROSPECT guideline for burn surgery; these choices follow the review literature and common burns unit practice.

Donor site: the defensible default for a thigh harvest is a fascia iliaca block, or a lateral femoral cutaneous nerve block for a purely lateral harvest, because the pain is from a superficial wound in a defined nerve territory and the one randomised trial showed a large effect. A retrospective protocol study found lower opioid use from the day of surgery with fascia iliaca catheters. The author's practice is a catheter when the thigh will be harvested and another session is planned within the week, and a single shot otherwise. Catheters go through unburned, uncolonised skin and come out at the first sign of infection.

Limb burns: a plexus or peripheral catheter can cover excision, grafting, dressing changes and physiotherapy on one limb and is the one situation where regional anaesthesia treats the burn itself.

Expect worst pain of 4 on the evening of surgery, 6 on days 1 and 2 as the donor site declares itself and the first dressing is disturbed, 5 on day 3 and still 4 at a week, when the next operation is usually already done. Rebound when a single-shot block recedes is high in a tolerant patient: increase the background opioid before the block wears off, not after. Escalation triggers: a sudden change in the character of pain with fever or a rising heart rate is infection or graft loss; burning, shooting pain and itch in healed areas is neuropathic and needs its own drugs; increasing opioid with decreasing effect calls for ketamine, rotation and the pain service, not more of the same.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Escharotomy. The emergency variant: circumferential full-thickness burns of the chest restrict ventilation and of a limb cut off its circulation as oedema builds under inelastic eschar. The release incisions are made through insensate burn, often at the bedside in the first hours; they bleed, and the patient may need to be anaesthetised for an extension into a fasciotomy.
- The first 48 hours. Surgery in the resuscitation phase means capillary leak, airway oedema still developing, and carbon monoxide or cyanide poisoning not yet excluded. Suxamethonium is still usable in the first day.
- Inhalation injury. Raises fluid requirement and mortality, and the lungs worsen over days. Use protective ventilation; transport and theatre ventilators may not match the intensive care settings.
- Massive transfusion. Planned, not a surprise: a rapid infuser primed, blood checked and in the room, calcium drawn up.
- Hypermetabolism and drugs. Higher doses of opioids, sedatives and non-depolarising relaxants; antibiotics and anticoagulants cleared faster; albumin low. Dose to effect and monitor.
- Sepsis. The commonest cause of death after the first days. A septic burn patient coming for excision of the source is a high-risk anaesthetic with vasopressors running; the surgery is the treatment.
- Children. Scalds are the common injury; relatively larger head surface area, faster heat loss, small blood volume so that a modest excision is a large fractional loss, and consideration of non-accidental injury. Intranasal and oral premedication matter when there is no easy vein.
- The elderly. Thin skin makes burns deeper and donor sites slow to heal, and physiological reserve for staged surgery is limited; mortality rises steeply with age for a given burn size.
- Small burns. A 2% hand or forearm burn in a fit adult is a day case under a brachial plexus block or general anaesthetic with a thigh donor block, and a pain score of 3.
- Reconstructive surgery months later. Contracture release under very different conditions: a healed patient, but a neck or mouth that may no longer open. See [skin lesion excision, skin graft and local flaps](skin-lesion-excision-skin-graft-and.html) for grafting in the elective setting.
- Thresholds. Arterial line, blood in theatre and a critical care bed for any excision over about 10% in an adult, any major burn, inhalation injury or sepsis; none of them for a small limb burn.
