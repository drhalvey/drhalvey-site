## What the surgeon is doing and why

Pelvic organ prolapse is a hernia of the pelvic floor. The vagina is a tube held in place at three levels: at the top by the uterosacral and cardinal ligaments, which suspend the cervix and vault from the sacrum and pelvic side wall; in the middle by fascia attached laterally to the pelvic wall, which supports the bladder in front and the rectum behind; and at the bottom by the perineal body and levator muscles. Childbirth, age and loss of oestrogen stretch or tear these, and the organs behind the weak point bulge into the vagina: the bladder through the anterior wall (cystocele), the rectum through the posterior wall (rectocele), and the uterus or vault down the middle. The patient feels a lump, drags, cannot empty her bladder or bowel properly, and is usually between 60 and 85.

The surgeon repairs each level through the vagina, with no abdominal incision. A descended uterus is removed from below: the cervix is circumcised, the peritoneum is entered in front (between bladder and uterus) and behind (the pouch of Douglas), and the ligaments and vessels are clamped, cut and tied from the bottom up, the reverse order of an [abdominal hysterectomy](abdominal-hysterectomy-and-myomectomy.html), until the uterus is delivered. The vault then has to be resuspended or it will prolapse again, either to the uterosacral ligaments high in the pelvis or to the sacrospinous ligament, a tough band running from the ischial spine to the sacrum, reached by tunnelling through the pararectal space. An anterior repair opens the vaginal skin over the bladder, plicates the fascia underneath in the midline and trims the excess skin; a posterior repair does the same over the rectum and usually rebuilds the perineal body, bringing the separated perineal muscles together. Colpocleisis, for the frail woman who is no longer sexually active, simply sews the anterior and posterior vaginal walls together and closes the canal: quick, effective and tolerable under local anaesthesia. A mid-urethral sling for stress incontinence is a tape passed under the urethra through small incisions and may be added.

All of this is done in high lithotomy, in a confined, vascular field that the surgeon first distends with a large volume of dilute local anaesthetic and adrenaline to open the tissue planes and reduce ooze.

The pain logic follows from the absence of an abdominal wall wound. What hurts is deeper and more varied: the tied pedicles and vault give a central pelvic ache like any hysterectomy; the plicated fascia and levator muscles ache and spasm; the perineal suture line is a small wound in the place the patient sits on and strains through; and a sacrospinous suture sits beside the pudendal nerve and the sacral nerve roots, giving a buttock and posterior thigh pain on that side that is common, sometimes sharp, and can last several weeks. None of it is loaded by coughing. Scores are moderate for two days and the opioid requirement is low: the randomised trials of added pudendal blocks report median day 1 scores of about 4 out of 10 and around 8 mg of morphine equivalents in 24 hours in their control groups. The route does not protect against chronic pain: in the Danish cohort vaginal hysterectomy was not significantly better than abdominal.

## Steps

### 1. The preoperative patient
**Why:** Prolapse surgery is elective quality-of-life surgery in the elderly; the surgeon has usually tried a pessary first and may have prescribed vaginal oestrogen to thicken the tissues.
**Anaesthesia:** Assess the patient, not the operation. Anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs decide whether a spinal is possible and when; get the plan written before the day. A murmur in an 80-year-old needs an echocardiogram before a spinal in lithotomy, not after. Screen for cognitive impairment and frailty, because delirium is the complication that lengthens stay, and its triggers (opioids, anticholinergics for the bladder, urinary retention, a catheter, sleep loss) are all present. Check hip and knee range: a replaced or fused hip limits the lithotomy position and should be tested awake.

### 2. Choice of anaesthetic
**Why:** The surgeon needs a relaxed pelvic floor, a still patient and 1-2 hours; no abdominal relaxation and no pneumoperitoneum are required.
**Anaesthesia:** A spinal covers the whole operation, provides the pelvic floor relaxation, and delivers the patient to recovery awake, pain-free and with her usual cognition; it is the author's default. Registry data show that only about 3% of vaginal hysterectomies and 6% of obliterative procedures are done under neuraxial anaesthesia in North America and that 30-day complications are the same either way, so this is preference and patient selection, not proven superiority. Use hyperbaric bupivacaine 0.5% 2.2-2.8 mL with fentanyl 15 micrograms for a hysterectomy (the peritoneum is opened and the uterus pulled on, so aim for T8 or higher) and 1.8-2.2 mL for repairs alone. Add intrathecal morphine 100 micrograms for the hysterectomy in a patient staying overnight with a catheter; omit it in the very elderly, in sleep apnoea and for repairs alone. Sedate lightly, with propofol at low rate, and avoid benzodiazepines in the over-70s. General anaesthesia with a supraglottic device is equally acceptable and is the better choice when the spinal is contraindicated, the patient cannot lie still, or a laparoscopic step is planned.

### 3. Lithotomy
**Why:** High lithotomy with the buttocks over the edge and slight head-down tilt gives the surgeon and two assistants room around the perineum.
**Anaesthesia:** Lifting both legs returns blood centrally and partly offsets the spinal's sympathetic block; lowering them at the end removes it, and the pressure falls in a vasodilated, slightly hypovolaemic patient. Lower one leg at a time and have a vasopressor drawn up. The common peroneal nerve at the fibular head, the femoral and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves from hip hyperflexion, and the sciatic from hip flexion with knee extension are the injuries of a long case; an awake patient under spinal cannot report them. Assistants lean on the legs. Check position again after the surgeon has adjusted the stirrups.

### 4. Infiltration with local anaesthetic and adrenaline
**Why:** Fluid injected under the vaginal epithelium lifts it off the fascia (hydrodissection) and the adrenaline reduces ooze from a very vascular surface.
**Anaesthesia:** Volumes are large, 20-60 mL in total across the operation, and the vagina absorbs quickly. Know the concentration: lidocaine 0.5-1% with adrenaline 1:200,000 is typical, and the total can approach the weight-based maximum in a 50 kg woman, on top of any intrathecal dose. Tachycardia, hypertension and ventricular ectopy within two minutes are absorbed adrenaline and pass in five; in a patient with coronary disease or a tight aortic valve ask for a more dilute solution or plain saline before the first injection. Some surgeons use vasopressin analogues instead, with the bradycardia and hypertension described on the [abdominal hysterectomy](abdominal-hysterectomy-and-myomectomy.html) card.

### 5. Vaginal hysterectomy
**Why:** Cervix circumcised, bladder pushed up, anterior and posterior peritoneum opened, then uterosacral and cardinal ligaments, uterine vessels and upper pedicles clamped and tied in sequence from below; the uterus is delivered, sometimes bisected to get it out, and the ovaries are usually left.
**Anaesthesia:** Traction on the uterus and opening the peritoneum produce visceral discomfort, nausea and bradycardia in a patient whose spinal has not reached the upper lumbar and lower thoracic segments; a small dose of fentanyl or deepening the sedation covers it, and a block that is clearly too low is a reason to convert early. The upper pedicle is tied at the limit of the surgeon's reach: if it slips it retracts into the abdomen, the field looks dry, and the patient becomes hypotensive in recovery. A laparoscopy or laparotomy to retrieve a lost pedicle changes the anaesthetic to tracheal intubation and relaxant at short notice.

### 6. Vault suspension: uterosacral or sacrospinous
**Why:** The vault is fixed either to the uterosacral ligaments from inside the peritoneal cavity or to the sacrospinous ligament through the pararectal space, usually on the right, with one or two permanent or slowly absorbed sutures placed two fingers medial to the ischial spine.
**Anaesthesia:** The pudendal neurovascular bundle runs behind the ischial spine and the inferior gluteal vessels cross the upper edge of the ligament: a suture or retractor into either produces bleeding in a space that cannot be seen and is controlled by packing, and occasionally by embolisation. Unexpected blood loss at this step is that. The same suture produces the buttock pain: warn the patient beforehand, and treat immediate severe buttock or leg pain with numbness or weakness in recovery as a trapped nerve that the surgeon needs to know about that day. Uterosacral suspension can kink a ureter, so the surgeon checks both jets at cystoscopy; a request for a dye or a small fluid bolus comes here.

### 7. Anterior repair
**Why:** The vaginal skin is opened in the midline, the bladder dissected off it, the fascia plicated to push the bladder back, and the skin trimmed and closed.
**Anaesthesia:** Venous ooze from the bladder base, more infiltration, and little else. Haematuria suggests a bladder injury.

### 8. Posterior repair and perineorrhaphy
**Why:** The posterior wall is opened from the perineum upward, the rectum dissected off, the rectovaginal fascia and levator edges plicated, and the perineal body rebuilt.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the part the patient will feel most: levator plication aches and spasms, and the perineal wound is sat on. Ask the surgeon to infiltrate the perineal body and levator edges with plain long-acting local anaesthetic (ropivacaine 0.5% 10-20 mL, within the cumulative dose) before closing. A diclofenac suppository placed here is common practice, but only with the surgeon's agreement and never through a fresh rectal repair without it.

### 9. Variants: colpocleisis and mid-urethral sling
**Why:** Colpocleisis strips rectangles of vaginal skin front and back and sews the walls together. A retropubic or transobturator sling passes a tape behind the pubis or through the obturator foramina with trocars.
**Anaesthesia:** Colpocleisis in a frail 88-year-old is the case for local infiltration with minimal sedation or a low-dose spinal: 45 minutes, little blood loss, home the next day. A retropubic sling trocar passes blind behind the pubic bone beside the bladder and, rarely, into the external iliac or obturator vessels: hypotension in recovery after a sling is a retropubic haematoma until excluded.

### 10. Pack, catheter and legs down
**Why:** A gauze pack compresses the suture lines overnight and a catheter drains the bladder past the swelling.
**Anaesthesia:** The pack is uncomfortable and hides bleeding. Record the estimated loss with scepticism and look at the patient, not the number. Lower the legs slowly, one at a time. Paracetamol and the NSAID (if renal function and age allow) are charted regularly from now; in the elderly with borderline renal function, a short course of a COX-2 inhibitor or paracetamol alone is the safer base.

### 11. Recovery and the first night
**Why:** The surgeon wants the patient sitting, eating and walking that evening, the pack and catheter out next morning, and a successful void before discharge.
**Anaesthesia:** As the spinal recedes over 3-4 hours, pain rises to its day 1 level without a sharp rebound; oral oxycodone 2.5-5 mg as needed is enough for most, and many need none. The three problems of the night are delirium, hypotension and retention. Keep opioid doses small, avoid sedatives and anticholinergics, return glasses and hearing aids, and treat pain that keeps the patient awake. A soaked pack, a falling pressure or a rising heart rate is a vault or pararectal haematoma. Failure to void after catheter removal is common after anterior repair and slings and is managed with a catheter and a later trial, not by waiting in pain.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is visceral and deep somatic without an abdominal wall wound, so a neuraxial block for the operation plus a simple oral regimen afterwards matches it. The defensible default is a spinal (alone or under light general anaesthesia), the surgeon's infiltration, paracetamol and an anti-inflammatory given regularly, and a low-dose oral opioid as needed for two or three days. Laxatives belong in the pain plan: the first hard stool through a fresh posterior repair is the most painful event of the first week.

Intrathecal morphine 100 micrograms is the author's option for vaginal hysterectomy in a robust patient staying overnight; there is no trial of it in this operation among the sources found, and the low opioid requirement in the control arms of the pudendal block trials suggests that many patients do not need it. It is left out for repairs alone and in the frail.

Pudendal nerve block looks logical, since the pudendal nerve supplies the perineum and lower vagina, and has been tested three times against sham or usual care. In 71 women, pain was equivalent in recovery and on day 4, with a day 1 difference that missed the trial's own threshold. In 44 women on an enhanced recovery pathway, bupivacaine 0.5% 20 mL made no difference to opioid use or pain at 24 and 48 hours. In 75 women having native tissue repairs, levator injections with pudendal blocks gave the same day 1 score as saline. The consistent reading is that once paracetamol, an NSAID and infiltration are in place, a pudendal block adds little, probably because much of the pain is from structures the pudendal nerve does not supply (the vault, the pedicles, the sacrospinous ligament).

Buttock pain after sacrospinous fixation is managed with reassurance that it is expected, regular anti-inflammatory if permitted, and time; it usually fades over 2-6 weeks. Pain that is immediate, severe and accompanied by numbness or weakness in the pudendal or sciatic distribution is nerve entrapment and may need the suture removed.

Rebound is not a feature of a receding spinal in this operation. Escalation triggers: escalating perineal or rectal pressure with a tense swelling is a haematoma; pain with fever and offensive discharge from day 3 is a vault infection; inability to void with suprapubic pain is retention, the commonest cause of distress on the first night.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Severe aortic stenosis or poor ventricular function. A full-dose single-shot spinal plus lithotomy changes is a poor combination. Options are general anaesthesia with an arterial line, a low-dose or incremental spinal, or local infiltration with sedation for the shorter operations. Dilute or omit the adrenaline.
- Anticoagulants. Common and decisive for the spinal; if the interval is inadequate, general anaesthesia is the plan and the restart time is agreed with the surgeon, who is worried about a vault haematoma.
- Conversion to laparoscopy or laparotomy. A lost pedicle, a uterus too large to deliver, or dense adhesions in the pouch of Douglas. An awake patient under a T8 spinal needs converting to general anaesthesia with a tube before the abdomen is inflated.
- Laparoscopic or robotic sacrocolpopexy. The abdominal alternative for vault prolapse: mesh from the vault to the sacral promontory, hours of steep head-down, and the plan on the [laparoscopic hysterectomy](laparoscopic-and-robotic-hysterectomy.html) card in an older patient.
- Mesh and sling complications. Removal of eroded or painful mesh is a longer, bloodier dissection in a patient who often has chronic pelvic pain and opioid use; plan it as a chronic pain case.
- Lithotomy neuropathy. Foot drop or a numb thigh on day 1 after a two-hour case under spinal is positional until proven otherwise; document and refer early.
- Postoperative delirium. Predictable, common and partly preventable in this age group; the spinal helps only if the sedation is light.
- Urinary retention and intrathecal opioid. Both the surgery and the drug cause it; with a catheter in overnight it rarely matters, but it can delay a same-day pathway.
- Obesity. Access is harder for the surgeon, the head-down lithotomy is harder on ventilation, and a spinal with the patient awake and breathing for herself is often the easier anaesthetic.

## Key sources

Evidence specific to anaesthesia for vaginal surgery is thin. The comparisons of regional and general anaesthesia are registry studies with propensity matching, which show equivalence of 30-day complications and say nothing about pain, delirium or satisfaction; the preference for a spinal on this card is the author's judgement. The pudendal block conclusion rests on three small randomised trials that agree with each other. The chronic pain figures come from one national cohort. The statements about lithotomy physiology and nerve injury, adrenaline infiltration, sacrospinous buttock pain, bleeding sites and delirium are standard teaching and are unsourced.
