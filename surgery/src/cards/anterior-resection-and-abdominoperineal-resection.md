## What the surgeon is doing and why

The rectum lies in a narrow bony funnel, wrapped in its own fatty envelope of lymphatics and vessels (the mesorectum), which is in turn enclosed by a thin fascia. Rectal cancer spreads first into that envelope. The operation that cures it is total mesorectal excision: the rectum and its mesorectum removed as one intact package by sharp dissection in the bloodless plane just outside the mesorectal fascia, all the way down to the pelvic floor. Stray inside the plane and tumour is left behind; stray outside it and the surgeon is in the presacral veins behind, the hypogastric nerves and pelvic plexus at the sides, and the prostate or vagina in front. Everything about the operation's length, position and morbidity follows from the fact that this dissection is done at arm's length in a space a few centimetres wide, often after radiotherapy has fused the planes.

What happens next depends on how low the tumour is:

- Anterior resection. The sphincters are kept. The rectum is divided below the tumour with a stapler, the left colon is brought down (which needs the inferior mesenteric artery divided and the splenic flexure freed for length) and joined to the rectal stump or the anal canal with a circular stapler passed through the anus. The lower the join, the higher the leak rate; in a national registry the leak rate after anterior resection was 8-9%, and three in four patients were given a temporary loop ileostomy to divert the stream while it healed.
- Abdominoperineal resection. When the tumour involves the sphincters or the levators, the anus goes too. After the abdominal phase a second team, or the same team after re-positioning, cuts around the anus from below, through the ischioanal fat and the levator muscles, to meet the pelvic dissection; the specimen comes out through the perineum, the perineal wound is closed (primarily, with mesh, or with a flap) and the patient has a permanent end colostomy. The extralevator version takes a wider cuff of levator and is often done prone.
- Hartmann's procedure. Resection with an end colostomy and a closed rectal stump, for the patient whose sphincters or physiology would not tolerate a low join.

The approach is laparoscopic, robotic, transanal (dissecting the lowest part from below), or open. Robotic assistance was expected to make the deep pelvis easier; the ROLARR trial found no significant reduction in conversion to open and no difference in complications or nerve-related outcomes, so the choice is the surgeon's and the anaesthetic consequences are those of a long head-down laparoscopy either way.

Pain logic. The abdominal part hurts like a colectomy: port sites and an extraction wound if minimally invasive, a long midline wound if open, plus a stoma trephine cut through the rectus muscle, which patients often rate as the sorest abdominal site. The pelvic dissection adds a deep, poorly localised visceral ache and bladder spasm for 24-48 hours. An abdominoperineal resection adds the perineal wound: divided levators and a large dead space closed under some tension, in a field that has usually been irradiated, sat on with every meal and prone to breakdown. That wound gives the operation its tail and its score of 4.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative state: what neoadjuvant treatment has done
**Why:** Most low and locally advanced rectal cancers are treated with chemoradiotherapy, or a full course of chemotherapy and radiotherapy, before surgery. The surgeon operates 6-12 weeks later in a fibrotic field.
**Anaesthesia:** Expect anaemia, weight loss, a degree of deconditioning, peripheral neuropathy from oxaliplatin and, after fluoropyrimidines, occasional coronary vasospasm in the history. Radiotherapy makes the dissection slower and wetter and raises the leak rate. Stoma siting has been done by the stoma nurse; the marks tell you which stoma is planned. Bowel preparation with oral antibiotics is usual, so the patient is dry.

### 2. Induction, intrathecal morphine, lines
**Why:** The case is long, the arms will be out of reach, and the pelvic phase needs deep relaxation and a stable circulation in an extreme position.
**Anaesthesia:** Intrathecal morphine before induction is the author's default for a minimally invasive resection: one injection covers the abdominal wounds, the pelvic visceral pain and the perineum for the first night. An epidural at T9-T11 is the choice for a planned open operation. Tracheal tube taped securely and its depth noted. Arterial line and two cannulae, because four to six hours of head-down tilt with tucked arms is not the setting in which to be looking for a vein. Urinary catheter, sometimes placed by the surgeon with ureteric stents when the pelvis is hostile. Warming from the start.

### 3. Lloyd-Davies positioning and the tilt test
**Why:** The legs are abducted and flexed in supports so that the perineum is accessible for the stapler or the perineal dissection, and the table is tilted 25-30 degrees or more head-down so the small bowel leaves the pelvis.
**Anaesthesia:** Hips flexed no more than needed, calves free of pressure, heels seated in the boots, no pressure on the fibular head. A non-slip mattress and a chest strap prevent sliding; shoulder braces, if used at all, sit laterally on the acromion. Tilt to the working position before draping and re-check tube depth, airway pressure and the pressure areas. Tape the eyes and pad them: corneal abrasion and conjunctival oedema are common after hours head-down. The legs are now above the heart in a patient whose arterial pressure you may be about to lower: this is the mechanism of well-leg compartment syndrome, and the reported risk factors are obesity, large calves, peripheral vascular disease, head-down tilt and time.

### 4. Abdominal phase: vessels, left colon and splenic flexure
**Why:** The inferior mesenteric artery and vein are divided to release the left colon and take the nodal pedicle; the left colon and usually the splenic flexure are freed so the colon reaches the pelvis without tension.
**Anaesthesia:** As for a left colectomy ([colectomy card](laparoscopic-and-open-colectomy.html)): bleeding from the pedicle or the spleen, changes of tilt for the flexure, steady-state effects of the pneumoperitoneum. The left ureter and the hypogastric nerves are identified here.

### 5. Pelvic dissection: total mesorectal excision
**Why:** Sharp dissection in the mesorectal plane, posteriorly first down the front of the sacrum, then laterally and anteriorly, to the pelvic floor.
**Anaesthesia:** The long phase: two to four hours head-down with gas. Compliance is at its worst, and peak pressures in an obese patient may force a compromise on tilt or insufflation pressure; pressure-controlled ventilation, PEEP and repeated recruitment help, and a lower insufflation pressure helps more. End-tidal to arterial CO2 gradient widens, so take a gas. Facial, conjunctival and laryngeal oedema accumulate. Fluid runs to the head, so keep the volume modest during this phase and give what is owed once the patient is level. The surgeon needs complete stillness: a cough moves the pelvis onto the scissors. The specific catastrophe is presacral venous bleeding. The basivertebral veins open directly into the sacrum, cannot be clamped, and bleed at high flow under head-down tilt; control is by packing, pressure, tacks or a muscle patch, and may take an hour. Declare a massive transfusion early, and expect the surgeon to pack and wait.

### 6. Rectal division and anastomosis, or the decision for a stoma
**Why:** The rectum is washed out, stapled across below the tumour and removed through the extraction wound; the colon is joined to the stump with a circular stapler passed from below, then tested with air under water or with a scope.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient is levelled for extraction. Perfusion of the colonic end is judged now, sometimes with indocyanine green 5-10 mg IV, so the arterial pressure should be at baseline and not propped on a high vasoconstrictor dose. A failed air test means a re-do or a stoma and another 30-60 minutes.

### 7. Defunctioning loop ileostomy
**Why:** A loop of terminal ileum is brought out through a trephine in the right rectus to divert the stream from a low anastomosis for about three months.
**Anaesthesia:** The trephine is a muscle-splitting wound that the midline blocks do not cover well; ask the surgeon to infiltrate it. From tomorrow the patient loses a litre or more a day of sodium-rich fluid through it, and dehydration with acute kidney injury is the commonest reason for readmission ([stoma card](stoma-formation-and-reversal.html)).

### 8. Perineal phase of an abdominoperineal resection
**Why:** The anus is closed with a purse-string and excised with a cuff of skin, ischioanal fat and levator, joining the pelvic dissection; the specimen is delivered through the perineum and the defect closed.
**Anaesthesia:** In Lloyd-Davies this is done with the legs raised further, which increases calf risk and abdominal pressure. If the surgeon turns the patient prone jackknife, it is a full prone turn at hour four of a long case: stomas are bagged first, the tube and lines secured again, eyes and face checked, and the abdomen must hang free. Bleeding comes from the prostatic or vaginal venous plexus anteriorly. If a rectus or gluteal flap is used to fill the defect, a plastic surgeon adds one to two hours and a flap that must not be sat on. Wound infiltration by the surgeon is worthwhile; a caudal or pudendal block is an option the evidence does not address.

### 9. Closure, levelling and emergence
**Why:** Ports, extraction wound and stoma are completed; a pelvic drain is common.
**Anaesthesia:** Level the table and the legs 15-20 minutes before the end and give the airway time: after hours head-down, check for a cuff leak and look at the face before extubating, and extubate sitting up. A patient with marked facial and conjunctival oedema and no leak stays intubated head-up for an hour or two. Reverse fully. Look at the calves and document that they are soft.

### 10. The first days
**Why:** The surgeon is watching for a leak, a pelvic collection, ileus, a stoma that works, and a bladder that empties.
**Anaesthesia:** The pelvic autonomic nerves have been handled, so urinary retention is common and the catheter stays for one to three days, longer after abdominoperineal resection. Ileus is more frequent than after colectomy. Calf pain, a tense calf or pain on passive ankle movement in recovery or overnight is compartment syndrome until a surgeon has examined it; neuraxial analgesia at lumbar level can mask it, low-dose intrathecal morphine does not. A leak declares itself on day 3-7 with tachycardia, pelvic pain, a change in the drain or atrial fibrillation.

## Pain plan

For minimally invasive anterior resection the skeleton is PROSPECT's laparoscopic colorectal guideline: paracetamol and wound infiltration, with opioid as rescue. Two things differ from the colonic operation. First, PROSPECT restricts its NSAID recommendation to colonic surgery: a low pelvic anastomosis is the join most at risk and the observational leak signal is taken more seriously there, so the default is no NSAID for a low anterior resection with an anastomosis, and surgeons vary. An abdominoperineal resection has no anastomosis, and a COX-2 inhibitor is reasonable once bleeding and renal function allow. Second, without an NSAID the plan needs another leg. The author's practice is intrathecal morphine 150-200 micrograms, which a 2026 trial supports for quality of recovery after laparoscopic colorectal surgery, with dexamethasone, and a low-dose ketamine infusion for the opioid-tolerant.

For the open operation, and for open abdominoperineal resection in particular, a thoracic epidural for 48-72 hours has the best evidence for the abdominal wound, with rectus sheath catheters as the alternative. Note what a thoracic epidural does not cover: the perineal wound is S2-S4. Intrathecal morphine covers it for the first day; after that the perineum is managed with regular paracetamol, opioid for sitting and dressing changes, a pressure-relieving cushion, and time. Pain there is provoked by sitting and persists for weeks, and neuropathic features and phantom rectal sensation are common enough to warn the patient about.

The epidural is not the plan for minimally invasive surgery: the enhanced-recovery trial showed slower recovery and more vasopressor use, and a hypotensive patient on a noradrenaline infusion is exactly what a marginal colorectal anastomosis does not need.

Escalation: day 1-2 pain that is abdominal and wound-based responds to a rescue TAP or rectus sheath block. Perineal pain that worsens after day 4 with fever is a wound collection. Pelvic pain with tachycardia is a leak.

## Pitfalls and variants

- High against low. A high anterior resection for a rectosigmoid tumour is a left colectomy with a slightly longer pelvic step, no stoma and a colectomy-like pain course. An ultralow resection with a hand-sewn coloanal join and an ileostomy is a five-hour operation.
- Robotic resection. A docked robot fixes the table unless integrated table motion is available; emergency undocking takes a minute or two that a presacral bleed does not have, so the team should have rehearsed it.
- Transanal dissection. A second insufflation from below at higher flow; carbon dioxide embolism through open pelvic veins has been reported, presenting as a sudden fall in end-tidal CO2 with hypotension.
- Lateral pelvic node dissection and beyond-plane resections. More bleeding from the internal iliac branches, longer, and obturator nerve at risk: this starts to resemble [pelvic exenteration](cytoreductive-surgery-with-hipec-and-pelvic.html).
- Glaucoma and cerebrovascular disease. Hours of steep head-down tilt raise intraocular and intracranial pressure; discuss a limit of tilt or an open approach beforehand.
- The obese male with a narrow pelvis. The hardest dissection, the worst ventilation, the highest conversion rate and the highest calf risk in one patient.
- Function afterwards. About a third of patients have major low anterior resection syndrome (urgency, clustering, incontinence), and bladder and sexual dysfunction follow nerve injury; none of it is yours to prevent, but it shapes how the patient remembers the operation.
- Perineal wound failure. Complication rates of a third to a half are reported even with adjuncts, so returns to theatre for washout and negative-pressure dressings are common, usually prone or in lithotomy with a painful wound.

## Key sources

Analgesia for rectal surgery is extrapolated from the colorectal PROSPECT guidelines, which pool colonic and rectal trials and single out rectal surgery only to withhold the NSAID recommendation. There is no procedure-specific trial evidence for the perineal wound. The positioning advice rests on case series and a scoping review of compartment syndrome, which is rare enough that it will never be studied prospectively. ROLARR remains the main randomised comparison of robotic and laparoscopic rectal resection.
