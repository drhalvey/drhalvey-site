## What the surgeon is doing and why

The parotid gland wraps around the back of the mandible in front of the ear. The facial nerve leaves the skull at the stylomastoid foramen, enters the gland from behind, and divides inside it into the branches that move the forehead, eyelids, cheek, lips and chin. The nerve therefore splits the gland into a larger superficial part and a smaller deep part, and almost every parotid operation is defined by where the lump sits relative to it. Most parotid tumours are benign (pleomorphic adenoma and Warthin tumour) and lie in the superficial part; they are removed because pleomorphic adenomas grow, recur if spilled and occasionally turn malignant.

In a superficial parotidectomy the surgeon makes an incision that runs down in front of the ear, curls under the lobe and extends into a neck crease, and lifts a skin flap forward off the gland. The great auricular nerve is met on the sternomastoid and its posterior branch saved where possible. The gland is then freed from the ear canal cartilage and the mastoid to find the main trunk of the facial nerve, using fixed landmarks (the cartilaginous pointer of the tragus, the posterior belly of digastric, the tympanomastoid suture). Once the trunk is seen, the superficial gland is peeled forward off the nerve, branch by branch, with fine scissors and bipolar. An extracapsular dissection removes a small, mobile, superficial lump with a cuff of gland and no formal nerve dissection. A total parotidectomy lifts the nerve off the deep lobe to remove what lies beneath it. For cancer invading the nerve, the involved segment is resected and bridged with a graft, often with a neck dissection.

The submandibular gland lies under the body of the mandible and is removed through a neck crease incision for stones, chronic infection or tumour. Three nerves bound that field: the marginal mandibular branch of the facial nerve in the flap above, the lingual nerve hooked down to the gland by its ganglion, and the hypoglossal nerve on the floor. The facial artery is ligated as it grooves the gland.

The pain logic: a skin flap and a gland, no muscle division and a sensory nerve that is usually cut. In a prospective comparison pain after parotid surgery was mild to moderate whatever the extent of resection. Score 2. The card is built around the nerve.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative review
**Why:** The surgeon needs a baseline for the facial nerve and a diagnosis that predicts how hard the nerve will be to save.
**Anaesthesia:** Note and record facial movement on both sides; weakness before surgery means malignancy and a nerve that may be sacrificed. Ask whether this is revision surgery, where scar hides the nerve and the monitor matters most. A deep lobe tumour can extend into the parapharyngeal space and push the tonsil and soft palate medially: look in the mouth, because that changes laryngoscopy. Trismus suggests malignant infiltration of the pterygoids or masseter and does not relax with induction. Most patients are otherwise well; Warthin tumour is a smoker's disease.

### 2. Induction and airway
**Why:** The surgeon stands at the side of the head, operates for two to three hours around the angle of the jaw, and needs the mouth and tube out of the way.
**Anaesthesia:** A reinforced tracheal tube, fixed to the opposite corner of the mouth with tape that does not cross the operated cheek. For relaxation the cleanest method is rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg IV for intubation followed by sugammadex 2 mg/kg IV once the tube is secured: in parotid surgery this restored the twitch to over 90% within 10 minutes and gave positive facial nerve signals in every patient at the first stimulation. The alternatives are a small dose of rocuronium and time, or intubation with remifentanil and propofol and no relaxant. Whichever is used, document a train-of-four of four with no fade before the surgeon reaches the nerve. Maintain with propofol and remifentanil TIVA.

### 3. Position, monitor and drapes
**Why:** The head is turned away and the surgeon or neurophysiologist places paired needle electrodes in the facial muscles (commonly frontalis, orbicularis oculi, orbicularis oris and mentalis), then drapes so the face can be seen.
**Anaesthesia:** Lubricate the eye on the operated side and cover it with a clear adhesive dressing; do not tape or pad it. Keep your own equipment off that side of the face: no tube ties, no temperature probe cable, no warming blanket edge over the cheek. Tilt 15 degrees head up. Check the neck is not over-rotated and that the cannula, cuff and circuit are reachable with the table turned. The monitor is confirmed by a tap test on the electrodes; it says nothing about the nerve until the first deliberate stimulation.

### 4. Incision and skin flap
**Why:** The flap is raised in the plane just above the parotid fascia; too thin and the skin dies, too deep and the surgeon is in the gland among nerve branches.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon infiltrates the incision line with adrenaline-containing local anaesthetic. Ask that it be kept superficial and away from the stylomastoid region: local anaesthetic that reaches the trunk produces a silent monitor and a palsy that lasts as long as the drug. For the same reason the author's practice is not to place an auriculotemporal block or a high cervical plexus block before the nerve dissection is finished. Stimulation at this stage is modest.

### 5. Finding the facial nerve trunk
**Why:** The surgeon dissects down in front of the ear canal and mastoid to the point where the trunk emerges, the most demanding part of the operation.
**Anaesthesia:** This is when the field must be dry and the patient absolutely still without relaxant. Remifentanil at an adequate rate with propofol titrated to processed EEG gives both; a blood pressure at the lower end of the patient's own normal range, not a formal hypotensive technique, is usually enough with head-up tilt. If the patient moves, deepen; do not paralyse. The stylomastoid artery bleeds just where the nerve is, and bipolar close to the trunk is used sparingly, which is another reason the surgeon will ask you for a lower pressure.

### 6. Dissection along the branches
**Why:** With the trunk and its first division identified, the gland is lifted off the nerve along each branch in turn. The surgeon stimulates suspected structures with a probe and listens.
**Anaesthesia:** An hour or more of steady state. The monitor will sound with mechanical irritation of the nerve as well as with stimulation; trains of activity during traction are a warning the surgeon acts on. If the signal disappears, the check is shared: the stimulator and its return electrode, electrode displacement, and from your side an unintended relaxant effect (a train-of-four takes seconds) or local anaesthetic. Facial electromyographic amplitude falls in proportion to depth of block and is unreliable at low twitch counts, so a partially paralysed patient gives a falsely reassuring silence.

### 7. Deep lobe, nerve sacrifice and graft
**Why:** For deep or malignant tumours the nerve is mobilised and retracted, or a segment is excised and replaced with a length of great auricular or sural nerve; a neck dissection may be added.
**Anaesthesia:** The case lengthens to four to six hours: urinary catheter, active warming and pressure care. A sural graft means a prepared leg and possibly a turn. Traction on a mobilised nerve makes temporary weakness likely even when the nerve is intact, which is worth knowing before you examine the face in recovery. The additional considerations of a neck dissection are in [laryngectomy, neck dissection and free flap reconstruction](laryngectomy-neck-dissection-and-free-flap.html).

### 8. Submandibular gland excision
**Why:** Through a crease incision two finger-breadths below the mandible, the surgeon raises the flap deep to the investing fascia to carry the marginal mandibular nerve up out of harm, ligates the facial vessels, frees the gland from the lingual nerve and divides the duct.
**Anaesthesia:** A shorter operation with the same rule: the marginal mandibular and hypoglossal nerves are checked by stimulation, so no relaxant after intubation. A flexible laryngeal mask is a reasonable airway if the surgeon is content, since the mouth is not entered. Chronic sialadenitis makes the dissection bloodier and the nerves harder to see. An acutely infected gland with floor of mouth swelling is a different operation: see [dental and neck abscess](dental-and-neck-abscess.html).

### 9. Haemostasis and closure
**Why:** The bed is checked dry under conditions that mimic waking, a suction drain is placed under the flap and the skin closed. Some surgeons interpose fascia or a muscle flap to reduce gustatory sweating later.
**Anaesthesia:** Bring the blood pressure back to the patient's normal and level the table when asked; a sustained inflation shows venous bleeding. This is the time for the regional analgesia: a superficial cervical plexus block low on the posterior border of sternomastoid, or surgeon infiltration of the wound edges with bupivacaine or ropivacaine. Give paracetamol 1 g IV, an NSAID or parecoxib 40 mg IV, and a small dose of long-acting opioid.

### 10. Emergence and recovery
**Why:** A cough or retch engorges the veins under a thin flap and fills the dead space with blood despite the drain.
**Anaesthesia:** Wake on a tapering remifentanil infusion, sitting up; remifentanil ranks highest among agents for preventing severe emergence cough. In recovery ask the patient to raise the eyebrows, close the eyes tightly, smile and show the lower teeth, and record the result with the time. Weakness of the lower lip alone is the marginal mandibular branch, the one most often stretched. If you placed a block before the patient woke, tell the surgeon: local anaesthetic spread can weaken the face for some hours and the two of you need to agree on what was seen.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is skin and soft tissue in the territory of the great auricular nerve (C2-C3) with a contribution from the auriculotemporal nerve in front of the ear. The great auricular is commonly divided, which leaves the lobe numb and removes much of the wound's sensation; preserving its posterior branch reduces long-term sensory disturbance without changing overall quality of life. The systemic plan is paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, regular for three to five days, with a few doses of oral opioid for the first night.

A superficial cervical plexus block covers the cervical part of the incision and the area behind and below the ear. It has trial support in parotid surgery as part of a regional technique, though in one randomised comparison a fifth of patients had a Horner syndrome with it, a sign that the injectate does not always stay superficial. The author's practice is to place it, or have the surgeon infiltrate, after the facial nerve work is finished, with about 10 mL of ropivacaine 0.375-0.5% kept subcutaneous along the posterior border of sternomastoid. This is author's judgement: no trial compares block timing, and there is no PROSPECT guideline for salivary gland surgery.

Expect worst pain of about 3 out of 10 on the first evening and day 1, with jaw ache on chewing, and little by day 3. There is no rebound. Pain with rapid swelling of the cheek in the first hours is a haematoma and goes back to theatre. Because pain is modest and nausea preventable, selected parotidectomies are done as day cases with or without a drain; pooled comparisons, of very low certainty, find complication rates similar to inpatient surgery.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Facial weakness in recovery. Temporary weakness is common after a full nerve dissection and usually recovers over weeks to months; permanent palsy is uncommon when the nerve was seen intact. Immediate dense palsy of all branches has three explanations: local anaesthetic, a neurapraxic trunk, or a divided nerve. Knowing exactly what you and the surgeon injected, and when, settles the first.
- Eye care. A weak orbicularis oculi leaves the cornea exposed: lubricant and taping at night are started in recovery, not at the first complaint.
- Relaxant given for coughing. The wrong answer to the right problem. A remifentanil or propofol bolus stops the cough in the same time and leaves the monitor working.
- Haematoma. The first few hours. It threatens the flap, not the airway, unless a neck dissection or bilateral surgery has been done; evacuation is under general anaesthesia with a normal airway approach.
- Parapharyngeal extension and trismus. Bulging of the lateral pharyngeal wall narrows the view at laryngoscopy; fixed trismus is planned as an awake tracheal intubation. See [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html).
- Radical parotidectomy for cancer. A long operation with nerve sacrifice, grafting, neck dissection and sometimes a free flap; arterial line, catheter and a high-dependency bed for the flap cases. This is the point at which invasive monitoring and critical care enter; blood is rarely needed.
- Salivary fistula and gustatory sweating. Later problems for the surgeon; neither changes your plan.
- Bleeding during submandibular excision. The facial artery is ligated twice, above and below the gland; a slipped tie retracts under the mandible. Brief, brisk and surgical.
- Day-case surgery. Reasonable for healthy patients having extracapsular dissection, superficial parotidectomy or submandibular excision where the surgeon discharges with or without a drain. For the antiemetic and discharge logic see [nausea, vomiting and day-case anaesthesia](../a/a11-ponv-day-case.html).
- For facial nerve monitoring inside the temporal bone see [middle ear surgery](middle-ear-surgery-tympanoplasty-mastoidectomy-cochlear.html).

## Key sources

The relaxant strategy rests on one small feasibility study in parotid surgery and two physiological studies from ear surgery; none measured nerve outcome, and the rule of full recovery before nerve dissection is consensus drawn from them. Pain intensity comes from a single prospective observational study. Regional analgesia in parotid surgery has one small randomised comparison of two blocks without a control group. The timing of the block relative to nerve dissection, the blood pressure target and the recovery assessment of the face are author's judgement. Day-case data are pooled observational studies of very low certainty.
