## What the surgeon is doing and why

A stoma is bowel brought through the abdominal wall and opened onto the skin. It is made for one of three reasons: to protect a join downstream (the loop ileostomy that accompanies most low anterior resections), to relieve or bypass something that cannot be removed today (an obstructing rectal cancer before chemoradiotherapy, perianal sepsis, a fistula, an incontinent or radiation-damaged rectum), or because the bowel end has nowhere else to go (the end colostomy of an abdominoperineal resection or a Hartmann's procedure, the end ileostomy after a colectomy for colitis).

The anatomy of the wound is the same every time and explains the pain. A disc of skin is removed at a site the stoma nurse has marked. The surgeon cuts a cross in the anterior rectus sheath, splits the rectus muscle in the line of its fibres, opens the posterior sheath and peritoneum, and makes a hole that admits two fingers: big enough not to strangle the bowel, small enough not to herniate. The bowel is drawn through and sutured to the skin, an ileostomy everted into a 2-3 cm spout to keep its corrosive effluent off the skin, a colostomy flush. The opening goes through the rectus because muscle around the bowel reduces herniation, and that same muscle is why it hurts to cough and sit up.

There are three ways to get the bowel to the hole. As the last step of a larger resection, which is covered on the [colectomy](laparoscopic-and-open-colectomy.html) and [anterior resection](anterior-resection-and-abdominoperineal-resection.html) cards. Through the stoma site alone, the trephine stoma, where the surgeon fishes for the sigmoid or the terminal ileum through the hole without seeing the rest of the abdomen. Or laparoscopically, with two or three ports, which lets the surgeon check which loop it is and which way it lies.

Reversal comes in two very different sizes:

- Closure of a loop ileostomy. An incision is made around the stoma, the bowel is freed from the skin, the fat and the rectus sheath, delivered, and either the opening is sutured or the loop is resected and stapled side to side. It goes back in through the same hole, the fascia is closed and the skin is left partly open or closed in a purse-string. Forty-five minutes to an hour, no laparotomy, but it is a small bowel anastomosis and it behaves like one: in a recent multicentre trial 16.4% of control patients had a postoperative ileus.
- Reversal of Hartmann's. The end colostomy is taken down and joined to a rectal stump that has been sitting scarred in the pelvis for six months or more. That requires a laparotomy or a difficult laparoscopy, adhesiolysis, mobilisation of the left colon and often the splenic flexure, identification of the stump (a probe or sizer is passed from below), a stapled colorectal anastomosis, and frequently a covering loop ileostomy. It is a major re-do colorectal resection with the leak and ureteric risks of one, and many patients never have it done.

Pain logic. Formation and loop closure are abdominal wall operations confined to one quadrant: moderate, movement-provoked, mostly settled by day 3, and well covered by infiltration or a unilateral block. Hartmann's reversal is the pain of whichever approach it took, plus the old stoma site.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative assessment: why this patient has, or needs, a stoma
**Why:** The indication is the anaesthetic risk. An elective loop ileostomy closure in a well patient three months after an anterior resection and a defunctioning colostomy for a near-obstructing rectal cancer are both listed as a minor case.
**Anaesthesia:** For formation, establish whether the bowel is obstructed (distension, vomiting, a CT report of dilated bowel), whether there is sepsis (perianal or pelvic), and whether the patient is on chemotherapy or steroids. For closure, look for the chronic ileostomy state: dehydration, hyponatraemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypokalaemia and a creatinine above baseline. Readmission with dehydration after ileostomy formation is associated with age 65 or over, obesity, diabetes, hypertension, renal comorbidity and diuretics, and the same patients arrive for closure with those deficits. Loperamide, codeine and oral rehydration solution on the drug chart are the clue. Correct magnesium and potassium before, not after. Adjuvant chemotherapy may have finished only weeks earlier.

### 2. Induction and airway
**Why:** The surgeon needs anything from a still abdomen for ten minutes to a full laparoscopy.
**Anaesthesia:** A stoma for obstruction is a rapid sequence induction with a tracheal tube after decompression with a nasogastric tube; do not be reassured by the word "trephine". An elective closure in a fasted patient with a working ileostomy can be a supraglottic airway or a spinal. Spinal anaesthesia to T6-T8 covers a closure or a trephine stoma in the patient whose lungs or heart argue against a general anaesthetic, though traction on the mesentery produces nausea and visceral discomfort that the block does not fully cover and that needs a small dose of opioid or sedation.

### 3. Trephine at the marked site
**Why:** The skin disc, cruciate incision in the anterior sheath, split of the rectus and opening of the posterior sheath are made at the stoma nurse's mark, which was chosen with the patient sitting and standing so the appliance will sit flat.
**Anaesthesia:** The inferior epigastric vessels run on the deep surface of the rectus and are the one source of real bleeding; a rectus sheath haematoma presents later as a painful swelling beside the stoma. If you are placing a rectus sheath block on that side, do it before the incision or leave the infiltration to the surgeon afterwards.

### 4. Finding and delivering the bowel
**Why:** Through a trephine the surgeon identifies the bowel by feel and by its taeniae or mesentery; laparoscopically the loop is chosen under vision, oriented, and passed to a grasper at the trephine.
**Anaesthesia:** Relaxation matters here: a tense rectus grips the surgeon's fingers and the bowel. For the laparoscopic method the usual pneumoperitoneum applies, briefly and with modest head-down tilt. Traction on the mesentery causes bradycardia and, under spinal, nausea. The well-known error is bringing out the wrong end or twisting the loop; it is discovered on day 2 when nothing works.

### 5. Maturation of the stoma
**Why:** The bowel is opened and its edge sutured to the dermis, everted for an ileostomy. A rod under a loop is used by some surgeons.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing is asked of you except not to let the patient cough the bowel back in. Infiltration of the site is done before the appliance goes on. Lighten the anaesthetic: this is ten minutes of suturing skin.

### 6. Closure of a loop ileostomy: mobilisation
**Why:** A circumstomal incision is deepened through fat to the rectus sheath, and the two limbs are separated from the sheath and the muscle all the way round until the loop is free into the peritoneal cavity.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step that varies: ten minutes in a thin patient with a three-month-old stoma, an hour in an obese patient with a parastomal hernia, and occasionally a conversion to a midline laparotomy when the bowel is torn or will not come up. Plan the airway and the relaxant for the hard version. A hole made in the bowel at this stage contaminates the wound.

### 7. Anastomosis and return of the bowel
**Why:** The loop is closed by suture or resected and stapled, checked for patency, and dropped back; the fascia is closed with a strong suture; the skin is closed in a purse-string that leaves a central hole to drain.
**Anaesthesia:** Relaxation for fascial closure, then reversal. The wound is infiltrated. The defunctioned downstream bowel has been idle for months and is narrow and atrophic, which is the mechanical basis of the ileus that follows; opioid sparing is the part of this that is yours.

### 8. Reversal of Hartmann's: adhesiolysis, stump and anastomosis
**Why:** The abdomen is re-entered, small bowel is dissected out of the pelvis, the rectal stump is found and cleared, the colostomy is taken down and mobilised with the left colon, and a circular stapled anastomosis is made and tested.
**Anaesthesia:** Run this as an open or laparoscopic left colectomy in a previously operated abdomen: tracheal tube, Lloyd-Davies, arterial line for the comorbid, warmth, deep relaxation, and blood available. Adhesiolysis is slow and enterotomies happen. The ureters are at risk in the scarred pelvis and stents are sometimes placed first. Obesity matters more here than in most colorectal surgery: in 16,027 elective reversals, a body mass index of 34-49.9 raised the odds of any complication by 38%. A covering ileostomy means the patient returns for the operation in step 6.

### 9. Emergence and the first days
**Why:** After formation the surgeon wants a pink, working stoma and a patient who can change the appliance. After closure the surgeon wants flatus.
**Anaesthesia:** A new ileostomy starts working within a day or two and can put out 1.5-2 L daily in the first week; the fluid chart needs the stoma output on it, and intravenous replacement is sodium-rich crystalloid, not dextrose. A dusky stoma on day 0 is a surgical review. After closure, vomiting and distension on day 2-3 is ileus and is treated with a nasogastric tube and patience; pain out of proportion with tachycardia is a leak. The partly open closure wound oozes and looks worse than it is.

## Pain plan

For formation and for loop ileostomy closure the pain is one wound in one quadrant, through muscle. No procedure-specific guideline exists; the nearest is PROSPECT's laparoscopic colorectal guideline, whose first line is paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, and wound infiltration. The defensible default is exactly that: generous infiltration of the stoma site by the surgeon in layers (skin, sheath, muscle), or a unilateral rectus sheath or TAP block when the patient is opioid tolerant or the dissection was long, regular paracetamol, and oral opioid for two or three days. The NSAID decision depends on what else is in the abdomen: reasonable for a stoma formed without an anastomosis in a patient with normal kidneys, withheld in the dehydrated ileostomy patient coming for closure until the creatinine is known, and withheld by many surgeons when the stoma is protecting a low rectal join made at the same operation.

The reason to care about opioid dose after a closure is the gut, not the pain score. With an ileus rate of about one in six, every opioid-sparing measure is worth taking and none of them is complicated: infiltration, paracetamol, dexamethasone, an antiemetic, early fluids and food.

Hartmann's reversal takes the plan of its approach: intrathecal morphine or TAP blocks if laparoscopic, an epidural or rectus sheath catheters if open, with no NSAID across a fresh colorectal anastomosis if the surgeon prefers.

Rebound is not a feature. Pain beside a new stoma that is getting worse is a haematoma, ischaemia or retraction; pain after closure that is getting worse is a leak or a wound infection, which is common enough in this contaminated wound that some surgeons leave it open deliberately.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The emergency stoma. Formed for large bowel obstruction, perforation, Fournier's gangrene or pelvic sepsis: full stomach, hypovolaemia and sepsis in a 40-minute operation. The risk is the patient's, and the arterial line and critical care discussion belong to the physiology, not the procedure length.
- The palliative stoma. Advanced pelvic or peritoneal malignancy, ascites, malnutrition and opioid tolerance. Keep it short, regional where possible, and agree the ceiling of care beforehand.
- High-output ileostomy. More than 1.5 L a day. Chronic sodium and magnesium loss, secondary hyperaldosteronism, and acute kidney injury. Do not anaesthetise for an elective closure with an uncorrected magnesium or a creatinine well above baseline.
- Parastomal hernia repair and stoma relocation. Often listed with stomas but really a hernia operation with mesh, an hour or two longer, sometimes laparoscopic: see the [incisional hernia card](umbilical-epigastric-and-incisional-hernia-repair.html).
- End ileostomy to pouch, and pouch surgery in general. Long pelvic operations with the considerations of a low anterior resection.
- Spinal for closure. Works well in the frail, with the caveat that an unexpectedly difficult mobilisation or a laparotomy needs conversion.
- Day-case closure. Done in selected fit patients in some units with a structured follow-up; the limiting factor is ileus at home on day 2, not pain.
- The stoma that never gets reversed. A proportion of "temporary" stomas become permanent because of leak, recurrence, frailty or the patient's choice; the Hartmann's patient who does come back is a selected, fitter one.

## Key sources

There is no PROSPECT guideline and little trial evidence on analgesia for stoma surgery; the plan here is extrapolated from colorectal resection and from the anatomy of the wound, and is the author's judgement. The risk data are stronger: dehydration and kidney injury after ileostomy formation, ileus after closure, and the effect of obesity on Hartmann's reversal are each supported by a meta-analysis, a randomised trial's control arm or a large registry.
