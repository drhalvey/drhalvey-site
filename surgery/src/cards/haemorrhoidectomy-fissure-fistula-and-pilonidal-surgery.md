## What the surgeon is doing and why

Four diseases share a list, a position and a surgeon, and very little else. Their pain could not be more different, and the commonest error on a proctology list is to give them all the same anaesthetic and the same discharge script.

Haemorrhoids are the anal cushions, vascular tissue above the dentate line that has slipped down and bleeds or prolapses. The key anatomical fact is the dentate line: above it the lining is visceral, insensitive mucosa; below it is anoderm with somatic innervation from the pudendal nerve (S2-S4). An excisional haemorrhoidectomy (Milligan-Morgan left open, Ferguson closed) dissects each cushion off the internal sphincter from the skin upwards, ligates the pedicle and removes it, leaving two or three wounds on the anoderm separated by bridges of skin. Those wounds sit on a sphincter that spasms in response, are bathed in stool, and are stretched open at every defaecation. The alternatives stay above the dentate line on purpose: stapled haemorrhoidopexy excises a ring of mucosa and lifts the cushions, and Doppler-guided artery ligation with mucopexy sutures the feeding vessels and hitches the prolapse. PROSPECT's summary is that excisional surgery is more painful than the other procedures; the surgeon chooses it anyway for large external components and for durability.

An anal fissure is a tear in the posterior anoderm kept open by a hypertonic internal sphincter and the ischaemia that causes. Surgery lowers sphincter tone: botulinum toxin injected into the internal sphincter, or lateral internal sphincterotomy, which divides the lower part of the muscle through a small incision. Both relieve pain; the patient often wakes better than they arrived.

A fistula is a track from the anal canal to the perianal skin, the chronic sequel of an abscess that began in an anal gland between the sphincters. The surgeon's dilemma is that laying the track open cures it and divides whatever sphincter it crosses. So the operation is staged: examination under anaesthesia to define the track, a loose seton to drain it, then fistulotomy for a low track, or a sphincter-preserving procedure (ligation of the intersphincteric track, an advancement flap) for a high one. Crohn's disease patients come back repeatedly.

Pilonidal disease is not anal at all: hair driven into midline pits in the natal cleft produces a chronic abscess over the sacrum. Operations range from pit excision under local anaesthetic through excision with the wound left open, to off-midline flap closures (Karydakis, Limberg) that flatten the cleft.

Pain logic. Haemorrhoidectomy is the purest example in the book of the mucosal mechanism: a raw, densely innervated surface, re-provoked daily, with a curve that does not fall until the wounds epithelialise in the second and third weeks. That shape, not the size of the wound, earns the 5. Everything else on the card is mild to moderate: perianal skin wounds, a muscle division that removes spasm, or a flap over the sacrum that hurts when sat on.

## Steps

### 1. Sorting the list
**Why:** The booking says "EUA and proceed" for half the list; the surgeon often decides the operation after looking.
**Anaesthesia:** Find out for each patient whether the plan may end as a haemorrhoidectomy or a flap, because that changes the block, the airway and the discharge script. Ask about anticoagulants and antiplatelets (secondary haemorrhage is the serious complication), Crohn's disease and its drugs, diabetes and the patient with a perianal abscess who looks unwell, and previous urinary retention. Opioid-tolerant and chronic pain patients do badly after haemorrhoidectomy and need a written plan before the day.

### 2. Choice of anaesthetic
**Why:** The surgeon needs a relaxed sphincter, a still patient and access to the perineum for 10-40 minutes.
**Anaesthesia:** Three techniques work. General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway plus a block or infiltration is the day-surgery default. A saddle spinal gives perfect conditions with the patient awake and suits prone jackknife: use a pencil-point needle, because cutting needles gave a high headache rate in exactly this ambulatory group, and a short-acting agent, because chloroprocaine met discharge criteria 40 minutes sooner than hyperbaric bupivacaine. Local infiltration with sedation is underused and, for pilonidal surgery, shortened hospital time compared with spinal. Spinal anaesthesia with a long-acting agent and liberal intravenous fluid is the recipe for urinary retention.

### 3. Positioning
**Why:** Lithotomy gives the surgeon a view up the canal; prone jackknife lets the haemorrhoids empty, gives the assistant room and is the only sensible position for the natal cleft.
**Anaesthesia:** In lithotomy keep the hips and knees moderately flexed and the fibular heads off the poles. Prone jackknife under general anaesthesia means a tracheal tube in most hands, a chest and pelvic roll so the abdomen is free, and care with the eyes and neck; under a saddle block or sedation the patient positions themselves, which is the argument for those techniques. Strapping the buttocks apart with tape is done before preparation.

### 4. Pudendal block or infiltration
**Why:** The somatic supply of the anoderm and external sphincter is the inferior rectal branch of the pudendal nerve. Blocking it relaxes the sphincter for the surgeon and removes the afferent drive from the wound.
**Anaesthesia:** For haemorrhoidectomy PROSPECT recommends a bilateral pudendal block and no longer recommends perianal infiltration as the analgesic technique. It can be done by landmark at the ischial spine, transperineally with ultrasound or a stimulator, or by the surgeon under direct palpation; 10 mL of long-acting local anaesthetic a side is usual practice. In a randomised trial under general anaesthesia the block cut dysuria from 40% to 14% as well as reducing pain. For fissure, fistula and abscess surgery, infiltration by the surgeon with adrenaline-containing solution is the norm and is sufficient.

### 5. Anal dilatation and examination
**Why:** A retractor is inserted and opened to see the canal; the fistula track is probed, hydrogen peroxide or dye may be injected through the external opening.
**Anaesthesia:** The most stimulating moment of the case, out of all proportion to its appearance. Under light general anaesthesia without a block, stretching the anus produces laryngospasm, breath-holding, bradycardia and a leg leaving the stirrup. Deepen before the retractor goes in, not after, with a bolus of propofol or a short-acting opioid, and ask the surgeon to say when they are about to start.

### 6. The definitive step
**Why:** Haemorrhoidectomy: each pedicle is dissected off the sphincter with diathermy or an energy device and transfixed. Sphincterotomy: the lower internal sphincter is divided under the skin laterally. Fistula: a seton is threaded and tied loosely, or the track laid open, or a flap raised. Abscess: incised, deroofed and the cavity broken down.
**Anaesthesia:** Short and bloodless. Give the dexamethasone early: PROSPECT supports a systemic steroid for haemorrhoidectomy pain. Give the intravenous paracetamol and NSAID now so they are working at emergence. Keep fluids to a few hundred millilitres. For an abscess in a septic patient, drainage is resuscitation, but the anaesthetic is that of sepsis: rapid sequence induction, vasopressor ready, antibiotics given, and a surgeon who will extend the incision if the fascia is involved.

### 7. Pilonidal excision or flap
**Why:** The sinus and its tracks are excised down to the presacral fascia; the wound is left open, marsupialised, or closed with a flap that moves the scar off the midline.
**Anaesthesia:** Prone, 30-90 minutes, skin and fat only. Local infiltration by the surgeon covers it. The patients are young, often obese and hirsute, sometimes smokers; airway and position are the only real questions. A flap means a drain and antibiotics and a wound that should not be sat on for a week or two.

### 8. Dressing, emergence and voiding
**Why:** A haemostatic sponge or a light pack is left in the canal by some surgeons and none by others; packs are painful and a source of retention.
**Anaesthesia:** Smooth emergence without coughing on a tube. In recovery the block is working and pain is 0-3, which is the moment to teach, not to reassure: the first oral doses go in before discharge. Retention follows pain, sphincter spasm, packs, fluid and neuraxial block, and the patient must pass urine before leaving. Early bleeding soaking the pad is a return to theatre.

### 9. Days 1 to 14 at home
**Why:** The wounds granulate and epithelialise over two to three weeks; the pedicle ligatures slough at 7-10 days.
**Anaesthesia:** The pain course is yours even though you will not see it. Worst pain comes with the first bowel motion, usually day 2-4, and stays at 5-7 with each motion for a week or more. Constipation from opioid and fear is the worst outcome, since a hard stool through a fresh wound is exactly what the plan is trying to avoid. Bleeding of more than a few clots between day 7 and 14 is secondary haemorrhage and needs the emergency department, particularly in anyone back on an anticoagulant.

## Pain plan

For excisional haemorrhoidectomy, follow PROSPECT, which is unusually specific. Before and during: paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, dexamethasone, and a bilateral pudendal block. After: paracetamol and the NSAID taken regularly for at least a week, opioid as rescue, and a topical agent chosen by the surgeon from those with evidence (metronidazole, diltiazem, sucralfate or glyceryl trinitrate; diltiazem and glyceryl trinitrate work by relaxing the internal sphincter, which is the spasm component of the mechanism). Oral metronidazole, once routine, has been dropped. Botulinum toxin into the internal sphincter at the time of surgery is supported for the same spasm-relieving reason. Laxatives from the day of surgery were recommended in the earlier PROSPECT review and are part of the analgesic plan, not an afterthought: a stool softener plus an osmotic agent, started before the first motion.

The opioid question is harder here than anywhere on a day-case list. The pain is severe enough to justify it and opioid-induced constipation directly worsens the mechanism. The defensible default is a small supply of an immediate-release opioid for three to five days, with an explicit instruction to take it 30 minutes before attempting a bowel motion, and never without the laxatives. Warm baths help spasm and cost nothing.

No block lasts long enough. A pudendal block with plain long-acting local anaesthetic gives 8-18 hours, and rebound on the first night is expected: say so, and make sure the oral regimen has started before it lifts. Liposomal bupivacaine has been used for infiltration in some countries; the author has not found procedure-specific guidance recommending it.

For the rest of the card: fissure surgery and setons need paracetamol and an NSAID for a few days. Stapled and ligation procedures cause a deep ache and tenesmus (a feeling of needing to pass stool) for several days; severe pain after a stapled procedure suggests the staple line is too low or there is pelvic sepsis. Pilonidal open wounds are painful at dressing changes; flaps hurt on sitting for one to two weeks.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The patient told it was a minor procedure. Expectation is the main modifiable determinant of the phone calls on day 3. Agree the script with the surgeon.
- Urinary retention. The commonest complication. Short-acting spinal or none, restricted fluid, pudendal block, no pack, good analgesia.
- Anticoagulants. Restarting too early converts the day 7-10 slough into a significant haemorrhage; the restart date is a joint decision written on the discharge summary.
- Crohn's perianal disease. Repeat examinations and setons in patients on biologics and steroids, often with chronic pain and many previous anaesthetics. Keep each one brief.
- Necrotising perineal infection. A perianal abscess with systemic illness, crepitus, skin necrosis or pain beyond the visible signs is a surgical emergency requiring wide debridement, critical care and a return to theatre within a day.
- Pregnancy and post-partum. Thrombosed haemorrhoids are common; surgery is avoided where possible, and lithotomy late in pregnancy needs lateral tilt.
- Prone under sedation. A sedated, obese patient face down with the head lower than the hips is an airway problem with poor access: use a saddle block with light or no sedation, or secure the airway.
- Sphincter injury and incontinence. Not yours to prevent, but the reason the surgeon will not simply lay open a high fistula however many times the patient returns.

## Key sources

Haemorrhoidectomy has one of the largest PROSPECT evidence bases of any day-case operation (84 trials in the 2023 update), although most trials test single interventions rather than the combination. The pudendal block recommendation has been stable across three versions of the guideline. For fissure, fistula and pilonidal surgery there are no procedure-specific pain guidelines, and the advice here is anatomical reasoning and the author's judgement; the choice between spinal, general and local techniques rests on small trials whose main outcomes were discharge time and headache.
