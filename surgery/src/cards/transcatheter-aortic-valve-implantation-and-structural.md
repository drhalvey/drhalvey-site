## What the operator is doing and why

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation treats aortic stenosis without removing the valve. A tissue valve sewn inside a metal stent frame is crimped onto a catheter, passed up the aorta from the femoral artery, positioned across the diseased valve and expanded. The frame pushes the calcified native cusps aside against the wall of the root and holds itself there by radial force. There is no bypass, no arrest and no suture line; the calcium that a surgeon would cut out is what the device grips.

That one sentence explains the complications. Because the cusps are displaced and not excised, they can be pushed over a coronary ostium, particularly when the ostia are low or the device is placed inside a failed surgical valve. Because the frame relies on radial force against an irregular calcified ring, blood can leak around it, and too much force can split the annulus. Because the conduction system runs just beneath the annulus, the frame can compress it. And because a large-bore delivery system has to travel through elderly iliac arteries and round the arch, the access vessels can tear and debris can go to the brain.

Two device families behave differently at deployment. A balloon-expandable valve is opened in a few seconds by inflating a balloon, during which the ventricle must not eject or it will fire the valve into the aorta: hence rapid pacing. A self-expanding valve is unsheathed gradually and can often be recaptured; it needs less or no rapid pacing but sits lower in the outflow tract and has the higher pacemaker rate. In low-risk patients the transcatheter route gave fewer deaths, strokes and readmissions at one year than surgery, which is why it is now the commoner way to treat aortic stenosis in the elderly and why the remaining surgical cases are described in [aortic valve replacement](aortic-valve-replacement.html).

The other structural procedures share the room, the imaging and the access. Edge-to-edge repair clips the two mitral (or tricuspid) leaflets together at the regurgitant jet through a transseptal venous approach, entirely under transoesophageal guidance. Left atrial appendage occlusion plugs the appendage in patients with atrial fibrillation who cannot take anticoagulants. Septal closure seats a double-disc device across a patent foramen ovale or atrial septal defect.

There is almost no pain. The anaesthetic question is not analgesia but how much anaesthesia a frail patient with a fixed cardiac output needs in order to lie still, and how you will rescue them in a remote location (see [remote site anaesthesia and procedural sedation](../a/a12-remote-site-sedation.html)).

## Steps

### 1. The plan and the room
**Why:** Suitability, access route, device and size are settled beforehand from the gated CT by the heart team. So is the answer to the hardest question: if this goes wrong, is this patient for emergency surgery.
**Anaesthesia:** Know that answer before you start. Read the CT report for the things that predict your emergencies: low coronary heights or a valve-in-valve procedure (coronary obstruction), heavy or eccentric annular and outflow calcium (rupture and leak), small or tortuous calcified iliac arteries (access injury), and pre-existing right bundle branch block (complete heart block). External defibrillator pads go on every patient. Check where the blood is, where the echo machine is, and that the anaesthetic machine and a difficult airway kit are actually in the room and not in the corridor.

### 2. Sedation or general anaesthesia
**Why:** The operator needs a still patient for about an hour and a stable pressure. Transoesophageal echo is not needed for transfemoral TAVI; fluoroscopy and a transthoracic probe are enough.
**Anaesthesia:** The one randomised trial found sedation and general anaesthesia equivalent for death, stroke, infarction, infection and kidney injury at 30 days and no different at one year, with far less vasopressor use under sedation; registry analysis suggests a slightly shorter stay. The defensible default is local anaesthesia with minimal sedation: a low-dose remifentanil or dexmedetomidine infusion, or small increments of a short-acting opioid, oxygen with capnography, and a patient who can still talk. Deep sedation is the worst of both: an obstructed airway, hypercapnia raising pulmonary pressures, and movement at the moment of deployment. Choose general anaesthesia from the start for the patient who cannot lie flat or still, a difficult airway that would be dangerous to manage under the image intensifier, alternative access, and anyone in whom transoesophageal echo is wanted. The haemodynamic goals are those of aortic stenosis: a vasopressor infusion connected before any sedative, sinus rhythm, rate 60-80 per minute.

### 3. Vascular access and heparin
**Why:** The delivery sheath goes into the better common femoral artery under ultrasound, with closure sutures placed before the artery is dilated. The other femoral artery takes a pigtail catheter for root angiography, and a femoral vein takes the pacing wire.
**Anaesthesia:** Local anaesthetic infiltration is all the analgesia needed. Heparin is given once the sheaths are in, to an activated clotting time above about 250-300 s. Advancing the large sheath through a narrow calcified iliac artery is painful and is the first moment for access injury; note the pressure before and after.

### 4. Pacing wire and capture test
**Why:** Rapid ventricular pacing drops the stroke volume to almost nothing so that the balloon and valve are not ejected. The wire also serves as backup pacing if the conduction system is injured.
**Anaesthesia:** Watch the test run: every spike must capture, and the arterial trace should fall to a mean of about 50 mmHg or less with a pulse pressure under 10-20 mmHg. Loss of capture during deployment is how valves embolise. A right ventricular wire can perforate a thin elderly ventricle, so a new effusion later in the case has two candidate causes. Many operators now pace through the stiff left ventricular guidewire and avoid the venous wire altogether.

### 5. Crossing the valve and placing the stiff wire
**Why:** A straight wire is worked across the stenotic orifice and exchanged for a stiff pre-shaped guidewire that sits coiled in the apex and carries everything that follows.
**Anaesthesia:** Catheters across a critically narrow valve obstruct it further and provoke ectopy and runs of ventricular tachycardia; the pressure sags. Support it with the vasopressor, not with fluid boluses. The stiff wire is the commonest cause of left ventricular perforation: tamponade from it may declare itself now or 20 minutes later.

### 6. Balloon valvuloplasty under rapid pacing
**Why:** Some valves are predilated with a balloon to crack the calcium and let the device cross and expand. Many operators omit this to reduce embolic debris and conduction injury.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the rehearsal for deployment. Before each pacing run, raise the systolic pressure to at least the patient's baseline with a vasopressor bolus, because a thick ventricle that starts a 15-second period of no coronary flow at a low pressure may not restart. Keep runs short, and insist on recovery of pressure and ST segments between them. If the pressure does not recover within seconds of pacing stopping, treat it at once with a vasopressor bolus, then adrenaline; think of acute severe regurgitation from the ballooned valve, coronary occlusion and tamponade, and say what you see. Slow recovery after valvuloplasty is the warning that the next run needs a better starting pressure.

### 7. Valve deployment
**Why:** The device is positioned against the angiographic annular plane and expanded: in seconds under rapid pacing for a balloon-expandable valve, or over a minute or two of gradual unsheathing, sometimes with slower pacing, for a self-expanding one.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient must not move, cough or breathe deeply. Under sedation, tell them what they will feel and lighten nothing and deepen nothing in the minute beforehand; under general anaesthesia, hold ventilation. Pressure is topped up before pacing as before. When pacing stops, the obstruction has gone: expect the systolic pressure to overshoot and be ready to turn the vasopressor down, since a hypertensive surge stresses the access vessels and a freshly instrumented root.

### 8. The first five minutes after deployment
**Why:** The operator checks position, leak and coronary flow with an aortogram and echo.
**Anaesthesia:** Run a fixed differential for hypotension after deployment and say it aloud. Low diastolic pressure and a wide pulse pressure: paravalvular or central regurgitation, which may need post-dilatation (another pacing run) or a second valve. ST elevation, ventricular arrhythmia and a ventricle that looks stunned on echo: coronary obstruction, needing immediate stenting or bypass. Rising heart rate, falling pressure, new effusion: wire perforation or annular rupture, needing pericardiocentesis, reversal of heparin and often surgery. Bradycardia or asystole: conduction block, paced through the wire that is still in place. Pressure falling with a good valve and an empty ventricle: bleeding from the access vessels, which may be hidden in the retroperitoneum. In severe stenosis before the valve is in, or with any of these after it, external compressions are a poor substitute for fixing the cause; femoral bypass or extracorporeal support is the bridge if the heart team has agreed the patient is for it.

### 9. Sheath removal and closure
**Why:** The delivery sheath is withdrawn and the pre-placed sutures tied, with an angiogram from the other side to confirm the artery is intact and patent.
**Anaesthesia:** This is when an iliac artery that was torn but tamponaded by the sheath starts to bleed. Hypotension and tachycardia in the minutes after sheath removal, sometimes with flank or back pain in an awake patient, is retroperitoneal haemorrhage until excluded; the operator can occlude the vessel with a balloon from the other groin and place a covered stent, and you need blood running. The physiology is that of the access injuries described for [endovascular aneurysm repair](endovascular-aneurysm-repair.html). Protamine is given at the operator's request, slowly.

### 10. Recovery and conduction surveillance
**Why:** The frame continues to press on the conduction tissue as it settles and the tissue swells.
**Anaesthesia:** New left bundle branch block is common and complete block can appear hours to days later, more often with self-expanding valves, deeper implants, predilatation and pre-existing right bundle branch block. The temporary wire stays in overnight for those at risk and telemetry continues; a proportion go on to a [permanent pacemaker](pacemaker-defibrillator-and-lead-extraction.html). Check the groins, the foot pulses and the neurology before leaving the patient, and expect delirium in the frail.

### 11. Mitral and tricuspid edge-to-edge repair
**Why:** Through the femoral vein and a puncture of the atrial septum placed at a precise height above the mitral valve, a clip is steered down onto the regurgitant jet, the two leaflets are grasped and the clip closed, creating a double-orifice valve. Every movement is guided by transoesophageal echo.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with a tracheal tube, because the probe is in for two to four hours and the operator needs apnoea or controlled breathing at the moment of grasping. These are heart failure patients with poor ventricles, pulmonary hypertension and often kidney disease: an arterial line, a careful induction and an inotrope available. General anaesthesia unloads the valve and makes the regurgitation look milder, so before the result is judged raise the pressure to the patient's usual with a vasopressor. A fall in left atrial pressure and the return of forward pulmonary vein flow is the sign of success; a rising transmitral gradient is the sign that another clip would cause stenosis. Complications that reach you: tamponade from the transseptal puncture, air or thrombus through the large left atrial sheath, oesophageal injury from prolonged probe manipulation, and bleeding from the venous access site.

### 12. Appendage occlusion and septal closure
**Why:** A self-expanding plug is delivered transseptally into the left atrial appendage and released once imaging shows a seal and a stable position. A septal occluder is deployed with one disc each side of the defect.
**Anaesthesia:** Either general anaesthesia with transoesophageal echo or sedation with intracardiac echo, by unit practice. The appendage is paper-thin and tamponade is the complication to be ready for: pads on, pericardial drain kit in the room, heparin reversal drawn up. The appendage is measured under filled conditions, so the operator may ask for fluid if the patient has been fasted dry. Any open sheath in the left atrium is a route for air: avoid deep spontaneous inspiration against an obstructed airway, which sucks air in, and treat sudden ST elevation or a neurological change as air embolism. A device that embolises is retrieved by snare or by a surgeon.

## Pain plan

The "block" on this card is local infiltration by the operator, and the plan is paracetamol. With percutaneous femoral access the worst pain is a 1 or 2 out of 10 in the groin and back, and opioids are not needed after the patient leaves the room; in this age group they mostly cause delirium, urinary retention and nausea. Avoid NSAIDs: kidney function is marginal, contrast has just been given and antithrombotic drugs are being started.

A surgical cut-down in the groin behaves like any groin incision and is helped by infiltration or an ilioinguinal block. Subclavian and carotid approaches leave a short neck or infraclavicular wound that paracetamol and a few opioid doses cover. Transapical access is a left anterior mini-thoracotomy through the fifth or sixth interspace: it hurts as a chest-wall wound does, compromises breathing in exactly the patients who were too frail for surgery, and justifies an intercostal, serratus or erector spinae block with a catheter if the unit runs one. Severe groin, flank or back pain after a transfemoral case is not a pain problem; it is a bleeding problem until imaging says otherwise.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Valve-in-valve inside a failed surgical bioprosthesis: the highest risk of coronary obstruction, because the old leaflets are pushed outwards into the sinuses. The operator may protect a coronary with a wire and undeployed stent beforehand.
- Severe left ventricular impairment or cardiogenic shock: pacing runs are poorly tolerated; discuss elective mechanical support or general anaesthesia with full monitoring.
- Conversion to general anaesthesia mid-case happens in a small proportion, for agitation, airway obstruction or a complication. Have the drugs drawn up, the pillow arrangement and the table position planned, and know how you will reach the airway with the image intensifier in place.
- Stroke is embolic and usually evident on waking or within hours: a neurological check at the end of the case is part of the anaesthetic.
- Kidney injury from contrast and hypotension: ask for the contrast total and avoid hypotension between pacing runs.
- Pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular dysfunction: the patient in whom hypercapnia from sedation matters most; lighter sedation or a controlled airway, not something in between.
- Transcatheter mitral valve replacement and valve-in-ring procedures add the risk of left ventricular outflow obstruction from the displaced anterior leaflet, managed as for systolic anterior motion in [mitral valve repair](mitral-valve-repair-and-replacement.html).

## Key sources

The sedation question has one randomised trial of 447 patients, powered for equivalence on a composite, supported by large registries with the usual selection bias; the evidence supports sedation as safe, not as superior. Rapid pacing technique, the differential for collapse and the conduct of edge-to-edge repair are consensus and operator practice. Pacemaker predictors come from pooled observational data.
