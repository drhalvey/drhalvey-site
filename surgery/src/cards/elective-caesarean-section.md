## What the surgeon is doing and why

A caesarean section delivers a baby through the front of the uterus because the vaginal route is unsafe, has failed before, or is declined. In the elective (category 4) case there is no clock: the woman is fasted, the fetus is well, and the timing suits the team. That is what makes this the operation on which the technique is learned and against which every emergency variant is measured.

The route is short and deliberately avoids cutting muscle. A low transverse skin incision 2-3 cm above the symphysis (Pfannenstiel, curved and sharp-dissected, or Joel-Cohen, straight, slightly higher and opened largely by blunt stretching) goes through skin and fat to the rectus sheath. The sheath is opened transversely, the two rectus bellies are separated in the midline and pulled apart, and the peritoneum is entered high to stay clear of the bladder. The lower uterine segment, thinned by late pregnancy, is opened transversely because it is less vascular than the upper segment, heals with a stronger scar and lies behind the bladder reflection. The baby is delivered through that opening with fundal pressure from the assistant, the cord is clamped after a delay, the placenta follows, and the uterus is closed in one or two layers. The sheath is the strength layer of the abdominal closure; the peritoneum is usually left open.

Three things about that sequence set the anaesthetic. First, there are two patients until the cord is clamped, and the second one is perfused by a vascular bed with no autoregulation, so maternal blood pressure in the ten minutes after the spinal is the fetal oxygen supply. Second, the uterus receives around 700 mL/min at term and the only thing that stops the placental bed bleeding after delivery is myometrial contraction, so the uterotonic is a haemostatic drug given by you on the surgeon's behalf. Third, the mother is awake, which means the block has to cover visceral traction on peritoneum and a uterus lifted out of the abdomen, not just the skin incision.

The pain logic is abdominal wall first and viscus second. The transverse incision stretches rather than divides the recti, which is why caesarean section scores 3 and an abdominal hysterectomy through a similar skin incision scores 4, but the sheath repair is loaded by every sit-up from a hospital bed, every cough and every lift of a 3.5 kg baby. Underneath is the hysterotomy and a uterus that contracts hard for several days, more in multiparas and during breastfeeding, which is a crampy visceral pain that responds better to an NSAID than to an opioid. PROSPECT describes the result as moderate to severe pain that affects recovery, breastfeeding and bonding. It peaks on day 1 when the intrathecal morphine has gone and the woman is mobilising, and has largely settled by the end of the first week. The background physiology (aortocaval compression, neuraxial dose reduction, the airway) is in [obstetric essentials](../a/a09-obstetric-essentials.html).

## Steps

### 1. Preparation and the plan for failure
**Why:** The obstetrician has chosen the date, usually at 39 weeks to minimise neonatal respiratory morbidity, and needs a fasted woman with a valid group and screen, a known placental site and a documented reason for the operation.
**Anaesthesia:** Read the placental site and the number of previous caesareans before anything else: an anterior low placenta over an old scar is a [placenta accreta spectrum](major-obstetric-haemorrhage-and-placenta-accreta.html) case until imaging says otherwise, and it does not belong on a routine list. Examine the airway and the back, record the booking haemoglobin and platelet count, and give the acid prophylaxis. Enhanced recovery starts here: clear fluids to two hours (a carbohydrate drink is reasonable), no sedative premedication, and an explanation of what pulling and pressure feel like so that the first tug is not interpreted as failure. The consent conversation covers conversion to general anaesthesia and what will be offered if she feels pain, because that conversation cannot be held properly mid-operation.

### 2. The spinal
**Why:** The surgeon needs a still, relaxed abdomen and a block that covers peritoneal traction; the paediatric and midwifery teams need an awake mother for skin-to-skin contact.
**Anaesthesia:** Hyperbaric bupivacaine 0.5% 2.2-2.5 mL (11-12.5 mg) with fentanyl 15 micrograms and preservative-free morphine 100 micrograms, through a 25 G or 27 G pencil-point needle at L3-4 or below, sitting or lateral. The bupivacaine dose is lower than for a non-pregnant adult because engorged epidural veins shrink the lumbar cerebrospinal fluid volume. Fentanyl buys intraoperative visceral cover; morphine buys the first night. PROSPECT recommends morphine 50-100 micrograms, and the dose meta-analysis explains the ceiling: going above 100 micrograms adds about 4.5 hours to the time before the first request for analgesia, does not change pain scores or 24-hour opioid use, and costs more itching and vomiting. Start the phenylephrine infusion and a crystalloid co-load as the injection finishes, not when the first low reading appears.

### 3. Tilt, pressure and block assessment
**Why:** Nothing surgical happens for ten minutes, and this is the most dangerous ten minutes of the anaesthetic for the fetus.
**Anaesthesia:** Supine with left uterine displacement, pressure cycling every minute. The consensus target is a systolic at or above 90% of an accurate baseline, with phenylephrine 25-50 micrograms/min adjusted up and down and boluses of 50-100 micrograms for dips. Nausea, yawning or a quiet patient in the first minutes is hypotension until the cuff says otherwise. Phenylephrine slows the heart by baroreflex; a heart rate in the 40s with a normal pressure needs the infusion turned down, and a low rate with a low pressure is the indication for ephedrine 6-9 mg IV or glycopyrronium 200 micrograms IV. In Australian practice metaraminol 0.5 mg boluses or an infusion are often substituted, with less trial evidence behind them. Test the block formally and write it down: bilateral loss of light touch to T5 (cold to T4 runs two segments higher and is less reliable as a predictor of comfort), dense sacral block, and inability to straight-leg raise. A block that has stopped at T8 at 15 minutes is a decision point before the knife, not after it.

### 4. Antibiotic, catheter, drapes and the check before incision
**Why:** Surgical site infection and endometritis are the commonest complications of the operation; the bladder is emptied to drop it out of the field.
**Anaesthesia:** Cefazolin 2 g IV goes in before skin incision: giving it after cord clamping to spare the baby is obsolete and leaves the wound unprotected at the moment of contamination. The urinary catheter and skin preparation are a second test of the sacral and low thoracic block. Lower the drape or use a clear one if the parents want to watch the birth. Calf compressors on.

### 5. Skin to peritoneum
**Why:** A Joel-Cohen entry opens the layers by blunt stretching in the direction of the fibres rather than sharp dissection, which is quicker and tears fewer small vessels and nerves; PROSPECT recommends it over Pfannenstiel on pain grounds. The peritoneum is opened high to protect the bladder.
**Anaesthesia:** The skin incision tests T11-12; rectus retraction and the peritoneal incision test T6-10. This is where an inadequate block declares itself. The guidance on intraoperative pain is explicit: believe her, ask the surgeon to stop, and decide between a fast-acting supplement and general anaesthesia with her rather than for her. Alfentanil 250-500 micrograms IV in increments, nitrous oxide 50% by mask, ketamine 10-20 mg IV, or an epidural top-up if a combined technique was used are the options before delivery; record what was said and done. Pain that is not controlled within a couple of minutes is treated with general anaesthesia.

### 6. Hysterotomy and delivery
**Why:** The lower segment is incised transversely and extended bluntly, the membranes are ruptured, the surgeon's hand lifts the presenting part into the wound and the assistant delivers the baby by pushing on the fundus.
**Anaesthesia:** Warn her: fundal pressure is felt as a heavy weight on the upper abdomen and chest even under a perfect block, and for a few seconds she will feel unable to breathe in. Tilt can come off once the baby is out. Liquor and blood arrive together in the suction; note the volume in the canister before and after. A difficult delivery of a deeply engaged or high floating head, or a breech with an entrapped head, is the one moment in an elective case when the surgeon may want acute uterine relaxation: glyceryl trinitrate 50-100 micrograms IV, repeated, works in under a minute and wears off before it matters for tone.

### 7. Cord clamping and the uterotonic
**Why:** Delayed clamping for at least 60 seconds transfuses the baby from the placenta. Once the cord is clamped the uterus must contract to close the spiral arteries of the placental bed.
**Anaesthesia:** The consensus statement on uterotonics makes the point that the traditional 5 IU bolus is several times what an elective uterus needs and produces vasodilatation, tachycardia, hypotension and ST changes. Give oxytocin 1 IU slowly and start an infusion (2.5-7.5 IU/h is the recommended range for elective cases), or carbetocin 100 micrograms slowly, which replaces the infusion. Reassess tone with the surgeon at two minutes. If the uterus is still soft, go to a second-line drug early rather than repeating oxytocin: ergometrine 250 micrograms IM or slow IV (not with hypertension), or carboprost 250 micrograms IM (not with asthma). Turn the phenylephrine infusion down as the oxytocin effect passes; the autotransfusion from the contracting uterus raises preload at the same time.

### 8. Placenta, exteriorisation and uterine closure
**Why:** The placenta is delivered by cord traction rather than manual removal, the cavity is checked, and the hysterotomy is closed in one or two layers. Some surgeons lift the uterus out of the abdomen to see the angles and the back wall; others repair it in situ.
**Anaesthesia:** Exteriorisation pulls on the broad ligaments and the peritoneum above the block's reliable visceral cover, and it is the commonest cause of nausea, retching and shoulder or chest discomfort in a previously comfortable patient. Check the pressure first, then treat with ondansetron and, if needed, a small dose of alfentanil; the fentanyl in the spinal was put there for this step. Venous air entrainment through open uterine sinuses with the uterus above the heart is common on Doppler and almost always silent; sudden breathlessness, chest pain and desaturation at this point should bring it to mind alongside the rarer catastrophes. Give dexamethasone 8 mg IV now.

### 9. Haemostasis and abdominal closure
**Why:** The angles of the hysterotomy and the bladder reflection are inspected, clot is cleared from the paracolic gutters, the peritoneum is left open and the sheath is closed with a continuous suture.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the time to total the blood loss honestly: suction minus liquor, weighed swabs, and what is under the drapes. More than 1,000 mL, a uterus that keeps relaxing, or a rising heart rate moves the case onto the [major haemorrhage](major-obstetric-haemorrhage-and-placenta-accreta.html) pathway, starting with TXA 1 g IV. Swabbing the gutters is the other predictable stimulus above the block. Non-closure of the peritoneum is a PROSPECT recommendation because it shortens the operation and reduces pain. A paracetamol 1 g IV dose and the first NSAID go in here unless bleeding, renal function or pre-eclampsia argue against the NSAID.

### 10. Skin, dressing and the decision about a block
**Why:** Subcuticular closure and a dressing; some surgeons infiltrate the wound or place an abdominal binder.
**Anaesthesia:** If intrathecal morphine went in, fascial plane blocks add little and PROSPECT does not recommend them routinely. If it did not (allergy, a spinal given without it, conversion to general anaesthesia), bilateral TAP or quadratus lumborum blocks or surgeon wound infiltration are the recommended substitute; see [general anaesthesia for caesarean section](general-anaesthesia-for-caesarean-section-and.html). An abdominal binder is a recommended, cheap adjunct.

### 11. Recovery, the first night and getting up
**Why:** The surgical aims after a straightforward caesarean are a contracted uterus, a dry wound, a mobile mother and an established feed.
**Anaesthesia:** Fundal checks and lochia every 15 minutes for the first hour; most postpartum haemorrhage after caesarean is recognised here, not in theatre. Intrathecal morphine at 100 micrograms carries a low but real risk of late respiratory depression, concentrated in women with obesity, sleep apnoea or co-administered sedatives: hourly sedation score and respiratory rate for the first 12 hours and 2-hourly to 24 hours is a common protocol, and no other long-acting opioid or sedative is charted in that window. Pruritus is common and responds to low-dose naloxone or nalbuphine where available; ondansetron helps some. The joint neurological monitoring guideline asks for a documented straight-leg raise by 4 hours after the spinal and escalation to the anaesthetist if that has not returned, because a dense block that is not regressing is the only early sign of a vertebral canal haematoma. Enhanced recovery from here is eating within an hour or two, catheter out and walking at 6-12 hours, and discharge the next day or the one after.

## Pain plan

The analgesic strategy is the spinal itself plus regular simple analgesia, and the block question on this card is really a question about intrathecal morphine. PROSPECT's recommendations for elective caesarean under neuraxial anaesthesia are: intrathecal morphine 50-100 micrograms (or diamorphine 300 micrograms where it is available), paracetamol, an NSAID, and intravenous dexamethasone after delivery, with opioids reserved for rescue. Surgical recommendations are a Joel-Cohen incision, leaving the peritoneum open and an abdominal binder. Wound infiltration, continuous wound infusion and TAP or quadratus lumborum blocks are recommended only when intrathecal opioid was not given.

The mechanism explains why this combination works. Morphine in the cerebrospinal fluid covers both the somatic wound and the visceral uterus for 12-24 hours. The NSAID is the specific treatment for afterpains, which are prostaglandin-driven, and is the single most opioid-sparing oral drug in this operation. Paracetamol is regular, not as-needed. The oral opioid is short-acting, small-dose and for rescue: a breastfeeding mother clears it into milk, and a sedated mother with intrathecal morphine on board is the respiratory depression case. Codeine and tramadol are avoided in breastfeeding women in Australian practice because ultra-rapid metabolisers deliver unpredictable doses to the infant. Gabapentinoids, intrathecal clonidine and dexmedetomidine, and ketamine are effective in trials but were left out of the PROSPECT recommendations because of sedation and other risks in this population.

A catheter is not part of the analgesic plan. A combined spinal-epidural is chosen for surgical duration or haemodynamic titration, and the epidural is removed at the end.

Rebound is moderate and predictable. The intrathecal morphine fades between 12 and 24 hours, which is when the woman first gets out of bed, and day 1 is the worst day. The plan fails when paracetamol and the NSAID have been charted as-needed, when the NSAID has been withheld without a reason, or when the first oral dose waits until pain has arrived. Escalation triggers: pain above 6 on movement on day 1 despite regular drugs deserves an examination before more opioid, looking for a wound or rectus sheath haematoma, a distended bladder after catheter removal, or ileus. Persistent unilateral pain with a tender lateral edge of the incision that outlasts the first week suggests ilioinguinal or iliohypogastric nerve entrapment in the sheath closure. The woman on opioid agonist treatment or with chronic pain is the phenotype that breaks the standard plan: keep the maintenance drug, add the intrathecal morphine anyway, add TAP or quadratus lumborum blocks, and plan with the pain service before the day.

## Pitfalls and variants

- High-risk variants that should not be on a routine list: placenta praevia, suspected accreta, three or more previous caesareans, large fibroids in the lower segment, BMI above 50, significant cardiac disease. These need a combined spinal-epidural or a planned general anaesthetic, two large cannulae, an arterial line for the accreta and cardiac cases, cross-matched blood and cell salvage. The threshold for critical care is ongoing bleeding beyond 2,000 mL, vasopressor dependence after haemostasis, or a hysterectomy.
- High block. Arm weakness and difficulty speaking are warnings; loss of grip, a whisper and bradycardia mean intubate. Reassure, support ventilation, give the vasopressor, and secure the airway with a rapid sequence induction if she cannot protect it.
- Failed or patchy spinal. Before incision the options are a repeat spinal with a reduced dose (risk of a high block if the first partly worked), a combined technique, or general anaesthesia. After incision the options narrow to supplementation or general anaesthesia within minutes.
- Surgeons vary on exteriorisation, on one or two layer closure, on a bladder flap, and on skin incision. Exteriorisation is the variant that changes your intraoperative antiemetic and analgesic needs.
- Uterotonic side effects are dose and speed related. Carbetocin shares oxytocin's vasodilatation; ergometrine causes vomiting and hypertension; carboprost causes bronchospasm, diarrhoea and desaturation.
- Phenylephrine and the heart rate. Treating baroreflex bradycardia with atropine while the pressure is high produces hypertension and headache; reduce the infusion instead.
- Pruritus, nausea and urinary retention are the price of intrathecal morphine; counsel for them before the operation.
- Shivering is common, multifactorial and distressing; warm the fluids and the patient, and it usually passes after delivery.
- Obesity. Longer needle, longer surgery, more hypotension, harder airway: a combined spinal-epidural gives a second chance at the block and a route for extension.
- Twins and polyhydramnios. More aortocaval compression before delivery and more atony after it; start with the higher end of the uterotonic range and have the second-line drugs in the room.
- Post-dural puncture headache after a pencil-point spinal is uncommon but presents on day 1-3; see [labour epidural analgesia and its complications](labour-epidural-analgesia-and-its-complications.html).

## Key sources

Elective caesarean section is one of the best-evidenced operations in the book: PROSPECT's 2021 update drew on 145 randomised trials, and the vasopressor and uterotonic consensus statements are built on dose-finding studies done in exactly this population. The limits are worth knowing. PROSPECT excluded emergency caesareans and those under general anaesthesia, so its recommendations are extrapolated on the neighbouring cards. The respiratory monitoring interval after intrathecal morphine and the 4-hour straight-leg raise check are guideline consensus rather than trial findings. The use of metaraminol in place of phenylephrine is Australian custom with far less trial evidence, and the advice here on prophylactic tranexamic acid is the author's judgement.
