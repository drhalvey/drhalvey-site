## What the surgeon is doing and why

Cancer that has invaded the detrusor muscle (diagnosed at [transurethral resection](transurethral-resection-of-bladder-tumour.html)) cannot be scraped out from inside. The curative operation removes the bladder with everything that shares its lymphatics and fascial envelope: in men the prostate and seminal vesicles, in women usually the uterus, ovaries and anterior vaginal wall, and in both the pelvic lymph nodes up to the common iliac vessels. The ureters are divided low. The patient then has two ureters and nowhere for the urine to go, so the second half of the operation is reconstruction.

There are two common reconstructions. An ileal conduit isolates 15-20 cm of terminal ileum on its mesentery, joins the ureters to its closed end and brings the other end out as a stoma in the right iliac fossa: quick, reliable, and the choice in nine out of ten patients in the largest recent trial. An orthotopic neobladder takes 50-60 cm of ileum, opens it along its length, folds it into a low-pressure sphere and sews it to the urethral stump, so that the patient voids by straining; it is longer, depends on a clean urethral margin and a motivated patient with good renal function, and adds a pelvic anastomosis. In both, the ileum is rejoined with a small bowel anastomosis.

So this is three operations in sequence: a deep pelvic cancer resection with its venous bleeding, an extended lymphadenectomy along the iliac vessels, and a bowel resection with urinary reconstruction. It is done on patients with a mean age near 70, most of them smokers, a third of them just out of cisplatin-based chemotherapy with the anaemia, renal impairment and deconditioning that brings. In-hospital complication rates of a half to three quarters were recorded in the two arms of a randomised trial from an expert centre. Almost none of that morbidity is surgical catastrophe; it is ileus, infection, cardiac events and thrombosis, which is why the anaesthetic decisions (fluid, analgesia, opioid exposure) show up in the outcomes.

The robotic operation with the diversion built inside the abdomen is now a real alternative. The multicentre trial found two more days alive and out of hospital at 90 days, fewer thromboembolic events (1.9% against 8.3%) and fewer wound complications (5.6% against 16.0%), with the same cancer outcomes. It replaces the laparotomy with four to six hours of steep head-down, which is the trade described on the [prostatectomy card](robotic-and-open-radical-prostatectomy.html).

The pain logic follows the incision. The open operation is a midline laparotomy: an abdominal-wall mechanism, severe on coughing and movement for three or four days, in a patient whose bowel has been handled and anastomosed and will distend. It scores 4. The visceral and pelvic component is modest. The analgesic problem is made harder by the fact that the obvious drug, opioid, prolongs the ileus that determines the length of stay.

## Steps

### 1. Before the day
**Why:** The pathway starts weeks earlier: nutrition, anaemia treatment, smoking, stoma siting, and for most patients neoadjuvant chemotherapy that finishes four to eight weeks before surgery.
**Anaesthesia:** Look for what cisplatin has left: reduced eGFR, low magnesium, anaemia, neuropathy. Haemoglobin and iron are worth correcting. Functional capacity predicts the course; a patient who cannot climb a flight of stairs needs an honest conversation and a critical care bed. The enhanced recovery guideline found no benefit from mechanical bowel preparation, so the patient should arrive hydrated, with carbohydrate drinks up to two hours before. The principles are in [the enhanced recovery chapter](../a/a06-eras-abdominal.html).

### 2. Epidural, induction and lines
**Why:** Everything is easier to place before a six-hour case than during it.
**Anaesthesia:** For open surgery, a thoracic epidural at T9-T11, tested before induction. For robotic surgery, intrathecal morphine or a plan for truncal blocks at the end; intrathecal morphine brings hourly sedation and respiratory rate observations for 24 hours, which the high dependency bed already provides. Arterial line and two large cannulae in every case; a central line if noradrenaline will run for hours through doubtful peripheral veins. Start the noradrenaline at a low rate from induction: it is part of the fluid strategy, not a rescue. Tracheal tube, orogastric tube for the case only, temperature probe, calf compression.

### 3. Position
**Why:** Open: the table is broken at the pelvis with head-down tilt to lift the pelvic contents into view; women and neobladder patients are in low lithotomy for perineal access. Robotic: lithotomy and steep head-down with the robot docked.
**Anaesthesia:** Hours in hyperextension or lithotomy: pad heels, sacrum and peroneal nerves, keep the hips modestly flexed, and note the time the legs go up, because compartment syndrome risk rises steeply after four hours. For the robotic case the anti-slide measures, test tilt, eye protection and airway pressure management are exactly those of robotic prostatectomy, for longer.

### 4. Laparotomy, mobilisation and division of the ureters
**Why:** The peritoneum over the bladder is opened, the ureters are found at the pelvic brim, clipped and divided, and their ends sent for frozen section.
**Anaesthesia:** From here there is no urine output to guide you until the conduit is draining, typically four hours later. Decide now what will stand in for it: arterial pressure, lactate, stroke volume variation or a cardiac output monitor. The trial that defines this operation's fluid management gave balanced crystalloid at 1 mL/kg/h with noradrenaline until the bladder was out, then 3 mL/kg/h, against 6 mL/kg/h throughout: complications fell from 73% to 52%, gastrointestinal complications from 37% to 6%, and cardiac complications from 48% to 20%.

### 5. Pelvic lymphadenectomy
**Why:** Nodes are cleared from the obturator fossa and along the external, internal and common iliac vessels on both sides; the count and the limits matter for staging and probably survival.
**Anaesthesia:** Long, quiet and occasionally sudden: an iliac vein tear is fast venous bleeding in a deep hole. The obturator nerve is in the field and a light plane shows as an adductor twitch with diathermy. Lymphadenectomy is the reason thromboembolism and lymphocele are common afterwards.

### 6. Cystectomy
**Why:** The vascular pedicles of the bladder are divided on each side, the plane behind the bladder is opened from the rectum, and in men the dorsal venous complex and urethra are divided at the prostatic apex; in women the anterior vagina is taken with the specimen and repaired.
**Anaesthesia:** The main blood loss of the operation, mostly venous, arrives here over 30-60 minutes, and a low central venous pressure helps: this is the point of the restrictive phase. Follow the suction and swabs, check a blood gas and haemoglobin, and transfuse on a number and the trend, not on habit. A rectal injury is repaired and may add a defunctioning stoma. Once the specimen is out and haemostasis is secure, the fluid rate goes up.

### 7. Bowel harvest and anastomosis
**Why:** The ileal segment is isolated 15-20 cm from the ileocaecal valve on its mesentery and the bowel is rejoined in front of it.
**Anaesthesia:** The quiet middle of the case. Catch up: temperature, glucose, antibiotic redose at four hours, a blood gas, the epidural infusion running. Bowel handling is the origin of the ileus to come; keep the patient warm and the fluid balance near zero.

### 8. Urinary reconstruction
**Why:** The ureters are spatulated and sewn to the ileal segment over fine stents that are brought out through the conduit. The conduit is brought through the rectus muscle as a stoma, or the neobladder is constructed and sewn to the urethra over a catheter.
**Anaesthesia:** A neobladder adds one to two hours. Urine reappears in the stoma bag or catheter at the end; a bolus of 250-500 mL of balanced crystalloid now to confirm flow is reasonable if the patient has been run dry. Stent position is the surgeon's concern; an oliguric first night is yours.

### 9. Closure and emergence
**Why:** Mass closure of the midline; one or two pelvic drains.
**Anaesthesia:** If there is no epidural, rectus sheath catheters are placed by the surgeon under vision or by you with ultrasound before closure. Reverse fully and extubate awake; most patients are extubated in theatre. Remove the orogastric tube: the guideline found early removal reduced morbidity and stay. Hand over the fluid totals, the vasopressor rate and the fact that urine output was absent by design for hours.

### 10. The first night
**Why:** The surgeon wants a perfused anastomosis, a draining conduit and a patient sitting out the next morning.
**Anaesthesia:** High dependency care. Hypotension with a working epidural is treated with a vasopressor through a reliable line and a check for bleeding, not with repeated crystalloid boluses. Low molecular weight heparin is timed around the epidural, and on a card that makes the epidural routine the intervals belong on the chart: a prophylactic dose is given no sooner than 4 hours after insertion or removal of the catheter, and the catheter is removed no sooner than 12 hours after the last prophylactic dose (24 hours if creatinine clearance is below 30 mL/min, which is not rare after cisplatin). The table is in [the truncal analgesia chapter](../a/a02-truncal-blocks-epidural.html) and the local guideline rules. Hyperchloraemic acidosis appears later in conduit and especially neobladder patients because ileum reabsorbs chloride from urine; avoid adding to it with saline.

### 11. Days 1-7
**Why:** Recovery is paced by the bowel. Stents come out at one to two weeks.
**Anaesthesia:** The epidural is stepped down at 48-72 hours to oral paracetamol and a small dose of oral opioid; an NSAID only if renal function is sound and the surgeon agrees. Vomiting and distension on day 2-4 is ileus and is treated with a nasogastric tube and patience. Where it is available, alvimopan shortened bowel recovery from 6.8 to 5.5 days and stay from 10.1 to 7.4 days in a trial of patients on opioid pumps without epidurals. Fever with rising drain creatinine is a urine leak; pleuritic pain is embolism until excluded.

## Pain plan

For the open operation the mechanism is a midline abdominal wall wound in a patient whose recovery depends on an early-moving bowel, so the plan is built to give strong dynamic analgesia with as little systemic opioid as possible. The defensible default is a thoracic epidural for 48-72 hours with paracetamol, as used in enhanced recovery pathways for open cystectomy (the guideline, by its own account, borrows much of its evidence from colorectal surgery) and as used in the trial that established restrictive fluid with noradrenaline. The epidural and the noradrenaline belong together: a sympathetic block managed with fluid alone undoes the fluid strategy.

Where an epidural is contraindicated, fails, or is not the unit's practice, the alternative is intrathecal morphine for the first day with bilateral rectus sheath or wound catheters for three days. A small randomised trial of 39 patients found no significant difference in rescue opioid between a single-shot posterior quadratus lumborum block and an epidural over two days, though on the day of surgery the block group used more (14 mg against 6.1 mg); it shows that the alternative is feasible, and it is too small to show equivalence. Truncal blocks are covered in [the truncal analgesia chapter](../a/a02-truncal-blocks-epidural.html).

For the robotic operation with intracorporeal diversion, the wounds are ports and a short extraction incision. Intrathecal morphine or bilateral TAP or rectus sheath blocks with a multimodal base is the author's practice by extrapolation from robotic prostatectomy; an epidural is not needed. Whenever intrathecal morphine is used on this card, sedation score and respiratory rate are recorded hourly for about 24 hours under the local protocol, and no background opioid infusion or sedative is charted with it.

Adjuncts: dexamethasone at induction; intravenous lidocaine where there is no epidural; NSAIDs used cautiously, since renal function is often reduced and the ureteric anastomoses are new. Rebound is moderate: the hard day is the day the epidural stops, usually day 3, so the oral regimen starts the day before. Escalation triggers: pain with distension and vomiting is ileus or obstruction; pain with fever and tachycardia after day 3 is a leak (urine or bowel) or collection; calf or chest pain is thromboembolism.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Fluid by reflex. The most studied modifiable factor in this operation. Restrictive until the bladder is out, vasopressor for pressure, replacement afterwards.
- The epidural that drops the pressure on the ward. Needs a vasopressor plan agreed in advance or it is turned off at 2 am and the patient has a laparotomy on a morphine pump.
- Robotic cystectomy. Longer head-down than any other robotic pelvic operation. Screen for the same contraindications, limit fluid to limit facial and airway oedema, and plan the emergence.
- Neobladder. Longer, more bowel, more acidosis, a catheter for weeks; selected fitter patients.
- Women. Anterior exenteration with vaginal reconstruction; lithotomy for all, more bleeding from the vaginal venous plexus.
- Post-chemotherapy patients. Anaemic, renally impaired, sometimes neuropathic: fewer NSAIDs, more transfusion, careful aminoglycoside use.
- The urgent and palliative variant. Cystectomy or diversion alone for intractable haematuria, fistula or after pelvic radiotherapy: frail, anaemic, sometimes septic patients in whom the risk is far higher and intensive care is planned.
- Venous thromboembolism. Cancer, pelvic surgery, lymphadenectomy and a week in bed: extended prophylaxis is standard, and the open trial arm had an 8.3% event rate.
- Hypothermia and pressure injury. Six hours, an open abdomen, a wet field.
- Late metabolic problems. Hyperchloraemic acidosis, vitamin B12 deficiency and stones belong to follow-up, but the acidosis can appear in the first week.

## Key sources

This operation has better anaesthetic evidence than most of urology. The restrictive fluid and noradrenaline trial is a well-conducted single-centre randomised trial with a large effect, and its protocol included an epidural, so it supports the combination and not either part alone. The enhanced recovery guideline is candid that most items are extrapolated from colorectal surgery. The robotic trial settles that intracorporeal robotic cystectomy is at least as safe at 90 days. Evidence on alternatives to the epidural is limited to one small trial, and the recommendations for robotic analgesia are the author's extrapolation. There is no PROSPECT guideline for cystectomy.
