## What the surgeon is doing and why

Early lung cancer is cured by removing the lobe that contains it together with the lymph nodes that drain it. A lobe is the natural unit because each has its own bronchus, its own arterial branches and its own vein, and the fissures give a plane to separate it from its neighbours. A segmentectomy applies the same logic one level down for a small peripheral tumour or a patient who cannot spare a lobe. A wedge resection ignores the anatomy: a stapler is fired across the lung periphery around a nodule, for diagnosis, for a metastasis, or as a compromise operation in a patient with no reserve.

An anatomical resection has the same four tasks whatever the access:

```
 Task                         What can go wrong for you
 ---------------------------  ------------------------------------
 1 Divide the lobar vein      little; shunt through the lobe stops
 2 Divide the arterial        thin-walled, low-pressure, high-flow:
   branches                   a tear bleeds fast and quietly
 3 Divide the lobar bronchus  the wrong bronchus, a stapled suction
                              catheter, a stump that leaks
 4 Complete the fissure,      air leak from raw lung; vagal and
   take the nodes             recurrent laryngeal nerve traction
```

The access is the variable. Video-assisted and robotic surgery use one to four ports and a 3-5 cm utility incision without spreading the ribs; a posterolateral or muscle-sparing thoracotomy divides or splits chest wall muscle and cranks the ribs apart. In the VIOLET trial of 503 patients, thoracoscopic lobectomy gave better physical function at five weeks and fewer serious adverse events after discharge (30.7% against 37.8%) with no early difference in cancer outcomes, which is why the thoracoscopic approach is now the default and thoracotomy is reserved for large central tumours, sleeve resections, dense adhesions and bail-outs.

Everything the surgeon needs from you follows from two facts. The operated lung must be still and collapsed for hours, so the other lung does all the gas exchange while lying underneath the mediastinum. And the patient will leave theatre with less lung than they arrived with, so the plan is built backwards from the first postoperative cough.

The pain logic: the mechanism is chest wall. The intercostal nerve runs in a groove under each rib, and a port levered in that space or a retractor spreading it injures the nerve directly; rib spreading also strains the costovertebral and costotransverse joints behind. The chest drain is a second generator, pressing on parietal pleura and moving with respiration until it comes out. The pain is evoked by exactly the movements that clear secretions. Thoracoscopic surgery reduces the intensity but not the character, and it sits at a 4. An open thoracotomy is the book's anchor for a 5: in pooled data, 57% of patients still have pain at three months and 47% at six months.

## Steps

### 1. Preassessment: can this patient lose this lung
**Why:** The surgeon decides resectability from the scan; operability is a physiological question and is yours to share. A lobectomy removes a calculable fraction of ventilation and gas exchange.
**Anaesthesia:** Calculate predicted postoperative FEV1 and DLCO from the segments to be removed out of 19 (right upper 3, middle 2, right lower 5, left upper 5, left lower 4), discounting segments already obstructed. The ACCP algorithm then sorts the patient: both values above 60% of predicted, low risk and no further testing; either between 30% and 60%, a stair climb or shuttle walk; either below 30%, or a poor walk test, a cardiopulmonary exercise test, where a peak oxygen consumption below 10 mL/kg/min marks high risk. Use the result to plan, not only to gate: a predicted postoperative value near 30% means a catheter technique, an arterial line, a high-dependency bed and a low threshold for a sublobar resection. Smoking cessation and prehabilitation are done in the weeks before, and the ERAS guideline recommends both.

### 2. Regional block and induction
**Why:** Nothing surgical yet. The block goes in while the patient can sit and report, and before any anticoagulant.
**Anaesthesia:** For thoracoscopic surgery, a paravertebral or erector spinae plane block at T4-T6 with ropivacaine 0.5% 20 mL, awake or just after induction, with a catheter left in if conversion is likely or reserve is poor. For a planned thoracotomy, a paravertebral catheter or a thoracic epidural at T5-T7, tested before induction. Give paracetamol 1 g IV and, unless renal function or the surgeon's pleurodesis plans argue against it, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor before incision, as PROSPECT recommends. Induce with short-acting agents and aim for extubation on the table. Arterial line before or after induction according to the heart.

### 3. Lung isolation: double-lumen tube or blocker
**Why:** The surgeon cannot operate on an inflating lung. Isolation also protects the dependent lung from blood and pus running downhill from the operated side.
**Anaesthesia:** A left double-lumen tube suits almost every lobectomy on either side: quick to place, stable when the surgeon handles the hilum, and each lung can be suctioned, inspected and given CPAP independently. A bronchial blocker through a single-lumen tube gives the same quality of collapse in the pooled trials, with less sore throat, hoarseness and airway injury, but is malpositioned more often and takes longer; choose it for the difficult airway, the patient who will stay intubated, or a tracheostomy. Whichever is used, the bronchoscope decides the position, not auscultation. See [one-lung ventilation and thoracic essentials](../a/a03-one-lung-ventilation.html) for sizing and troubleshooting, and [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html) when the airway is the problem.

### 4. Lateral positioning
**Why:** Lateral decubitus with the table flexed opens the rib spaces on the operative side and lets the collapsed lung fall away from the hilum.
**Anaesthesia:** Tubes move during the turn: a double-lumen tube usually comes out by a centimetre as the neck extends, so look again with the bronchoscope once the patient is settled. Keep the neck neutral, the dependent eye and ear free, the dependent brachial plexus off-loaded with a chest roll placed under the ribcage and not in the axilla, and the upper arm no more than 90 degrees. Gravity now helps you: perfusion favours the dependent, ventilated lung, which is why oxygenation on one lung is better lateral than supine.

### 5. Port placement, lung collapse and the start of one-lung ventilation
**Why:** The first port goes in blind or under limited vision, the camera confirms the pleural space is free, and the remaining ports and the utility incision are placed. The surgeon inspects for pleural disease that would change the operation.
**Anaesthesia:** Stop ventilation to the operative lung before the first port so the trocar does not meet an inflated lung, and open that lumen to air. Denitrogenating with a high FiO2 beforehand speeds absorption collapse. Change the ventilator to 4-6 mL/kg predicted body weight with PEEP and a recruitment manoeuvre to the dependent lung. The evidence on how much PEEP is not settled: PROTHOR found that 10 cmH2O with recruitment gave no fewer pulmonary complications than 5 cmH2O, at the cost of more hypotension, while iPROVE-OLV found that PEEP titrated to best compliance with postoperative high-flow oxygen reduced severe complications from 15% to 6%. The defensible reading is moderate PEEP adjusted to compliance and haemodynamics, low driving pressure, and FiO2 reduced as soon as the saturation allows. Saturation typically reaches its lowest point 15-30 minutes after collapse and then improves as hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction takes effect.

### 6. Hypoxaemia on one lung
**Why:** The collapsed lung is still perfused until its artery is divided, so there is an obligatory shunt for the first part of the operation.
**Anaesthesia:** Work through a fixed order. FiO2 to 1.0. Bronchoscope down the tube, because most desaturation is a malpositioned tube or a plugged bronchus. Recruit the ventilated lung and adjust its PEEP. Check cardiac output and haemoglobin. If those fail, oxygen to the operated lung: low-flow insufflation or CPAP of 2-5 cmH2O through the open lumen, which interferes less with an open thoracotomy than with thoracoscopic vision, so say what you are doing. Intermittent two-lung ventilation is always available and should not be delayed for the sake of the surgical tempo. In an anatomical resection the surgeon can end the problem by clamping or dividing the pulmonary artery branch to the lobe. A right-sided operation, a supine position, normal spirometry and a previous contralateral resection all predict worse oxygenation.

### 7. Hilar dissection: vein and artery
**Why:** The lobar vein is divided first in most sequences, then each arterial branch is dissected out of its sheath and stapled or tied. The pulmonary artery wall is thin and tears along its length.
**Anaesthesia:** This is when the blood must already be available and the second cannula must already run. A pulmonary artery injury during thoracoscopic surgery is controlled first by the surgeon pressing a swab on it; your job in that minute is to say the pressure, give volume, call for blood and for the thoracotomy tray, and not to distract. Traction on the hilum and pericardium causes runs of atrial ectopy and falls in output that stop when the retraction stops. After the vein is stapled the lobe is congested; after the artery is divided the shunt through that lobe ends and saturation often improves.

### 8. Bronchus division
**Why:** The lobar bronchus is cleared of nodes and stapled flush, so that no long stump is left to pool secretions. Before firing, the surgeon clamps the stapler and wants proof that the remaining lobes still inflate.
**Anaesthesia:** Before the stapler closes, withdraw anything that might be in that bronchus: suction catheter, blocker, bronchoscope. On request, inflate the operative lung gently by hand; the lobes that are staying should expand and the one that is going should not. Then look down the tube with the bronchoscope if there is any doubt about which bronchus is in the jaws, particularly with the right upper lobe and the left upper division. For a sleeve resection the airway is open in the field and you may be asked to hold ventilation or advance the tube into the opposite main bronchus.

### 9. Fissure, specimen and lymph node dissection
**Why:** Incomplete fissures are stapled, the lobe is bagged and delivered through the utility incision, and hilar and mediastinal node stations are cleared for staging.
**Anaesthesia:** Raw stapled lung is where the postoperative air leak comes from. Subcarinal and paratracheal dissection works beside the vagus, the left recurrent laryngeal nerve in the aortopulmonary window, the thoracic duct and the oesophagus: expect bradycardia with traction and treat it by asking for a pause before reaching for a drug. A patient who is light will cough when the carina is touched, and a cough during hilar work is dangerous; keep neuromuscular block measurable until the nodes are out.

### 10. Leak test and re-expansion
**Why:** The chest is filled with warm saline and the lung inflated so the bronchial stump and the staple lines can be watched for bubbles. The surgeon then needs to see the remaining lobes fill the space.
**Anaesthesia:** Suction the operative bronchus first. Inflate by hand, slowly, to a sustained 20-30 cmH2O and hold it while the surgeon looks; say the pressure aloud. Higher pressures stress the stump and the staple lines for no gain. A steady stream from the stump means it is revised now. Then recruit both lungs and return to protective two-lung ventilation with the lowest FiO2 that holds the saturation.

### 11. Drains, closure and blocks under vision
**Why:** One apical drain, sometimes two, is placed through a port site. In a thoracotomy the ribs are approximated with pericostal sutures, which can trap the intercostal nerve below.
**Anaesthesia:** If the regional technique was not placed at the start, this is the surgeon's opportunity: a paravertebral catheter tunnelled under direct vision, or intercostal blocks at the incision level and two spaces above and below. Top up an existing catheter now so that emergence is covered. Reverse neuromuscular block against a quantitative monitor; residual weakness in a patient who has just lost a lobe is a reintubation.

### 12. Emergence, extubation and the first three days
**Why:** The surgeon wants a patient who is awake, breathing deeply without pain, with the drain swinging and bubbling less each day.
**Anaesthesia:** Extubate sitting up, in theatre. Coughing on the tube raises airway pressure across fresh staple lines, so aim for a smooth emergence with the block working and consider a remifentanil or lidocaine technique. Then three things arrive on a timetable. Pain peaks on days 1-2, when the single shot has gone and physiotherapy starts. Atrial fibrillation appears on days 2-4, more often in older men and after larger resections: continue beta-blockers through the perioperative period, keep potassium and magnesium replete, and treat new fibrillation by rate control and a search for a cause (hypoxia, sputum retention, pain, a leak). Sputum retention and lobar collapse come from a patient who cannot cough, which returns the problem to analgesia. A persistent air leak keeps the drain in and the pain generator with it.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is chest wall and it is evoked by coughing, so the plan is a regional technique that covers the hemithorax, basic analgesia given regularly from before the incision, and opioid only as rescue. PROSPECT has a procedure-specific guideline for each access and they differ.

For thoracoscopic and robotic surgery PROSPECT recommends paracetamol with an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started before or during surgery and continued, a paravertebral or erector spinae plane block as first choice, a serratus anterior plane block as second choice, and opioids kept for rescue. The author's practice is a single-shot paravertebral block before incision with ropivacaine 0.5% 20 mL, dexamethasone 8 mg IV, and the surgeon's intercostal infiltration at the drain site. A thoracic epidural is more than this operation needs when it stays thoracoscopic, and it brings hypotension and a urinary catheter that work against day 1 mobilisation; that is judgement, set against the guideline's first-choice list. A catheter (paravertebral or erector spinae, ropivacaine 0.2% at 8-12 mL/h or as programmed intermittent boluses) is worth placing when conversion is likely, when the patient is opioid-tolerant, or when predicted postoperative lung function is marginal.

For open thoracotomy the 2025 PROSPECT update recommends either a thoracic epidural or a paravertebral block as first-line, by patient and clinician preference, with erector spinae plane, rhomboid intercostal and intercostal nerve blocks as second-line options for patients who can have neither. The Cochrane review behind that equivalence found the same analgesia at rest and on coughing, and markedly less hypotension, nausea, pruritus and urinary retention with paravertebral block (see [analgesia for the trunk](../a/a02-truncal-blocks-epidural.html)). The defensible default is therefore a paravertebral catheter, with an epidural chosen when the surgeon cannot place a catheter, when the incision is bilateral, or when the unit runs epidurals better than it runs paravertebral infusions. A continuous technique stays until the drains are out.

Rebound risk is high. A single shot placed at 8 am is gone by the evening, and the first night after thoracoscopic lobectomy is when an unplanned PCA is started. Chart the oral opioid and the regular NSAID in advance. Escalation triggers: a patient who cannot take a deep breath on day 1 needs a rescue block or catheter that day, not a higher PCA bolus; shoulder-tip pain on the operated side is phrenic referral from the drain or the diaphragm, is not covered by any chest wall block, and responds to an NSAID and paracetamol.

Chronic pain is the long tail. Roughly half of thoracotomy patients have pain at six months, with a neuropathic character in a proportion. No analgesic technique has been shown to prevent it; the modifiable factors are the surgical ones (no rib spreading, no pericostal suture across the nerve) and good acute control, whose effect is plausible and unproven.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Conversion to thoracotomy. Bleeding, adhesions, or an oncological reason. The pain plan changes from a 4 to a 5 in the time it takes to open: place or ask for a catheter before closure.
- Wedge resection and lung biopsy. Short, often a fit patient with a nodule, sometimes a breathless patient with interstitial disease for diagnosis. The second group tolerates one-lung ventilation badly, has the highest risk of an acute exacerbation afterwards, and deserves the lowest pressures and FiO2 you can run.
- Segmentectomy. Longer than a lobectomy and with more raw lung, so more air leak. The surgeon may ask for inflation and deflation, or IV indocyanine green, to show the intersegmental plane; the dye briefly lowers the pulse oximeter reading.
- Robotic surgery. Carbon dioxide insufflation at 5-8 mmHg into the chest reduces venous return and raises end-tidal carbon dioxide; the arms limit your access; emergency undocking has to be rehearsed.
- Sleeve resection. The airway is divided and re-anastomosed: a blocker or a long tube in the opposite bronchus, a longer operation, and bronchoscopy at the end to look at the anastomosis.
- Previous contralateral resection or a single functioning lung. Selective lobar collapse with a blocker, or intermittent apnoea, replaces conventional one-lung ventilation.
- Persistent air leak and the trapped drain. Suction settings and drain timing are surgical, but the analgesic plan must last as long as the drain does.
- Emergency variants. Massive haemoptysis and traumatic lung laceration need isolation to protect the good lung first and resection second; a bleeding lobe is isolated with a double-lumen tube or a blocker placed under bronchoscopic vision through blood, with rigid bronchoscopy as the fallback.
- When more than a lobe has to go, see [pneumonectomy](pneumonectomy.html); when the pleura is the disease, see [pleural surgery](pleural-surgery-decortication-pleurodesis-and-pneumothorax.html); for staging beforehand, see [mediastinoscopy](mediastinoscopy-and-anterior-mediastinal-mass.html).

## Key sources

The analgesic recommendations rest on two PROSPECT guidelines and a Cochrane review, which is as good as procedure-specific evidence gets, though most of the underlying trials are small and the paravertebral against epidural comparison has little to say about chronic pain. The ACCP thresholds for predicted postoperative function are guideline-level and derived from cohort data in an era of open surgery; thoracoscopic resection is tolerated at lower values than the algorithm implies. On ventilation, two large recent trials point in different directions on PEEP and recruitment, and the card's advice to individualise is an interpretation of both. The incidence of atrial fibrillation, the leak-test pressure and the timing of hypoxaemia after collapse are given as consensus without verified figures.
