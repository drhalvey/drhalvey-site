## What the surgeon is doing and why

A chronic subdural haematoma is a disease of the shrinking brain. With age and atrophy the brain pulls away from the dura and the bridging veins are stretched across a widening gap. A minor fall, often forgotten, tears one. The small bleed is not reabsorbed: it provokes an inflammatory membrane on the inner surface of the dura, full of fragile new vessels fed by the middle meningeal artery, and those vessels leak and re-bleed into the collection over weeks. The clot liquefies into a dark, thin fluid under a little pressure. By the time the patient presents, with headache, a hemiparesis, falls or confusion that the family dates to a few weeks ago, there may be 100 mL of fluid and a centimetre of midline shift. Anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs make all of this more likely and are present in a large share of patients.

Because the collection is liquid, it does not need a craniotomy. The surgeon makes one or two burr holes over the thickest part, coagulates and opens the dura and the outer membrane, and the fluid runs out. The cavity is irrigated with warm saline until the return is clear, and a soft drain is left in the subdural or subgaleal space for 24-48 hours on gravity drainage with the patient nursed flat. The drain is the best-evidenced step of the operation: in the randomised trial it reduced recurrence needing redrainage from 24% to 9.3% and six-month mortality from 18.1% to 8.6%.

The surgical aim is the reverse of most cranial surgery. Elsewhere the surgeon wants a slack brain; here the surgeon wants the compressed hemisphere to re-expand and fill the space, because a persisting cavity re-accumulates. That is why you do not hyperventilate or give osmotic agents, why hydration is generous, why nitrous oxide is excluded (the cavity is filled with saline and inevitably some air), and why the patient lies flat afterwards.

The operation has three variants that change your plan. A twist-drill craniostomy is a bedside procedure under local anaesthesia. A mini-craniotomy is used for thick membranes, septated or solid collections and recurrences, takes longer and usually means general anaesthesia. Middle meningeal artery embolisation in the angiography suite, alone or added to surgery, attacks the membrane's blood supply; in the EMBOLISE trial adding it reduced repeat surgery from 11.3% to 4.1%.

The pain logic is the simplest in this group. Two short scalp incisions and two holes, no muscle, with a dura that hurts for the seconds it is being coagulated. Local anaesthetic infiltration covers it completely, and afterwards paracetamol is enough. The anaesthetic question is therefore not analgesia but how much sedation, if any, a frail and sometimes confused person needs to lie still while someone drills their skull.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment: the patient, not the procedure
**Why:** The surgeon has an urgent but rarely immediate operation. A patient who is alert with a mild deficit can wait hours for reversal and optimisation; one with a falling conscious level cannot.
**Anaesthesia:** Establish the conscious level, the deficit and the capacity to cooperate, since that decides local or general anaesthesia. Then the reasons for the fall and the anticoagulant: atrial fibrillation, a mechanical valve, recent stents, a pacemaker, alcohol, cognitive impairment. Mean age in the large dexamethasone trial was 74. Ask about steroids: dexamethasone was once widely prescribed for this condition, but in the Dex-CSDH trial it produced fewer favourable outcomes (83.9% against 90.3%) and more adverse events despite fewer re-operations, so it is no longer routine and should not be started as an anaesthetic antiemetic habit. Check sodium and glucose.

### 2. Anticoagulant and antiplatelet reversal
**Why:** The membranes bleed easily and the surgeon is relying on the drain, not on dissection, so haemostasis has to come from normal coagulation. Recurrence and acute rebleeding into the cavity are the operation's main failures.
**Anaesthesia:** Reversal is finished and confirmed before incision. Warfarin: vitamin K 5-10 mg IV and four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate 25-50 units/kg IV to an INR of 1.4 or below, repeated measurement after the dose; concentrate is preferred to plasma because it works within minutes and without the volume. Dabigatran: idarucizumab 5 g IV, after which urgent procedures in the RE-VERSE AD cohort began at a median of 1.6 hours with normal haemostasis in 93%. Apixaban and rivaroxaban: for a stable patient, wait 24-48 hours from the last dose according to renal function; for an urgent case, prothrombin complex concentrate or andexanet by local protocol. Aspirin alone is usually accepted. Clopidogrel and other P2Y12 inhibitors are a discussion: delay five to seven days where the neurology allows, platelet transfusion where it does not. The thrombotic risk of reversal is real, about 7% at 90 days in the idarucizumab cohort, and is the reason the restart date matters. For a mechanical mitral valve or a recent coronary stent, involve cardiology before reversal, not after.

### 3. Choosing local or general anaesthesia
**Why:** The surgeon needs a head that does not move during drilling and dural opening, for 30-45 minutes.
**Anaesthesia:** Both work. A meta-analysis of 22 mostly observational studies and 3,917 patients found local anaesthesia associated with fewer complications and shorter surgery and stay, with no difference in recurrence; selection bias is obvious, since the sicker and more agitated patients are given general anaesthesia, so read it as showing that local is safe in those selected for it. The defensible default, as author's judgement: local anaesthesia with minimal sedation for the cooperative patient who can lie flat; general anaesthesia for the agitated, the dysphasic who cannot follow instructions, the patient with a Glasgow Coma Score below about 13, bilateral or mini-craniotomy cases, and anyone who cannot lie flat and still. A confused patient under drapes with a drill on the skull is a poor candidate for sedation, because the usual response to restlessness, more sedative, produces an obstructed, hypercapnic and more restless patient.

### 4. Local anaesthesia with sedation
**Why:** The scalp and periosteum are infiltrated at each burr hole site; the surgeon adds more before the dura is coagulated.
**Anaesthesia:** Lidocaine 1% with adrenaline for onset mixed with or followed by ropivacaine or bupivacaine for duration, 10-15 mL per site, or a unilateral scalp block. Sedation is conversation first. If a drug is needed, dexmedetomidine 0.2-0.7 micrograms/kg/h without a loading dose, or propofol by low-dose target-controlled infusion, with capnography and nasal oxygen; avoid midazolam in the elderly, which lasts into the night and feeds delirium. Warn the patient about the noise and vibration of the drill, which is felt as pressure and heard through bone, and about a brief deep ache when the dura is touched. Keep a hand on theirs: movement is usually a need for reassurance or a full bladder.

### 5. General anaesthesia
**Why:** The same operation, with a guaranteed still head.
**Anaesthesia:** A small dose is the principle. Titrated propofol with a short-acting opioid, a supraglottic airway where the patient is fasted and the head is only modestly turned, or a tracheal tube for reduced conscious level and a full stomach. Maintain with sevoflurane or desflurane at low concentration, or propofol. Protect blood pressure with a vasopressor from induction, since the compressed hemisphere depends on it and these patients are often dehydrated from days of poor intake. Ventilate to a normal carbon dioxide, 38-42 mmHg. No nitrous oxide. Surgeon infiltration is still done for post-operative analgesia.

### 6. Burr holes, dural opening and drainage
**Why:** A perforator drill cuts through the skull and disengages at the inner table. The dura and outer membrane are coagulated and opened in a cross, and the fluid escapes under pressure.
**Anaesthesia:** Rapid decompression of a chronically compressed hemisphere occasionally produces a brief bradycardia or a rise in pressure, and rarely a seizure. The surgeon lets the fluid out gradually for that reason. Under local anaesthesia the patient often becomes more alert and moves the weak limb better within minutes, which the surgeon enjoys hearing.

### 7. Irrigation and drain
**Why:** Warm saline is flushed between the two holes until clear, the cavity is left full of saline to exclude air, and a drain is tunnelled out through a separate stab incision.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon may ask for help with re-expansion: keep the patient normocapnic or allow carbon dioxide to drift a little above normal, give 500 mL of crystalloid if the patient is dry, and under general anaesthesia apply a brief sustained inflation as the last hole is closed so the brain rises and displaces air. Head position is set so the frontal burr hole is the highest point for the same purpose.

### 8. Closure, emergence and lying flat
**Why:** The scalp is closed in two layers around the drain, which is connected to a bag at or just below head level.
**Anaesthesia:** Wake promptly and calmly, extubate lying flat or nearly so, and avoid coughing, which can pull blood into the cavity. Examine power and speech in recovery and record them. Paracetamol 1 g IV or oral. The drain bag hangs level with or just below the head, never on suction; a bag dropped to the floor siphons and can cause an acute bleed or pneumocephalus.

### 9. The first 48 hours
**Why:** The surgeon is waiting for the brain to re-expand and for the drain output to fall before removing it.
**Anaesthesia:** The complications that come back to you are: failure to improve or a new deterioration (acute rebleed, tension pneumocephalus or a seizure, all needing a scan); delirium, which is common, multifactorial and made worse by enforced bed rest, a catheter, opioids and benzodiazepines; and thrombosis off anticoagulation, including stroke, pulmonary embolism and valve thrombosis. Agree the restart date for the anticoagulant before the patient leaves the surgical team's care; it is an individual trade between recurrence and embolism, commonly days to a few weeks.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is skin, galea and periosteum at two small sites, so the strategy is infiltration plus paracetamol, and the plan exists mainly to avoid harm. Long-acting local anaesthetic at the incision and drain sites, placed by the surgeon or as a scalp block, covers the operation and the first evening. Regular paracetamol covers the rest. Most patients report less headache than before surgery.

Opioids are rarely required and are the drugs most likely to cause trouble: sedation that obscures the neurological observations, carbon dioxide retention, constipation, falls and delirium in a group selected for frailty. If one is needed, a small dose of oral oxycodone (2.5 mg) or intravenous fentanyl, once. NSAIDs are not used, as author's judgement: the disease is a bleeding membrane, the patients have renal impairment and are about to restart anticoagulants. Gabapentinoids and tramadol have no place, tramadol in particular because seizures are a recognised complication of the condition.

Escalation: worsening headache after drainage is not expected. With drowsiness or a new deficit it is an acute rebleed or tension pneumocephalus and is scanned; with a drain in place and a positional pattern it may be over-drainage, and the bag height is checked first.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The comatose or rapidly deteriorating patient. This is the emergency variant: treated as an acute mass lesion with a tracheal tube, immediate reversal given as the patient goes to theatre, and osmotic therapy if a pupil is dilating, which is the one circumstance where the re-expansion rule is suspended. See [traumatic brain injury](traumatic-brain-injury.html).
- Acute-on-chronic collections. Fresh clot within the old fluid does not drain through a burr hole and the surgeon converts to a mini-craniotomy, with more blood loss and a longer anaesthetic.
- Bilateral collections. Both sides are drained at one sitting; draining one side alone can shift the brain across.
- Middle meningeal artery embolisation. Done under local anaesthesia with light sedation or general anaesthesia in the angiography suite, with the considerations in [remote site anaesthesia](../a/a12-remote-site-sedation.html); procedural stroke was reported in the trial.
- Pacemakers and defibrillators. Monopolar diathermy on the scalp is close to the device's field; arrange bipolar where possible and have a magnet available.
- Cervical spine and other injuries from the fall. A fractured hip or wrist may be awaiting its own operation; the head is usually drained first.
- Post-operative seizures. Prophylaxis is surgeon-dependent. A patient who does not wake as expected may be in non-convulsive status.
- Recurrence. About one in ten with a drain; the second operation is the same anaesthetic in a patient who is a little frailer.
- The principles for raised intracranial pressure that this card inverts are in [neuroanaesthesia essentials](../a/a08-neuroanaesthesia-essentials.html).

## Key sources

The drain has a clean randomised trial. Dexamethasone has a large negative one. Embolisation has a recent positive randomised trial with a safety caveat. The choice of anaesthetic rests on a meta-analysis of largely non-randomised comparisons that is confounded by indication, so the recommendation here is a selection rule and the author's judgement, not a finding. Reversal regimens are standard doses supported by one small randomised trial for warfarin and a cohort study for dabigatran; restart timing has no good evidence. The advice on re-expansion manoeuvres is surgical custom.
