## What the surgeon is doing and why

The gallbladder is a blind-ended bag hanging off the bile duct by a narrow cystic duct and fed by one end artery. Stones that form in it cause trouble in three ways: they impact in the neck and the bag contracts against them (biliary colic, then acute cholecystitis as the wall becomes inflamed and infected), they escape into the common bile duct and block it (jaundice, cholangitis), or they pass the ampulla and trigger pancreatitis. Removing the bag removes the stone factory. Nothing is reconstructed: the operation is a dissection, two or three clips and an extraction.

The whole operation is organised around one risk, the bile duct injury. The cystic duct and artery lie in a small triangle beside the common hepatic duct, and an inflamed, shortened or anomalous triangle is where the common duct gets mistaken for the cystic duct, clipped and divided. The surgeon's defence is the critical view of safety: the lower third of the gallbladder is lifted off the liver bed and the triangle is cleared of fat and fibrous tissue until two, and only two, structures are seen entering the gallbladder. Only then are clips applied. An intraoperative cholangiogram, routine in most Australian practice, maps the anatomy and shows any duct stones. Everything that makes the view hard (acute inflammation, a fibrosed contracted gallbladder, obesity, cirrhosis, previous upper abdominal surgery) lengthens the case, and when the view cannot be achieved the safe exits are a subtotal cholecystectomy, a fundus-first dissection or conversion to an open subcostal incision.

Stones in the duct are dealt with in one of two ways. A transcystic exploration passes a basket or a 3 mm choledochoscope through the cystic duct, with saline flushing and a relaxed sphincter: it adds 20-40 minutes and nothing to the recovery. A choledochotomy opens the common duct directly, retrieves the stones and closes the duct with fine sutures, often over a drain: it adds an hour, a bile leak risk and a night or two in hospital. The alternative is to finish the cholecystectomy and leave the stones for a postoperative ERCP.

To see any of this the surgeon needs a working space, made by insufflating carbon dioxide to 12-15 mmHg, and gravity, supplied by tilting the patient head up and left side down so that the bowel and omentum fall away from the liver. Those two requirements, gas and tilt, are the anaesthetic.

The pain logic follows from what is cut and stretched. Four port wounds go through skin, fascia and muscle, and the umbilical one is enlarged to deliver the gallbladder. The gallbladder bed on the liver is a raw diathermied surface. The peritoneum is stretched and acidified by the gas, and the bubble that remains under the right hemidiaphragm irritates the phrenic nerve and is felt at the shoulder tip, typically the next morning when the patient sits up. None of this is deep, sustained or loaded by movement in the way an upper abdominal incision is, so the pain is moderate, peaks in the first 24 hours and has largely gone by day 3. It is still more than the day-case label suggests: the 179-procedure cohort found that small operations given little analgesia produced some of the highest day 1 scores, and laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the commonest operation after which patients go home under-treated.

## Steps

### 1. Induction, airway and the day-case plan
**Why:** The surgeon needs a relaxed abdominal wall, an empty stomach and a still field for under an hour.
**Anaesthesia:** A tracheal tube is the default because the abdomen will be inflated, the patient tilted head up and the population has reflux, obesity and diabetes in abundance. For acute cholecystitis with vomiting, a rapid sequence induction. Give the dexamethasone at induction (it needs an hour or two to work and PROSPECT grades it A for both nausea and pain), paracetamol and the NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor before or during the case, and choose short-acting opioid: this is a day case in a patient group (female, non-smoking, laparoscopic surgery) that sits at the top of the nausea risk scores. An orogastric tube to deflate the stomach improves the surgeon's view of the triangle and comes out at the end.

### 2. Pneumoperitoneum and first port
**Why:** Carbon dioxide is insufflated through an open (Hasson) cut-down at the umbilicus or, less often now, a Veress needle, to lift the abdominal wall off the viscera.
**Anaesthesia:** Peritoneal stretch is a vagal stimulus: bradycardia, nodal rhythm and occasionally asystole occur in the first minute, more often in young fit patients and with rapid insufflation. Ask the surgeon to stop and release the gas, give atropine 600 micrograms IV or glycopyrrolate 200-400 micrograms IV, and restart slowly once the rate recovers. The opposite pattern, a falling end-tidal carbon dioxide with hypotension, is gas embolism (a needle or trocar in a vein) or major haemorrhage; a retroperitoneal vessel injury can bleed out of sight with almost nothing in the field. Once inflated, expect a rise in airway pressure, a fall in compliance and functional residual capacity, a rise in systemic vascular resistance and mean arterial pressure, and a climb in end-tidal carbon dioxide over 15-20 minutes that needs a 20-30% increase in minute ventilation.

### 3. Reverse Trendelenburg and left tilt
**Why:** Gravity moves the colon, omentum and stomach away from the gallbladder so the fundus can be pushed up over the liver.
**Anaesthesia:** Head-up tilt on top of pneumoperitoneum reduces venous return; in a fasted, dehydrated or septic patient the pressure falls as the table moves. A vasopressor bolus and 250-500 mL of crystalloid deal with it; a patient with fixed cardiac output or severe aortic stenosis needs the tilt applied slowly and an arterial line in place. Secure the patient against sliding, pad the left arm, and note that the tube can migrate towards the carina as the diaphragm rises.

### 4. Dissection of the hepatocystic triangle
**Why:** The gallbladder is retracted up and out, and the peritoneum, fat and fibrous tissue around the cystic duct and artery are cleared until the critical view of safety is achieved.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step the surgeon cannot do through a moving or tight abdomen. A cough or a breath against the ventilator while instruments are in the triangle is how ducts and arteries are torn, so keep neuromuscular block measurable and adequate until the gallbladder is detached. Deep block is also what makes low-pressure pneumoperitoneum workable: the meta-analysis of 44 trials found less pain and less shoulder pain at low pressure with no more conversions, and the Cochrane review found the operation could be completed at low pressure in about 90%. If the surgeon is willing to work at 8-10 mmHg, your part of the bargain is the relaxation.

### 5. Cholangiogram
**Why:** A catheter is passed into the cystic duct and contrast injected under fluoroscopy to confirm the anatomy before anything irreversible is cut and to show stones in the duct.
**Anaesthesia:** The table is levelled or tilted, the image intensifier comes in over the chest, and everyone steps back. Hold ventilation in expiration for the few seconds of each exposure so the image is sharp. Spasm of the sphincter of Oddi mimics a distal stone; remifentanil and fentanyl are blamed more often than is fair, but glucagon 0.5-1 mg IV or hyoscine butylbromide 20 mg IV relaxes the sphincter within a minute and settles the question. Iodinated contrast in the biliary tree rarely causes a reaction, but a known anaphylaxis to contrast is worth declaring at the team brief.

### 6. Bile duct exploration (when stones are found)
**Why:** Transcystic flushing, basket retrieval or choledochoscopy clears small stones through the cystic duct; larger or multiple stones need a choledochotomy, stone extraction and primary duct closure, sometimes over a T-tube or a stent, with a subhepatic drain.
**Anaesthesia:** The case has just lengthened by 30-90 minutes, so re-dose the relaxant, reconsider warming and fluid, and give cefazolin 2 g IV if none was given. Saline irrigation through the choledochoscope runs under pressure into the duodenum and the peritoneal cavity: litres of room-temperature fluid cool the patient and distend the gut. Manipulating an infected duct can produce a bacteraemic shower with a sudden vasodilated hypotension and a temperature spike in recovery; a patient who came in with cholangitis should have therapeutic antibiotics running and a noradrenaline infusion within reach. Glucagon is requested again for the stone flush.

### 7. Clipping, division and gallbladder bed dissection
**Why:** The cystic duct and artery are clipped and divided, and the gallbladder is peeled off the liver with diathermy.
**Anaesthesia:** Bleeding from the liver bed or a slipped cystic artery clip is usually controlled laparoscopically but is the commonest reason for a sudden loss of view and a conversion. In the cirrhotic patient with portal hypertension the bed bleeds like a varix, which is why Child-Pugh B and C patients need crossmatched blood, an arterial line and a critical care bed for what is nominally a minor operation.

### 8. Conversion to open (when the view cannot be made safe)
**Why:** Dense inflammation, bleeding, a suspected duct injury or anatomy that cannot be defined are reasons to stop and open through a right subcostal (Kocher) incision; conversion is a safety decision, not a complication.
**Anaesthesia:** The pain mechanism has changed from laparoscopic to abdominal wall. A subcostal incision divides rectus and the lateral muscles close to the costal margin and hurts with every breath and cough for days, with the atelectasis that follows. Add subcostal TAP or rectus sheath blocks before waking (catheters if the unit runs them), convert the opioid plan to a PCA, cancel the day-case discharge and tell recovery what happened. In a patient with severe chronic lung disease an epidural placed after the event is reasonable.

### 9. Extraction, lavage and deflation
**Why:** The gallbladder is bagged and delivered through the umbilical port, which may need enlarging; the right upper quadrant is washed with saline and the gas is let out.
**Anaesthesia:** Three surgical habits recommended by PROSPECT happen here and are worth asking for: saline lavage with suction, deliberate aspiration of the residual gas with the patient flat or head down, and local anaesthetic infiltrated into every port site (the umbilical and epigastric fascia, not just the skin). A few recruitment breaths as the gas leaves help to expel the bubble under the diaphragm and re-expand the bases. Reverse the neuromuscular block against a quantitative monitor: residual weakness in a head-up, obese day case is an avoidable readmission.

### 10. Emergence, recovery and discharge
**Why:** The surgeon expects the patient home the same day, eating, and back for nothing.
**Anaesthesia:** Pain in recovery is at its worst in the first two hours and is a mixture of port-site pain (sharp, localised, responds to the infiltration and to fentanyl 25-50 microgram IV increments) and deep right upper quadrant and shoulder-tip pain (visceral and phrenic, responds poorly to opioid and better to the NSAID, sitting up and time). Severe pain that does not fit either pattern, a rising heart rate or a falling pressure in recovery is bleeding until excluded. Nausea is the commoner reason for unplanned admission than pain. Discharge with regular paracetamol and an NSAID for three to five days, a small supply of oral opioid, and an explanation that shoulder pain on day 1 is gas.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is ports plus peritoneum plus a small raw bed, peaking early and fading by day 3, with no component that a nerve block covers better than an anti-inflammatory does. PROSPECT's 2018 update, built on 258 trials, is unusually clear. Recommended: paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started before or during surgery, dexamethasone, and port-site local anaesthetic infiltration, with opioid kept for rescue. Recommended surgical measures: low-pressure pneumoperitoneum, saline lavage and aspiration of the gas at the end. Not recommended unless the basic regimen is impossible: gabapentinoids, intraperitoneal local anaesthetic and TAP blocks. The reasoning on TAP blocks is that they add nothing measurable to infiltration plus an NSAID, not that they do not work.

So the block for this operation is the strategy as a whole. Before incision: paracetamol 1 g IV or oral, a COX-2 inhibitor or NSAID (parecoxib 40 mg IV is the common intraoperative choice), dexamethasone 8 mg IV. During: short-acting opioid, with or without a modest dose of a longer-acting one before the end; the author's practice is to keep the total small because nausea, not pain, is what keeps these patients in. At the end: the surgeon's infiltration, 20-30 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% or bupivacaine 0.25% divided between the ports with the fascial layer included, within the weight-based maximum. Afterwards: regular paracetamol and NSAID for three to five days and an oral opioid for breakthrough on day 0 and 1.

The plan changes in three situations. Where NSAIDs are contraindicated (renal impairment, the dehydrated septic patient, anticoagulation with a bleeding concern), the anti-inflammatory arm has gone and a bilateral subcostal TAP block is the defensible substitute; this is author's judgement consistent with PROSPECT's "unless basic analgesia is not possible". In the opioid-tolerant or chronic pain patient, add the TAP block and a ketamine bolus of 0.25-0.5 mg/kg IV and expect an overnight stay. After conversion to open, the card for an upper abdominal incision applies: fascial plane catheters or an epidural, PCA, and chest physiotherapy.

Rebound is not a feature because no dense block is wearing off; infiltration fades over four to eight hours into a pain that the oral regimen is already covering. Escalation triggers: pain that worsens after day 2 rather than improving, particularly with fever, jaundice, distension or shoulder pain that is new, is a bile leak, a retained stone or a collection, and belongs to the surgeon the same day.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Acute cholecystitis. The "hot gallbladder" done within days of admission is the same operation in oedematous, bleeding tissue: longer, a higher conversion rate, a septic and often dehydrated patient, and a full stomach. Fluid resuscitate before induction, give the antibiotic, and do not promise a day case.
- Cholangitis and jaundice. Sepsis, coagulopathy from vitamin K malabsorption, and renal vulnerability. Check the INR, avoid NSAIDs when the creatinine is rising, maintain urine output, and plan critical care when there is organ dysfunction.
- Cirrhosis. Child-Pugh A is an elective case with attention to bleeding; B and C carry real mortality from haemorrhage, ascites leak and decompensation. Arterial line, crossmatch, a conservative fluid plan and a higher-acuity bed.
- Vascular or bowel injury at entry. Rare, catastrophic and yours to recognise: hypotension, tachycardia and a falling end-tidal carbon dioxide soon after the first trocar, with or without blood in the field. Call it, get help and blood, and expect a midline laparotomy.
- Gas embolism, capnothorax and subcutaneous emphysema. A sudden fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide with hypotension is embolism (stop gas, head down, left side down, support the circulation). A rising end-tidal carbon dioxide with rising airway pressure and desaturation is gas in the pleura through a diaphragmatic defect or dissection plane; it usually resolves with release of the pneumoperitoneum, PEEP and time, and rarely needs a drain. Crepitus in the neck at the end warrants a longer wait before extubation.
- Pregnancy. Cholecystectomy is the commonest non-obstetric abdominal operation after appendicectomy; second trimester is preferred, left lateral tilt, low insufflation pressure, normocapnia and obstetric input on fetal monitoring.
- The patient unsuitable for pneumoperitoneum. Severe pulmonary hypertension, a ventriculoperitoneal shunt without a valve or a patient who decompensates on insufflation: options are low pressure, an abdominal wall lift, or an open operation under general or neuraxial anaesthesia. Cholecystectomy under spinal or epidural alone is feasible and occasionally the right answer for end-stage lung disease, with shoulder-tip pain the limiting symptom.
- Subtotal cholecystectomy and drains. A bail-out for the frozen triangle; a drain, a bile leak risk and an inpatient stay follow.
- Unplanned admission. The usual causes are nausea, pain, a late finish and a drain. Morning lists, TIVA or a volatile with triple antiemetic prophylaxis in the high-risk patient, and a full multimodal regimen are what keep the day-case rate up.

## Key sources

The analgesic evidence is among the best in the book: a PROSPECT update drawing on 258 randomised trials, with grade A recommendations for the basic regimen and for the surgical measures. The evidence for low-pressure pneumoperitoneum has strengthened since the 2014 Cochrane review, which judged safety unproven on low-quality trials; the 2022 meta-analysis of 44 trials rates the pain benefit as high-quality evidence. The statements about bradycardia at insufflation, embolism, capnothorax, cirrhosis and the conduct of the cholangiogram are standard teaching and are unsourced. The advice to use a subcostal TAP block when NSAIDs cannot be given is the author's reading of the PROSPECT caveat, not a trial result.
