## What the surgeon is doing and why

Bariatric operations work by changing gut anatomy so that appetite, satiety and glucose handling change; the mechanical restriction is the smaller part of the effect. Two operations account for nearly all primary procedures.

Sleeve gastrectomy removes the greater curve. The surgeon divides the gastrocolic and short gastric vessels from a few centimetres short of the pylorus up to the angle of His, then staples the stomach vertically alongside a 36-40 Fr bougie that you have passed, leaving a narrow tube along the lesser curve and extracting about three quarters of the stomach through a port. There is no anastomosis, only a staple line 20 cm or more long. The remaining tube is high-pressure and cannot relax to accommodate, which is why it restricts, why it refluxes, and why it hurts and provokes nausea in the first day.

Roux-en-Y gastric bypass creates a 20-30 mL pouch from the top of the stomach, divides the jejunum, brings the distal end up to the pouch (gastrojejunostomy) and rejoins the proximal end further down (jejunojejunostomy). Food bypasses the stomach, duodenum and first metre or so of jejunum. Two anastomoses, two mesenteric defects to close, and a longer operation. The one-anastomosis bypass uses a longer pouch and a single loop join. Bypass is preferred for severe reflux and often for type 2 diabetes; sleeve is quicker and commoner.

Both are done laparoscopically through five ports with the patient steeply head up and a retractor holding an enlarged, fatty left lobe of the liver off the stomach. Most programmes prescribe a very low calorie diet for two weeks beforehand to shrink that liver, which also leaves the patient mildly dehydrated on the day.

The surgery is short and, in experienced hands, uneventful. The card exists because of the patient. Class 3 obesity changes the airway (a short safe apnoea time, a harder mask, usually an easy laryngoscopy if positioned well), the lungs (low functional residual capacity that falls further supine and under pneumoperitoneum, atelectasis within minutes), drug handling (lipophilic drugs accumulate, dosing weight matters), and the first night (obstructive sleep apnoea in the majority, diagnosed or not, in a patient who has had opioids). The surgical complications that matter, staple line bleed and leak, present in a body whose abdomen cannot be examined, so they present as tachycardia.

The pain logic: laparoscopic, moderate, peaking in the first 24 hours and largely over by day 3, which the PROSPECT group describes as moderate pain in the immediate postoperative period. The wall component is five ports through thick tissue, with the extraction port stretched. The visceral component is distinctive: gastric spasm and distension of the new sleeve, experienced as epigastric tightness, retching and nausea, and poorly responsive to opioids, which make the nausea worse. Analgesia and antiemesis are one plan here, not two.

## Steps

### 1. Preassessment
**Why:** The surgeon has selected the patient through a multidisciplinary programme and set the preoperative diet.
**Anaesthesia:** Screen for sleep-disordered breathing (STOP-Bang, and a venous bicarbonate for hypoventilation); those on CPAP bring the machine. Look for the cardiorespiratory consequences that change the level of care: pulmonary hypertension, right heart strain, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, poorly controlled diabetes. Ask about GLP-1 receptor agonists and reflux. Assess the airway and the veins. Reassure about the things patients fear and that are rarely problems, and be straightforward about nausea.

### 2. Positioning for induction and preoxygenation
**Why:** Nothing surgical; this is the step that determines the safety of the next two minutes.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient walks in and positions themselves on the operating table, ramped until the ear is level with the sternal notch, table 25-30 degrees head up. Preoxygenate with a tight-fitting mask and 5-10 cmH2O of PEEP or pressure support, or high-flow nasal oxygen, to an end-tidal oxygen above 0.9. Functional residual capacity is small and oxygen consumption high, so the store you build is the only margin there is.

### 3. Induction and airway
**Why:** The surgeon needs a secure airway and a deflated stomach.
**Anaesthesia:** Propofol dosed to lean body weight and titrated, short-acting opioid, rocuronium (lean or adjusted weight; 1.2 mg/kg ideal body weight for a rapid sequence) or suxamethonium by total body weight. Videolaryngoscope first. Rapid sequence induction for reflux, a previous band or sleeve, diabetes with gastroparesis, or recent GLP-1 agonist use. Recruit the lungs once the tube is in and apply PEEP of 8-12 cmH2O. Dexamethasone now. An arterial line only where the cuff will not read reliably on a conical arm or the heart demands it; a forearm or wrist cuff often solves the first problem.

### 4. Head-up positioning and pneumoperitoneum
**Why:** Steep reverse Trendelenburg and 15 mmHg of carbon dioxide move the omentum and bowel away from the stomach.
**Anaesthesia:** Secure the patient with a footplate and straps and check they do not slide when the table tilts. Protect the arms, which are heavy and sit badly on boards: brachial plexus and ulnar injuries and gluteal pressure injury are the positional risks, with rhabdomyolysis after long revision cases. Head-up tilt helps the lungs and costs preload; with the pneumoperitoneum expect a vasopressor requirement in a patient who has been on a restricted diet. Recruit again after insufflation. Calf compressors must fit and be on.

### 5. Liver retraction and gastric mobilisation
**Why:** The left lobe is lifted and the greater curve is freed by dividing the gastrocolic and short gastric vessels up to the left crus.
**Anaesthesia:** Bleeding risk is the short gastrics and the splenic tip, small in volume but difficult for the surgeon to reach in a deep, fatty field: deep neuromuscular block makes a measurable difference to their working space at this point. The stomach should be empty and flat; pass an orogastric tube to decompress, then remove it.

### 6. Bougie and stapling
**Why:** The bougie sits along the lesser curve as a template so the sleeve is neither too tight (stricture) nor too loose; the stapler is fired five to seven times against it. In a bypass the pouch is created the same way with a narrower tube or none.
**Anaesthesia:** Before the first firing, remove everything else from the oesophagus and stomach: orogastric tube, temperature probe, and any oesophageal stethoscope. Count them out aloud. Pass the bougie lubricated, gently, and under the surgeon's view; it should slide, and resistance means stop. Do not move it once stapling has begun unless asked. Stapling across a tube or probe is a recognised and wholly preventable injury.

### 7. Anastomoses (bypass)
**Why:** The gastrojejunostomy and jejunojejunostomy are made with staplers or sutures and the mesenteric defects are closed to prevent internal hernia.
**Anaesthesia:** A longer case with the same priorities. The surgeon may ask you to pass a tube or an anvil carrier through the mouth, or to advance a gastroscope for inspection; lubricate and never force it past the pouch.

### 8. Leak test and haemostasis
**Why:** The staple line or the gastrojejunostomy is tested with air under saline or with methylene blue down your tube, and oversewn or clipped where it bleeds.
**Anaesthesia:** Instil or insufflate exactly what is asked and no more, then aspirate it back. Some surgeons ask for the systolic pressure to be raised to 130-140 mmHg for a minute to reveal staple line bleeding before they close; do it with a vasopressor bolus, and tell them the number.

### 9. Extraction, blocks and closure
**Why:** The resected stomach is pulled out through a stretched 12-15 mm port; the fascia at that site is closed.
**Anaesthesia:** If TAP blocks were not done after induction, the surgeon can place them under laparoscopic vision now, watching the peritoneum bulge as local anaesthetic is injected from outside; this is quicker and more reliable than ultrasound through 8-10 cm of subcutaneous fat. Port sites are infiltrated as well. Total local anaesthetic dose is calculated on lean or ideal weight, not actual. Final recruitment manoeuvre before the gas comes out.

### 10. Emergence and extubation
**Why:** The surgeon wants no coughing or retching on the staple line and a patient who walks the same evening.
**Anaesthesia:** Reverse to a train-of-four ratio above 0.9 with sugammadex, measured not assumed. Extubate fully awake, sitting at 30-45 degrees, obeying commands, and go straight on to oxygen with the patient's own CPAP or high-flow nasal oxygen if there is sleep apnoea. Residual block, residual volatile and opioid act together on an upper airway that obstructs when relaxed, which is why the technique favours propofol or a low-solubility volatile, short-acting opioid and a small total dose.

### 11. Recovery and the first night
**Why:** The surgeon is watching for bleeding in the first 24 hours and a leak in the first week.
**Anaesthesia:** Nausea and epigastric spasm peak in the first six hours: treat with a different class of antiemetic from those already given, hyoscine butylbromide 20 mg IV for spasm in some units, and sips. Pain is treated with the regular non-opioids and small doses of oral or IV opioid by lean weight. Continuous oximetry overnight, CPAP when asleep, sitting up. A sustained heart rate above 120, with or without pain, fever or a falling haemoglobin, goes to the surgeon at once.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is ports plus gastric spasm, moderate and short, in a patient for whom every milligram of opioid has a respiratory and an emetic cost. The plan is therefore built to make opioid a rescue drug.

PROSPECT has reviewed sleeve gastrectomy twice. The 2019 review of 18 trials could recommend little beyond paracetamol and an NSAID with opioid rescue, suggested port-site infiltration in place of TAP blocks, and advised caution with gabapentinoids. The 2026 update, with 39 further trials, recommends paracetamol with an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, bilateral TAP blocks (ultrasound- or laparoscopic-guided), port-site infiltration, and intravenous dexamethasone for both analgesia and nausea, with opioids reserved for rescue. Its two changes are that the TAP block is now recommended and gabapentinoids are no longer advised. There is no separate PROSPECT guideline for gastric bypass; the same regimen is applied on mechanism, and the ERAS Society bariatric guideline makes the same multimodal, opioid-sparing recommendation while acknowledging that much of its evidence is extrapolated.

In practice: paracetamol 1 g IV and parecoxib 40 mg IV or an equivalent at induction (surgeons vary on NSAIDs after bypass because of marginal ulcer risk at the gastrojejunostomy, and many restrict them to the first days or avoid them long term); dexamethasone 8 mg IV; bilateral subcostal or lateral TAP blocks with ropivacaine 0.2-0.375% to a total within the lean-weight maximum; port-site infiltration; propofol TIVA with remifentanil or a modest fentanyl dose. Afterwards, regular soluble or liquid paracetamol and a short NSAID course where allowed, with a small supply of liquid opioid.

Opioid-sparing adjuncts are widely used and the evidence is modest but consistent in direction. Meta-analyses of opioid-free techniques in bariatric surgery (dexmedetomidine, ketamine, lidocaine and magnesium in various combinations) show less early nausea and vomiting, slightly lower early pain scores, no reduction in 24-hour opioid consumption, and more bradycardia. The author's practice is opioid-sparing, not opioid-free: ketamine 0.25-0.5 mg/kg IV by ideal body weight and a low-dose dexmedetomidine or lidocaine infusion for patients with severe sleep apnoea or a history of severe nausea, accepting that this is judgement.

Rebound is low: the TAP block fades at 8-16 hours into pain already covered by oral drugs. Escalation triggers are the reason for the trap line. Pain that is increasing after the first day, left shoulder pain, tachycardia, fever, breathlessness or a sense of doom is a leak. Severe pain with vomiting weeks to years after bypass is an internal hernia. Neither is an analgesic problem.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Staple line bleed. First 24 hours: tachycardia, falling haemoglobin, sometimes haematemesis or melaena. Return to theatre in an anticoagulated patient with a stomach full of blood: rapid sequence induction.
- Leak. Day 1-7, commonest at the top of the sleeve near the angle of His. Septic, tachycardic, and coming back for laparoscopy, washout and drainage or an endoscopic stent.
- Revision surgery and band removal. Adhesions around the old band or sleeve, longer surgery, higher leak and bleed rates, and a reflux history that makes every induction a rapid sequence.
- Obesity hypoventilation and pulmonary hypertension. Daytime hypercapnia, raised bicarbonate, right heart strain: arterial line, minimal opioid, extubation on to non-invasive ventilation, and a high-dependency bed.
- Failed or difficult intubation. Uncommon when ramped, but desaturation is rapid and mask ventilation may need two people and an airway adjunct. A second-generation supraglottic device is the rescue; have it open.
- Drug dosing errors. Total body weight dosing of propofol, opioid or local anaesthetic is an overdose; ideal body weight dosing of suxamethonium or sugammadex is an underdose.
- GLP-1 receptor agonists. Retained gastric contents despite standard fasting are reported; guidance on withholding is evolving and varies between organisations.
- Rhabdomyolysis. Long cases, very high weight: gluteal or back pain and dark urine afterwards. Check creatine kinase.
- Venous access. Ultrasound-guided peripheral cannulation is often quicker than repeated attempts; a central line is rarely needed.
- Nutritional and drug absorption changes later. Modified-release and enteric preparations behave unpredictably after bypass; thiamine deficiency follows prolonged vomiting. These matter when the patient returns for other surgery.
- The post-bariatric patient for unrelated surgery. A sleeve refluxes; a bypass patient may have dumping, hypoglycaemia and marginal ulcers, and should avoid NSAIDs long term.

## Key sources

Sleeve gastrectomy has a current PROSPECT guideline, updated in 2026, and the change in its TAP block recommendation between editions shows how fast the trial base has grown. Bypass has no procedure-specific analgesic guideline. The ERAS Society guideline is broad and candid about low-quality evidence. The Association of Anaesthetists and Society for Obesity and Bariatric Anaesthesia guideline dates from 2015 and remains the reference for positioning, dosing weights and sleep apnoea, though it predates the widespread use of GLP-1 agonists. The opioid-free meta-analyses pool small trials of differing drug combinations and should be read as a signal on nausea, not proof of superiority. The heart rate threshold of 120 for suspecting a leak or bleed is long-standing bariatric teaching and is unsourced, as are the positional injury and rhabdomyolysis statements.
