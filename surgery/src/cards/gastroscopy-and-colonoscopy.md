## What the surgeon is doing and why

The endoscopist passes a flexible videoscope along a hollow organ, inflates it with gas so the walls separate from the lens, and looks. Everything else is done down a 3 mm working channel: forceps for a biopsy, a snare for a polyp, a needle for adrenaline or a lifting solution, a clip, a balloon, a band. There is no incision and no tissue is left under tension, so the operation produces almost no pain afterwards. What it produces during the examination is the anaesthetic problem.

Gastroscopy takes the scope through the mouth, over the tongue base, past the laryngeal inlet and through cricopharyngeus. That is the most reflexogenic 15 cm in the body, and the endoscopist needs it quiet for about 20 seconds. The patient lies left lateral so that pooled fluid sits in the fundus away from the lens and the cardia; a bite block holds the mouth open and protects a scope that costs more than the anaesthetic machine. The airway is the endoscopist's working corridor. You cannot hold a mask on it, you cannot see the mouth well, and the scope fills part of the pharynx. Stimulation is intense for half a minute and then almost absent, which is the pattern that makes a propofol bolus overshoot.

Colonoscopy is longer and is a mechanical problem. The colon is fixed at the rectum, the descending colon and the ascending colon, and is free on a mesentery at the sigmoid and transverse. Pushing a scope through a free segment forms a loop, and the loop stretches the mesentery rather than advancing the tip. Mesenteric stretch is the pain of colonoscopy and the trigger for the vagal bradycardia. The endoscopist reduces loops by withdrawing and twisting, by a nurse pressing on the abdomen, and by rolling the patient. The airway is at the far end of the patient from the operator, which is why colonoscopy sedation is forgiving and gastroscopy sedation is not.

Three decisions follow from the anatomy. First, depth: both examinations are done in most Australian units under propofol titrated to deep sedation, which in the gastroscopy patient is functionally a general anaesthetic without an airway device. Second, the stomach: sedation abolishes airway reflexes in a patient lying with the mouth dependent, so anything in the stomach matters, and the list now contains many patients on glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists whose stomachs do not empty on the usual timetable. Third, the room: endoscopy suites are remote sites with fast turnover, and the standard of monitoring, assistance and recovery is the one in the [remote site chapter](../a/a12-remote-site-sedation.html), not a lesser one.

The pain logic is brief. The mechanism is visceral distension and mesenteric stretch, it ends when the scope comes out, and the residual is retained gas. Carbon dioxide is absorbed through the bowel wall and exhaled within minutes where air stays for hours; the pooled trials show less pain for up to 24 hours with carbon dioxide and no clinically relevant rise in carbon dioxide. Persistent or rising pain after endoscopy is not on this card's pain curve and is treated as a complication.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-procedure assessment and the stomach
**Why:** The endoscopist needs an empty stomach to see the mucosa and a clean colon to find flat polyps; the bowel preparation is split so that the second dose finishes about two hours before the procedure, because a colon cleaned the night before is coated with small bowel effluent by morning.
**Anaesthesia:** The second dose of preparation is a clear fluid and is compatible with a two-hour fast, so a correctly prepared colonoscopy patient is not a full stomach. The patients who are: gastric outlet obstruction, achalasia, a food bolus, an acute upper gastrointestinal bleed, and a proportion of those on a GLP-1 receptor agonist. In fasted patients having gastroscopy, increased residual gastric content was seen in 24.2% of semaglutide users against 5.1% of non-users, and on gastric ultrasound in 56% of weekly GLP-1 receptor agonist users against 19% of controls, with no relationship to how long the drug had been withheld. Whether that translates into more aspiration is unsettled, and a large database analysis reports no association during endoscopy. The Australian multi-society recommendation follows the physiology: continue the drug, clear fluids only for the 24 hours before the standard six-hour fast, and consider erythromycin when that was not done. Ask every patient about the drug by name, because weight-loss prescriptions are often missing from the medication list. Nausea, fullness or reflux on the day counts for more than the date of the last injection.

### 2. Set-up in the endoscopy room
**Why:** The room is arranged around the scope stack, the image screen and the endoscopist's line of sight; the trolley, not an operating table, is the working surface.
**Anaesthesia:** Establish before the first case that the trolley tips head-down, that suction is on your side and working with a Yankauer attached, and that a self-inflating bag, airways, a laryngoscope and tubes are in the room rather than in a cupboard down the corridor. Know who your assistant is and that they are not also the endoscopy nurse. Monitoring is ECG, non-invasive pressure, oximetry and waveform capnography from a nasal sampling cannula. Capnography is the only monitor that shows obstruction and apnoea when they happen rather than a minute later; in pooled randomised trials adding it to oximetry reduced hypoxaemia by about a quarter. Start oxygen before the first dose, since a pre-oxygenated patient buys the time to fix an obstructed airway.

### 3. Positioning, bite block and oxygen delivery
**Why:** Left lateral keeps gastric fluid in the fundus for gastroscopy and puts the sigmoid in its most favourable lie for colonoscopy; the bite block goes in while the patient can still cooperate.
**Anaesthesia:** The lateral position is the airway's friend: the tongue falls forward and secretions drain out of the mouth. Standard nasal cannulae at 2-4 L/min are adequate for the slim, short case. For the obese, the patient with sleep apnoea or lung disease, and the long therapeutic gastroscopy, high-flow nasal oxygen changes the margin: in at-risk patients under deep sedation it cut desaturation to 92% or below from 33.5% to 9.4%, and in nearly 2,000 low-risk gastroscopies it took hypoxia from 8.4% to zero. It does this by apnoeic oxygenation and a little airway pressure, and it conceals hypoventilation in doing so. Capnography reads poorly under high flow, so watch the chest and the trace together and do not read a saturation of 99% as proof of breathing.

### 4. Induction of sedation
**Why:** The endoscopist cannot start until the patient tolerates the scope at the lips; a list of 12 depends on this taking one minute, not five.
**Anaesthesia:** Propofol alone, in increments or as a target-controlled infusion, is the defensible default: fast, antiemetic and gone in minutes. For gastroscopy the first dose must cover pharyngeal intubation, typically 1-1.5 mg/kg IV in a fit adult and half that or less in the elderly, given over 30-60 seconds so that breathing continues. Fentanyl 25-50 micrograms IV or alfentanil 250-500 micrograms IV blunts the gag and reduces the propofol needed, at the cost of more apnoea when the stimulus stops. Midazolam adds amnesia and a slower discharge and is not needed with propofol. Lidocaine spray to the pharynx helps the lightly sedated patient and adds little to deep sedation. The elderly, the frail and the hypovolaemic patient with a bleed need a fraction of the textbook dose; give it and wait.

### 5. Oesophageal intubation with the gastroscope
**Why:** The scope is steered over the tongue base and through cricopharyngeus under vision; a swallow helps in the awake patient and is absent in the sedated one.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the peak stimulus and the moment of laryngospasm, coughing and breath-holding if the patient is light, and of obstruction and apnoea 30 seconds later if the dose was generous. A jaw thrust from behind the patient's head with the scope in place is nearly always enough; a nasopharyngeal airway placed before the scope goes in is the next step in the patient with sleep apnoea. If the airway cannot be held with the scope in, the scope comes out: say so early, because an endoscopist who is 10 seconds from finishing will otherwise ask for 10 more.

### 6. Examination, insufflation and biopsy of the upper tract
**Why:** The stomach is distended to flatten the rugae, the scope is retroflexed to see the cardia and fundus, the duodenum is entered to its second part, and biopsies are taken; the whole diagnostic sequence takes about five minutes.
**Anaesthesia:** Stimulation falls sharply once the scope is in the stomach, so the patient who needed a large induction dose now stops breathing. Gastric distension splints the diaphragm and promotes regurgitation around the scope; ask for the stomach to be deflated before withdrawal. Pooled fluid is aspirated through the scope, which is the one advantage of having the endoscopist in the airway: if the stomach is found full of food, stop, suction, and decide together between abandoning and a tracheal tube. Belching and a cough on withdrawal are the usual signs that something reached the larynx.

### 7. Colonoscope insertion and loop reduction
**Why:** The scope is advanced to the caecum against the tendency of the sigmoid and transverse colon to loop; position changes and abdominal pressure straighten the scope so that push at the anus becomes advance at the tip.
**Anaesthesia:** Mesenteric stretch at the sigmoid and again at the splenic and hepatic flexures is the painful part and the cause of vagal bradycardia and hypotension; a patient who moves here is telling the endoscopist about a loop, which is useful information. A small opioid dose or a propofol increment timed to the flexures works better than a deeper level throughout. Bradycardia responds to the loop being reduced; atropine 300-600 micrograms IV is for the heart rate that stays below 40. Rolling a deeply sedated patient supine removes the airway advantage of the lateral position at the moment the abdomen is being compressed, so take the head yourself during the turn. In bidirectional lists the gastroscopy is done first, while the airway has the most attention, and the same sedation continues.

### 8. Withdrawal, polypectomy and endoscopic resection
**Why:** The mucosa is inspected on the way out, over at least six minutes, because that is when polyps are found. Small polyps are cold-snared; larger sessile lesions are lifted on a submucosal injection and resected with diathermy, and the defect may be clipped.
**Anaesthesia:** Stimulation is low and constant, so propofol requirement falls; this is where over-sedation happens on long cases. Large resections can take more than an hour and justify a planned technique: target-controlled propofol, high-flow oxygen, and a low threshold for a supraglottic airway in the patient who obstructs. Diathermy near a pacemaker or defibrillator needs the same thought as in theatre. Know which anticoagulant was stopped and when, since the bleeding that matters is delayed by days. Sharp pain with a rigid abdomen during a resection is a perforation: carbon dioxide under tension in the peritoneum splints the diaphragm and can obstruct venous return, and needle decompression may be needed before the surgeon arrives.

### 9. Therapeutic gastroscopy and the acute bleed
**Why:** Variceal banding, injection and clipping of an ulcer, dilatation of a stricture, food bolus removal and gastrostomy insertion are all done through the same scope, but each puts blood, food or a full stomach under an unprotected larynx.
**Anaesthesia:** These are the cases that are not sedation cases. An actively bleeding patient has a stomach full of blood, is hypovolaemic, and is often cirrhotic and encephalopathic: resuscitate first, then a rapid sequence induction with a tracheal tube, large-bore access, blood available and ceftriaxone 1 g IV for the cirrhotic patient. The same applies to achalasia, where the oesophagus holds days of food above a closed sphincter, to a food bolus, and to gastric outlet obstruction. Dilatation is a brief, intense stimulus with a real perforation rate; chest pain or surgical emphysema afterwards is mediastinal air. The principles of two operators in one airway are in the [shared airway chapter](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html).

### 10. Recovery and discharge
**Why:** The unit's throughput depends on patients walking out within the hour, and the endoscopist wants to give results to someone who will remember them.
**Anaesthesia:** Propofol alone gives a patient who is conversing in five minutes; added midazolam and opioid delay that and are the usual reason for a slow list. Recovery staff need the same monitoring and oxygen as any post-anaesthesia unit, because the hypoventilating patient is often the one who arrived there two minutes after a top-up. New cough, wheeze, a rising oxygen requirement or a fever after gastroscopy is aspiration and stays for a chest radiograph and observation. Discharge criteria and the escort rule are those of any day-case sedation ([day-case chapter](../a/a11-ponv-day-case.html)).

## Pain plan

There is nothing to block and the plan is the conduct of the sedation. The mechanism is transient visceral distension and mesenteric stretch with no tissue injury, so intra-procedural discomfort is managed by depth and a small, timed opioid dose, and post-procedural discomfort is managed by having used carbon dioxide and by walking. Paracetamol 1 g orally is the ceiling of what a normal recovery needs. A sore throat after gastroscopy settles within a day.

The decisions that carry the weight are about depth and airway. Propofol-only deep sedation is the author's default for both examinations, with opioid sparingly for colonoscopy. A planned general anaesthetic with a tracheal tube is the correct plan, and not an escalation, for active bleeding, obstruction, achalasia and known retained contents; a supraglottic airway with a gastroscope channel is a reasonable middle course for the long therapeutic case in a patient who is fasted and obstructs under sedation. That last choice is author's judgement: the trials compare oxygen delivery, not airway devices.

Escalation is by symptom. Severe abdominal pain, distension and shoulder-tip pain after colonoscopy is perforation and needs imaging and a surgeon. Chest or neck pain after dilatation is oesophageal perforation. Neither is treated with opioid and discharge.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Aspiration under sedation. In a Medicare population, colonoscopy with anaesthesia services carried an aspiration pneumonia rate of 0.14% against 0.10% without. The absolute risk is small and the association may be confounded, but the direction is what the physiology predicts: deeper sedation, fewer reflexes.
- The obese patient with sleep apnoea. Obstructs at the first dose, desaturates fast and is hard to mask-ventilate lateral with a scope in. Head-up tilt, high-flow oxygen, a nasopharyngeal airway placed early and a smaller first dose given more slowly.
- The frail elderly patient. Arm-brain circulation time is long and the dose is a third of the adult figure; hypotension, not hypoxia, is the common adverse event.
- GLP-1 receptor agonists and the unexpected full stomach. If solid food is seen at gastroscopy, the examination is of no diagnostic value and the airway is at risk: stop and rebook after a clear fluid day unless the indication is urgent.
- Bowel preparation in the elderly and the renal patient. Hypovolaemia, hyponatraemia and hypokalaemia arrive with the patient; the first propofol dose finds the hypovolaemia.
- Carbon dioxide retention. Not clinically relevant in the pooled trials, but those excluded severe lung disease; watch the capnograph in the patient with chronic hypercapnia during a long resection.
- Non-anaesthetist sedation and nurse-led lists. Midazolam and fentanyl to conscious sedation keeps verbal contact and airway reflexes; the consultant is called to these lists for the patient who failed that approach, who is by selection the difficult one.
- Children. Gastroscopy in a child is a general anaesthetic with a secured airway in most units, since the scope occupies a larger share of a smaller pharynx; see [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).
- The next rung up. Endoscopic ultrasound, ERCP and enteroscopy are longer, often prone and carry their own complications: see [ERCP and advanced endoscopy](ercp-and-advanced-endoscopy.html).

## Key sources

The oxygenation evidence is strong: two large randomised trials show that high-flow nasal oxygen prevents hypoxaemia during sedated endoscopy, in low-risk and at-risk patients. The capnography evidence is consistent across small trials. The GLP-1 receptor agonist evidence is of two kinds that do not yet agree: studies of stomach contents show a clear excess, and studies of aspiration events do not, so the fasting recommendation rests on mechanism and consensus. No trial compares airway strategies for therapeutic gastroscopy, and the statements here on when to intubate are author's judgement.
