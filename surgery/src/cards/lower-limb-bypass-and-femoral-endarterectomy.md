## What the surgeon is doing and why

Atherosclerosis of the leg arteries produces two different patients. The claudicant has enough flow at rest and not enough on exercise; surgery is rarely needed. The patient with chronic limb-threatening ischaemia does not have enough flow at rest: the foot hurts at night, hangs out of bed for gravity's help, and ulcers or gangrene will not heal. That patient needs pulsatile flow restored to the foot or will lose the leg. Nearly every patient on this card is of the second kind, which means they arrive with weeks of poor sleep, regular opioids, diabetes, renal impairment and coronary disease, and often an infected foot.

The surgeon restores flow in one of three ways, often combined.

A common femoral endarterectomy cores the plaque out of the one artery that is too mobile and too important to stent, the common femoral at the groin crease, and its branch the profunda. The artery is exposed through a groin incision, clamped, opened, cleared and closed with a patch. It is short, superficial, and often combined with a stent or angioplasty above or below it under the same anaesthetic (a hybrid procedure).

A bypass carries blood around a long occlusion. Inflow is usually the common femoral artery. Outflow is the popliteal artery above or below the knee, or a tibial or pedal artery at the calf or ankle. The conduit is the patient's own great saphenous vein whenever there is one, either reversed or left in situ with its valves cut, because vein stays open far longer than prosthetic graft below the knee. Harvesting it means an incision or a series of incisions along the whole length of the thigh and often the calf. Where there is no vein, arm vein is harvested or a prosthetic graft is tunnelled.

An extra-anatomic bypass (femorofemoral crossover or axillofemoral) brings inflow from another limb through a subcutaneous tunnel, avoiding the abdomen in a patient too frail for aortic surgery or with an infected field.

Two randomised trials frame who gets a bypass. In BEST-CLI, patients with a good single-segment saphenous vein had fewer major limb events or deaths after surgery than after endovascular treatment (42.6% against 57.4%). In BASIL-2, patients needing below-knee revascularisation had worse amputation-free survival with vein bypass first (63% against 53% with major amputation or death), and the difference was deaths, most of them cardiovascular and respiratory. The two results are not as contradictory as they look: the surgical patient with good vein and adequate fitness does well, and the frail patient is harmed by a long operation. For the anaesthetist the message of BASIL-2 is that perioperative cardiorespiratory morbidity is what decides the outcome of bypass.

What the surgery does to tissue is superficial but extensive. The arteries and the vein lie under skin, fat and deep fascia in the groin and medial thigh; exposure of the below-knee popliteal detaches part of the medial gastrocnemius and soleus origin; a tibial exposure goes between calf muscles. Nothing is cut across, no bone is involved, and no cavity is entered. The pain logic is therefore long skin and soft tissue wounds, moderate in intensity, with the groin the sorest part because it is stretched with every movement of the hip. Two things sit on top. First, reperfusion: a chronically ischaemic leg swells when flow returns, tightening the wounds and occasionally the compartments. Second, the patient's baseline: ischaemic rest pain has a neuropathic element, most patients are on regular opioids, and a block that removes the rest pain for the first time in weeks is often more appreciated than the surgery. Successful revascularisation removes the rest pain within a day or two; persisting or returning foot pain is a sign of graft failure.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative assessment: the heart, the drugs and the clock
**Why:** The surgeon is working against tissue loss; these cases are urgent, and long delays for investigation cost limbs.
**Anaesthesia:** Assume coronary disease. Functional capacity cannot be assessed in a patient who cannot walk. Optimise what can be done in days: heart failure, rate control, anaemia, glucose. Continue aspirin, statin and beta-blocker. The antithrombotic list decides the technique: clopidogrel or ticagrelor within the guideline interval, therapeutic low molecular weight heparin, a direct oral anticoagulant or a heparin infusion each rule out neuraxial anaesthesia, and the surgeon will often not want them stopped. Ask about the conduit plan: one leg, both legs or an arm prepared changes your access and the length of the case.

### 2. Choice of anaesthetic
**Why:** The surgeon needs a still leg for 2-5 h and has no preference between techniques for the graft itself.
**Anaesthesia:** The randomised evidence is small and old: four trials and 696 patients show no difference between neuraxial and general anaesthesia in death, myocardial infarction or amputation, with less pneumonia after neuraxial. A population study of 20,988 patients found neuraxial anaesthesia associated with lower 30-day mortality (3.2% against 4.4%, adjusted odds ratio 0.68) and fewer cardiopulmonary and renal complications, but hospital-level use ranged from under 1% to over 90%, so institutional differences cannot be excluded. The defensible default is neuraxial anaesthesia when the antithrombotic timing allows it and the case will fit: a spinal for a femoral endarterectomy or an above-knee bypass under 2.5-3 h, a combined spinal-epidural or epidural for a distal vein bypass, which outlasts any single-shot spinal. A patient with orthopnoea, a chronic cough, severe back pain or restless rest pain in the other leg will not lie still for 4 h awake, and general anaesthesia is the better choice. Intra-operative heparin is compatible with neuraxial technique provided at least 1 h has passed since an atraumatic needle placement.

### 3. Positioning and preparation
**Why:** The whole leg from umbilicus to toes is prepared, externally rotated and flexed over a bolster, so that the surgeon can reach the groin, the medial thigh, the popliteal fossa from the medial side and the ankle without repositioning.
**Anaesthesia:** A long time in a fixed position on fragile skin: pad the contralateral heel, the sacrum and both elbows, and do not let the ischaemic foot rest against anything hard. Lower-body forced-air warming cannot be used on the operative leg and should not be used on an ischaemic contralateral leg; warm the upper body and the fluids. Under spinal anaesthesia the patient's shoulders and back will ache by the third hour; light sedation with propofol by target-controlled infusion or dexmedetomidine, and permission to shift the upper body, get most patients through.

### 4. Vein harvest and arterial exposure
**Why:** The great saphenous vein is exposed through a long medial incision or skip incisions, its tributaries ligated, and either removed and reversed or left in place for an in situ graft. The inflow and outflow arteries are exposed at the same time, often by two surgeons.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the longest phase and the one in which blood is lost unnoticed: slow venous ooze along 60-80 cm of wound into swabs and drapes. Weigh swabs, check a haemoglobin at 2 h, and transfuse to a threshold appropriate for coronary disease. Stimulation is constant and moderate. In a redo groin, dissection through scar is slow and the femoral vein and nerve are at risk.

### 5. Tunnelling
**Why:** A reversed vein or prosthetic graft is passed through a tunnel made with a long blunt instrument, either deep (subsartorial, between the heads of gastrocnemius) or subcutaneous, from one wound to the other.
**Anaesthesia:** The tunneller is the single most stimulating moment of the operation: a sudden, deep, tearing stimulus along the whole thigh. Under general anaesthesia give a bolus of opioid or deepen beforehand; under a spinal that is wearing off or a patchy epidural this is when it shows. For an axillofemoral graft the tunnel runs subcutaneously down the lateral chest and abdominal wall from the infraclavicular fossa, outside the territory of any lower limb block, and needs general anaesthesia or a planned truncal block.

### 6. Heparin and clamps
**Why:** Heparin prevents thrombosis in the clamped, low-flow limb. Clamps or soft slings occlude the inflow and outflow arteries.
**Anaesthesia:** Unfractionated heparin 70-100 units/kg IV, time announced, with a repeat dose at 90-120 min or guided by activated clotting time in long cases. Unlike the aorta, clamping a femoral artery in a chronically ischaemic leg with established collaterals causes no measurable haemodynamic change. The limb beyond the clamps is not much more ischaemic than it was on the ward.

### 7. Anastomoses or endarterectomy and patch
**Why:** For a bypass, the proximal anastomosis is sewn end to side on the femoral artery and the distal on the popliteal or tibial vessel, the latter with fine suture under loupes on a 2-3 mm artery. For an endarterectomy, the plaque is dissected out of the common femoral and profunda origin and the arteriotomy closed with a vein or bovine pericardial patch.
**Anaesthesia:** The distal anastomosis on a small calcified tibial vessel needs an absolutely still leg. Under neuraxial anaesthesia a sedated patient who twitches or coughs is a problem; lighten or deepen but do not leave them in between. Under general anaesthesia neuromuscular block is not required if depth is adequate. Maintain normotension: graft flow in the first hour is pressure-dependent, and surgeons will ask for the pressure to be kept up at the time the clamps come off.

### 8. Clamp release, completion assessment and adjunctive endovascular work
**Why:** Flow is restored and the surgeon checks the graft by Doppler, flow measurement or completion angiogram. In a hybrid case an iliac stent or a distal angioplasty is done now through the open artery.
**Anaesthesia:** Reperfusion of one chronically ischaemic leg produces little systemic effect: a small fall in pressure and rise in end-tidal carbon dioxide at most. Acute-on-chronic ischaemia with a paralysed, insensate leg is different and is on [embolectomy and fasciotomy for acute limb ischaemia](embolectomy-and-fasciotomy-for-acute-limb.html). A completion angiogram adds contrast to kidneys that are often impaired; keep the patient filled and the pressure up. A poor completion result means revision, another 30-60 min, and a top-up of heparin and of the epidural.

### 9. Haemostasis and closure
**Why:** Long wounds are closed in layers, with particular care at the groin, where lymphatic leak and infection threaten the graft.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask the surgeon to infiltrate all wounds with long-acting local anaesthetic, within the weight-based maximum for the whole leg: ropivacaine 0.2% in volume covers a long wound better than a small volume of a concentrated solution. Protamine is the surgeon's decision. Check a final haemoglobin and temperature; a cold, anaemic patient with coronary disease should be warmed and transfused before emergence, not after.

### 10. Recovery and graft surveillance
**Why:** A graft that occludes in the first hours can be rescued if it is noticed.
**Anaesthesia:** Hourly foot colour, capillary refill, Doppler signal and pain. A neuraxial block that is still dense hides a cold foot's pain but not its Doppler signal; say so in the handover. Hypotension is treated promptly because the graft needs pressure. Return to theatre for graft thrombosis or bleeding is common enough (the first 24 h) that the neuraxial catheter, if there is one, is left in until the next morning.

### 11. Days 1 to 3
**Why:** The limb is reperfused; the patient's coronary arteries are not.
**Anaesthesia:** Myocardial infarction after vascular surgery is typically silent, in the first 72 h, and associated with tachycardia, anaemia, hypotension and pain. Troponin surveillance, a transfusion threshold of 80 g/L or higher in coronary disease, and good analgesia are the measures available. Reperfusion oedema peaks on day 2-3; elevation is the treatment, and a tense, painful calf with pain on passive toe movement is compartment syndrome.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is extensive but superficial soft tissue, on a background of ischaemic and often neuropathic pain in an opioid-exposed patient. No single block covers the incisions of a femorodistal bypass: the groin is ilioinguinal, genitofemoral and femoral territory, the medial thigh and calf are femoral and saphenous, and the deep popliteal and tibial dissection and the foot are sciatic. The analgesic strategy is therefore layered, and choosing the layers is author's judgement, since neither the randomised trials nor the Cochrane review reported pain outcomes.

Layer one is the anaesthetic. A spinal with or without intrathecal morphine 100-150 micrograms gives 12-24 h of good analgesia where neuraxial timing allows; an epidural continued to the next morning does the same for longer cases. Layer two, for every patient whatever the anaesthetic, is surgeon infiltration of all wounds at closure. Layer three is regular paracetamol and the patient's own opioid continued at baseline with immediate-release doses on top: opioid tolerance is the most common reason for a bad first night, and under-dosing a patient who was taking oxycodone for rest pain is the usual error. Gabapentinoids already prescribed for neuropathic rest pain are continued, not started.

Under general anaesthesia, a fascia iliaca or femoral block with ropivacaine 0.375% 20-30 mL covers the thigh and saphenous incisions; it does not cover the groin skin crease fully or anything in sciatic territory. Antiplatelet therapy is not a contraindication to these superficial compressible blocks.

The catheter decision is selective and follows the foot, not the bypass. A popliteal sciatic catheter is justified when the patient has severe rest pain, foot ulceration or a planned debridement or toe amputation under the same anaesthetic: it treats the pain that brought them in, covers the foot procedure and the below-knee wound, and can run for 3-5 days. For a femoral endarterectomy or an above-knee bypass in a patient without tissue loss, no catheter is needed.

NSAIDs are usually withheld: renal impairment, contrast, heparin and antiplatelets are present in most patients.

Expect worst pain on day 1 as the neuraxial block or wound infiltration wears off and the patient first walks, falling steadily from day 3. Escalation triggers are diagnostic. Return of foot or calf pain after a comfortable interval is graft occlusion. A tense calf with pain on passive stretch is compartment syndrome. A swollen, painful groin is haematoma or, later, lymphocoele or infection. With a dense block in place, none of these declare themselves by pain, so the Doppler and the calf examination have to be done regardless.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Isolated common femoral endarterectomy. A 60-120 min operation through one groin incision. A spinal is ideal. It can be done under local infiltration with sedation in the highest-risk patients, with the surgeon topping up around the femoral sheath; ilioinguinal and genitofemoral territory need separate infiltration.
- Femorofemoral crossover. Two groin wounds and a suprapubic subcutaneous tunnel: a spinal covers it.
- Axillofemoral bypass. The frailest patients, an infraclavicular wound, a chest wall tunnel and one or two groins. General anaesthesia, with the blood pressure cuff and arterial line on the arm opposite the donor axillary artery.
- Arm vein harvest. Adds a long arm wound, removes that arm for lines and cuff, and lengthens the case by an hour or more.
- Redo surgery. Scarred groins, longer dissection, more blood loss, higher infection risk and often a prosthetic conduit.
- Dialysis patients. Fluid and potassium limits, a fistula arm to protect, calcified vessels that are difficult to clamp and sew, and the worst outcomes of any subgroup.
- The diabetic foot. Sepsis from the foot may be driving the patient's physiology. Debridement or minor amputation is often added at the end; plan the sciatic block or catheter for it.
- Neuraxial anaesthesia wearing off. A single-shot spinal fails at 2.5-3 h and vein bypasses overrun. If there is doubt, use a catheter technique or general anaesthesia from the start; conversion with the leg open and the patient heparinised is the avoidable version.
- Graft thrombosis and return to theatre. Usually within 24 h, usually general anaesthesia, with a heparin infusion running. A neuraxial catheter in place is not removed until coagulation timing allows.
- The emergency variant. Acute limb ischaemia, graft occlusion and bleeding from an infected groin (a herald bleed from an anastomotic blowout is a major haemorrhage call) all bypass the elective plan; see [embolectomy and fasciotomy for acute limb ischaemia](embolectomy-and-fasciotomy-for-acute-limb.html). The endovascular alternative is on [peripheral angioplasty and stenting](peripheral-angioplasty-and-stenting.html).

## Key sources

Who should have a bypass is informed by two modern randomised trials that reached different answers in different populations, and both identify cardiorespiratory death as the outcome that matters. The anaesthetic technique question has four small randomised trials showing no difference except less pneumonia, and one large observational study favouring neuraxial anaesthesia with obvious scope for confounding by hospital. None of these reported pain. There is no PROSPECT guideline for lower limb revascularisation, and the layered pain plan, the selective sciatic catheter and the advice on opioid-tolerant patients are the author's judgement.
