## What the surgeon is doing and why

Three operations share a card because they share a patient group, a theatre list and often a single anaesthetic. Each reshapes a contour by removing or adding volume and tailoring the skin over it, and each has one step that explains its pain.

Breast reduction takes weight off a patient with neck, back and shoulder symptoms from heavy breasts. The nipple and areola cannot simply be moved: they are kept alive on a pedicle of dermis and gland (inferior or superomedial), and the surgeon removes a wedge of 300-1,500 g of breast from around that pedicle, lifts the nipple to its new position and closes the skin as a vertical or inverted-T scar. A mastopexy is the same skin operation with little or no gland removed. Nothing below the breast is disturbed: pectoral fascia is the floor of the dissection.

Augmentation places a silicone implant through a 4-5 cm incision, usually in the inframammary fold. The pocket is either on top of the muscle (subglandular or subfascial) or under it (subpectoral, usually "dual plane", with the lower origin of pectoralis major released so the implant sits under muscle above and gland below). Muscle cover hides the implant edge in a slim patient, which is why it is common, and it is the painful version.

Abdominoplasty removes the lower abdominal skin and fat as a hip-to-hip ellipse. The surgeon lifts the whole abdominal skin as a flap off the rectus sheath up to the xiphoid and costal margins, leaving the umbilicus on its stalk, then repairs the separation of the rectus muscles left by pregnancy or weight gain by suturing the sheaths together in the midline. The flap is pulled down, the excess cut off, the umbilicus brought out through a new opening and the wound closed with the table flexed.

```
 reduction       skin + gland removed, nipple on a pedicle   no muscle
 augmentation    implant under pectoralis major              muscle stretched
 abdominoplasty  flap lifted, rectus sheaths plicated,       fascia tightened,
                 skin closed in flexion                      closure under tension
```

The pain logic follows the diagram. Reduction is long wounds and raw gland: skin and soft tissue, moderate for two days, and many patients say the relief of weight outweighs it. Subpectoral augmentation is muscle stretch and spasm: a tight, crushing chest for 48-72 hours that then goes quickly. Abdominoplasty with plication is abdominal wall under tension, loaded by breathing, coughing and standing, and is the most painful and longest of the three. The card's score of 3 describes augmentation and abdominoplasty with the plan in place; reduction alone is a 2.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment and marking
**Why:** The surgeon marks the patient standing, because the landmarks move when she lies down. The markings are the operative plan.
**Anaesthesia:** A healthy population with a few specific questions. Smoking and nicotine in any form threaten the nipple pedicle and the abdominoplasty flap, and most surgeons insist on abstinence. The combined oral contraceptive and hormone replacement raise thrombotic risk. Patients after massive weight loss may be anaemic and iron, B12 or protein deficient (see [bariatric surgery](bariatric-surgery-sleeve-gastrectomy-and-gastric.html)); glucagon-like peptide-1 agonists are common in this group and carry their own fasting considerations. Ask how many procedures are planned and for how long. Do not wash off the markings with the block preparation.

### 2. Induction and positioning
**Why:** Both breasts or the whole abdomen are exposed, both arms are out, and the table will be sat up or flexed during the case.
**Anaesthesia:** Propofol TIVA is the author's default for nausea in a group at high risk. A supraglottic airway suits a 60-minute augmentation; intubate for reduction and abdominoplasty. Wrap and secure the arms to the boards, strap the pelvis, support the head so it cannot roll when sitting, and rehearse the sitting position before draping. Warm from the start: the exposed area is large and the patients are often slim. Place calf compressors before induction.

### 3. Infiltration
**Why:** Dilute lidocaine or saline with adrenaline is infiltrated along the incisions and through the tissue to be resected, to reduce bleeding and define planes. In lipoabdominoplasty the flanks and upper abdomen are infiltrated and suctioned first.
**Anaesthesia:** Know the recipe and the volume. With bilateral breast infiltration, an abdominal field and blocks to follow, the local anaesthetic total is the anaesthetist's problem. Tumescent lidocaine is absorbed slowly, with estimated safe ceilings of 28 mg/kg without liposuction and 45 mg/kg with it, and peak plasma levels many hours later, after the patient has gone home. Bupivacaine or ropivacaine in a block adds to that toxicity and peaks earlier. Adrenaline absorption produces tachycardia and hypertension for a few minutes.

### 4. Breast reduction: pedicle and resection
**Why:** The areola is incised, the pedicle de-epithelialised and the wedges of gland and fat removed, usually with diathermy, and weighed so that the sides match.
**Anaesthesia:** The longest and bloodiest breast step. Blood loss is steady and under-estimated because it is in the specimen and the swabs: a large bilateral reduction can lose 500 mL, which matters in a small or anaemic patient. Keep the blood pressure normal so that bleeding points are found now. The plume from prolonged diathermy of fat is considerable.

### 5. Breast shaping and sitting up
**Why:** The skin is tailor-tacked with staples, the patient is sat up and the surgeon judges symmetry and nipple position, adjusting and repeating as needed. Nipple colour is checked after closure.
**Anaesthesia:** Sitting to 60-80 degrees under anaesthesia drops the blood pressure: give vasopressor as the table moves. Check head, tube and arms each time. A dusky nipple at the end is venous congestion of the pedicle and the surgeon will release sutures; hypotension, cold and vasoconstriction do not help. In gigantomastia the nipple may be removed and replaced as a free graft.

### 6. Augmentation: pocket and implant
**Why:** Through the inframammary incision the surgeon develops the pocket, releases the lower fibres of pectoralis major for a dual-plane position, checks haemostasis with a lighted retractor, trials sizers with the patient sitting, and inserts the implant through a sleeve without touching skin.
**Anaesthesia:** Muscle release with diathermy makes the arm and shoulder jump whatever the level of paralysis. The pleura lies under the ribs at the medial and inferior edge of the pocket, and a pneumothorax from dissection or from a block needle is rare and presents as rising airway pressure or as breathlessness in recovery. If a pectoral nerves block has not been done, the surgeon can flood the pocket and inject between the pectoral muscles under direct vision before the implant goes in. This is the step that produces the postoperative chest tightness.

### 7. Abdominoplasty: flap elevation and the umbilicus
**Why:** The lower incision is made, the umbilicus is cut free from the skin and left on its stalk, and the flap is raised off the anterior rectus sheath to the xiphoid, dividing the perforating vessels that supplied it. The flap now lives on the intercostal vessels entering laterally.
**Anaesthesia:** A large raw surface: heat and fluid loss rise and continue until closure. Blood loss is from the perforators and is moderate. The surviving blood supply to the flap is marginal at its lower central edge, which is why smokers lose skin there and why the surgeon objects to hypotension and to cold.

### 8. Rectus plication
**Why:** The medial edges of the rectus sheaths are sutured together in one or two layers from xiphoid to pubis to narrow the waist and restore the midline.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the physiological event of the operation. Abdominal volume falls, intra-abdominal pressure rises, the diaphragm is pushed up, and you see it as higher peak pressure, lower compliance and sometimes lower saturation. Full neuromuscular block makes the plication easier and the reading more reassuring than it will be once the patient is breathing alone; a large rise in an obese patient or one having a hernia repaired at the same time is worth voicing before the second layer goes in. The plication is also the main source of pain and the target for the abdominal wall block, which the surgeon can place under direct vision now, into the plane between internal oblique and transversus or within the rectus sheath.

### 9. Flexed closure, drains and garment
**Why:** The table is flexed 30-40 degrees at the hips, the flap advanced, the excess skin excised, the umbilicus inset and the wound closed in layers over drains or with quilting sutures that close the dead space. A compression garment or binder goes on.
**Anaesthesia:** Flexion pools blood in the legs and can drop the pressure. Reverse neuromuscular block fully, because a patient with a tight abdomen needs all her respiratory muscle. A binder applied too tightly adds to the abdominal pressure. Keep the patient flexed for transfer and in bed.

### 10. Emergence
**Why:** A quiet wake-up protects the pocket, the flap and the plication.
**Anaesthesia:** A remifentanil-based emergence with the patient flexed and sitting up, antiemetics given, and long-acting opioid in a modest dose already working. A bout of coughing can produce a breast haematoma within minutes, visible as one side swelling in recovery, and that is the commonest reason for an unplanned return to theatre in this card.

### 11. Recovery, discharge and the first night
**Why:** The surgeon wants early walking, the garment on, and the patient home or in a ward bed with clear instructions.
**Anaesthesia:** Day-case discharge after augmentation needs oral analgesia already started and nausea controlled. After abdominoplasty the patient walks flexed, with help, on the evening of surgery; calf compression continues until she is properly mobile. The first night at home falls inside the window for peak lidocaine levels after tumescent infiltration and for residual sedation, so a responsible adult stays with her.

## Pain plan

The plan follows the mechanism of the particular operation. All three get paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor before or during surgery and regularly afterwards, dexamethasone 8 mg IV, and opioid for rescue: the PROSPECT recommendations for breast surgery, which were written for cancer operations and are applied here by analogy. No procedure-specific guideline exists for aesthetic breast surgery or abdominoplasty.

Breast reduction: skin and gland, no muscle. Tumescent infiltration with long-acting local anaesthetic by the surgeon is sufficient for most patients, and a block is optional. Expect worst pain of 3-4 on day 1, falling quickly.

Subpectoral augmentation: muscle stretch, in the territory of the medial and lateral pectoral nerves with the intercostals supplying the skin and inframammary fold. A Pecs II block, or the surgeon's equivalent under direct vision, fits that anatomy; the supporting evidence is the meta-analysis in cancer surgery showing lower opioid use and pain scores against systemic analgesia. Spasm responds poorly to opioid; a short course of a muscle relaxant at night is common surgical practice without trial support. A paravertebral block covers the skin and misses the pectoral nerves. Expect 5 on day 1 and a rapid fall after day 3.

Abdominoplasty: abdominal wall under tension. A TAP or rectus sheath block placed by the surgeon after plication is the defensible default. The one meta-analysis found longer time to first analgesia and less nausea with a TAP, without a significant difference in total opioid use, from a small and mostly observational literature, so the claim is modest. Catheters are used in some practices for 48 hours; the author regards them as rarely necessary. Expect 5 on days 1 and 2 with movement and coughing, 3-4 on day 3 and 2 at a week, with the patient gradually straightening.

Gabapentinoids carry a PROSPECT recommendation for breast surgery, but in day-case aesthetic patients the author's judgement is to avoid adding sedation to an opioid in someone going home. Rebound when a single-shot block recedes is moderate in augmentation and abdominoplasty: start the oral regimen in recovery and give a bedtime opioid dose on the first night.

Escalation triggers: one breast larger, tenser and more painful than the other is a haematoma; breathlessness after augmentation or a chest wall block is a pneumothorax until a film shows otherwise; breathlessness, calf pain or collapse in the first three weeks after abdominoplasty is thromboembolism; pain with a tight, distended abdomen and falling urine output after a tight plication is raised abdominal pressure.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Combined procedures. Breast and abdomen in one sitting means four to six hours, a larger local anaesthetic total, more heat and blood loss and more thrombotic risk. The decision to combine is made in the consulting room; you can still influence the order (shortest and cleanest first) and the stopping point.
- Venous thromboembolism. Tight plication, flexion, a binder, hours of surgery and often oestrogen. Compression devices and early walking are universal. Chemoprophylaxis is contested: a review of 24 series and 9,502 abdominoplasties found no significant reduction in thromboembolism and more bleeding with routine anticoagulation. It is a single-author analysis of retrospective data and many surgeons still give low molecular weight heparin to higher-risk patients; follow an agreed policy.
- Local anaesthetic toxicity. The emergency that happens at home. Total the lidocaine in the tumescent solution and the long-acting agent in blocks separately, and have lipid emulsion in the facility.
- Haematoma. The return to theatre: a recent anaesthetic, sometimes a full stomach, usually a stable patient. After abdominoplasty a large haematoma can hide under the flap with only tachycardia and a falling haemoglobin.
- Massive weight loss. Larger resections, poorer tissue, anaemia and more wound problems; the circumferential operations are in [liposuction and body contouring](liposuction-and-body-contouring.html).
- Free-standing facilities. Much of this surgery is done away from a main hospital: the resuscitation drugs, a second pair of trained hands and a transfer plan are part of the pre-list check.
- Gynaecomastia. Excision and liposuction in young men: short, mildly painful, done with a supraglottic airway.
- Implant exchange and capsulectomy. Longer, bloodier and nearer the pleura than a primary augmentation.
- Oncoplastic use. A therapeutic mammoplasty is a breast reduction around a cancer: see [wide local excision and sentinel node biopsy](wide-local-excision-and-sentinel-node.html).
- Invasive monitoring, blood and critical care are not needed for the standard operations. A group and screen is reasonable for very large reductions and combined cases in small or anaemic patients.

## Key sources

Evidence specific to aesthetic surgery is sparse and mostly retrospective. The pectoral block and systemic analgesia recommendations are extrapolated from cancer surgery. The abdominoplasty TAP meta-analysis is small. The thromboprophylaxis review is one author's synthesis and is cited to show that the question is open, not to settle it. The tumescent lidocaine ceilings come from a volunteer study. The pain scores for each operation and the advice on combined cases are the author's judgement.
