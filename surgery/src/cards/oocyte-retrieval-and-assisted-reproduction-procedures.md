## What the surgeon is doing and why

In vitro fertilisation needs mature eggs outside the body. For ten to twelve days the woman injects gonadotrophins that make both ovaries grow many follicles at once instead of one; each ovary swells from the size of a walnut to that of an orange or larger. A final trigger injection starts oocyte maturation, and ovulation would follow about 38-40 hours later. The eggs must be collected after they mature and before they are released, which fixes the procedure at roughly 36 hours after the trigger. That timing is the first thing an anaesthetist needs to understand about this list: a case cannot be moved to tomorrow.

Collection is done with a transvaginal ultrasound probe carrying a needle guide. The stimulated ovaries lie in the pouch of Douglas, a centimetre or two beyond the vaginal fornix. The specialist pushes the needle through the vaginal wall and the ovarian capsule, aspirates the nearest follicle, then steers the needle tip from follicle to follicle inside the ovary, usually through a single capsular puncture, and repeats on the other side. The fluid goes straight to the embryologist next door, who counts the eggs under a microscope while the procedure continues. Ten to thirty follicles are typical. The iliac vessels lie on the pelvic side wall just behind the ovary, the bladder is in front and bowel is beside it, so a patient who moves when the needle is in is the hazard.

Everything else in assisted reproduction is smaller. Embryo transfer, days later or in a later cycle, passes a soft catheter through the cervix under ultrasound and needs no anaesthetic unless the cervix is difficult or the patient cannot tolerate a speculum. In the male partner with no sperm in the ejaculate, sperm is retrieved by needle from the epididymis or testis under local anaesthesia, or by opening the testis under the microscope and searching the tubules, which is a general anaesthetic of one to three hours.

The complication that belongs to this specialty is ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome. Ovaries that have over-responded release vasoactive mediators, chiefly after exposure to human chorionic gonadotrophin, that make capillaries leak. Fluid moves from the circulation into the peritoneum and pleura: ascites, haemoconcentration, oliguria, effusions and thrombosis, arterial as well as venous. It appears three to seven days after collection, or later if pregnancy supplies more gonadotrophin, and the guidelines describe its management as supportive, with paracentesis for the tense abdomen and multidisciplinary care for the critical case.

The pain logic: during the procedure, the vaginal wall and ovarian capsule punctures and the dragging on the ovary are sharp and visceral, severe enough that a still patient needs deep sedation or more. Afterwards the ovaries are swollen, have been needled repeatedly and may sit in a small pool of blood, which gives a period-like cramp and bloating for 24-48 hours, proportional to the number of follicles. It responds to paracetamol and time. It should improve each day; pain that grows is a complication.

## Steps

### 1. The list and the patient
**Why:** Cases are scheduled at 20-30 minute intervals to the hour dictated by each woman's trigger injection.
**Anaesthesia:** These are healthy women in their thirties and forties under considerable emotional and financial strain, and a calm, brief, competent encounter matters to them more than usual. Three things need a specific look. Body habitus: polycystic ovary syndrome brings obesity, insulin resistance and sleep apnoea, and those are the patients in whom propofol sedation with a natural airway in lithotomy goes wrong. Fasting: if she is not fasted, the answer is a different technique (paracervical block with minimal sedation, a spinal, or a rapid sequence general anaesthetic), because the eggs will ovulate if you wait six hours. Hyperstimulation risk: a high follicle count, a slim young woman with polycystic ovaries, or abdominal distension already present tells you the ovaries are large, the pelvis is tender and the days after will be harder. Oncology patients preserving fertility before chemotherapy are the exception to "healthy": ask what the cancer is and where, since a mediastinal mass or a recent thrombosis changes everything.

### 2. The room
**Why:** The procedure room adjoins the laboratory so that follicular fluid reaches a warm stage within seconds; it is small, dim so the ultrasound screen can be seen, and often on a different floor or site from the main theatres.
**Anaesthesia:** This is remote-site anaesthesia and the standard is the same as in theatre: capnography on every sedated patient, a tipping couch, suction, oxygen, a self-inflating bag, airway devices, emergency drugs and a trained assistant who is not the scrub nurse. Check where help comes from and how long it takes. The laboratory has its own rules that reach into the room: the vagina is prepared with saline or culture medium, not antiseptic, because antiseptics are toxic to oocytes, and clinics restrict perfumes and volatile cleaning agents near open dishes.

### 3. Sedation or anaesthesia
**Why:** The specialist needs ten to twenty minutes of a completely still pelvis.
**Anaesthesia:** The common recipe is propofol by target-controlled infusion or boluses with a short-acting opioid: alfentanil 250-500 micrograms IV, fentanyl 50-100 micrograms IV, or a remifentanil infusion. In practice this is deep sedation or general anaesthesia without an airway device, and it should be run as such. Supplemental oxygen by nasal cannula or mask with capnography sampling, a jaw thrust available, and a low threshold for a supraglottic airway. The Cochrane review of 24 trials found no technique superior for pain or for pregnancy outcome, with high satisfaction across all of them, so the choice is governed by safety and stillness. A paracervical block by the specialist reduces the sedative dose and, on very low-quality evidence, the nausea; it does not cover the ovarian capsule and is not enough alone for most women. Give paracetamol 1 g IV or orally before the start.

### 4. Lithotomy, probe and preparation
**Why:** Legs in supports, a saline wash of the vagina, and the probe with its needle guide positioned against the fornix nearest the ovary.
**Anaesthesia:** The probe is uncomfortable but not painful; let the sedation reach its target before the needle is loaded, not after. Lithotomy with a natural airway raises the regurgitation risk a little and reduces lung volume in the obese: a slight head-up tilt of the couch helps and does not trouble the operator.

### 5. Needle puncture and aspiration of the first ovary
**Why:** One pass through the vaginal wall and capsule, then follicle after follicle is drained by suction, the needle turned and advanced within the ovary; some specialists flush each follicle.
**Anaesthesia:** The two peaks of stimulation are the first puncture on each side and any manipulation that pulls the ovary on its pedicle. A patient who is light at those moments moves her pelvis, and the needle tip is then a few millimetres from the internal iliac vein. Deepen before each puncture: a propofol bolus of 20-40 mg IV about 30 seconds ahead, on the specialist's word. Apnoea after the opioid and propofol together is common and brief; support the airway and wait, because the stimulus is about to return. Vagal bradycardia from ovarian traction occurs and settles when the needle stops.

### 6. The second ovary and the count
**Why:** The needle is withdrawn, the probe moved to the other fornix and the process repeated; the embryologist calls the running total.
**Anaesthesia:** The second side is usually quicker. With a high follicle count the procedure runs to 25-30 minutes and the cumulative propofol dose is that of a short general anaesthetic; this is the patient in whom a supraglottic airway from the start would have been the cleaner choice. Stop the infusion as the last follicles are drained.

### 7. Haemostasis and emergence
**Why:** The specialist inspects the vaginal puncture sites with a speculum and presses on any that ooze; occasionally a stitch is needed.
**Anaesthesia:** Vaginal ooze is common and trivial. Bleeding you cannot see is from the ovary or a pelvic vessel into the peritoneum, and nothing at this stage will tell you. Lower the legs, sit the patient up as she wakes, and send her to recovery with oxygen until fully responsive.

### 8. Recovery and discharge
**Why:** The woman goes home within an hour or two with instructions about the transfer or freeze plan.
**Anaesthesia:** Cramping is usual and paracetamol is the standard answer. Clinics vary on NSAIDs around collection and transfer because of a theoretical concern about prostaglandins, ovulation and implantation: follow the clinic's policy, and if NSAIDs are excluded a small dose of oral oxycodone 5 mg covers the few who need more. Before discharge she should be haemodynamically steady when standing and her pain should be settling. Pain that is increasing, shoulder-tip pain, faintness, pallor or a rising heart rate is intraperitoneal bleeding and needs the specialist, a haemoglobin and an ultrasound, not a taxi. The written advice must name the hyperstimulation symptoms (increasing bloating, weight gain, reduced urine, breathlessness, vomiting) and whom to ring.

### 9. Embryo transfer
**Why:** A catheter carrying one embryo is passed through the cervix to the mid-cavity under abdominal ultrasound with a full bladder.
**Anaesthesia:** Usually none. An anaesthetist is asked for cervical stenosis, vaginismus or severe anxiety, and a small dose of propofol sedation is enough; there is no evidence in the sources found that it affects the outcome, and the same lack of evidence means it should not be offered as a way of improving success.

### 10. Surgical sperm retrieval
**Why:** For obstructive azoospermia, sperm is aspirated by needle from the epididymis or testis. For non-obstructive azoospermia the testis is opened under an operating microscope and individual tubules are sampled until sperm are found, often on the same day as the partner's egg collection.
**Anaesthesia:** Needle aspiration is done under spermatic cord block and infiltration by the urologist, with or without light sedation. Microdissection is a 1-3 hour general anaesthetic with a supraglottic airway and a cord block for analgesia, as on the [scrotal surgery](scrotal-and-penile-surgery.html) card. The men carry their own diagnoses: Klinefelter syndrome, previous chemotherapy, cystic fibrosis with absent vasa, and spinal cord injury, where electroejaculation or any scrotal stimulus in a lesion above T6 provokes autonomic dysreflexia and is done under spinal or general anaesthesia with an antihypertensive ready.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a brief, intense visceral stimulus followed by a mild inflammatory cramp, so the plan is anaesthesia for the first and paracetamol for the second. No block is needed after the procedure and none has a role beyond the paracervical block that some specialists add at the start.

During collection, the Cochrane review (24 trials, 3,160 women) is the evidence base and its conclusion is permissive: no single method was superior, combined sedation and opioid with or without paracervical block or acupuncture relieved pain better than one modality alone, and no technique was shown to change pregnancy rates. A narrative review reaches the same practical position, that sedation is the most used mode and that a paracervical block lowers the sedative dose. Propofol with remifentanil or alfentanil is described in the recent trial literature as the widely used regimen. The defensible default is therefore propofol with a short-acting opioid, titrated to immobility with the airway actively managed, plus a paracervical block if the specialist uses one.

General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway is the better choice, on the author's judgement, for a body mass index above about 40, known sleep apnoea, a high follicle count with a long procedure expected, transabdominal collection (ovaries out of vaginal reach), and the patient who moved last time. Spinal anaesthesia is the option for the unfasted patient at the fixed hour and for the patient with a serious airway or respiratory problem; in one small trial it gave lower postoperative pain scores than sedation with paracervical block. A short-acting agent such as prilocaine 2% or chloroprocaine, where available, suits a 20-minute procedure.

Afterwards: regular paracetamol for one to two days, NSAIDs only if the clinic allows, a heat pack, and oral opioid for the occasional patient with many follicles. Rebound does not occur. Escalation triggers are the diagnoses that matter: pain increasing over the first hours is bleeding; sudden severe unilateral pain with vomiting in the following days is torsion of an enlarged ovary; progressive distension, breathlessness and oliguria from day 3 is hyperstimulation; fever and pelvic pain after a week is an abscess, more often when an endometrioma was punctured.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome coming to theatre. For paracentesis, torsion, an ectopic or haemorrhage. The patient is intravascularly depleted despite looking fluid-overloaded, haemoconcentrated, hypoalbuminaemic and prothrombotic, with tense ascites, effusions and sometimes renal impairment. Treat as the ascitic patient on the [gynaecological oncology](gynaecological-oncology.html) card on a smaller scale: head-up induction, rapid sequence if tense, careful fluid with albumin when ascites is drained, arterial line for the severe case, thromboprophylaxis, and a higher-acuity bed. Laparoscopy for torsion or bleeding follows the [laparoscopic gynaecology](laparoscopic-gynaecology.html) card with very large, fragile ovaries in the way.
- The unfasted patient. The date cannot move. Paracervical block with minimal sedation, a spinal, or a rapid sequence induction with a tracheal tube are the options; deep sedation with a natural airway is not one of them.
- Obesity and sleep apnoea. The commonest reason for an airway event on this list. Decide before the first dose, not at the first desaturation.
- Fertility preservation before cancer treatment. Lymphoma with a mediastinal mass, breast cancer on the day after a staging procedure, or a patient already anticoagulated: get the oncology details, and do not sedate a patient who cannot lie flat.
- Intraperitoneal haemorrhage. Rare, late and easily missed in a discharged patient; the clinic's after-hours pathway needs to lead to a hospital with a gynaecologist.
- Drug effects on the oocyte. Agents can be measured in follicular fluid, and laboratory studies report effects at high concentrations, but clinical trials have not shown a difference in pregnancy rates between techniques. The honest position is that it is not known whether any standard agent matters, and that a short, effective anaesthetic is preferable to a long, inadequate one.
- Antiemetic choice. Avoidance of metoclopramide and droperidol because of prolactin release is long-standing custom without outcome data; ondansetron and propofol make the question moot.
- Donors. Egg donors are having a procedure with no benefit to themselves: the threshold for anything other than an uneventful anaesthetic is correspondingly low.
- Related procedures on other cards. Hysteroscopy before a cycle is on the [hysteroscopy](hysteroscopy-dilatation-and-curettage-and-minor.html) card; salpingectomy for hydrosalpinx and ovarian drilling are on the [laparoscopic gynaecology](laparoscopic-gynaecology.html) card.

## Key sources

The evidence for anaesthetic technique is a Cochrane review that is large in trial count and weak in quality: most comparisons rest on one or two small trials graded low or very low, and its main message is the absence of a demonstrated difference. The guidelines cited for hyperstimulation syndrome are fertility-society and college documents addressed to gynaecologists, and say little about anaesthesia; the advice here on anaesthetising these patients is standard management of ascites and hypovolaemia applied to the condition. The timing of collection after the trigger, the laboratory's preparation rules, the dose ranges for sedation and the thresholds for choosing general anaesthesia are standard practice or the author's judgement and are unsourced. No PROSPECT review covers these procedures.
