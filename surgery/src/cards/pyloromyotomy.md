## What the surgeon is doing and why

Infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis is an acquired thickening of the circular muscle of the pylorus that appears between about two and eight weeks of age, more often in boys. The thickened muscle narrows and lengthens the pyloric canal until the stomach cannot empty. The infant vomits milk forcefully after every feed, stays hungry, and loses weight. Ultrasound makes the diagnosis by measuring the muscle.

The operation is one of the simplest in surgery. Ramstedt's pyloromyotomy is a longitudinal cut through the serosa and the hypertrophied muscle on the anterior, relatively avascular surface of the pylorus, from the gastric antrum to just short of the duodenum. The muscle is then spread apart until the intact mucosa bulges into the cleft and the two halves of the pylorus move independently. Nothing is removed and nothing is sutured; the mucosa is left untouched and the muscle remodels over weeks. The only way to do harm is to tear the mucosa, usually at the duodenal end where the wall thins abruptly, so the surgeon finishes by testing for a leak. The approach is either open (a supraumbilical skin-crease or right upper quadrant incision, with the pylorus delivered into the wound) or laparoscopic (an umbilical camera port and two stab incisions, with the pylorus split in situ). In the randomised trial of 180 infants, laparoscopy shortened the time to full feeds and the postoperative stay by a few hours each, with similar complications.

The operation cures the obstruction, but the obstruction is not what threatens the infant. Days of vomiting gastric juice remove hydrogen ion, chloride and water. The kidney then defends volume at the expense of acid-base balance, and the chain is worth holding in mind because it dictates the fluid:

```
 vomiting gastric acid
        |
        v
 loss of H+, Cl-, water  ->  metabolic alkalosis, low chloride
        |
        v
 volume depletion -> aldosterone -> kidney keeps Na+,
                                    spends K+ and H+
        |
        v
 low potassium, acid urine despite alkaline blood
        |
        v
 chloride and volume replaced (0.9% saline, then potassium)
        |
        v
 kidney can excrete bicarbonate again: alkalosis corrects
```

An alkalotic infant compensates by breathing less. Bicarbonate equilibrates slowly into cerebrospinal fluid, so the central chemoreceptors stay alkaline for hours after the plasma has been corrected, and far longer if it has not. Add residual anaesthetic or any opioid and the result is apnoea in recovery. That is why this is a medical emergency followed by a scheduled operation: surgery waits, for a day or two if necessary, until the infant is rehydrated and the chloride and bicarbonate are back in range.

The pain logic: three small wounds or one short incision, and a muscle split that the infant does not appear to feel much once the stomach empties. This is a 2 on wall pain alone, managed by infiltration and paracetamol. The plan is shaped less by the amount of pain than by the need to avoid opioid in an infant with a recently alkaline respiratory centre.

## Steps

### 1. Resuscitation on the ward
**Why:** The surgeon has made the diagnosis and is waiting for you and the paediatric team to declare the infant fit; the pylorus will be the same tomorrow.

**Anaesthesia:** Assess dehydration clinically (fontanelle, capillary refill, urine output, weight against birth centile) and by the gas: a high bicarbonate, low chloride and low potassium grade the severity. A shocked infant gets 0.9% sodium chloride 10-20 mL/kg IV, repeated to effect. Then maintenance at about 1.5 times the usual rate with a glucose-containing fluid of at least half-normal and preferably isotonic sodium content, with potassium chloride 10-20 mmol/L added once urine is passing. Nasogastric losses are replaced with 0.9% sodium chloride. Chloride is the treatment: the alkalosis cannot correct until the kidney has enough chloride and volume to let bicarbonate go. Repeat the gas and electrolytes every 6-12 hours. Commonly used thresholds for theatre are chloride above 100 mmol/L, bicarbonate below 28-30 mmol/L, sodium above 130 mmol/L and potassium above 3 mmol/L; units differ by a few mmol/L and none of the numbers has trial support.

### 2. Listing and timing
**Why:** Pyloromyotomy is scheduled on a daytime list with a surgeon who does it regularly, because there is no penalty for a short wait.

**Anaesthesia:** Refuse the case at 2 am with a bicarbonate of 34 mmol/L. Look at the trend, not one result: a bicarbonate that has fallen from 38 to 29 mmol/L with a normal chloride is ready; a normal-looking potassium in a still-alkalotic infant is not reassuring, because it will fall as the pH corrects. Check the glucose, since stores are small after days of poor intake, and keep the glucose-containing maintenance running to theatre. Ask about prematurity: an ex-premature infant under 60 weeks postmenstrual age has an apnoea risk of its own on top of the alkalosis (see [paediatric inguinal hernia](paediatric-inguinal-hernia-orchidopexy-and-circumcision.html)).

### 3. Emptying the stomach
**Why:** The stomach sits behind a closed outlet and contains milk, secretions and sometimes barium, whatever the fasting time.

**Anaesthesia:** With monitoring on and before any drug, pass or unblock a wide-bore orogastric or nasogastric tube and aspirate with the infant supine, then turned left lateral, then right lateral. The repeat aspirations recover a worthwhile further volume. This step does more to reduce the consequences of regurgitation than any choice of induction drug. Leave the tube in: the surgeon will need it for the leak test. Atropine 20 micrograms/kg IV is given by some before induction to guard against bradycardia at laryngoscopy; surveys show practice is split.

### 4. Induction and intubation
**Why:** Nothing surgical yet, but the surgeon should be in the room: the induction is the dangerous part of the case.

**Anaesthesia:** The debate is between a classic rapid sequence induction with apnoea and cricoid pressure, a modified sequence with gentle mask ventilation, and an inhalational induction. A neonate has a high oxygen consumption and a small functional residual capacity, so an apnoeic interval long enough for a relaxant to work reaches hypoxaemia before intubating conditions. In a cohort of 296 pyloromyotomies, hypoxaemia occurred in 30% after classic rapid sequence induction and 17% after a modified sequence, an adjusted odds ratio of 2.8 and of 6.5 among neonates; a second intubation attempt carried an odds ratio of 11.4; nobody aspirated. A series of 269 infants managed almost entirely by inhalational induction after gastric emptying also recorded no aspiration. Awake intubation has been abandoned.

The defensible default is therefore a controlled sequence: preoxygenate, propofol 3 mg/kg IV (or sevoflurane if the cannula has failed), rocuronium 0.6-1 mg/kg IV, pressure-limited mask ventilation at under 12 cmH2O until the relaxant has worked, then one unhurried laryngoscopy. Cricoid pressure distorts an infant airway and is omitted by most. Suxamethonium 2 mg/kg IV remains acceptable; sugammadex has removed the main argument for it. Expect a difficult first look in about one in twenty infants of this age, and have a smaller tube and a videolaryngoscope open.

### 5. Positioning and preparation
**Why:** The surgeon needs the epigastrium flat and still, and for laparoscopy stands at the infant's feet with the screen at the head.

**Anaesthesia:** You lose the patient under drapes. Before that, confirm tube depth by auscultation in the final position, tape it, and site a second point of venous access if the first is tenuous. Heat loss begins with skin preparation: warming mattress, warmed fluid, a hat, and the theatre warm before the infant arrives. Maintain with sevoflurane or desflurane in air and oxygen. Give paracetamol 7.5 mg/kg IV (the dose for a term infant under 10 kg) now.

### 6. Access: incision or ports
**Why:** Open, a short transverse or supraumbilical incision splits the rectus or the linea alba and the pylorus is delivered. Laparoscopic, a 3-5 mm umbilical port and two epigastric stab incisions are placed and the abdomen insufflated to about 6-8 mmHg at low flow.

**Anaesthesia:** For laparoscopy the umbilical vein may still be patent at this age, and carbon dioxide entering it is a rare cause of sudden collapse at insufflation: watch the capnograph and the saturation as gas goes in. Even 8 mmHg splints a neonatal diaphragm; expect to raise the inspiratory pressure and rate (see [paediatric laparoscopic and thoracoscopic surgery](paediatric-laparoscopic-and-thoracoscopic-surgery.html)). The response to incision is the test of whether the infiltration and volatile are enough. If the heart rate climbs, deepen the volatile or give remifentanil 0.5-1 microgram/kg IV; fentanyl 1 microgram/kg IV is the fallback, not the routine.

### 7. The myotomy
**Why:** The surgeon scores the serosa along the avascular line, splits the muscle with a blunt spreader or knife down to mucosa, and separates the halves until they move independently. Traction on the stomach to deliver the pylorus (open) or to steady it (laparoscopic) is brief.

**Anaesthesia:** Traction on the stomach and mesentery can provoke bradycardia in an infant whose cardiac output depends on rate; ask the surgeon to release and it recovers, with atropine 20 micrograms/kg IV if it does not. The abdomen must be still for the two minutes of the split: maintain neuromuscular block or adequate depth, and do not let the relaxant wear off unmonitored here. This is the whole operation and it takes minutes.

### 8. Leak test
**Why:** A mucosal tear at the duodenal end is the one serious intraoperative complication, and an unrecognised one causes peritonitis.

**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon asks you to inject 40-60 mL of air down the gastric tube while the pylorus is held under saline or watched for bubbles and bile. Confirm the tube is in the stomach before injecting, inject slowly, and aspirate the air back afterwards so the stomach is not left distended against the diaphragm for emergence. A perforation is sutured and covered with omentum; the infant then stays nil by mouth with the tube on drainage for a day or more, and the fluid plan changes accordingly.

### 9. Closure and infiltration
**Why:** Ports are removed under vision or the fascia and skin are closed with absorbable sutures.

**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon infiltrates each wound: levobupivacaine or bupivacaine 0.25% to a ceiling of 0.8 mL/kg (2 mg/kg) in total. Calculate the volume aloud; in a 3.5 kg infant it is under 3 mL. A rectus sheath block is an alternative for a supraumbilical incision and the same ceiling applies. Reverse rocuronium against a monitor (sugammadex 2 mg/kg IV, or 4 mg/kg IV after a recent dose), turn off the volatile, and do not give opioid for the end of the case.

### 10. Emergence and extubation
**Why:** The surgeon wants a baby who can feed in a few hours.

**Anaesthesia:** These infants wake slowly. The alkaline cerebrospinal fluid, the volatile and the lack of stimulation from a small infiltrated wound combine to produce a long quiet period, and the temptation is to extubate a regularly breathing but unrousable infant. Wait for grimacing, hip flexion, eye opening and a regular respiratory pattern. Extubate awake, in the lateral position, with suction ready.

### 11. Recovery and the first night
**Why:** Feeds restart within hours on a graded regimen; some vomiting on the first day is expected from gastric atony and oedema at the myotomy.

**Anaesthesia:** Apnoea and saturation monitoring continue for 12-24 hours, longer for an ex-premature infant. Continue glucose-containing maintenance fluid until feeds are established and check a glucose in recovery. Regular paracetamol is usually sufficient. Vomiting that persists beyond a few days suggests an incomplete myotomy; fever, distension and a rising heart rate on day 1 suggest a missed perforation.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a small abdominal wall wound in an infant whose respiratory control has just been disturbed. The plan follows from both halves of that sentence: local anaesthetic at the wound, paracetamol, and as little opioid as the operation allows.

Infiltration of the ports or incision by the surgeon is the default because it is quick, effective for wounds of this size and adds nothing to the anaesthetic time. Ultrasound-guided rectus sheath or transversus abdominis plane blocks are reasonable alternatives and are favoured by some units for open surgery; a survey of paediatric anaesthetists on both sides of the Atlantic found wide variation in whether any regional technique is used. No trial shows that a block is better than infiltration for this operation, so the choice is author's judgement. Whatever is used, the total local anaesthetic dose is counted against the 2 mg/kg ceiling, shared between surgeon and anaesthetist.

Paracetamol 7.5 mg/kg IV intraoperatively is followed by regular oral or intravenous dosing at neonatal intervals. NSAIDs are not used at this age. Intraoperative analgesia is the volatile agent plus the local anaesthetic; remifentanil is the best fit when more is needed, because its effect ends when the infusion or bolus does. Morphine and long-acting opioids are avoided. Sucrose and feeding are the effective postoperative adjuncts.

Spinal anaesthesia as the sole technique has been described in series and avoids the airway altogether; it needs a quick open operation and a surgeon who accepts a moving diaphragm, and it is not common practice.

Rebound is low because there is no long-acting block to wear off. An infant who is distressed and not settling with paracetamol and a feed on the first night should be examined for a leak before being given opioid.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Operating too early. The single avoidable error. A bicarbonate above 30 mmol/L, a chloride below 100 mmol/L or a clinically dry infant is not ready, whatever the pressure on the list.
- The late presenter. Severe dehydration, a bicarbonate above 40 mmol/L, hypoglycaemia and sometimes unconjugated jaundice. Resuscitation takes 48 hours or more; correct slowly and recheck potassium as the pH falls.
- Induction choice. The evidence favours ventilation through induction over classic apnoeic rapid sequence; it does not distinguish between intravenous and inhalational routes. Both depend on a properly emptied stomach.
- Hypoxaemia at laryngoscopy. Strongly linked to repeated attempts. One prepared attempt by the most experienced person present is the plan.
- Laparoscopic versus open. Laparoscopy needs neuromuscular block or deep anaesthesia for a still field, adds pneumoperitoneum, and carries the patent umbilical vein risk; the randomised trial found faster feeding and discharge with similar complication rates. Surgeons vary, and incomplete myotomy and mucosal perforation occur with both.
- Mucosal perforation. Recognised by the leak test and repaired; converts a 24-hour stay into several days nil by mouth.
- Hypoglycaemia. Small glycogen stores, days of poor intake, and maintenance fluid interrupted for transfer. Check at induction and in recovery.
- Hypothermia. A 3.5 kg infant exposed for skin preparation cools within minutes and a cold infant is another cause of slow emergence and apnoea.
- The ex-premature infant. Apnoea risk from prematurity and from alkalosis add together; these infants need the longer monitoring period and a bed with apnoea monitoring booked in advance. See [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).
- Emergency variant. There is none for the pylorus. The emergency form of this condition is hypovolaemic shock on presentation, which is treated with saline, not surgery.

## Key sources

There is no PROSPECT guideline for pyloromyotomy and the 179-procedure pain cohort did not include infants. The preoperative targets for chloride and bicarbonate are consensus figures repeated across reviews, with small differences between units and no trial behind any of them; the physiological argument for correcting the alkalosis is strong and the link to postoperative apnoea rests on that argument and on case reports. The induction debate has moved on observational data only: two single-centre cohorts with no aspiration events and a clear signal that apnoeic rapid sequence induction produces hypoxaemia. The comparison of laparoscopic and open surgery is the one randomised trial in the field. Local anaesthetic dose ceilings, paracetamol dosing and the duration of apnoea monitoring are standard paediatric practice, stated here without a source.
