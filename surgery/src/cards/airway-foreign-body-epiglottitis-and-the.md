## What the surgeon is doing and why

Three different problems share this card because they share one piece of physics. Flow through a tube falls with the fourth power of its radius, so an airway that has lost half its diameter has lost most of its capacity, and the patient compensates with effort, posture and muscle tone. Anaesthesia takes away all three. In each case the surgeon is there to do something definitive to the narrowing, and in each case the anaesthetic problem is to get the patient from awake and compensating to anaesthetised and secure without passing through obstructed and unrescuable.

An inhaled foreign body is a disease of toddlers: a peanut, a seed, a piece of carrot or a small plastic part goes down during a laugh or a fall. Most pass the larynx and lodge in a main or lobar bronchus, more often the right in older children. A proximal object in the larynx or trachea threatens asphyxia; a distal one causes a ball-valve with air trapping, then collapse and pneumonia over days. The surgeon removes it with a rigid ventilating bronchoscope: a straight metal tube with a light, a telescope, a side arm for the breathing circuit and a channel for optical forceps. The object is grasped and, because it is usually wider than the scope's lumen, forceps, scope and object are withdrawn together through the cords.

Epiglottitis is a bacterial cellulitis of the epiglottis and aryepiglottic folds. In children it was the classic airway emergency before Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccination and is now rare; a swollen, cherry-red epiglottis sits over the inlet of a small larynx and can close it within hours. In adults the same infection (supraglottitis) is now the usual presentation, the airway is larger, and most patients are managed with antibiotics, steroid and observation. The surgeon's role is to examine with a nasendoscope where that is safe, to stand ready with a rigid scope and a tracheostomy set while the airway is secured, and to drain an abscess if one has formed.

The adult obstructed airway is usually a laryngeal or hypopharyngeal tumour, sometimes bilateral cord palsy, angioedema, a deep neck infection or a subglottic stenosis. The surgeon will debulk, dilate, biopsy or bypass it. The decision that matters is how the airway is first secured.

There is no pain logic of consequence: no incision, score 1.

## Steps

### 1. Assess what, where and when
**Why:** The surgeon decides urgency from the same information you need for the anaesthetic.
**Anaesthesia:** For a foreign body the three questions from the largest literature review are what was inhaled (organic material swells and fragments; a button battery or sharp object is urgent in itself), where it is (history of choking then wheeze and reduced air entry on one side means bronchial; stridor, voice change or cyanotic episodes mean laryngeal or tracheal) and when (hours, or days with fever and consolidation). For any stridor, the phase gives the level:

```
 Level of narrowing       Sound                 Voice    Front-of-neck rescue
 -----------------------  --------------------  -------  ----------------------
 Supraglottic, pharynx    inspiratory, snoring  muffled  yes (below the lesion)
 Glottic                  inspiratory stridor   hoarse   yes
 Subglottic, upper        biphasic stridor      normal   only if the neck can
   trachea                                                 be entered below it
 Lower trachea, bronchi   expiratory wheeze     normal   no: rigid bronchoscope
```

Stridor at rest, sitting forward, drooling, use of accessory muscles, a quiet exhausted child or a falling saturation mean the airway is critical and investigation stops. A stable patient gets a chest radiograph (expiratory film or decubitus views for air trapping) or, in adults, nasendoscopy and CT, both of which change the plan more than anything you can do at the bedside.

### 2. Decide timing and assemble the team
**Why:** A distal bronchial object in a stable child is retrieved more safely by a rested, fully equipped team in daylight than by whoever is present at 2 am.
**Anaesthesia:** Urgent means now for laryngeal or tracheal objects, respiratory distress, button batteries and any deteriorating stridor; otherwise wait for fasting and the right people. In the review of nearly 13,000 paediatric cases aspiration of gastric contents was not reported, which supports not delaying for fasting when the airway is at risk. The other figure from that review is sobering: most in-hospital deaths were in previously stable children, during retrieval. Brief the team in the room. State the primary plan, what will be done if ventilation is lost, and who does it. See the rigid bronchoscope assembled with the right size for the child, the light and telescope working, optical forceps tested on a similar object, and the circuit connector fitting the side arm.

### 3. Preparation
**Why:** The surgeon needs a dry, quiet airway and a child who has not been made to cry.
**Anaesthesia:** Crying increases flow, turbulence and obstruction. Keep the child with the parent, in the position the child has chosen, with oxygen wafted and not forced. A cannula goes in before induction if the child tolerates it under topical anaesthetic; if a struggle would threaten the airway, it goes in the moment the child is asleep. Nebulised adrenaline 0.5 mL/kg of 1 in 1,000 (maximum 5 mL) buys time in croup, supraglottic oedema and post-instrumentation swelling. Give dexamethasone 0.15-0.6 mg/kg IV early. An antisialogogue (glycopyrrolate 5-10 microgram/kg IV) dries the field and blunts vagal reflexes from the scope.

### 4. Induction with spontaneous ventilation
**Why:** Until the surgeon has seen the object or the lesion, nobody knows whether positive pressure will ventilate past it or drive it further in.
**Anaesthesia:** The conventional approach, and the defensible default for a proximal or unknown obstruction, is to keep the patient breathing. Sevoflurane in 100% oxygen is slow in an obstructed child, since the same narrowing that limits air limits vapour, and needs patience, gentle continuous positive pressure and no assistance beyond that. The intravenous alternative is propofol titrated in small increments or by infusion with remifentanil at a low rate, or dexmedetomidine or ketamine, each chosen because breathing continues at a depth that tolerates a scope. No nitrous oxide where there is air trapping. Take the child deep: the test is no response to a firm jaw thrust.

### 5. Laryngoscopy and topical anaesthesia
**Why:** The surgeon or anaesthetist looks at the larynx first; an object sitting at the cords is lifted out with Magill forceps and the case is over.
**Anaesthesia:** Spray the cords and trachea with lidocaine under direct vision, 3-4 mg/kg at most, and give it two or three minutes. This is what allows a spontaneously breathing child to tolerate a metal tube through the larynx without coughing or laryngospasm. Mask the child deep again before the scope goes in.

### 6. Rigid bronchoscope in: sharing ventilation
**Why:** The scope passes the cords and becomes the airway; the surgeon advances it into each main bronchus in turn.
**Anaesthesia:** Connect the circuit to the side arm. Volatile delivery now leaks around the scope and whenever the telescope is removed, so change to or add propofol TIVA. With the telescope in, the lumen left for gas is narrow and expiration is slow: watch for breath stacking, and ask for the telescope to be withdrawn into the trachea if saturation falls. When the scope is deep in one bronchus the other lung is not ventilated. The choice between continued spontaneous breathing and relaxant with controlled ventilation is open: a meta-analysis of 864 children found no difference in desaturation, with less laryngospasm and a shorter procedure under controlled ventilation. The author's practice is spontaneous ventilation until the object is seen and judged, then a relaxant if it is distal and the surgeon wants stillness.

### 7. Retrieval
**Why:** The object is grasped under vision and brought out with the scope as one unit, because it will not pass up the lumen.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the moment of greatest risk. As the object comes through the subglottis and cords, the narrowest point, it can be stripped off the forceps and left occluding the trachea. Have the child deep or paralysed for the pass so the cords are open. If it is dropped and ventilation is impossible, the surgeon pushes it back down into a main bronchus, usually the one it came from, and you ventilate the other lung while the plan is reset. Swollen organic objects fragment, and several passes may be needed. Give 100% oxygen before each pass.

### 8. Second look and waking
**Why:** The surgeon re-inspects for fragments, a second object, pus and mucosal damage.
**Anaesthesia:** When the scope is out, take over with a face mask, or a tracheal tube if the procedure was long, the stomach is full or the lungs are soiled. Expect bronchospasm and oedema after organic objects: salbutamol, the steroid already given, and nebulised adrenaline for stridor. Wake the child fully before leaving theatre and observe overnight.

### 9. Epiglottitis in a child
**Why:** The surgeon stands scrubbed with a rigid bronchoscope and tracheostomy set while the airway is secured, and confirms the diagnosis by direct view once it is.
**Anaesthesia:** Do not examine the throat, lay the child down or cannulate on the ward. Bring the child to theatre sitting on the parent's lap. Inhalational induction with sevoflurane in oxygen, sitting, with continuous positive pressure; cannula and atropine once asleep; laryngoscopy when deep. The inlet may be unrecognisable: a bubble on gentle chest compression shows the way. Use a tube at least one size smaller than predicted. Then antibiotics, intensive care, and extubation after a day or two when a leak has returned and the swelling has settled under vision.

### 10. Epiglottitis in an adult
**Why:** Most adults can be watched; the surgeon grades the swelling by nasendoscopy and repeats it.
**Anaesthesia:** In a meta-analysis of over 10,000 adults about one in ten now needs airway intervention, but when intubation is attempted it fails in about 1 in 25, far above the rate in ordinary practice. Those who need a tube are the ones with stridor, drooling, inability to lie flat, rapid onset and an abscess. Options are awake tracheal intubation, sitting, with careful topicalisation and a small tube (over half the described cases were managed awake); induction in theatre with the surgeon ready, by an operator with a videolaryngoscope and a clear limit on attempts; or tracheostomy under local anaesthesia for the worst. High-flow nasal oxygen extends the time available for each. Whichever is chosen, the front of the neck is marked and the scalpel is open before starting.

### 11. The adult with an obstructing lesion
**Why:** The surgeon knows from the clinic what the tumour or stenosis looks like and what will be done to it.
**Anaesthesia:** There is no consensus technique; the published review and the national audit agree only that the decision must be made deliberately and jointly. Work through it by level. A supraglottic or glottic lesion that a small tube can pass, with a recent nasendoscopy to prove it, suits awake tracheal intubation, accepting that a bulky friable tumour can bleed or that the scope can complete the obstruction (the cork in the bottle). A lesion that fills the glottis is bypassed by tracheostomy under local anaesthesia; published series report immediate survival of about 97%. A subglottic or tracheal narrowing cannot be bypassed from the neck at all: the rescue is a rigid bronchoscope in the surgeon's hand, and induction is in theatre with it ready, as on the [rigid bronchoscopy, stents and tracheal resection](rigid-bronchoscopy-stents-and-tracheal-resection.html) and [mediastinoscopy and anterior mediastinal mass](mediastinoscopy-and-anterior-mediastinal-mass.html) cards. Inhalational induction in an adult with a fixed obstruction is slow and has no guaranteed way back; if general anaesthesia is chosen, the author's preference is a controlled intravenous induction with high-flow nasal oxygen running, relaxant available, a single best attempt and the surgeon scrubbed.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is mucosal contact only. Paracetamol, and an NSAID if there is no bleeding concern, are sufficient after rigid bronchoscopy; topical lidocaine to the larynx is the intraoperative analgesic and reflex suppressant, and means nothing by mouth for about two hours afterwards. No opioid beyond the short-acting drug used for the procedure is needed, and long-acting opioids are avoided in a patient whose airway is still swollen.

Epiglottitis is painful in itself: the sore throat and odynophagia are severe out of proportion to what can be seen in the mouth. In the unintubated adult under observation use paracetamol and an NSAID, with small titrated doses of opioid only where staff can watch the airway; sedatives are avoided. The intubated patient is managed on the unit's sedation and analgesia regimen. A tracheostomy done as part of this card is covered in [tracheostomy](tracheostomy.html).

## Pitfalls and variants

- Total obstruction after induction. The scenario everything above exists to prevent. In a child with a foreign body: deepen, laryngoscope, remove a visible object, or have the surgeon pass the rigid scope and push the object distally. In an adult with a supraglottic or glottic lesion: one optimised attempt, then scalpel cricothyroidotomy without delay, as the Difficult Airway Society guideline directs.
- Front-of-neck access that cannot work. Cricothyroidotomy sits below the cords and above everything else; it does nothing for a tracheal tumour, a mid-tracheal foreign body or a mediastinal mass.
- Negative-pressure pulmonary oedema. After relief of obstruction or after laryngospasm: frothy sputum and hypoxaemia within minutes to an hour, treated with positive pressure, oxygen and time.
- Button batteries. Oesophageal impaction is commoner than airway and is a time-critical emergency because of caustic injury; removal is by rigid oesophagoscopy with a tracheal tube in place.
- Oesophageal foreign body with stridor. A large object in the upper oesophagus compresses the trachea from behind in a small child; rapid sequence induction and a tube, not a spontaneous breathing technique.
- Croup and bacterial tracheitis. Subglottic, in the younger child; steroid and nebulised adrenaline work for croup, and intubation when needed is with a tube one or two sizes smaller. Tracheitis produces thick pus that blocks tubes.
- Angioedema. Swelling of tongue and larynx that can progress in minutes; treat the cause (adrenaline for anaphylaxis, C1 esterase inhibitor or icatibant for hereditary and ACE inhibitor types) and secure the airway early, awake, if the tongue is involved.
- Deep neck infection. Covered in [dental and neck abscess](dental-and-neck-abscess.html).
- Adult foreign body. Usually food or a dental fragment, in an older or neurologically impaired patient; flexible bronchoscopy through a laryngeal mask or tube retrieves most, with rigid bronchoscopy as backup. See [bronchoscopy, EBUS and interventional pulmonology](bronchoscopy-ebus-and-interventional-pulmonology.html).
- The general strategy for anticipated difficulty is in [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html), and paediatric equipment sizes and doses in [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).

## Key sources

Foreign body management rests on a literature review of case series and a meta-analysis of small trials; neither identifies a best technique, and preservation of spontaneous ventilation for proximal or uncertain obstruction is long-standing consensus, not a trial result. The adult epiglottitis figures are from a meta-analysis of observational studies with a separate summary of 128 individually described cases. The adult obstructed airway has no comparative evidence at all: a narrative review, a national audit of complications, guidelines for unanticipated difficulty and for awake intubation, and case series of awake tracheostomy. The level-by-level approach, the preference for intravenous over inhalational induction in adults, drug doses for nebulised adrenaline, steroid and topical lidocaine, and the paediatric epiglottitis sequence are consensus and author's judgement.
