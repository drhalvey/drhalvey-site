## What the surgeon is doing and why

The tubes and ovaries lie at the bottom of the pelvis behind the uterus, covered by small bowel. To operate on them through 5 mm ports the surgeon needs two things: a space, made by insufflating carbon dioxide, and an empty pelvis, made by tipping the patient head down so that the bowel slides towards the diaphragm. A manipulator clamped to the cervix lets an assistant lever the uterus forwards to expose what lies behind it. Those three things (gas, tilt and an instrument in the uterus) are common to every operation on this card. What differs is the pathology, and it runs from nothing at all to a litre and a half of free blood.

Diagnostic laparoscopy answers a question: why the pelvic pain, why the infertility. The surgeon inspects, may run dye through the cervix to see it spill from the tubes, may biopsy or ablate spots of endometriosis, and leaves. Sterilisation occludes each tube with a spring clip or, increasingly, removes both tubes (bilateral salpingectomy, which also removes the site where most high-grade serous ovarian cancers are thought to arise). A clip leaves a segment of living tube crushed and ischaemic, which is why clip sterilisation hurts more than its ten minutes suggest. Ovarian cystectomy strips a cyst wall out of the ovary and preserves the rest; oophorectomy divides the infundibulopelvic ligament, which carries the ovarian vessels and has the ureter beneath it. Torsion is an ovary twisted on that pedicle, venous outflow lost first and arterial inflow later: the surgeon untwists it, judges viability and fixes or removes the cause. Endometriosis excision peels diseased peritoneum off the pelvic side wall, the uterosacral ligaments, the bladder and the rectum, with the ureters dissected out to protect them; it is the long operation on this card and it is done on women who already have chronic pain.

An ectopic pregnancy is a gestation implanted in the tube, which cannot stretch to hold it. It bleeds into the tube wall, then through it. The operation is a salpingectomy (the tube and its pregnancy stapled or diathermied off the mesosalpinx) or, where the other tube is damaged, a salpingostomy that opens the tube and milks the pregnancy out. Unruptured, it is a 40-minute semi-elective case. Ruptured, it is intraperitoneal haemorrhage in a young woman with excellent compensation, and the operation is the resuscitation.

The pain logic is that of any short laparoscopy, plus whatever was done in the pelvis. Port wounds are sharp and localised. The peritoneum has been stretched and bathed in carbonic acid. The gas left under the diaphragm irritates the phrenic nerve and is felt at the shoulder tip, in up to 80% of women in the Cochrane review, typically worse on day 1 than on the day of surgery. The pelvic component varies: almost nothing after an inspection, a deep ischaemic cramp after clips, a visceral ache after cystectomy, and peritoneal irritation from blood after an ectopic. None of it is loaded by movement in the way a laparotomy wound is, so it peaks early and has largely gone by day 3. The 179-procedure cohort is the warning against treating it as trivial.

## Steps

### 1. Triage before the anaesthetic room
**Why:** The same booking ("laparoscopy, query ectopic") covers a stable woman with a 2 cm tubal mass and a woman with a belly full of blood. The surgeon decides the urgency on pain, ultrasound free fluid and trend.
**Anaesthesia:** Take your own view of the circulation. A young woman maintains systolic pressure by vasoconstriction and may be bradycardic, not tachycardic, with haemoperitoneum (a vagal response to blood on the peritoneum), so a normal pressure and a rate of 70 with pallor, shoulder-tip pain, dizziness on sitting or a distended abdomen is a ruptured ectopic that has not declared itself yet. A point-of-care haemoglobin is falsely reassuring in the first hour. For that patient: two large cannulae, blood requested through the haemorrhage protocol, the surgeon scrubbed and the abdomen prepared before induction. Do not delay theatre to "optimise"; the bleeding stops when the mesosalpinx is clamped.

### 2. Induction and airway
**Why:** The surgeon needs a relaxed abdominal wall, an empty stomach and bladder, and a patient who will not move while a trocar is being placed blind.
**Anaesthesia:** Elective: propofol, a short-acting opioid, rocuronium and a tracheal tube is the default because the patient will be head down with a pneumoperitoneum, and reflux, obesity and shared airway pressure all argue for it; a second-generation supraglottic device for a 15-minute sterilisation in a slim patient is common practice and defensible. An orogastric tube deflates the stomach if a left upper quadrant (Palmer's point) entry is planned. Give dexamethasone 8 mg IV, paracetamol 1 g and the NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor now. Shocked ectopic: rapid sequence induction with ketamine 1-1.5 mg/kg IV or a sharply reduced propofol dose, rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg IV, vasopressor in hand, and blood running. Torsion: severe pain and repeated vomiting mean a full stomach whatever the fasting time.

### 3. Positioning, bladder and uterine manipulator
**Why:** Low lithotomy gives vaginal access for the manipulator and room between the legs for an assistant; arms are tucked so the surgeon can stand at the shoulder; the bladder is emptied so it is out of the way of the suprapubic port.
**Anaesthesia:** Tucked arms mean no access to the cannula or the cuff once draped: check that the line runs, the cuff cycles and the pulse oximeter reads before the drapes go on, and pad the ulnar nerves. Legs in boots with the weight on the heels, not the calves or the fibular head. Placing the manipulator means a tenaculum on the cervix, which is a vagal stimulus under light anaesthesia. No manipulator is used when there is an intrauterine pregnancy to protect; the surgeon should say so, and the anaesthetist should know the pregnancy test result for every woman of reproductive age on the list.

### 4. Entry and pneumoperitoneum
**Why:** A Veress needle or an open cut-down at the umbilicus, or a needle at Palmer's point when adhesions are expected, then carbon dioxide to lift the abdominal wall off the aorta and iliac vessels before the first trocar goes in.
**Anaesthesia:** The umbilicus sits over the aortic bifurcation in a slim woman, and blind entry is the step with the catastrophic complication: a major vessel injury bleeds into the retroperitoneum with little in the view, and presents to you as hypotension, tachycardia and a falling end-tidal carbon dioxide within minutes of the first port. Say it early, because the response is a midline laparotomy and the haemorrhage protocol. Gas embolism (needle in a vein) looks similar with a more abrupt capnograph fall. Much more often, peritoneal stretch produces a sudden sinus bradycardia or a few seconds of asystole, especially in fit young women: the surgeon releases the gas, glycopyrrolate 200-400 micrograms IV or atropine 600 micrograms IV is given, and insufflation restarts slowly. In a hypovolaemic ectopic the pneumoperitoneum compresses the vena cava that the patient was relying on; keep volume going in and ask for the lowest pressure that gives a view.

### 5. Trendelenburg
**Why:** Gravity clears the small bowel from the pelvis; without it the adnexa cannot be seen.
**Anaesthesia:** Head-down tilt pushes the diaphragm and carina towards the tube tip, so re-check for endobronchial migration when the saturation drifts or airway pressure rises after the tilt. Compliance and functional residual capacity fall; pressure-controlled ventilation with 5-8 cmH2O of PEEP and a 20-30% rise in minute ventilation holds oxygenation and carbon dioxide in most patients. In an obese patient at 30 degrees, plateau pressures above 30 cmH2O are a reason to negotiate less tilt or less gas. The position supports the blood pressure of a bleeding patient while she is in it and removes that support when the table is levelled, so finish the resuscitation before the end of the case, not after.

### 6. Inspection, dye test and tubal surgery
**Why:** The pelvis and upper abdomen are inspected; dilute methylene blue may be injected through the cervix to demonstrate tubal patency; tubes are clipped across the isthmus or removed along the mesosalpinx with a sealing device.
**Anaesthesia:** These are the short cases, and the risk is finishing with a paralysed, opioid-loaded patient ten minutes after induction. Plan the relaxant for a 20-minute case and have sugammadex available. Clip sterilisation has a pain profile out of proportion to the surgery: the crushed tube is ischaemic and produces a deep bilateral cramp for several hours that the port-site local does not touch. Surgeons vary in dripping local anaesthetic on the tubes or injecting the mesosalpinx before clipping; either is worth requesting, and a longer-acting opioid dose before waking is justified for clips where it is not for a diagnostic inspection. Salpingectomy cuts the tube out and hurts less afterwards. Dye spilled into the peritoneum is absorbed slowly and rarely affects the pulse oximeter reading; a transient low reading with a good trace shortly after a dye test is the dye.

### 7. Cystectomy, oophorectomy, torsion and endometriosis
**Why:** The cyst is stripped from the ovarian cortex and removed in a bag; an ovary is removed by sealing the infundibulopelvic ligament; a torted ovary is untwisted; endometriotic peritoneum and nodules are excised with the ureters and rectum under direct vision.
**Anaesthesia:** A dermoid cyst that spills causes a chemical peritonitis, so the surgeon will lavage with litres of warmed saline: keep the patient warm and expect more pain. Untwisting a torted ovary is occasionally followed by transient hypotension as the congested pedicle reperfuses. Endometriosis excision is the outlier: two to four hours of head-down pneumoperitoneum (eyes, face and airway oedema, calf compression), ureteric stents or a cystoscopy at the end, sometimes a bowel shave or resection with a colorectal surgeon, and a patient who may arrive on regular opioids, gabapentinoids and hormone suppression. Treat that case as major surgery: arterial line by comorbidity, active warming, a planned overnight stay, and the opioid-tolerant plan in the pain section.

### 8. Ectopic pregnancy: clearing the blood and removing the tube
**Why:** The surgeon suctions the haemoperitoneum to find the tube, grasps it to stop the bleeding, and removes it. In unstable patients some surgeons go straight to a Pfannenstiel or lower midline laparotomy because it is the faster route to a clamp in their hands.
**Anaesthesia:** Control comes within minutes of the camera going in, and from that moment the task changes from keeping up to catching up. Until then accept a systolic of 80-90 mmHg in a conscious-before-induction patient rather than diluting her with crystalloid; after control, restore volume with blood. Transfuse on the clinical picture and the suction canister, not the first haemoglobin. Cell salvage of intraperitoneal blood in ectopic pregnancy is established practice where a service exists. Give TXA 1 g IV early. Keep her warm: a litre of room-temperature lavage and cold gas add to haemorrhagic hypothermia. A Rhesus-negative woman needs anti-D. If the surgeon converts to laparotomy, the pain mechanism becomes abdominal wall and the plan in the pain section changes with it.

### 9. Deflation and the shoulder-tip measures
**Why:** The pelvis is washed, haemostasis checked at low pressure, the ports removed under vision and the gas let out.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step where the anaesthetist and surgeon between them decide tomorrow's shoulder pain. With the patient still head down and the port valves open, give several slow manual inflations to 30-40 cmH2O, holding each for about five seconds, while the surgeon presses gently on the abdomen: the lungs push the bubble from under the diaphragm towards the open ports. The Cochrane review found that a specific technique for releasing the gas reduced shoulder pain severity at 24 hours and analgesic use, and a later meta-analysis of 14 trials found a reduction of about 1.5 points out of 10 at 24 hours. Leaving saline in the upper abdomen also reduced incidence and severity; subdiaphragmatic local anaesthetic and warmed humidified gas did not clearly help. Skip the manoeuvre in the patient with bullous lung disease or an unstable circulation. Port-site infiltration goes in now, into fascia and peritoneum at the umbilicus, not just skin. Reverse against a quantitative monitor.

### 10. Emergence, recovery and discharge
**Why:** The surgeon expects a same-day discharge for everything elective on this card.
**Anaesthesia:** Nausea is the commonest cause of unplanned admission: female, non-smoking, laparoscopic, pelvic surgery and postoperative opioid put most of these patients at high risk before any history of motion sickness is added. Pain in recovery is port-site (responds to fentanyl 25-50 microgram IV increments) and visceral pelvic cramp (responds to the NSAID already given and to time). Pain that is severe, diffuse and not settling, with tachycardia, is bleeding or a visceral injury until the surgeon has reviewed. Discharge with regular paracetamol and an NSAID for three to five days, a small supply of oral opioid, and the explanation that shoulder pain on day 1 is gas and improves on lying flat.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is ports plus peritoneum plus a variable pelvic visceral component, early and short. No procedure-specific PROSPECT review exists for short gynaecological laparoscopy; the nearest is the laparoscopic hysterectomy guideline, which recommends paracetamol, an NSAID and dexamethasone as the baseline with opioid for rescue and found the evidence for TAP blocks inconsistent. The same shape applies here with more force, because the pelvic wound is smaller.

So the block is the strategy as a whole. Before incision: paracetamol 1 g, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor (parecoxib 40 mg IV is the common choice), dexamethasone 8 mg IV. During: short-acting opioid, with a modest longer-acting dose before the end for clip sterilisation, cystectomy and ectopic. At the end: the gas-release technique and the surgeon's port infiltration (20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% or bupivacaine 0.25% divided between the ports, within the weight-based maximum). Afterwards: regular paracetamol and NSAID for three to five days with oral opioid for breakthrough on days 0 and 1.

Three patients need more. The chronic pelvic pain or endometriosis patient, often opioid tolerant and with a sensitised nervous system, does badly on the standard plan: the author's practice is a bilateral TAP block, ketamine 0.25-0.5 mg/kg IV at induction, continuation of all her usual medication on the day, and a booked bed. That is judgement, not trial evidence. The patient who cannot have an NSAID (renal impairment, a bleeding ectopic until haemostasis is certain) has lost the main anti-inflammatory arm, and a TAP block is the reasonable substitute. The ectopic converted to laparotomy has an abdominal wall wound: wound infiltration or bilateral TAP or quadratus lumborum blocks before waking, a PCA, and the plan from the [abdominal hysterectomy](abdominal-hysterectomy-and-myomectomy.html) card on a smaller scale.

Rebound is not a feature because no dense block is wearing off. Escalation triggers: pain that worsens after day 1 instead of improving, fever, distension, vomiting or new shoulder pain on day 2 or 3 suggest a bowel or ureteric injury, a port-site hernia or a haematoma, and belong to the surgeon the same day. After salpingostomy, persistent trophoblast can bleed days later.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The compensated ectopic. Normal pressure, slow or normal pulse, pale, uncomfortable lying flat. She decompensates at induction. Blood in the room, surgeon scrubbed, reduced-dose induction, and no waiting for results.
- Laparotomy instead of laparoscopy. A surgeon's choice for the collapsed patient or the hostile abdomen. Opening a tense haemoperitoneum releases tamponade and the pressure falls again: warn the surgeon you are ready before the peritoneum is incised.
- Heterotopic and early intrauterine pregnancy. Adnexal surgery for torsion or a cyst with a wanted intrauterine pregnancy is covered on the [non-obstetric surgery in pregnancy](cervical-cerclage-and-non-obstetric-surgery.html) card: no manipulator, left tilt once the uterus is large enough to matter, low insufflation pressure and normocapnia.
- Vascular and bowel injury at entry. Rare and yours to recognise. Unexplained hypotension after the first trocar is a vessel until proven otherwise; bowel injury presents late, on day 2 or 3, as the patient who is getting worse instead of better.
- Capnothorax and subcutaneous emphysema. Rising end-tidal carbon dioxide with rising airway pressure and desaturation in a head-down patient is gas in the pleura or endobronchial migration of the tube; check the tube first, then ask for the gas to be released.
- Tubo-ovarian abscess and pelvic sepsis. A septic patient having a drainage procedure in a position that hides hypovolaemia: fluid and antibiotics before induction, noradrenaline available, and a higher-acuity bed afterwards.
- Postpartum sterilisation. A mini-laparotomy at the umbilicus within a day or two of delivery is usually done under spinal anaesthesia; the uterus is still large, the patient is treated as a full stomach if general anaesthesia is needed, and it belongs with the obstetric cards.
- The long head-down case in an obese patient. Facial and airway oedema, sliding on the table, brachial plexus stretch and high airway pressures; a leak test or a look before extubation after several hours.
- Unplanned admission. Nausea, pain after clips, a late finish and urinary retention are the usual causes; triple prophylaxis and the gas-release manoeuvre are the two things that change it.

## Key sources

The shoulder-tip pain evidence is a Cochrane review of 32 trials with low to moderate quality evidence and a later meta-analysis with high heterogeneity; the direction of effect is consistent and the manoeuvre is free, which is why it is recommended here. The baseline analgesic regimen is borrowed from the PROSPECT review of laparoscopic hysterectomy because there is no procedure-specific guideline for short gynaecological laparoscopy. The management of the ruptured ectopic (permissive hypotension until control, transfusion on clinical grounds, cell salvage, TXA) is standard haemorrhage practice applied to this setting and is unsourced on this card; TXA in particular is an extrapolation. The statements about clip pain, the opioid-tolerant endometriosis patient and the use of TAP blocks are the author's judgement.
