## What the surgeon is doing and why

Benign prostatic hyperplasia is an adenoma growing in the transition zone of the prostate, inside a compressed shell of peripheral zone that surgeons call the capsule. The adenoma narrows and lengthens the prostatic urethra, the bladder works against the obstruction until it either thickens and becomes irritable or stretches and fails, and the man presents with a poor stream, retention, infections, stones or obstructed kidneys. Every operation on this card removes adenoma from inside the urethra and leaves the capsule, the external sphincter just beyond the verumontanum, and the neurovascular bundles outside the capsule alone.

There are two ways to take the adenoma out.

Resection (TURP) carves it away from the inside in chips with a diathermy loop, working outwards until capsule fibres appear. It is quick and suits glands up to about 80 mL. Its weakness is that the loop cuts across the venous sinuses of the gland and leaves them open to pressurised irrigant for the rest of the resection. Monopolar current needs an irrigant with no electrolytes, classically 1.5% glycine, which is hypotonic; bipolar current returns through the loop itself and runs in saline.

Enucleation (holmium or thulium laser, sometimes bipolar) finds the plane between adenoma and capsule at the apex and peels each lobe off the capsule whole, as a finger did in the open operation, pushing the lobes into the bladder. A morcellator then sucks them up and minces them. It removes more tissue, seals vessels at the capsule as it goes, and has no size limit, which is why it has displaced open prostatectomy for very large glands. Its costs are time, a learning curve, and a second instrument with blades working inside the bladder. Vaporisation techniques ablate tissue without removing it and sit at the minor end.

For you the operation is defined by three things that have nothing to do with incisions. First, an open venous bed under a column of irrigating fluid, so fluid goes into the patient: a mild absorption of 1-2 L happens in 5-10% of resections and is easily missed. Second, bleeding you cannot see or measure, diluted in litres of irrigant. Third, the population: men in their seventies and eighties with coronary disease, atrial fibrillation, anticoagulants, chronic kidney impairment from back-pressure, and brains that tolerate neither hyponatraemia nor sedation.

The pain logic is simple. Nothing somatic is cut. The pain generators are a raw prostatic fossa, a 20-24 Fr three-way catheter with a 30 mL balloon pulled down onto the bladder neck, and the detrusor spasm both provoke. It feels like an unbearable need to void, not like a wound, and opioids do little for it. That is a mucosal mechanism at a score of 2, and the analgesic plan is mostly not analgesics.

## Steps

### 1. The patient, the antithrombotics and the urine
**Why:** The surgeon chooses the technique partly on gland size and partly on whether the patient can stop anticoagulation. Men in retention have had a catheter for weeks and their urine is colonised.
**Anaesthesia:** Confirm what was stopped and when, since it decides whether a spinal is available. Laser enucleation is offered to men who cannot stop antithrombotics, but the pooled data are plain that it bleeds more when they continue: transfusion and clot tamponade were several times commoner than in men off treatment. Check sodium, creatinine and haemoglobin; a man with chronic retention and a creatinine of 250 has little reserve for fluid absorption or NSAIDs. Culture-directed antibiotics go in before instrumentation, because the first pass of the scope through a colonised urethra is a bacteraemia. Ask which energy and which irrigant.

### 2. Spinal or general anaesthesia
**Why:** The surgeon needs a still pelvis and a relaxed pelvic floor for 30-180 minutes. Either technique delivers that.
**Anaesthesia:** A spinal to T10 is the traditional default and the author's choice for resection: it covers bladder distension and the prostate, it leaves an awake patient who reports the early symptoms of absorption (nausea, restlessness, visual blurring, headache) and the shoulder-tip pain of a perforation, and it provides analgesia into recovery. Keep the block no higher than it needs to be, since a block to T6 abolishes the abdominal pain that announces a capsular perforation and vasodilates an old man with a fixed cardiac output. Sedation should be light enough to preserve those warnings or it throws the advantage away. No trial shows a difference in mortality or major morbidity between spinal and general anaesthesia for this operation; the argument for spinal is monitoring, and it is strongest with glycine. General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway is usual for long enucleations, for patients on antithrombotics, and for those who cannot lie still. Avoid intrathecal morphine: retention and pruritus in a man with a catheter and a trial of void ahead.

### 3. Lithotomy and irrigation set-up
**Why:** The scope needs a straight line through the urethra and continuous flow to see through blood.
**Anaesthesia:** Arthritic hips, hip replacements and fixed spines limit the position; move both legs together and accept less flexion. The irrigant bag height sets the driving pressure: about 60 cm above the pubis is enough, and every extra 10 cm increases absorption. Litres of fluid run over the patient and through the bladder; warm it and use forced air above the waist, because elderly men under a spinal cool fast and shivering makes resection unsafe. In head-down lithotomy venous return rises and then falls abruptly when the legs come down at the end with a sympathetic block still present.

### 4. Cystoscopy and landmarks
**Why:** The surgeon identifies the ureteric orifices, the bladder neck and the verumontanum. The verumontanum is the distal limit: the external sphincter lies immediately beyond it, and every cut stays proximal.
**Anaesthesia:** Bladder distension through a patchy block produces discomfort, hypertension or a vagal bradycardia. Under general anaesthesia a penile erection from stimulation at a light plane obstructs the scope; deepen and it resolves.

### 5. Resection of the adenoma (TURP)
**Why:** Chips are cut from bladder neck to verumontanum, lobe by lobe, until the transverse fibres of the capsule show. The surgeon coagulates arteries as they are cut but cannot close venous sinuses, which gape and take in irrigant whenever intravesical pressure exceeds venous pressure.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the absorption phase and the clock matters: risk rises with resection time (the traditional limit is 60 minutes), with gland size, with capsular breach and with bag height. With glycine, absorbed fluid is hypotonic: dilutional hyponatraemia, falling osmolality, cerebral oedema, plus the effects of glycine itself (transient visual loss, encephalopathy from ammonia). The early picture is a rise in pressure with a slowing pulse from the volume load, then nausea, confusion, restlessness and visual symptoms in the awake patient, and under general anaesthesia nothing until hypotension, bradycardia or a slow wake-up. If you suspect it, tell the surgeon, who coagulates and stops; measure sodium; and treat on symptoms and number together. A symptomatic patient with a sodium below 120 mmol/L gets hypertonic saline in small boluses (3% saline 100-150 mL IV, with the sodium rechecked after each), which is the treatment the pharmacology supports; a diuretic alone worsens the sodium loss. The boluses have a stopping rule: the aim is to reverse cerebral oedema, not to restore a normal number, so stop when the neurological symptoms resolve, which takes a rise of only a few mmol/L. After that the correction is slow. The absorbed water is excreted over the following hours and the sodium climbs without help, so further hypertonic saline risks overshoot, and the total rise in the first 24 hours is kept within the limit set by the local hyponatraemia guideline because too fast a rise risks osmotic demyelination. Recheck the sodium every hour or two until it is stable, and manage the patient in a monitored bed. With bipolar resection in saline the same absorption is volume overload and hyperchloraemic acidosis without the brain: the Cochrane review of 59 trials found bipolar cut TUR syndrome by about five-sixths and transfusion by more than half. Bleeding is estimated from the colour of the outflow, the heart rate and a point-of-care haemoglobin, not from the suction bottle.

### 6. Enucleation of the lobes (laser alternative to step 5)
**Why:** The laser opens the plane at the apex and the surgeon uses the scope beak as a finger to shell each lobe off the capsule towards the bladder, coagulating perforators at their origin. Because vessels are sealed at the capsule before the adenoma is detached, the bed is drier than a resected one.
**Anaesthesia:** Long, steady, low-stimulus work in saline. The problems are duration (hypothermia, lithotomy time, a large cumulative irrigant volume with the possibility of saline overload in a man with a poor ventricle) and the occasional capsular perforation with extravasation into the retroperitoneum. Pooled randomised trials give enucleation less hyponatraemia, a smaller haemoglobin drop and fewer transfusions than resection, at the cost of a longer operation.

### 7. Morcellation
**Why:** The enucleated lobes float in the bladder and are too large to pull out. A morcellator with reciprocating blades and suction is passed through a nephroscope and minces them.
**Anaesthesia:** The one step where a general anaesthetic patient must not move or cough at all. The bladder is kept fully distended so its wall stays away from the blades; if the wall is sucked in, the result is a bladder perforation and a laparotomy. Make sure the patient is deep or the spinal is still dense before it starts, and tell the surgeon if the block is receding. If the inflow runs dry the bladder collapses onto the blade, so the irrigation bags are everyone's business.

### 8. Haemostasis, three-way catheter and irrigation
**Why:** The surgeon coagulates the fossa, evacuates chips or fragments, and places a large three-way catheter with the balloon in the bladder, sometimes on traction against the bladder neck to tamponade the fossa. Saline irrigation runs continuously to stop clot forming.
**Anaesthesia:** Bringing the legs down returns blood to vasodilated legs: expect a pressure drop and have a vasopressor in hand. This is the time for paracetamol IV; an NSAID is added only if kidney function and the surgeon's view of bleeding allow, and for most men nothing more is needed. The catheter balloon on the trigone is what the patient will feel first on waking or as the spinal recedes.

### 9. Recovery
**Why:** The surgeon wants clear or rose-coloured outflow and a comfortable patient with irrigation running freely.
**Anaesthesia:** Four problems, in order of frequency. Bladder spasm and catheter discomfort: an urgent, cramping need to void in an agitated man; check that the catheter is draining before treating it as pain, then give an antimuscarinic (hyoscine butylbromide 20 mg IV in recovery, or oxybutynin 2.5-5 mg orally once the patient is drinking, at the lower dose in the oldest men because of delirium); in pooled trials a pudendal or dorsal penile nerve block reduced catheter discomfort for the first 8-12 hours, which is an option for the patient who has had it badly before. A blocked catheter: irrigation running in and not out distends the bladder, and under a receding spinal that presents as lower abdominal pain, hypertension, bradycardia and sweating; the treatment is a bladder washout, not labetalol. Bleeding: outflow that stays dark red despite fast irrigation, with tachycardia, needs the surgeon and a haemoglobin. Late TUR syndrome: absorption from a perivesical collection continues after the case, so confusion or seizure in recovery after a glycine resection is a sodium until proved otherwise.

### 10. Ward and trial without catheter
**Why:** Irrigation is weaned as the urine clears and the catheter removed on day 1-2 after resection, often earlier after enucleation.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing is needed from you unless the patient returns: clot retention is the commonest reason, and the man who comes back to theatre for evacuation of clot has been bleeding for hours, is anaemic and hypovolaemic behind a normal-looking pressure, and has a full bladder. Resuscitate first, cross-match, and choose general anaesthesia if any antithrombotic has been restarted.

## Pain plan

The analgesic strategy is the spinal for the day of surgery and treatment of the bladder afterwards. Regular paracetamol is the base. NSAIDs work well on detrusor spasm but are often excluded by kidney function, age and a fresh raw fossa, and the surgeon's preference rules. Antimuscarinics are the specific treatment for catheter-related discomfort and spasm, with the usual caution about delirium in the oldest patients. Opioids are a poor match for this pain and a good way to produce confusion and constipation, which makes the patient strain and bleed; a few small doses are reasonable, a patient-controlled pump is not. Pain scores of 2-3 with the catheter in and 1-2 after it comes out are typical, with stinging on voiding for one to two weeks while the fossa re-epithelialises. Enucleation patients report more early dysuria than resection patients in the pooled trials.

No catheter technique and no truncal block applies. The pudendal or dorsal penile nerve block for catheter discomfort is supported by a meta-analysis of six small trials; the author's judgement is that it is worth keeping for the patient with a history of severe catheter intolerance and not as routine.

Escalation triggers: severe suprapubic pain is a blocked catheter or clot retention until the bladder has been washed out; abdominal pain with distension and shoulder-tip pain is perforation with extravasation; new confusion is sodium, sepsis or retention before it is opioid.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Monopolar in glycine against bipolar or laser in saline. The single most important fact to establish at the briefing. Hyponatraemic TUR syndrome is a monopolar complication; saline absorption still overloads a failing heart. When hypertonic saline is needed, it is stopped when symptoms resolve and the sodium is then allowed to rise slowly.
- TUR syndrome under general anaesthesia. No symptoms to report. Unexplained hypertension with bradycardia, then hypotension, ECG changes, or failure to wake: send a sodium.
- Capsular and bladder perforation. Extraperitoneal extravasation gives periumbilical or suprapubic pain and a tense lower abdomen; intraperitoneal gives shoulder-tip pain, nausea and a rigid abdomen. A high block hides both.
- The emergency variant. Clot retention and secondary haemorrhage (typically at one to two weeks when the eschar separates), and urosepsis after instrumentation of infected urine. These are the cases that need large-bore access, blood and occasionally critical care; the elective operation almost never does.
- Antithrombotics. Know the plan for restarting; a spinal on the day does not license a therapeutic dose that evening.
- Severe aortic stenosis or a poor ventricle. A carefully titrated general anaesthetic with an arterial line is as defensible as a spinal; what the patient cannot tolerate is a litre of absorbed fluid, so ask for bipolar or laser and a short resection.
- Pacemakers and defibrillators. Monopolar diathermy low in the pelvis with a return plate on the thigh; bipolar avoids most of the problem.
- Chronic retention. Post-obstructive diuresis and a creatinine that will improve; avoid nephrotoxins.
- Prostate cancer channel resection. Same operation, friable tissue, more bleeding, sometimes fibrinolysis; this is where tranexamic acid has its best case.
- Hypothermia. Large volumes of room-temperature irrigant in an elderly man under a spinal; warm the fluid.

## Key sources

Fluid absorption is well described: the 2006 review remains the reference for mechanism, monitoring and treatment, and the Cochrane review settles that bipolar resection in saline makes TUR syndrome and transfusion rarer without changing urinary outcome. The enucleation literature consists of urological meta-analyses of modest trials that agree on less bleeding and longer operating time. The choice between spinal and general anaesthesia rests on reasoning about monitoring, not on outcome trials, and is the author's judgement. There is no PROSPECT guideline for transurethral prostate surgery; the catheter-discomfort block evidence is from small trials in mixed urological surgery.
