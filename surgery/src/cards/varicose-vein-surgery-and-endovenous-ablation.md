## What the surgeon is doing and why

Varicose veins are a plumbing fault with one cause. The valves in a superficial trunk, usually the great saphenous vein from groin to ankle and less often the small saphenous vein from the back of the knee to the ankle, stop closing. Blood that should drain up into the deep system at the groin falls back down the trunk under gravity, and the pressure of that column distends the tributaries into visible varicosities and, in time, damages the skin at the ankle. The surgeon's aim is to take the incompetent trunk out of circulation so that the column no longer exists, and then to deal with the bulging tributaries.

There are three ways to abolish the trunk and they produce different tissue injury.

Thermal ablation destroys the vein from inside. Under ultrasound the vein is punctured at the knee or calf, a laser fibre or radiofrequency catheter is advanced to just below the saphenofemoral junction, and energy is delivered as the device is withdrawn, heating the wall until it contracts and occludes. The vein stays in the leg and is resorbed over months. Before any heat is applied, the operator injects tumescent fluid around the whole length of the vein within its fascial envelope. That fluid does three jobs: it anaesthetises the vein, it compresses the vein onto the catheter, and it is a heat sink protecting skin and the saphenous nerve. Tumescent infiltration is therefore part of the operation, not an anaesthetic option, and is given even when the patient is asleep.

Open surgery removes the vein. Through a groin crease incision the saphenofemoral junction is dissected and tied flush with the femoral vein, with its tributaries divided. A stripper is passed down the trunk to the knee and the vein is pulled out, inverting on itself, tearing its tributaries as it goes. The track fills with blood.

Foam sclerotherapy injects a detergent foam that strips the endothelium chemically. It needs only needle punctures, is the least painful and has the highest rate of the trunk reopening.

Whichever is used for the trunk, the tributaries are treated by phlebectomy (hook avulsion through 2-3 mm stab incisions, sometimes twenty or more per leg) or by foam.

The trial evidence says the methods are clinically close. In the CLASS trial of 798 patients, quality of life at six months was similar after laser, foam and surgery, complications were fewest after laser, and foam closed the trunk less reliably. Guidelines now favour thermal ablation, and open stripping is reserved for veins that are too tortuous or too large to catheterise, for recurrent disease in the groin, and for units without the equipment.

The pain logic differs by method and the evidence here is good. Stripping and avulsion cause immediate bruising pain along the thigh that is worst in the first three days and fades with the bruise. Thermal ablation causes little immediate pain, because the tumescent block is still working, and then a delayed inflammatory pain: a tight, pulling cord along the medial thigh as the heated vein wall becomes a sterile phlebitis. In a randomised comparison with both operations done under tumescent anaesthesia, patients had more pain after laser than after stripping on days 7, 10 and 14. Radiofrequency is consistently less painful than the older bare-fibre lasers: mean pain over ten days was 22 mm against 34 mm in one trial, and 1.21 against 2.58 on a ten-point scale in another, with stripping at 2.25. The score of 2 on this card covers all methods; the shape of the curve depends on which was used, and the trajectory given has a flat tail to day 7 for that reason.

## Steps

### 1. Marking, risk assessment and choice of anaesthetic
**Why:** The surgeon marks the veins with the patient standing, because they empty and vanish when the patient lies down.
**Anaesthesia:** The choice is driven by extent and by the patient. Unilateral endovenous ablation with a modest number of phlebectomies is comfortably done under tumescent anaesthesia alone or with light sedation, and in many centres without an anaesthetist. General anaesthesia is reasonable for open stripping, bilateral procedures, extensive avulsions, redo groin surgery, needle-phobic patients and prone small saphenous surgery in a patient who cannot lie prone awake. The patients are mostly well, often women of working age, with obesity the commonest comorbidity. Assess thromboembolic risk yourself: previous thrombosis, thrombophilia, oestrogen therapy, obesity and a long bilateral procedure are the triggers for pharmacological prophylaxis.

### 2. Positioning
**Why:** The vein must be full to be cannulated and empty to be ablated or stripped. The table is tilted head up for access and head down for treatment. The small saphenous vein requires access to the back of the knee.
**Anaesthesia:** For the great saphenous vein the patient is supine with the hip abducted and externally rotated and the knee flexed. Head-down tilt of 15-30 degrees in an obese patient under general anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway raises airway pressure and reflux risk: choose the airway with the tilt in mind. For the small saphenous vein the patient is prone or lateral. Under general anaesthesia that means either induction and airway placement supine followed by turning, or, in a slim patient for a short procedure, self-positioning prone with a supraglottic airway placed in that position by an anaesthetist experienced in it; a tracheal tube is the conventional choice. A short-acting spinal avoids the question.

### 3. Vein access and catheter placement (endovenous)
**Why:** The vein is punctured under ultrasound at the lowest point of reflux, a sheath placed, and the laser fibre or radiofrequency catheter advanced until its tip is 2 cm below the saphenofemoral junction.
**Anaesthesia:** A cold, anxious, fasted patient has constricted veins that are hard to puncture: warm the patient and the room, avoid vasoconstrictors before access, and allow clear fluids until shortly before the procedure. Under sedation keep the patient responsive, since the operator relies on them to report heat.

### 4. Tumescent infiltration
**Why:** Under ultrasound the operator injects dilute local anaesthetic into the saphenous fascial compartment along the entire treated length, until the vein is surrounded by a 1 cm halo of fluid and lies more than 1 cm from the skin.
**Anaesthesia:** Multiple needle passes along the thigh are the most uncomfortable part of an awake procedure; this is when a short-acting opioid or a brief deepening of sedation helps. The solution is typically lidocaine 0.05-0.1% with adrenaline and sodium bicarbonate in saline, and 250-500 mL per leg is usual. Record the volume and concentration and convert to mg/kg. Absorption from tumescent infiltration is slow and peaks hours later, after discharge, which is reassuring for the procedure and a reason not to add a large dose of another amide local anaesthetic on top. Under general anaesthesia the same infiltration is still required for its heat-sink function.

### 5. Thermal ablation
**Why:** Energy is delivered as the device is withdrawn: continuously for laser, in 7 cm segments of 20 s for segmental radiofrequency.
**Anaesthesia:** With adequate tumescence the patient feels nothing or mild warmth. Sharp or burning pain during energy delivery means the tumescence is inadequate at that level, and it is the patient's report that prevents a skin burn or a saphenous nerve injury: the operator stops and adds fluid. Deep sedation and general anaesthesia remove that warning, which is one reason operators prefer a conscious patient. Laser eye protection applies to everyone in the room, including the patient.

### 6. Groin dissection and saphenofemoral ligation (open surgery)
**Why:** Through a 4-6 cm groin crease incision the saphenofemoral junction is exposed, every tributary divided, and the saphenous vein tied flush with the common femoral vein to prevent recurrence from a residual stump.
**Anaesthesia:** Under general anaesthesia this needs moderate depth and no neuromuscular block. Bleeding from a torn tributary or the femoral vein itself is the only source of significant haemorrhage in this operation; it is controlled by pressure and head-down tilt, and is the reason redo groins are done by experienced surgeons. Ask for infiltration of the groin wound before closure.

### 7. Stripping
**Why:** The stripper is passed from the groin to an exit incision just below the knee, the vein is tied to it and pulled out. Stripping stops at the knee because below it the saphenous nerve lies against the vein.
**Anaesthesia:** A brief, intense stimulus. Head-down tilt and immediate compression of the track limit the haematoma. Some surgeons infiltrate tumescent solution along the track beforehand, which reduces both bleeding and post-operative pain and should be added to the local anaesthetic count.

### 8. Phlebectomy
**Why:** The marked tributaries are hooked out through stab incisions and avulsed in segments.
**Anaesthesia:** Repetitive moderate stimulation over 15-30 min, each avulsion a tug. Under local anaesthesia every site needs its own infiltration, and this is where the lidocaine total climbs. Avulsions around the ankle and lateral calf risk the saphenous and sural nerves; patches of numbness afterwards are common and usually temporary.

### 9. Compression, emergence and discharge
**Why:** Bandages or stockings are applied from foot to groin before the leg is lowered, to compress the treated vein and the avulsion tracks.
**Anaesthesia:** Wake the patient with the bandages already on. Give paracetamol and an NSAID before or during the procedure so that they are working on arrival in recovery. Walking within the hour is both the thromboprophylaxis and the discharge criterion, so avoid anything that weakens the quadriceps. Discharge with regular paracetamol, an NSAID for 7-10 days if tolerated, and an explanation of the second-week phlebitic pain after thermal ablation so that it does not produce an emergency attendance.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is subcutaneous: a bruised track after stripping, a heated and inflamed vein after ablation, and multiple small stab wounds. The analgesic strategy is local anaesthetic infiltration by the operator plus regular paracetamol and an NSAID, which is sufficient for nearly every patient and matches the day-case requirement to walk immediately.

For endovenous ablation, tumescent anaesthesia gives several hours of analgesia. The NSAID matters more here than after most minor surgery, because the delayed pain is inflammatory: a 7-10 day course of ibuprofen or a similar agent is standard advice and treats the phlebitic cord of the second week. Radiofrequency ablation and modern long-wavelength, radial-tip laser fibres hurt less than the bare-fibre 810-980 nm lasers used in the comparative trials, so the laser figures quoted on this card are likely to overstate current practice; that qualification is the author's reading, since the cited trials used the older fibres.

For open stripping and extensive phlebectomy under general anaesthesia, ask the surgeon to infiltrate the groin wound and the stab sites, give intravenous paracetamol and an NSAID, and use short-acting opioid intra-operatively. A few doses of oral opioid may be supplied for the first two or three nights; most patients do not use them. In the four-arm randomised trial the mean pain after stripping was 2.25 out of 10 and time to normal function four days.

Nerve blocks have a narrow role. A femoral nerve block gives excellent analgesia for the thigh but produces quadriceps weakness that prevents safe walking and same-day discharge, so the author does not use it. A saphenous block in the adductor canal is motor-sparing but covers only the medial leg below the knee, where little of the pain is. A short-acting spinal with prilocaine or chloroprocaine is a good anaesthetic for prone or bilateral surgery and contributes nothing to post-operative analgesia.

Rebound is low. Escalation triggers: calf pain and swelling is deep vein thrombosis until scanned, and thermal ablation carries its own variant, thrombus extending from the treated vein into the femoral vein, found on routine duplex. A hot, red, tender cord along the thigh in week two is the expected phlebitis and is treated with NSAID and compression. A burning or numb patch at the medial ankle or lateral foot is saphenous or sural neuropraxia. Skin burns are rare and present as a painful blister over a superficial segment.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Local anaesthetic toxicity. The pitfall of this operation. Tumescent solution is dilute but the volume is large; bilateral procedures double it; surgeons add plain lidocaine or bupivacaine to stab wounds. Agree one person to keep the total, use the dilute solution for the stab sites as well, and keep lipid emulsion available wherever tumescent anaesthesia is used.
- Small saphenous vein. Prone or lateral position. The sural nerve and the tibial and common peroneal nerves are close to the saphenopopliteal junction, so foot drop or sensory change afterwards is a surgical complication to report, not a block effect, unless a block was done.
- Bilateral surgery. Longer, more local anaesthetic, more bruising and a higher thrombosis risk. General anaesthesia or a spinal is usual, with pharmacological prophylaxis.
- Redo groin surgery. Scar tissue around the femoral vein, a longer operation, real bleeding risk, antibiotic prophylaxis and general anaesthesia.
- Foam sclerotherapy. Usually an office procedure without an anaesthetist. Transient visual disturbance, migraine and, rarely, neurological events are reported after foam and are attributed to bubbles crossing a patent foramen ovale.
- Non-thermal, non-tumescent ablation. Cyanoacrylate glue and mechanochemical ablation need only a puncture-site injection, cause little procedural pain and avoid the tumescent volume; glue can produce a delayed inflammatory reaction along the vein.
- Obesity. Veins lie deep, tumescent volumes are larger, positioning is harder, and thrombosis risk is higher.
- Venous ulcer patients. Older, with comorbidity and limited mobility; endovenous ablation under local anaesthesia alone suits them, and early ablation is the treatment of the ulcer, not an optional extra.
- Anticoagulated patients. Thermal ablation can be performed without stopping anticoagulation in many units; open surgery cannot. A spinal is then excluded and tumescent local anaesthesia is the technique.
- The emergency variant. There is almost none. Bleeding from a varix at the ankle is managed with elevation and pressure and later ablation. The emergency within the operation is femoral vein injury during groin dissection: head-down tilt, pressure, large-bore access and a vascular surgeon.

## Key sources

Varicose vein treatment is unusually well served by randomised trials that measured post-operative pain directly. They agree that all methods give similar quality of life, that radiofrequency is less painful than the laser fibres of that era, and that laser pain is delayed into the second week. Their limitation is that technology has moved on from bare-fibre 810-980 nm lasers. There is no PROSPECT guideline. Evidence on anaesthetic technique itself is thin, because most trials standardised on tumescent local anaesthesia; the advice against femoral block and the emphasis on local anaesthetic arithmetic are the author's judgement.
