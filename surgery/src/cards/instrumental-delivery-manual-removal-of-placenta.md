## What the surgeon is doing and why

Three short operations share this card because they share a patient (a woman in or just after the second stage), a position (lithotomy), a block (low neuraxial) and a hazard (bleeding that has started before you are called). They differ in what the obstetrician's hands are doing, and that sets the block height.

Instrumental delivery. When the second stage stalls or the fetus is compromised with the head low in the pelvis, the obstetrician can complete the birth with a vacuum cup or forceps instead of a caesarean. A low or outlet lift-out is done in the delivery room under a pudendal block or the existing epidural. A mid-cavity or rotational delivery (the head is higher, or lies transverse or posterior and must be turned with Kielland's forceps, a rotational vacuum or the hand) has a real chance of failing, so it is run as a trial in theatre with the caesarean set open. The instrument applies traction along the pelvic curve during contractions while the mother pushes. The cost is tissue: an episiotomy is usual with forceps, the vaginal walls are stretched and bruised by the blades, and the anal sphincter tears more often than after a spontaneous birth.

Manual removal of placenta. After the baby, the uterus should shear the placenta off its wall and expel it within 30 minutes with active management. If it does not, either the placenta has separated and is trapped behind a closing cervix, or it is still adherent. Meanwhile the uterus cannot contract around it, so the placental bed bleeds. The obstetrician passes a hand through the cervix, finds the edge of the placenta, and sweeps it off the wall with the fingers while the other hand steadies the fundus through the abdomen. The cavity is then checked for fragments. A hand at the fundus stimulates visceral afferents well above the pelvis, which is why this needs a higher block than its perineal route suggests.

Perineal repair. Tears are graded by depth. First and second degree tears (skin, then perineal muscle) and episiotomies are sutured in the room. A third degree tear involves the anal sphincter complex (3a less than half the external sphincter thickness, 3b more than half, 3c into the internal sphincter) and a fourth degree tear opens the anal or rectal mucosa. These are repaired in theatre because the surgeon needs light, an assistant and a relaxed sphincter: the torn ends of the external sphincter retract into the ischioanal fat and have to be retrieved and brought together without tension, in layers, from the mucosa outward. A repair that fails means faecal incontinence in a young woman.

The pain logic is perineal. Sutured mucosa, skin and muscle in an oedematous bed are pressed on by sitting and stretched by defaecation, so pain is moderate, peaks on day 1-2 as the swelling peaks, and has a tail to the first comfortable bowel motion. It is far less than an abdominal wound, and manual removal on its own leaves nothing beyond afterpains.

## Steps

### 1. The call: which operation, and how much blood
**Why:** The obstetrician is moving to theatre because the room has run out of analgesia, light or safety margin.
**Anaesthesia:** Establish which of the three it is, whether the baby is still inside, and the measured blood loss. Then look at the woman rather than the monitor: pallor, a heart rate above the systolic pressure, air hunger and anxiety mean hypovolaemia whatever the cuff reads. Cannula, bloods and a fluid bolus before transfer. For retained placenta and for any tear with brisk bleeding, give TXA 1 g IV now: the trial that established it showed the benefit only when it was given within three hours of birth.

### 2. Trial of instrumental delivery: set up as a caesarean
**Why:** The national guideline advises that an attempt with a higher chance of failure is made where an immediate caesarean can follow, and that the attempt is abandoned when there is no descent with each pull or delivery is not imminent after three pulls of a correctly applied instrument.
**Anaesthesia:** Treat it as a category 2 caesarean that may be averted. Acid prophylaxis, tilt, vasopressor drawn up, the scrub team with the abdominal set open, the neonatal team present. The failed trial is the worst version of an emergency caesarean: a head now driven deeper into the pelvis, a bruised lower segment and a fetus that has had traction. See the second-stage section of the [emergency caesarean card](emergency-caesarean-section-by-category.html).

### 3. The block for the trial
**Why:** Forceps need a relaxed, pain-free pelvic floor and vagina (S2-4) and tolerance of traction on the uterus and its ligaments (T10); the caesarean that may follow needs T4.
**Anaesthesia:** Block for the caesarean, because there will be no time to extend it. With a working epidural: lidocaine 2% with adrenaline 1:200,000 15-20 mL in fractions with fentanyl 50-100 micrograms, started in the room, which the top-up meta-analysis found to be the fastest solution. The dense block removes the urge to push; that is the accepted trade for being ready to operate, and the obstetrician coaches pushing by palpating contractions. Without an epidural: a spinal at caesarean dose (hyperbaric bupivacaine 0.5% 2.2-2.5 mL with fentanyl 15 micrograms). Whether to add intrathecal morphine is a judgement about the chance of caesarean: the author's practice is to include 100 micrograms when the obstetrician rates failure as likely, and to accept the pruritus if the baby is delivered vaginally. Sitting for the spinal is hard with a head on the perineum; lateral is easier.

### 4. Application, rotation and traction
**Why:** The cup or blades are applied between contractions and checked for position; rotation is completed first, then traction follows the pelvic axis with each contraction and maternal push.
**Anaesthesia:** Keep the tilt and the pressure up: this is still two patients, and a fetus that needed help does not tolerate maternal hypotension from a fresh block. A sustained fetal bradycardia during a failing attempt converts the case to a category 1 caesarean with the block you have; if the block is not adequate, it is a general anaesthetic. Tell the woman when to push.

### 5. Birth, shoulders and the third stage
**Why:** The head delivers over an episiotomy; shoulder dystocia is commoner after a mid-cavity instrumental birth; then the cord is clamped and the placenta delivered by controlled cord traction.
**Anaesthesia:** Shoulder dystocia is managed by the obstetric team with hip hyperflexion and suprapubic pressure; your part is to drop the table flat, take the wedge out and be ready for bleeding afterwards. Uterotonic for a vaginal birth is oxytocin 5-10 IU IM in most protocols, or 5 IU slowly IV if there is a line and a monitor; the smaller intravenous doses used at caesarean are appropriate if the woman is hypotensive. An instrumental birth after a long labour is a set-up for atony, and for the three complications that fill the rest of this card.

### 6. Retained placenta: volume first, then the technique
**Why:** While the placenta is in, the uterus cannot retract; the choice the obstetrician faces is between waiting, which bleeds, and operating, which needs anaesthesia.
**Anaesthesia:** Decide between neuraxial and general on the volume state. A woman who has lost under a litre, is warm, has a normal heart rate and is not actively bleeding gets a neuraxial block: epidural top-up of 10-15 mL of lidocaine 2% with adrenaline, or a spinal with hyperbaric bupivacaine 0.5% 1.8-2.2 mL and fentanyl 15-25 micrograms, aiming for T6. A woman who is tachycardic, pale or still bleeding gets resuscitation, a second cannula and a rapid sequence induction with a reduced dose of induction agent or ketamine 1-1.5 mg/kg IV, because a spinal will remove the vasoconstriction that is holding her pressure. Morphine is not added to the spinal: there is no wound to cover.

### 7. The hand in the uterus
**Why:** The cervix and lower segment begin to close within minutes of birth and may grip the obstetrician's wrist or trap a separated placenta; the hand has to reach the fundus to find the plane.
**Anaesthesia:** If the obstetrician cannot get through, the uterus needs relaxing for two or three minutes. Under neuraxial block: glyceryl trinitrate 50-100 micrograms IV, repeated once or twice, with phenylephrine following it. Under general anaesthesia: a volatile agent above 1 MAC briefly. This is relaxation to permit an operation and is a different question from the one the GOT-IT trial answered, in which sublingual glyceryl trinitrate given as a medical treatment did not deliver retained placentas and was followed by more transfusion. Fundal counter-pressure and the hand at the fundus are felt as deep upper abdominal discomfort when the block is below T8; alfentanil 250-500 micrograms IV covers the minute or two it lasts.

### 8. After the placenta: tone, fragments and the one that will not separate
**Why:** The obstetrician checks that the cavity is empty and the uterus contracts; an oxytocin infusion is run for several hours.
**Anaesthesia:** Switch from relaxing to contracting the instant the placenta is out: stop any volatile excess, oxytocin 5 IU slowly IV, then 40 IU over 4 hours, with ergometrine or carboprost if tone is poor. Antibiotic cover is given because a hand has been in the cavity. If the obstetrician reports no plane of cleavage, the placenta is morbidly adherent: pulling harder causes torrential bleeding or inverts the uterus. The correct surgical move is to stop, and your move is the [major haemorrhage card](major-obstetric-haemorrhage-and-placenta-accreta.html): senior help, blood, conversion to general anaesthesia, and preparation for laparotomy.

### 9. Examination and grading of the tear
**Why:** The obstetrician examines the vagina, cervix and perineum systematically, including a rectal examination, because a sphincter tear behind intact skin is easy to miss and a high vaginal or cervical tear bleeds out of sight.
**Anaesthesia:** For a third or fourth degree repair in a stable woman: epidural top-up of 10 mL of lidocaine 2% with adrenaline given sitting, or a low-dose spinal with hyperbaric bupivacaine 0.5% 1.5-2 mL and fentanyl 15-25 micrograms, kept sitting for two to three minutes to weight the block to the sacral roots. That gives 60-90 minutes of surgical anaesthesia with a relaxed sphincter and little hypotension. An examination under anaesthesia for bleeding of unknown source is a different case: assume a paravaginal haematoma or a cervical tear, assume more loss than measured, and choose general anaesthesia if there is any doubt about volume.

### 10. Sphincter repair
**Why:** The mucosa is closed first, then the internal sphincter, then the external sphincter by an end-to-end or overlapping technique, then the perineal body and skin. Muscle tone pulls the sphincter ends apart, so the surgeon needs them slack.
**Anaesthesia:** A neuraxial block provides the relaxation without neuromuscular blocking drugs; if general anaesthesia was necessary, give a non-depolarising agent for the sphincter layers. Keep the legs no higher and no longer in stirrups than needed: this woman has already spent an hour in lithotomy pushing, and common peroneal and femoral neuropathies after birth are positioning injuries. Total the blood loss again at the end, including what was lost in the room. Paracetamol 1 g IV and an NSAID before the block recedes; rectal diclofenac is convenient after a third degree repair and is avoided through a repaired fourth degree tear.

### 11. Recovery and the days after
**Why:** The repair is protected by soft stool, clean wounds and an empty bladder; the surgical instructions are laxatives, antibiotics and a catheter for 12-24 hours.
**Anaesthesia:** A catheter stays in until sensation has returned, because a neuraxial block plus perineal oedema plus pain reliably causes retention, and one episode of overdistension can damage the bladder for months. The straight-leg raise check at 4 hours applies. Chart regular paracetamol and an NSAID, with a small supply of oral opioid, and write the laxative on the same chart: opioid-induced constipation is the direct enemy of a sphincter repair. Late bleeding after retained placenta is from retained fragments or atony; recheck the fundus and the haemoglobin before the ward round.

## Pain plan

The analgesic strategy is a neuraxial block chosen for the operation, followed by simple oral analgesia. No PROSPECT guideline exists for these procedures, and the plan below is consensus practice and the author's judgement.

The block matches the anatomy. Perineal repair needs only the sacral roots, so a small hyperbaric dose given sitting is enough and wears off within 2-3 hours, leaving the woman able to walk to her baby. Manual removal needs T6 for a few minutes and then nothing. A trial of instrumental delivery needs T4 because of the operation it may become. The labour epidural catheter, where there is one and it works, does all three and is removed afterwards.

Long-acting neuraxial opioid is usually not worth its cost here. Perineal pain is moderate and NSAID-responsive, and intrathecal morphine adds pruritus, nausea and a longer period of urinary retention in a woman whose bladder is already at risk. The exception is the trial of instrumental delivery with a high probability of caesarean. Regular paracetamol and an NSAID are the base, because the pain is from oedema and inflammation in bruised tissue. Ice packs and avoiding prolonged sitting help more than they sound. Oral opioid is kept to a few doses, always with a stool softener and an osmotic laxative. Codeine is avoided: it constipates and it is unreliable in breastfeeding. Lidocaine gel or spray on the wound has little trial support and is harmless.

Rebound is low: the block recedes onto a regimen that is already working. Escalation triggers matter more than escalation drugs. Severe, increasing perineal or rectal pain within hours of delivery, with a normal-looking repair and an urge to defaecate, is a paravaginal or ischioanal haematoma, and the treatment is a return to theatre. Pain increasing after day 3 with fever or offensive discharge is infection or breakdown of the repair. New inability to void after the catheter is out needs a bladder scan, not reassurance.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The bleeding variant. Any of the three becomes a major haemorrhage case once measured loss passes 1,000 mL and is continuing, or earlier with tachycardia and a narrow pulse pressure. From that point: second anaesthetist, two cannulae, blood, an arterial line once loss passes 1,500 mL, general anaesthesia if the block is not already established, and high dependency care afterwards.
- Uterine inversion. Rare, usually from cord traction on a fundal placenta; shock is out of proportion to visible loss because of intense vagal stimulation as well as bleeding. The obstetrician replaces the fundus by hand and needs a relaxed uterus: glyceryl trinitrate IV or general anaesthesia with a volatile, atropine for the bradycardia, then uterotonics the moment it is reduced.
- Sequential instruments. Vacuum followed by forceps carries more maternal and neonatal trauma than either instrument alone; if the vacuum has failed in the room, expect a tear and a tired uterus whatever happens next.
- Low or outlet delivery in the room. A pudendal block and infiltration by the obstetrician are adequate; a sitting epidural top-up of 8-10 mL improves an existing block within five minutes.
- Retained placenta under an existing epidural after a long labour. The catheter that gave good labour analgesia may give only T10 with a top-up; test it before the hand goes in.
- Undiagnosed accreta. Suspect it when there is a previous caesarean or uterine surgery and no plane; stop pulling.
- Pre-eclampsia. Ergometrine is contraindicated; the platelet count sets whether a spinal can be given.
- Nerve palsies. Foot drop after a mid-cavity forceps is usually a lumbosacral trunk compression by the head or blade, or a peroneal palsy from the stirrup; examine, document and exclude a central lesion, as set out on the [labour epidural card](labour-epidural-analgesia-and-its-complications.html).
- Surgeons vary on overlap or end-to-end sphincter repair, on rectal analgesia after fourth degree tears, and on the antibiotic regimen and its duration.

## Key sources

The obstetric framework comes from a national guideline; the antibiotic recommendation after instrumental birth from a large trial; tranexamic acid from the WOMAN trial. The anaesthetic content of this card has little trial evidence: block heights, the choice between spinal and general anaesthesia for retained placenta, the omission of intrathecal morphine for perineal surgery and the doses of intravenous glyceryl trinitrate for uterine relaxation are standard teaching and the author's judgement. The GOT-IT trial is cited for what it does not support, which is glyceryl trinitrate as a medical alternative to manual removal.
