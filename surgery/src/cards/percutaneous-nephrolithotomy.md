## What the surgeon is doing and why

Stones too large to be dealt with from below, by convention over 2 cm, and the branching staghorn stones that fill the renal pelvis and calyces, are removed through a hole made directly into the kidney. The retrograde route described on the [ureteroscopy card](cystoscopy-ureteroscopy-and-stent-procedures.html) works through a 3 mm ureter and cannot clear that volume. The percutaneous route gives a 1 cm rigid working channel straight onto the stone.

The operation has three parts. From below, in lithotomy, a ureteric catheter is passed so that the collecting system can be filled with contrast and so that fragments cannot fall down the ureter. Then, with the patient prone (or in a modified supine position with the flank raised), a needle is passed under fluoroscopy or ultrasound through the skin of the flank, the muscle, the perirenal fat, the renal capsule and the parenchyma into the tip of a posterior calyx. The line through a calyx tip is chosen because it crosses the least vascular plane of the kidney. A wire follows the needle, the tract is dilated over the wire with a balloon or serial dilators to 24-30 Fr (14-20 Fr for the mini technique), and a sheath is left in place. Through the sheath a rigid nephroscope breaks the stone with ultrasonic, pneumatic or laser energy and sucks or picks out the fragments under continuous saline irrigation. At the end the tract is drained with a nephrostomy tube, or left with only an internal stent (tubeless).

Each part creates its own problem. The hole goes through a kidney that takes a fifth of the cardiac output, so bleeding is expected and is mostly venous, controlled by the sheath and later the tube; occasionally a segmental artery is injured and declares itself hours or days later. The irrigation runs for an hour or more through an organ with open veins, around a stone that is frequently a ball of bacteria and endotoxin (infection stones are made by urease-producing organisms), so fluid and bacteria enter the circulation; this is why sepsis, not bleeding, is the lethal complication. And the kidney lies under the ribs: the upper pole is covered by pleura, so an access above the 12th rib, which gives the best line to a staghorn, crosses the pleural reflection in a proportion of patients, and irrigant or air can enter the chest.

The pain logic is a small hole with a deep mechanism. The skin wound is trivial. The tract passes through muscle and close to the intercostal or subcostal nerve, the capsule and parenchyma have been stretched to 1 cm, the collecting system has been distended, and a tube lies across all of it and moves with respiration. Pain is moderate, pleuritic in character, worst on day 1 and falls abruptly when the tube is removed. It is unilateral and confined to two or three dermatomes around T10-T12, which is the territory a paraspinal block covers.

## Steps

### 1. Before the day
**Why:** The surgeon plans the puncture from the CT: which calyx, above or below the 12th rib, and whether colon or pleura lies in the path.
**Anaesthesia:** Antithrombotics must be stopped; this is a puncture of a solid vascular organ that cannot be compressed. Read the urine culture and what was done about it: a positive pre-operative culture roughly triples the odds of post-operative sepsis in pooled data, and diabetes, pyuria, multiple tracts and a long operation add to it. Check renal function and haemoglobin, group and screen, and note the planned position. Patients with staghorn stones include some with spinal cord injury, recurrent infections and poor lungs, in whom prone ventilation and dysreflexia both need a plan.

### 2. Induction, lithotomy and ureteric catheter
**Why:** A cystoscopy in lithotomy places the ureteric catheter (and a urethral catheter) before the turn.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with a tracheal tube is the default. Regional anaesthesia is a genuine alternative: in nine randomised trials it gave less pain on day 1, less analgesic use, a third of the nausea and vomiting, and the same stone clearance, transfusion and fever rates. The author's judgement is that those trials selected slim, cooperative patients for short cases, and that two hours prone, awake, with rib-level visceral stimulation and no airway is a poor plan for a staghorn stone or an obese patient. Place the cannula where you can reach it prone, secure the tube for a turn, give the antibiotic so that it is in the tissues before the kidney is punctured, and consider the paraspinal block now if it is to be done awake.

### 3. Turning prone
**Why:** Prone gives the widest field for posterior calyceal puncture and room for several tracts. Supine modifications are increasingly used: no turn, easier combined retrograde work, and in a meta-analysis of 43 studies a shorter operation and fewer overall and major complications with the same stone-free rate.
**Anaesthesia:** For the prone case: disconnect, turn with enough people, reconnect, and confirm tube position and ventilation at once. Chest and pelvic supports with the abdomen free, so that the diaphragm moves and the vena cava is not compressed. Neck neutral, eyes free of pressure and checked, arms forward with shoulders under 90 degrees, breasts and genitalia clear, the ureteric and urethral catheters not kinked. Recheck the eyes after the image intensifier is positioned. For the supine case, the hazards are the usual lithotomy ones and a flank bolster that tilts the pelvis.

### 4. Puncture of the calyx
**Why:** The needle must enter the tip of the chosen calyx along its axis; a puncture through the infundibulum or pelvis crosses larger vessels. The collecting system is distended with contrast or air through the ureteric catheter to make the target bigger.
**Anaesthesia:** The kidney moves 3-5 cm with ventilation. The surgeon will ask you to hold the breath, usually in expiration for an upper pole or supracostal puncture so that the lung is out of the path. Do it promptly, say how long you can give, and restart when told. Air injected retrogradely to outline a posterior calyx is a recognised route for gas embolism; an abrupt fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide during the puncture phase is that until excluded.

### 5. Tract dilatation and sheath
**Why:** The tract is dilated in one pass with a balloon or stepwise with metal or plastic dilators, and the working sheath is advanced into the calyx. The sheath then tamponades the parenchyma it passes through.
**Anaesthesia:** The most stimulating minute of the operation and the start of the bleeding. Blood loss cannot be seen: it leaves in the irrigant and soaks the drapes. Follow heart rate, pressure and, in a long case, a point-of-care haemoglobin. A tract that is lost during dilatation has to be re-made; a second or third tract multiplies bleeding and sepsis risk, so ask how many are planned.

### 6. Nephroscopy and stone fragmentation
**Why:** The stone is broken and removed piece by piece; flexible scopes reach calyces the rigid scope cannot. Irrigation is continuous and for standard sheaths drains freely around the scope; mini sheaths drain less well and run at higher intrarenal pressure.
**Anaesthesia:** Time is the modifiable risk. Irrigant is absorbed through opened veins and across any perforation, bacteria go with it, and heat is lost: warm the saline, warm the patient, and track the minutes of nephroscopy as you would a tourniquet. Bacteraemia can announce itself on the table as unexplained tachycardia, a falling pressure and a rising end-tidal carbon dioxide or temperature; tell the surgeon, who should stop and drain. Large-volume absorption of saline shows as rising airway pressure and oedema, not hyponatraemia.

### 7. Checking for extravasation and the chest
**Why:** A torn pelvis lets irrigant into the retroperitoneum or peritoneum; a supracostal tract can let it into the pleura.
**Anaesthesia:** A slowly rising peak pressure and falling compliance during a supracostal case is a hydrothorax until proved otherwise. Ask the surgeon to screen the chest with the image intensifier before the drapes come down; fluid or air is far easier to deal with now than after extubation. A distended abdomen under a prone patient is hard to see; look when the patient is turned.

### 8. Exit: nephrostomy tube or tubeless
**Why:** A nephrostomy tube tamponades the tract, drains the kidney and keeps access for a second look. Tubeless exit, with a ureteric stent and sometimes a haemostatic plug, is used after uncomplicated single-tract cases.
**Anaesthesia:** The exit choice is the main determinant of post-operative pain. Pooled randomised trials found that leaving no tube reduced analgesic requirement and stay with no excess of transfusion in uncomplicated cases. If a tube is placed, ask the surgeon to infiltrate the tract down to the capsule with long-acting local anaesthetic before the sheath comes out, and do the paraspinal or intercostal block before the turn if it was not done at the start.

### 9. Turn supine and emergence
**Why:** The case is over when the patient is on the bed.
**Anaesthesia:** Turn with the same care for the tube, lines and nephrostomy. Before extubation examine the chest (auscultation or ultrasound), the abdomen, and the face and airway for dependent oedema after a long prone case with litres of fluid. Reduced air entry on the operated side with high airway pressure needs a chest radiograph and usually a drain before the patient is woken.

### 10. Recovery and the first night
**Why:** The surgeon watches the colour of the nephrostomy drainage and the temperature chart.
**Anaesthesia:** Sepsis usually presents in the first hours with rigors, fever, tachycardia and hypotension: cultures, broad-spectrum antibiotics, fluid, early noradrenaline and a critical care referral, without waiting for it to declare itself. Bleeding presents as frank blood in the tube or catheter with tachycardia; the surgeon clamps the nephrostomy to tamponade the system, and arterial bleeding that persists goes to interventional radiology for embolisation, not back to theatre. Pleuritic pain with desaturation is a missed hydrothorax or pneumothorax.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is unilateral, deep and confined to the lower thoracic dermatomes, with a tube that re-provokes it on every breath until it is removed, so the plan is a single-shot paraspinal block to cover the first night, simple analgesia, and early tube removal as the surgeon allows. The defensible default is an erector spinae plane block at T9-T11 on the operated side (20-30 mL of ropivacaine 0.375%), or a paravertebral block at the same level in practised hands; ten small randomised trials pooled show lower opioid consumption with moderate certainty and lower pain scores at most time points, with the caveat that few were properly blinded. Intercostal blocks around the tract and peritubal infiltration by the surgeon are simpler alternatives aimed at the same nerves. There is no PROSPECT guideline for this operation.

On top of that: paracetamol; an NSAID only where the urine is clearing, the kidney function is sound and the surgeon agrees, since this is a bleeding kidney; and oral opioid for the first day or two. A catheter technique is not justified because the pain has a built-in end point when the tube comes out. If a ureteric stent is left, its symptoms take over for the following week and are managed as on the ureteroscopy card.

Rebound is moderate: a block placed at induction wears off in the early hours of the first night with the tube still in. Escalation triggers: pain with fever is sepsis or an obstructed kidney; pain with breathlessness is the pleura; severe loin pain with a blocked or blood-filled tube is clot colic or bleeding.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Sepsis. Positive culture, infection stones, diabetes, multiple tracts and long nephroscopy time are the predictors. The high-risk patient gets an arterial line and a monitored bed for the first night.
- Bleeding. Usually venous and self-limiting. Transfusion in a few percent, embolisation in far fewer, nephrectomy very rarely. Delayed haematuria at one to two weeks is a pseudoaneurysm or arteriovenous fistula.
- Supracostal access. Hydrothorax and pneumothorax; screen the chest before waking.
- Prone positioning. Eyes, neck, brachial plexus, abdomen free. The obese patient with a staghorn is the hardest version, and the case for a supine approach.
- Supine PCNL and combined retrograde surgery. No turn, simultaneous work from above and below, better airway access; flank exposure is narrower. Increasingly common.
- Mini and micro PCNL. Smaller tract, less bleeding and pain, higher intrarenal pressure and longer fragmentation time; the sepsis logic applies with more force.
- Colonic injury. A retrorenal colon in the path of the needle; found at the nephrostogram or later as faecal drainage.
- Staged procedures. A second look through the same tract in a few days is quick and can often be done with sedation.
- The urgent relative. Percutaneous nephrostomy for an infected obstructed kidney is done by radiology under local anaesthesia with sedation; you are asked to help when the patient is too septic or agitated to lie prone, which is a resuscitation with a procedure attached.
- Spinal cord injury. Autonomic dysreflexia from renal pelvic distension, contractures that prevent standard positioning, and colonised urine.

## Key sources

The literature is large, urological and mostly observational. The sepsis risk factors and the supine against prone comparison come from meta-analyses of heterogeneous cohorts and are best read as direction, not size. The randomised evidence for regional anaesthesia and for the erector spinae block is consistent but drawn from small single-centre trials with weak blinding. The tubeless meta-analysis predates mini techniques but its finding, that the tube is the pain generator, has held. Advice on breath-holding, chest screening and embolisation is standard practice without trials.
