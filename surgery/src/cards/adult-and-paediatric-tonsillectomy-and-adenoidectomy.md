## What the surgeon is doing and why

The palatine tonsil sits in a fossa between two muscular pillars, with its capsule lying directly on the superior constrictor. It is removed for one of two reasons. In recurrent tonsillitis or after a quinsy the tissue itself is the disease. In obstructive sleep-disordered breathing, which is now the commoner indication in young children, the tonsils and adenoids are simply too large for the pharynx they sit in, and removing them widens the airway. The two indications produce two different patients: the older child or adult with scarred, adherent tonsils who will have the worst pain, and the three-year-old with huge, soft tonsils and disturbed nights who will have the worst airway.

In an extracapsular tonsillectomy the surgeon grasps the tonsil, incises the mucosa of the anterior pillar, finds the plane between capsule and constrictor and peels the tonsil off from the upper pole downwards, dividing the lower pole at the tongue base. The dissection is done with cold steel, bipolar diathermy or a coblation wand; the bed is then dried with ties, bipolar or packs. Nothing is closed. What remains is two raw areas of constrictor muscle a few centimetres across, with exposed branches of the glossopharyngeal nerve, that granulate and re-epithelialise over about two weeks. In an intracapsular tonsillectomy (tonsillotomy) the surgeon shaves the tonsil tissue down with a coblation wand or microdebrider and leaves the capsule as a biological dressing over the muscle; it is used mainly for obstruction in children, hurts less and bleeds less, at the price of occasional regrowth. Adenoidectomy removes the lymphoid pad from the roof of the nasopharynx by curette or suction diathermy under mirror or endoscopic vision.

The pain logic follows from the open wound. This is the book's mucosal mechanism: a raw surface that cannot be rested, stretched by every swallow, day and night, lying on a muscle that goes into spasm. Diathermy adds a zone of thermal injury. As the fibrin slough separates around days 4-7 the bed is briefly raw again, which is why adults report their worst days late and why the secondary bleed belongs to the same week. Tonsillectomy sits among the 25 most painful of 179 operations on day 1 in the Gerbershagen cohort, and in adults the pain lasts well beyond a week. That course, not the peak, earns the score of 5. No nerve block reaches the wound safely and reliably, so the plan is systemic. For the patient who returns bleeding see the [bleeding tonsil](bleeding-tonsil.html) card.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative assessment
**Why:** The surgeon has listed the patient for infection or for obstruction, and the indication sets the risk. A child with obstruction is having the operation because the airway is inadequate during sleep.
**Anaesthesia:** Sort the child into a phenotype before anything else. Snoring with witnessed apnoeas, restless sleep, mouth breathing, failure to thrive, age under three, obesity, trisomy 21, craniofacial or neuromuscular disease all move the child towards an overnight monitored bed and a reduced opioid plan; a polysomnogram or overnight oximetry showing a low saturation nadir settles it. The 2019 otolaryngology guideline carries a specific action statement on inpatient monitoring for the youngest children and those with severe sleep apnoea. Ask about bleeding in the patient and the family. A current or recent upper respiratory infection raises the risk of laryngospasm, bronchospasm and desaturation; the decision to proceed weighs symptoms (fever, wet cough, wheeze, lethargy) against the fact that these children are rarely free of a runny nose. Sedative premedication is used cautiously or not at all in severe obstruction.

### 2. Induction and the airway
**Why:** The surgeon needs the mouth, the midline of the tongue and the oropharynx; the airway device must lie flat on the tongue under the gag blade.
**Anaesthesia:** Inhalational or intravenous induction by preference and by cannula. Children with large tonsils obstruct early: an oral airway, jaw thrust and continuous positive pressure are needed sooner than usual. Two devices work. A cuffed south-facing preformed tracheal tube gives a secure airway and full protection from blood; a flexible reinforced laryngeal mask protects the larynx from blood trickling from above, avoids relaxant and laryngeal stimulation, and in a 400-child multicentre trial produced fewer respiratory adverse events than a tube (22.5% against 57.5%), with three conversions. The mask is the more demanding choice: it depends on a surgeon used to it and on your readiness to change to a tube if ventilation is lost when the gag opens. The author's practice is a tube for the very young, the obese, the reflux-prone and the unfamiliar surgeon. Tape either device in the midline to the chin.

### 3. Positioning and the gag
**Why:** Neck extension brings the tonsils into the surgeon's line of sight. The Boyle-Davis gag opens the mouth, and its split blade holds the tongue and the tube forward in the midline.
**Anaesthesia:** Opening the gag is the moment the airway is lost. Watch the capnograph and the airway pressure as the blade goes in and again as the gag is suspended: a kinked tube, a tube pushed into a bronchus by neck movement, a mask folded or displaced, or a tube pulled out with the blade all appear here. Tell the surgeon and have the gag released; do not try to fix it around the instrument. Avoid extension in trisomy 21 beyond a neutral, supported position because of atlantoaxial instability. Protect the eyes and the teeth; loose deciduous teeth are documented beforehand and looked for afterwards.

### 4. Adenoidectomy
**Why:** The adenoid pad is removed to open the nasopharynx and the Eustachian tube orifices. The soft palate is retracted with catheters through the nose and the pad is curetted blind or ablated with suction diathermy under a mirror.
**Anaesthesia:** Curettage produces brisk bleeding that is controlled by a pack left in the nasopharynx for a few minutes, so blood loss is front-loaded and hidden in swabs and suction. Nasal catheters can cause bleeding of their own. The nasopharyngeal pack is on the count and must come out before extubation.

### 5. Tonsil dissection
**Why:** The surgeon develops the plane between capsule and muscle from the upper pole down. Cold steel leaves less thermal injury; bipolar and coblation are faster and drier as the dissection proceeds.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the most stimulating part of a short operation and it needs depth, not relaxant: a propofol bolus or deepening the volatile, with short-acting opioid. Give the intraoperative analgesia now so that it is working at the end: paracetamol 15 mg/kg IV, dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV and a titrated opioid, typically fentanyl 1 microgram/kg IV, halved in severe sleep apnoea. Diathermy in the mouth with an uncuffed tube and a leak of oxygen-rich gas is a fire risk; a cuffed tube or a well-seated mask, a low inspired oxygen fraction and no nitrous oxide remove it. The technique has consequences beyond the day: in the national audit of 11,796 tonsillectomies the haemorrhage rate was about three times higher after bipolar diathermy dissection than after cold steel with ties.

### 6. Haemostasis
**Why:** The fossa is dried with ties or bipolar and inspected with the pillars retracted. The surgeon wants a dry bed at a normal blood pressure, because a bed that is dry only under hypotension bleeds in recovery.
**Anaesthesia:** Keep the blood pressure at the patient's normal level through haemostasis; this is not a hypotensive operation. Give the NSAID at this point if the surgeon and the unit use intraoperative dosing, or leave it for the ward: the evidence does not show an increase in bleeding with NSAIDs but cannot exclude one, and the PROSPECT recommendation is to include them. Give ondansetron.

### 7. Gag release and pharyngeal toilet
**Why:** Relaxing the gag restores venous drainage and unmasks bleeding the gag was compressing. The surgeon re-inspects, suctions the nasopharynx and removes the pack.
**Anaesthesia:** Suction is done under direct vision with the gag still in or with a laryngoscope, in the midline and away from the fossae, including behind the soft palate where a clot can sit unseen above the airway and fall onto the larynx at extubation. Confirm aloud with the scrub nurse that the nasopharyngeal pack and any throat pack are out. Check the tube or mask position again once the gag is removed.

### 8. Emergence and extubation
**Why:** The surgeon has left two raw surfaces that will ooze onto the larynx; coughing and straining raise venous pressure in the bed.
**Anaesthesia:** There are two safe states and a dangerous one between them. Deep extubation or mask removal, in the lateral head-down (tonsil) position with an oral airway, gives a quiet emergence and lets blood drain out of the mouth, but needs a recovery unit that can manage an unprotected airway in a deeply anaesthetised child. Awake extubation waits for eye opening and purposeful movement. Laryngospasm happens in the light plane between the two, provoked by blood or secretions on the cords. A randomised trial of deep against awake mask removal in at-risk children found no overall difference in respiratory events, with more events in the recovery unit after awake removal. In severe sleep apnoea the argument for awake removal, with the child's own airway tone back, is stronger.

### 9. Recovery
**Why:** The surgical concerns in the first hours are primary haemorrhage and an airway that has been operated on but is not yet better.
**Anaesthesia:** A child who swallows repeatedly, becomes pale and tachycardic, or vomits blood is bleeding until examined; blood pressure falls late. Emergence delirium is common after sevoflurane in this age group and is hard to tell from pain: treat pain first. Titrate rescue opioid in small increments, for example morphine 0.02-0.05 mg/kg IV, and wait between doses; in the child with documented nocturnal desaturation start at half that. Residual obstruction, oedema and blood mean the first night is not better than the night before surgery.

### 10. Discharge and the first fortnight
**Why:** The fossae heal by secondary intention over about two weeks; the slough separates around the end of the first week.
**Anaesthesia:** Day-case discharge needs a period of observation (commonly 4-6 hours, because most primary bleeds declare themselves within it), drinking, controlled pain and nausea, a carer who understands the bleeding instruction, and a home within reasonable reach. The discharge prescription is the anaesthetist's business because it decides the week: regular paracetamol and ibuprofen for at least seven days in children and longer in adults, with a small supply of a rescue opioid for adults and older children and none for the young child with sleep apnoea.

## Pain plan

No block treats this wound, so the plan is the PROSPECT regimen: paracetamol, an NSAID and a single intraoperative dose of dexamethasone, with opioid as rescue. PROSPECT also recommends honey after surgery and acupuncture as adjuncts, lists ketamine (in children), dexmedetomidine and gabapentinoids as options when a first-line drug cannot be given, and found the evidence for local anaesthetic infiltration of the fossae inconsistent. Infiltration also carries its own hazards in this site (intravascular injection, glossopharyngeal and vagal block), so the defensible default is not to rely on it.

The NSAID question is the one surgeons differ on. The Cochrane review found no significant increase in bleeding and less vomiting; a 688-child non-inferiority trial found bleeding needing theatre in 2.9% with ibuprofen against 1.2% with paracetamol and could not declare ibuprofen non-inferior. The same uncertainty hangs over dexamethasone: a meta-analysis of 29 trials found no increase in bleeding events but more returns to theatre. Against both stands what happens without them: more opioid in a population where opioid is the proven killer. In the randomised comparison of ibuprofen with oral morphine after tonsillectomy for sleep-disordered breathing, desaturation on the first night improved in 68% of children on ibuprofen and 14% on morphine, with similar analgesia. The author's judgement is that paracetamol, ibuprofen and one dose of dexamethasone is the right default for almost every child, and that the conversation with a surgeon who prefers to avoid NSAIDs is about what replaces them.

Opioids are titrated to the phenotype. Children with recurrent nocturnal hypoxaemia need less morphine for the same pain score, and codeine, whose conversion to morphine depends on CYP2D6, has killed ultra-rapid metabolisers after this operation and is contraindicated in children. For the child with severe sleep apnoea: short-acting opioid in theatre at half dose, no long-acting opioid, no opioid to take home, and a monitored bed. For adults the problem is the opposite one of under-treatment: expect worst pain of 6 out of 10 through the first week with the regimen in place, referred otalgia, and a late peak as the slough lifts. Adults need paracetamol and an NSAID for 10-14 days, a rescue opioid supply sized for a week, and advice to dose before meals because eating and drinking are what keep the bed clean and the pain controlled.

Escalation triggers: pain that prevents drinking leads to dehydration and readmission within days; pain that suddenly worsens with fever and fetor suggests infection of the bed; and any fresh blood is not a pain problem.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Severe obstructive sleep apnoea. The emergency-grade variant of an elective operation. Pulmonary hypertension and right heart strain in the worst cases; airway obstruction at induction and after extubation; opioid sensitivity. Plan a monitored bed with continuous oximetry before starting, and critical care for the child with cor pulmonale, craniofacial disease or neuromuscular weakness. In a series of 111 deaths and serious injuries after tonsillectomy, more than half the children were at risk of sleep apnoea and events occurred in theatre, in recovery, on the ward and at home.
- Primary haemorrhage. Within 24 hours, usually within six. Return to theatre with a hypovolaemic patient, a stomach full of blood and an airway obscured by it: see [bleeding tonsil](bleeding-tonsil.html). Invasive monitoring and blood are needed only here.
- Quinsy tonsillectomy and the adult with trismus. Trismus from pterygoid spasm usually relaxes with induction, but a large peritonsillar abscess can rupture at laryngoscopy; aspirate it first where possible and plan the airway as for [dental and neck abscess](dental-and-neck-abscess.html).
- Laryngeal mask against tube. Surgeon and unit dependent. The mask fails by displacement under the gag; the tube fails by kinking and bronchial migration. Uncuffed tubes leak oxygen onto diathermy.
- Intracapsular technique. Less pain, a faster return to eating and fewer bleeds; the pain score for a coblation tonsillotomy in a small child is nearer 3 than 5.
- Adenoidectomy alone. A day case with little pain (paracetamol and ibuprofen for a few days); the bleeding comes from the nasopharynx and the pack must be removed.
- Coagulation disorders. Von Willebrand disease and haemophilia need a haematology plan (desmopressin, factor, TXA) agreed beforehand; NSAIDs are then usually avoided.
- Post-obstructive pulmonary oedema. After relief of long-standing obstruction or after laryngospasm: pink frothy sputum and desaturation in recovery, managed with positive pressure and time.
- The shared airway in general, and tube choice for other head and neck surgery, are covered in [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html); paediatric dosing and fluids in [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html); the antiemetic logic in [nausea, vomiting and day-case anaesthesia](../a/a11-ponv-day-case.html).

## Key sources

The analgesic regimen rests on a PROSPECT review of 226 randomised trials and is the best-evidenced part of the card. The safety of NSAIDs and dexamethasone with respect to bleeding is unresolved in the strict sense: no trial has shown harm, and none has been large enough to exclude it. The opioid and sleep apnoea material comes from one small prospective study, one randomised trial, case reports and a closed-claims and survey series. The laryngeal mask data are from one large recent multicentre trial in experienced hands. Extubation position and depth, observation times before discharge and the opioid dose reductions given here are consensus and author's judgement.
