## What the surgeon is doing and why

The thymus sits in the anterior mediastinum behind the manubrium, an H-shaped gland of two lobes with upper horns reaching into the neck along the thyrothymic ligaments and lower horns spreading over the pericardium. It is removed for two reasons that often coexist.

The first is myasthenia gravis. In most patients with generalised disease the trigger is an antibody against the acetylcholine receptor on the muscle side of the neuromuscular junction, and the thymus is where the autoimmune response is thought to be sustained. Removing it does not cure the disease on the day; it shifts its course over months to years. The MGTX trial randomised 126 patients without thymoma to extended transsternal thymectomy plus prednisone or prednisone alone and found lower disease scores over three years with surgery. The operation is therefore elective, done when the patient is as well as medical treatment can make them, and never in a crisis.

The second is thymoma, a tumour of thymic epithelium, which is removed on oncological grounds whether or not the patient has myasthenia. A proportion of thymoma patients are myasthenic, and some become so only after resection. A small encapsulated thymoma is the same operation as a thymectomy for myasthenia. A large or invasive one is a mediastinal mass with everything that implies for induction (see [mediastinoscopy and anterior mediastinal mass](mediastinoscopy-and-anterior-mediastinal-mass.html)) and may need resection of pericardium, lung, phrenic nerve or innominate vein.

What has to be removed is the gland and all the fat around it, because thymic tissue is scattered through mediastinal fat: from one phrenic nerve to the other, from the diaphragm to the thyroid. The borders are the hazards.

```
        neck: upper horns, thyrothymic ligaments
   -------------------------------------------------
   LEFT INNOMINATE VEIN crosses in front; thymic
   veins (2-4) enter its lower border
   -------------------------------------------------
   R phrenic   |   thymus and fat on    |  L phrenic
   on SVC and  |   pericardium, aorta   |  on the
   pericardium |   and pulmonary trunk  |  pericardium
   -------------------------------------------------
        diaphragm: cardiophrenic fat pads
```

The thymic veins are short and drain straight into the left innominate vein; tearing one is the serious bleed of the operation. The phrenic nerves mark the lateral limit of dissection, and a phrenic palsy in a myasthenic patient is a respiratory catastrophe, so surgeons stay off them even at the cost of leaving fat.

The route has moved. The trial operation was a full sternotomy. Most thymectomies are now thoracoscopic or robotic, from the right or the left with three ports and carbon dioxide insufflation, sometimes bilateral or subxiphoid; a transcervical approach through a collar incision is used in some centres for small glands. Sternotomy remains for large or invasive tumours. In a meta-analysis of risk factors for postoperative crisis, an open thoracic approach was itself a predictor.

The pain logic: for the minimally invasive operation the mechanism is chest wall from ports and a drain, on one side, without rib spreading: a 3, peaking on days 1-2 and settling fast. The sternotomy variant is a 4. What makes the analgesic plan matter more than the score suggests is the disease: opioid-induced hypoventilation and sedation in a patient with marginal bulbar and respiratory muscle strength is a route to reintubation, so regional and non-opioid analgesia are doing more than treating pain.

## Steps

### 1. Neurological optimisation and timing
**Why:** The surgeon operates when the neurologist says the disease is as controlled as it will be. There is no urgency in non-thymomatous disease.
**Anaesthesia:** Establish the pattern and severity: ocular only, generalised, bulbar (speech, chewing, swallowing, choking on fluids) or respiratory. Bulbar symptoms and a previous crisis are the strongest predictors of postoperative crisis in the pooled data, together with high pyridostigmine requirement, poor lung function and long disease duration. Measure vital capacity sitting and lying. A patient with active bulbar or respiratory weakness is deferred for intravenous immunoglobulin or plasma exchange in the weeks before surgery, arranged with the neurologist. Review the CT for the size of any thymoma. List the drugs: pyridostigmine dose and timing, steroid, steroid-sparing agents. Put the patient first on the list, when the morning pyridostigmine dose is working.

### 2. The morning's drugs
**Why:** Nothing surgical.
**Anaesthesia:** Give the usual pyridostigmine with a sip of water. Omitting it to reduce relaxant requirement is an older practice that trades a small pharmacological convenience for a weak, anxious patient with poor swallowing; the review literature favours continuing it. Give the usual steroid and supplement for the operation. No sedative premedication in anyone with bulbar or respiratory involvement. Check the antibiotic and any other planned drug against the list that worsens transmission: aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, magnesium, and to a lesser degree beta-blockers and calcium channel blockers.

### 3. Monitoring before any relaxant
**Why:** Nothing surgical.
**Anaesthesia:** Attach the quantitative neuromuscular monitor and record a baseline after induction and before any relaxant. A myasthenic patient may show fade on the baseline train-of-four, and you need to know that before you interpret later readings. The adductor pollicis can be relatively spared when bulbar and ocular muscles are weak, so a normal reading at the thumb overestimates the airway muscles; treat it as a minimum, not a guarantee.

### 4. Induction and the relaxant decision
**Why:** The surgeon needs a still field and, for thoracoscopic work, a collapsed lung. Neither strictly needs paralysis.
**Anaesthesia:** Three workable strategies. First, no relaxant: propofol with remifentanil, or a deep volatile anaesthetic, gives intubating conditions in most myasthenic patients because the disease has already done part of the work, and a remifentanil infusion keeps the patient still. Second, a reduced dose of rocuronium, starting at about a fifth to a quarter of the usual dose and titrating to the monitor, because sensitivity to non-depolarising agents is increased and variable; reverse with sugammadex. Third, suxamethonium for a rapid sequence, accepting that the patient is resistant and needs a larger dose, around 1.5-2 mg/kg, and that pyridostigmine prolongs its action by inhibiting plasma cholinesterase. The second strategy has the best data: in 117 myasthenic patients given rocuronium and sugammadex, recovery to a ratio of 0.9 took a mean of 117 seconds with no reintubations, and in a national database of 795 thymectomies sugammadex was associated with half the rate of postoperative crisis (4.3% against 8.7%). A second database study found relaxant with sugammadex no worse than avoiding relaxant. The author's practice is low-dose rocuronium with sugammadex for double-lumen tube placement and robotic cases, and no relaxant for a single-lumen tube and sternotomy.

### 5. Lung isolation and positioning
**Why:** The surgeon approaches from one side with the patient semi-supine, the ipsilateral lung collapsed, and carbon dioxide blown in to push the mediastinal pleura and lung away.
**Anaesthesia:** A left double-lumen tube or a blocker, checked by bronchoscope; see [one-lung ventilation and thoracic essentials](../a/a03-one-lung-ventilation.html). Some units avoid lung isolation and use a single-lumen tube with small tidal volumes and capnothorax alone. The operative arm is dropped back or held forward to clear the axilla for the ports: pad it and limit shoulder extension, because a brachial plexus stretch in a patient who is already weak will be blamed on the disease and missed. With a robot docked over the chest your access to the airway is limited; secure everything first and agree the emergency undocking drill. Place the cannula in the right arm or a leg: if the left innominate vein is torn or clamped, fluid given through the left arm goes nowhere useful.

### 6. Capnothorax and entry
**Why:** Insufflation at 6-8 mmHg opens the retrosternal space, which is otherwise a slit between the sternum and the heart.
**Anaesthesia:** Positive intrathoracic pressure on the right side compresses the right atrium and the cavae: expect a fall in pressure at insufflation, treat with a vasopressor and a little volume, and ask for the pressure to be reduced if it does not recover. End-tidal carbon dioxide rises by absorption; increase the rate, not the tidal volume. If the opposite pleura is opened, which is intended in an extended thymectomy, gas enters the ventilated side, airway pressure rises and saturation falls: the remedy is PEEP on that lung and a lower insufflation pressure.

### 7. Dissection off the pericardium and along the phrenic nerves
**Why:** The lower horns and pericardial fat are lifted off the pericardium, working upwards, with the phrenic nerve on each side as the lateral limit.
**Anaesthesia:** Diathermy near the phrenic nerve makes the diaphragm jump if the patient is not paralysed, which the surgeon may use as a warning or may find a nuisance; this is the argument some surgeons make for no relaxant. Pressure on the heart from instruments in a tight space produces ectopy and transient hypotension. Keep the patient deep enough not to cough: remifentanil does this without relaxant.

### 8. The innominate vein and the thymic veins
**Why:** The gland is peeled off the front of the left innominate vein and each thymic vein is clipped or sealed. This is the step the surgeon slows down for.
**Anaesthesia:** A torn vein under capnothorax may not bleed dramatically at first because insufflation pressure exceeds venous pressure; instead carbon dioxide enters the vein. A sudden fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide with hypotension at this step is gas embolism: tell the surgeon, stop insufflation, flood the field, support the circulation. Bleeding that cannot be controlled through the ports means sternotomy, so the saw should be in the room and blood grouped. Venous access must not depend on the left arm.

### 9. Upper horns, the opposite side and the specimen
**Why:** The cervical horns are teased down out of the neck, the contralateral pleura is opened to clear fat to the far phrenic nerve, and the specimen is bagged and removed through an enlarged port or a subxiphoid incision.
**Anaesthesia:** Traction on the upper horns pulls near the recurrent laryngeal nerves and the thyroid veins. Once both pleurae are open, both lungs will need recruiting at the end and one drain may serve both sides. For an invasive thymoma this is where the operation escalates: pericardial resection, a wedge of lung, sacrifice of one phrenic nerve, or clamping and reconstruction of the innominate vein or cava, which needs lower limb access, an arterial line, and sometimes a sternotomy.

### 10. Drain, re-expansion and closure
**Why:** A single drain is placed through a port, the gas is released, and the lung is inflated under vision.
**Anaesthesia:** Recruit both lungs with a sustained manual inflation. If the block was not placed at the start, the surgeon can inject the intercostal nerves under vision now. Stop the remifentanil only when the dressings are on. Give paracetamol and the NSAID.

### 11. Reversal and extubation
**Why:** The surgeon wants the patient extubated, because postoperative ventilation in myasthenia lengthens everything.
**Anaesthesia:** If rocuronium was used, give sugammadex by the monitor (2 mg/kg for a train-of-four count of two or more, 4 mg/kg for deeper block) and wait for a ratio above 0.9 that is at least as good as the baseline. Do not give neostigmine: in a patient already taking pyridostigmine the receptors are near-maximally exposed to acetylcholine, the response is unpredictable, and an overdose produces weakness of its own. Then test what the monitor cannot: sustained head lift, a strong cough, tongue protrusion, tidal volume and vital capacity on the tube. Extubate awake and sitting. If any of these is poor, keep the tube and say why; a planned few hours of ventilation is better than an emergency reintubation of a weak patient with secretions.

### 12. The first 72 hours
**Why:** The surgeon removes the drain on day 1; the neurologist and you watch for crisis.
**Anaesthesia:** Restart pyridostigmine at the usual dose and time, by mouth or nasogastric tube. If the gut is unavailable, intravenous neostigmine is substituted at a small fraction of the oral pyridostigmine dose under neurological advice; requirements can fall after surgery, so do not exceed the preoperative dose. Weakness has the three causes named in the trap. Residual relaxant is excluded by the monitor. Myasthenic crisis (weak, dry, large pupils, tachycardic) is treated by supporting ventilation early, with non-invasive ventilation where bulbar function allows and intubation where it does not, and by immunoglobulin or plasma exchange. Cholinergic crisis (weak, wet, small pupils, bradycardic, cramping, with secretions and bronchospasm) is treated by withholding anticholinesterase and supporting ventilation. In practice the two are hard to separate at the bedside and the safe response to both is the same first step: secure ventilation, then sort out the drug. Pain, infection, a missed pyridostigmine dose and the listed antibiotics are the usual triggers.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is port-site chest wall pain on one side with a drain for a day, and the patient is one in whom opioid side effects are dangerous. The plan is a hemithoracic block, regular non-opioid analgesia, and the smallest possible opioid dose.

PROSPECT's thoracoscopic guideline is the nearest procedure-specific evidence: paracetamol with an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, a paravertebral or erector spinae plane block as first choice, serratus anterior plane block as second, opioid for rescue. The author's practice for thoracoscopic and robotic thymectomy is a single-shot erector spinae or paravertebral block at T4-T5 on the port side with ropivacaine 0.375-0.5% 20 mL, placed after induction, with intraoperative remifentanil and no long-acting opioid. Use an amide local anaesthetic: ester agents are metabolised by plasma cholinesterase, which pyridostigmine inhibits. Keep the dose conservative, because a high bilateral block that weakens intercostal muscles is tolerated less well here. A bilateral approach needs bilateral blocks at reduced volume.

For sternotomy, bilateral parasternal intercostal plane blocks or generous infiltration by the surgeon, with paracetamol and an NSAID, and a low-dose opioid PCA without background. A thoracic epidural is an option when respiratory reserve is poor and the unit is practised with it, and the older anaesthetic literature in myasthenia used it as a way to avoid both relaxant and opioid. See [analgesia for the trunk](../a/a02-truncal-blocks-epidural.html). For the sternotomy itself, the general approach matches the cardiac cards such as [aortic valve replacement](aortic-valve-replacement.html).

All of this is extrapolation: no trial has tested analgesic technique in thymectomy for myasthenia, and the recommendation is judgement built on the mechanism and on the disease.

A catheter is rarely needed. Rebound when a single shot wears off on the first night is moderate and is met with the regular oral regimen and a small dose of an immediate-release opioid. Escalation triggers: pain that prevents coughing in a patient with a falling vital capacity needs a repeat block, not more opioid; breathlessness blamed on pain should prompt a vital capacity measurement and a look at the chest radiograph for a raised hemidiaphragm.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The bulky thymoma. Treat as an anterior mediastinal mass: symptoms lying flat, CT for airway and vascular compression, and a staged induction for the high-risk patient. The prospective study of airway patency under anaesthesia is reassuring for most adults but did not establish that conventional induction is safe in the symptomatic patient.
- Undiagnosed myasthenia. A thymoma patient with no neurological diagnosis who is unexpectedly slow to recover from a normal dose of relaxant has declared it. Reverse with sugammadex, monitor, and refer.
- Transcervical thymectomy. Collar incision, sternal retractor lifting the manubrium, single-lumen tube, day 1 discharge, pain score 2. The innominate vein is at the bottom of a deep hole.
- Robotic surgery. Longer set-up, restricted access, the arm position, and capnothorax throughout. Whether it improves on thoracoscopic surgery is not settled.
- Phrenic nerve sacrifice or injury. In myasthenia this may mean prolonged ventilation; the surgeon will accept an incomplete resection to avoid it.
- Steroid-dependent patients. Hyperglycaemia, poor wound and sternal healing, adrenal suppression: continue and supplement.
- Pregnancy, and other surgery in the myasthenic patient. The relaxant, monitoring, antibiotic and crisis rules on this card apply to any operation; magnesium for pre-eclampsia is the classic interaction.
- Emergency and high-risk variant. Thymectomy is never an emergency. The emergency is the myasthenic patient needing other urgent surgery, or a re-exploration for bleeding from the innominate vein, where a modified rapid sequence with rocuronium and immediate availability of sugammadex is the cleanest plan.
- Lambert-Eaton syndrome is not the same disease: these patients are sensitive to both classes of relaxant and do not respond to anticholinesterases in the same way.

## Key sources

The case for the operation rests on one good randomised trial of the transsternal approach; the less invasive approaches are supported by observational comparisons. Risk factors for postoperative crisis come from a meta-analysis of 25 retrospective studies with heterogeneous definitions. The sugammadex evidence is a large case series and two Japanese database analyses, consistent with each other but observational; no randomised trial compares relaxant strategies in myasthenia. Statements about suxamethonium resistance, the reduced rocuronium dose, the sparing of adductor pollicis, ester local anaesthetics and the conversion from oral pyridostigmine to intravenous neostigmine are standard pharmacological teaching; specific dose ratios were not verified during drafting and are given as ranges or omitted. The analgesic plan is extrapolated from the PROSPECT thoracoscopic guideline.
