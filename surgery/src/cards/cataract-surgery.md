## What the surgeon is doing and why

A cataract is a lens that has gone cloudy. The lens sits inside a thin transparent capsule slung behind the iris by zonular fibres. The operation removes the cloudy contents and leaves the capsular bag in place to hold a folded acrylic lens.

Through a 2-3 mm incision at the edge of the cornea the surgeon fills the front of the eye with a viscoelastic gel to hold its shape, tears a neat circular window in the front of the capsule (the capsulorhexis), separates the lens from its capsule with a jet of fluid, and then breaks up and aspirates the nucleus with an ultrasonic probe (phacoemulsification) while irrigation fluid keeps the eye pressurised. The soft cortex is aspirated, the folded lens is injected into the bag where it opens, the gel is washed out, antibiotic is injected and the incision seals itself without a suture. It takes 10 to 20 minutes and is the commonest operation in medicine.

The surgeon needs three things from you: an eye that does not hurt, an eye and head that do not move at the critical moments (the capsulorhexis and the phacoemulsification next to a posterior capsule a few micrometres thick), and a patient who can lie flat and quiet for a quarter of an hour. Akinesia of the eye is no longer needed by most surgeons; an eye that the patient can hold still on request is enough.

The pain logic is the simplest in the book. Nothing is cut that hurts afterwards, and the score is 1. There is no analgesic plan to write. The card is about choosing the least anaesthetic that works, in a population with a mean age in the seventies and a long list of comorbidities that do not need investigating for this operation.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment on the day
**Why:** The surgeon wants the list to run at four to six cases an hour without cancellations.
**Anaesthesia:** No routine tests: a Cochrane review concluded that routine preoperative medical testing does not increase the safety of cataract surgery. What matters is found by asking and looking. Can the patient lie flat for 20 minutes (orthopnoea, kyphosis, back pain, reflux)? Can they keep still (tremor, chronic cough, restless legs, claustrophobia)? Can they hear and follow instructions (deafness, dementia, language)? Blood pressure is measured but a single high reading in an anxious patient is not a reason to cancel; pain and a hard eye are the real hazards of a pressure surge, and they are rare. Diabetics eat and take their medication as normal for local anaesthesia. Ask the surgeon for the axial length: an eye longer than 26 mm is a myopic globe with a thin posterior wall, sometimes with a staphyloma, and is the eye that a sharp needle perforates.

### 2. Anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents
**Why:** The corneal incision is avascular, so the operation does not bleed; the block might.
**Anaesthesia:** Continue them. In the audit of 55,567 cataract operations, 5.1% of patients were on warfarin and 1.9% on clopidogrel; both groups had more minor block complications (subconjunctival haemorrhage) but no significant increase in sight-threatening haemorrhage from the anaesthetic or the operation. Stopping an anticoagulant for a cataract exposes the patient to stroke for no ocular gain. Check that a warfarin INR is within the patient's therapeutic range, and choose a technique that does not put a sharp needle behind the globe: topical, or a sub-Tenon block with the expectation of a red eye. Direct oral anticoagulants are handled the same way in most units.

### 3. Choosing the anaesthetic
**Why:** Surgeons differ in how much eye movement they accept and how long they take.
**Anaesthesia:** In the United Kingdom national survey of an estimated 375,000 local anaesthetics, sub-Tenon blocks were the most used (42.6%), then peribulbar (30.6%), topical with or without intracameral lidocaine (about 21%) and retrobulbar (3.5%); only 4.1% of cataracts were done under general anaesthesia. Practice has moved further towards topical since. The choice follows the surgeon and the eye:

```
Topical +/- intracameral   Routine cataract, fast surgeon, patient
                           who can fix on the light and cooperate
Sub-Tenon                  Dense or complicated cataract, anxious
                           patient, small pupil, longer case,
                           trainee surgeon, anticoagulated patient
Peribulbar                 Akinesia required and sub-Tenon not
                           possible (scarred conjunctiva, buckle)
General anaesthesia        Cannot lie flat or still, cannot
                           communicate, child, patient's refusal
```

### 4. Topical and intracameral anaesthesia
**Why:** Drops anaesthetise the cornea and conjunctiva; preservative-free lidocaine injected into the anterior chamber by the surgeon numbs the iris and ciliary body.
**Anaesthesia:** No injection, no bruising, vision back at once. The patient feels pressure and cold water, sees a bright light, and can move the eye, so cooperation is the whole technique. Iris manipulation, a stretched small pupil and a long case become uncomfortable. No monitoring beyond the pulse oximeter is needed for an unsedated patient. The survey found no life-threatening complications reported with topical or intracameral anaesthesia.

### 5. Sub-Tenon block
**Why:** Local anaesthetic placed in the space between the globe and its fascial sheath spreads back to the ciliary nerves and, in larger volume, to the motor nerves within the muscle cone.
**Anaesthesia:** After topical drops and povidone-iodine, a small snip through conjunctiva and Tenon's capsule in the inferonasal quadrant, a blunt curved cannula slid along the sclera past the equator, and 3-5 mL of lidocaine 2% (with or without bupivacaine and hyaluronidase). Anaesthesia is reliable and quick; akinesia depends on volume. The common effects are chemosis and a subconjunctival haemorrhage, more so on antithrombotic drugs, which look alarming and do not matter. A prospective series of 6,000 blocks recorded no serious block-related complication and 98.8% acceptability, and the national survey found fewer serious complications than with needle blocks. Serious events are rare but reported, including brainstem spread, so the monitoring and the intravenous cannula are the same as for a needle block.

### 6. Peribulbar and retrobulbar blocks
**Why:** A needle delivers local anaesthetic outside or inside the muscle cone for dense anaesthesia and akinesia.
**Anaesthesia:** A Cochrane review found nothing to choose between the two for pain, akinesia or serious complications, which are rare with both; chemosis is commoner with peribulbar and lid haematoma with retrobulbar injection. The risks are those of a sharp needle in a closed space: globe perforation (the long myopic eye), retrobulbar haemorrhage with a tense proptosed orbit, optic nerve injury, muscle injury with lasting double vision, and spread along the optic nerve sheath to the brainstem. Brainstem anaesthesia shows within about 15 minutes of the block as drowsiness, confusion, shivering, contralateral eye signs, then apnoea and cardiovascular collapse, and is treated by supporting ventilation and circulation until it wears off; seven of the eight cases in the national survey followed needle blocks. Stay with the patient after any orbital injection, and press on a retrobulbar haemorrhage while calling the surgeon, who decides between waiting, canthotomy and postponement.

### 7. Sedation
**Why:** The surgeon wants a calm patient; some ask for sedation as routine.
**Anaesthesia:** Most patients need none: in the survey 92.1% of cataracts were done under local anaesthesia without sedation. In a cohort of 19,250 operations, intravenous sedatives were associated with more adverse medical events whichever local technique was used, and combinations of opioid, sedative and hypnotic carried the highest rate (4.04%). A meta-analysis of 12 trials found intravenous sedation reduced pain compared with none, but was no better than oral sedation. The practical rule is anxiolysis without sleep: midazolam 0.5-1 mg IV, or nothing and a hand to hold. A patient who falls asleep under the drape obstructs, snores, and then wakes with a start and moves the head while the probe is in the eye. A short-acting bolus to cover a needle block (propofol 20-30 mg IV or alfentanil 250 micrograms IV) is a different thing and is over before draping. See [remote site anaesthesia and procedural sedation](../a/a12-remote-site-sedation.html).

### 8. Positioning and the drape
**Why:** The microscope needs the corneal plane horizontal and the head still at a fixed distance.
**Anaesthesia:** Spend the time here. Pillow under the knees, chin level with the forehead, support behind a kyphotic neck or tilt the whole table head-down with the back raised to bring the face flat. The adhesive drape covers the nose and mouth: run air or oxygen at 4-6 L/min under a bar or tube to flush exhaled carbon dioxide, which otherwise drives breathlessness and panic. Agree a signal (a squeeze of the hand) for a cough or a need to move, and tell the patient the surgeon will stop first. An assistant holding the patient's hand is worth more than a milligram of midazolam.

### 9. The operation: when stillness matters
**Why:** The capsulorhexis, phacoemulsification and cortex removal all take place a hair's breadth from the posterior capsule; a sudden movement tears it and lets vitreous forward.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing to give and everything to watch. Warn the surgeon before any intervention that might make the patient move. If the posterior capsule ruptures the surgeon adds an anterior vitrectomy and the case runs to 45 minutes or more; a topical anaesthetic may need a sub-Tenon top-up by the surgeon. The oculocardiac reflex is uncommon in cataract surgery but occurs with the block injection and with traction. The rare catastrophe is suprachoroidal haemorrhage: sudden severe pain, loss of the red reflex and a hardening eye, as a choroidal vessel bleeds behind the retina. It occurs in about 1 in 800 intraocular operations across all types. The surgeon closes the eye at once; your part is to stop coughing or straining, treat pain and control a high blood pressure.

### 10. General anaesthesia when it is needed
**Why:** A few patients cannot give the surgeon a still head by any other means.
**Anaesthesia:** A supraglottic airway, propofol induction, maintenance of your choice with or without a small dose of relaxant, and the breathing circuit run down the chest away from the surgeon. The aims are an immobile central eye, a normal or low intraocular pressure (normocapnia, head slightly up, no coughing or bucking on the airway device) and a smooth wake. Depth must be adequate before the first incision, because the stimulus is small until it is not. The patient with dementia often does better with a general anaesthetic than with a sedated local, and both eyes in one sitting may be reasonable to spare a second anaesthetic.

## Pain plan

There is no pain to plan for. The block or the drops wear off within one to four hours into a comfortable eye; paracetamol covers the gritty, watery sensation of the first evening, which comes from the corneal surface and the antiseptic. Topical steroid and NSAID drops prescribed by the surgeon for inflammation do the rest. Rebound does not occur.

That makes pain a warning sign. Severe pain in the first hours, especially with nausea, is a high intraocular pressure (retained viscoelastic) and the surgeon should check it. Pain, redness and falling vision between days 2 and 7 is endophthalmitis until proven otherwise and is a same-day emergency. Double vision that persists after the first day follows needle blocks and large volumes, from local anaesthetic toxicity to an extraocular muscle.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Cancelling for numbers. Blood pressure, glucose and INR within the patient's usual range do not predict harm in cataract surgery. Symptoms do: unstable angina, decompensated heart failure and an inability to lie flat are reasons to defer.
- The long eye. Axial length over 26 mm and a sharp needle is the setting for globe perforation. Use sub-Tenon or topical.
- The only eye. Everything is the same and everyone is more careful; the threshold for general anaesthesia is lower.
- Sedation drift. A top-up because the patient is restless usually makes the patient more restless. Stop, uncover the face, speak to them.
- Deaf or non-English-speaking patients. Hearing aid left in on the non-operative side; an interpreter in theatre.
- Parkinsonian tremor and chronic cough. Take usual medication; a cough suppressant beforehand; low threshold for general anaesthesia if the head moves.
- Alpha-blockers. Tamsulosin causes a floppy iris and a longer, harder operation; the surgeon needs to know and may prefer a block to drops.
- Complicated cataract. Dense white lens, weak zonules, small pupil, previous vitrectomy: longer, more manipulation, sub-Tenon block from the start.
- Children. Always general anaesthesia, with tracheal tube or supraglottic airway, and often examination and measurement of both eyes; see [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).
- Emergency and high-risk variant. Cataract surgery is elective. The patient at risk is the frail one given too much sedation; the eye at risk is the one with a needle block on a long globe. Invasive monitoring, blood and critical care have no place. The combined procedure of cataract with vitrectomy is on the [vitreoretinal surgery](vitreoretinal-surgery.html) card, and glaucoma procedures on [glaucoma, corneal graft and oculoplastic surgery](glaucoma-corneal-graft-and-oculoplastic-surgery.html).

## Key sources

Cataract anaesthesia is well served by large observational datasets: a national survey of complications by technique, a prospective series of 6,000 sub-Tenon blocks, an audit of antithrombotic drugs in over 48,000 patients and a cohort of 19,250 operations relating sedation to adverse events. Cochrane reviews cover preoperative testing and needle block comparisons. The joint college guideline is cited as the governing document without quoting figures from it. The 26 mm threshold, the timing of brainstem anaesthesia, sedative doses and the advice on draping are consensus and author's judgement.
