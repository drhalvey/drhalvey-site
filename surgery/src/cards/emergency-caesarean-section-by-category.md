## What the surgeon is doing and why

The operation is the same lower segment caesarean described on the [elective card](elective-caesarean-section.html). What changes is the reason, the clock and the state of the uterus, and those three things decide the anaesthetic.

The reason is given as a category. The four-grade classification was built by asking anaesthetists and obstetricians to grade real cases and keeping the scheme they agreed on most often: category 1 is an immediate threat to the life of the woman or fetus (cord prolapse, abruption, uterine rupture, a sustained fetal bradycardia, a fetal scalp pH in the acidotic range); category 2 is maternal or fetal compromise that is not immediately life-threatening (a deteriorating cardiotocograph, failure to progress with a pathological trace); category 3 needs early delivery but nobody is compromised (failure to progress with a normal trace, a booked elective case who arrives in labour); category 4 is elective. The widely used audit standards are a decision-to-delivery interval of 30 minutes for category 1 and 75 minutes for category 2. The category is a statement about the fetus or mother, not an instruction about the anaesthetic, and it can change in either direction between the delivery room and the theatre: a bradycardia that recovers with a position change is no longer a category 1.

The clock sets the choice of technique. A working epidural can be extended to a surgical block in about ten minutes if the top-up is started in the room. A spinal from a standing start takes six to eight minutes in practised hands. A rapid sequence induction takes under two. The surgeon needs none of these to be perfect; the surgeon needs to know which one is coming and when the knife can go in.

The uterus has laboured. The lower segment is thinner and better formed than in an elective case if the cervix is fully dilated, but it is also oedematous and friable, the head may be wedged deep in the pelvis, and the hysterotomy is more likely to tear laterally into the uterine vessels or down toward the cervix when the baby is delivered. Hours of oxytocin have down-regulated the receptors, so the uterus contracts less well afterwards, and infection after prolonged rupture of the membranes makes that worse. More blood loss, more uterotonic, and more need for a second cannula follow from the indication alone.

The pain mechanism is the abdominal wall, as for the elective case, with two additions: more tissue handling at speed, and a woman who has been in pain and awake for a day already. Score and trajectory are close to the elective operation, with a slower tail. The evidence base is thinner, because PROSPECT excluded unplanned caesareans from its review.

## Steps

### 1. The call and what the category means
**Why:** The obstetrician states the category and the indication so that every member of the team is working to the same clock.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask three questions in the room: the indication, whether the fetal heart has recovered, and what is running in the epidural. Then decide the technique and say it aloud with a time: "epidural top-up now, knife in ten minutes" or "general anaesthetic, knife in three". For anything other than a category 1 for a non-recovering bradycardia, there is almost always time for a neuraxial technique, and saying so lowers the temperature. Check the airway assessment that was made when the epidural was sited; if none was made, make it now.

### 2. Intrauterine resuscitation on the way
**Why:** Most fetal compromise in labour is reduced oxygen delivery from uterine hyperstimulation, cord compression or maternal hypotension, and the obstetric team can improve all three while the theatre is being opened.
**Anaesthesia:** These are partly your interventions. Full left lateral (or whichever side improves the trace), oxytocin infusion off, a crystalloid bolus, and a vasopressor if the pressure is low after a recent epidural top-up, which is a common and reversible cause of a new bradycardia. Terbutaline 250 micrograms SC or IV stops a hyperstimulated uterus for long enough to move; expect maternal tachycardia and a softer uterus after delivery. For cord prolapse the presenting part is held off the cord by a hand in the vagina, by a head-down or knee-chest position, or by filling the bladder with 500 mL of saline, and none of that is released until the surgeon is scrubbed.

### 3. Choosing between top-up, spinal and general anaesthesia
**Why:** The surgeon can start as soon as the abdomen is anaesthetised; the limiting step in a category 1 delivery is usually the anaesthetic, and everybody in the room knows it.
**Anaesthesia:** The decision turns on what is already in place and how the fetus is doing after resuscitation.

```
 Working epidural (bilateral, few top-ups)  ->  extend it, start in the room
 Poor epidural (unilateral, many top-ups)   ->  remove it; spinal with a
                                                reduced dose, or GA
 No epidural, trace recovered or cat 2-3    ->  spinal
 No epidural, cat 1, trace not recovering   ->  rapid sequence spinal if one
                                                attempt is feasible, else GA
 Coagulopathy, hypovolaemia, sepsis with    ->  GA
 shock, maternal refusal, failed block
```

The meta-analysis of failed epidural conversion identifies the patients who will let you down: more clinician top-ups during labour (odds ratio 3.2), greater urgency (odds ratio 40), and a non-obstetric anaesthetist (odds ratio 4.6). An epidural that has needed rescuing twice in labour is a poor epidural, whatever the pump says. Modelled times for a category 1 case are 100 seconds for a rapid sequence induction and a little over six minutes for a rapid spinal; whether six minutes matters depends on whether the bradycardia has recovered.

### 4. Extending the epidural
**Why:** A catheter that is already in the right place is the fastest route to a surgical block that keeps the mother awake.
**Anaesthesia:** Lidocaine 2% with adrenaline 1:200,000, 15-20 mL in divided doses, with fentanyl 50-100 micrograms. In the meta-analysis of top-up solutions this was faster than bupivacaine or levobupivacaine 0.5% by a mean of 4.5 minutes, bupivacaine and levobupivacaine doubled the need for intraoperative supplementation, ropivacaine 0.75% gave the best quality of block, and fentanyl speeded onset. Some units add sodium bicarbonate 8.4% 2 mL per 20 mL to shorten onset further; it must be mixed fresh and it introduces a drawing-up error at the worst time. Give the first 10 mL in the delivery room after aspirating the catheter, with a vasopressor in your hand and monitoring attached, and travel with the patient; give the rest on the table. This is 400 mg of lidocaine into an epidural space that may contain a vein: watch for tinnitus, perioral tingling or agitation, and know where the lipid emulsion is. Test as for any caesarean (light touch to T5 bilaterally, dense sacral block). If the block is inadequate at ten minutes, do not keep adding volume.

### 5. Rapid sequence spinal
**Why:** For the category 1 case without an epidural, the surgeon and the baby gain more from a mother who is awake and has her own airway, provided the spinal does not cost more time than the fetus has.
**Anaesthesia:** The technique strips the spinal to its essentials: a no-touch approach with gloves only and a single wipe of chlorhexidine, one operator and one attempt (or a strict time limit), hyperbaric bupivacaine 0.5% 2.5 mL with fentanyl 25 micrograms if it is to hand and without it if drawing it up would delay, pre-oxygenation running during the attempt so that conversion to general anaesthesia loses nothing, and surgery starting when the block reaches T10 and is still rising. In the original series the spinal took a median of two minutes to perform and four to work, with three of 25 converted to general anaesthesia before incision. Morphine is omitted from the rapid version by many because it needs a separate ampoule; the price is paid in the pain plan. Left lateral for insertion keeps the uterus off the cava in a compromised fetus and lets an assistant keep the presenting part off a prolapsed cord.

### 6. General anaesthesia when the clock or the block demands it
**Why:** A non-recovering bradycardia, a major abruption, a ruptured uterus or a bleeding praevia cannot wait for a block, and a hypovolaemic or septic mother will not tolerate one.
**Anaesthesia:** The technique and the failed intubation plan are on the [general anaesthesia card](general-anaesthesia-for-caesarean-section-and.html). Three points belong here. First, re-check the fetal heart on the table when the indication is fetal: a recovered trace buys time for a spinal, and an absent heartbeat changes the indication entirely. Second, agree with the obstetrician before induction whether you would wake the mother or proceed if intubation failed; for a category 1 fetal indication the usual answer is to proceed on a supraglottic airway. Third, the surgeon scrubs and drapes before induction so that the induction-to-delivery time is as short as possible.

### 7. Incision to delivery in a laboured uterus
**Why:** In a first-stage caesarean the head is high and delivery is easy. At full dilatation, and especially after a failed instrumental delivery, the head is impacted in the pelvis and has to be disimpacted: pushed up from the vagina by an assistant, lifted with a balloon device, or bypassed by delivering the baby feet first (reverse breech extraction).
**Anaesthesia:** A second-stage caesarean is a different operation from a first-stage one. Ask for it by name. The uterus is clamped around the baby, and the surgeon may want it relaxed: glyceryl trinitrate 50-100 micrograms IV repeated at one-minute intervals (or two sublingual sprays of 400 micrograms each) is the fast option under neuraxial block, with a vasopressor to follow; under general anaesthesia a volatile agent deepened briefly does the same. The manoeuvres pull hard, and an awake mother needs warning. The baby is more likely to need resuscitation, so the neonatal team should already be present.

### 8. Uterotonic and bleeding after labour
**Why:** A uterus that has been driven with oxytocin for hours has fewer available receptors and contracts poorly after delivery; infection makes it worse.
**Anaesthesia:** The uterotonic consensus statement recommends larger doses at intrapartum caesarean than at elective: oxytocin 3 IU over at least 30 seconds followed by an infusion (7.5-15 IU/h), or carbetocin 100 micrograms slowly, and a second-line drug early if tone is poor at two minutes. Have ergometrine and carboprost in the room for every second-stage case. Give TXA 1 g IV at the first suggestion that the loss is more than expected.

### 9. Extensions, angles and the bladder
**Why:** Lateral extension of the hysterotomy tears the ascending branch of the uterine artery at the angle; downward extension runs toward the cervix and vagina behind the bladder. The surgeon exteriorises the uterus to see and suture the tear, and identifies the ureter if the repair is lateral.
**Anaesthesia:** This is where an emergency caesarean turns into a [major haemorrhage](major-obstetric-haemorrhage-and-placenta-accreta.html): bleeding that is surgical, fast and partly hidden in the broad ligament. Second cannula, blood called for, weighed swabs. Exteriorisation and deep sutures in the pelvis are painful under an extended epidural that was only just adequate; alfentanil 250-500 micrograms IV or conversion to general anaesthesia are the honest options, and prolonged difficult surgery is a reason to convert. Haematuria in the catheter bag means the bladder has been entered and adds time.

### 10. Closure and setting up the analgesia
**Why:** Closure follows the elective sequence; a drain is more likely.
**Anaesthesia:** Decide the analgesia by what the anaesthetic was. Epidural top-up: morphine 2-3 mg down the catheter after delivery, then remove the catheter at the end if the platelet count and clotting are not in question. Spinal with morphine: nothing more. Rapid spinal without morphine, or general anaesthesia: bilateral TAP or quadratus lumborum blocks before leaving theatre, which is what PROSPECT recommends when neuraxial opioid has not been given. Paracetamol 1 g IV and an NSAID unless there has been major bleeding, pre-eclampsia with renal impairment or a low platelet count.

### 11. Recovery, review and the conversation afterwards
**Why:** The obstetric priorities are a contracted uterus, measured loss, and a mother reunited with a baby who may have gone to the nursery.
**Anaesthesia:** Emergency cases bleed late more often: fundus and lochia every 15 minutes for the first hour, a repeat haemoglobin if the loss exceeded 1,000 mL, and thromboprophylaxis charted at the higher risk level. Neuraxial morphine monitoring and the 4-hour straight-leg raise check apply as for the elective operation, with one extra caution: a woman who has had an epidural for 12 hours and then 20 mL of lidocaine can have a dense block for longer, and a block that is not regressing by 4 hours after the last dose still needs a doctor at the bedside. See the mother the next day. A category 1 delivery, pain during surgery or a general anaesthetic are each associated with distress afterwards, and an explanation of what happened and why is part of the anaesthetic.

## Pain plan

No procedure-specific guideline covers the unplanned caesarean: PROSPECT excluded these cases and says so. The mechanism is the same abdominal wall incision and contracting uterus, so the defensible default is to apply the elective recommendations by whichever route the anaesthetic allows. That is the author's extrapolation.

After an epidural top-up, epidural morphine 2-3 mg is the equivalent of the intrathecal dose and lasts a similar 12-24 hours, with the same pruritus, nausea and need for respiratory observations. After a spinal, intrathecal morphine 100 micrograms as usual. After a rapid sequence spinal without morphine or a general anaesthetic, the woman has no long-acting neuraxial opioid and will have wound pain as soon as the local anaesthetic recedes at 2-3 hours: bilateral TAP or quadratus lumborum blocks, a short course of oral opioid charted regularly for the first 24 hours, and occasionally intravenous patient-controlled analgesia for the first night. In every case regular paracetamol and an NSAID are the base, dexamethasone 8 mg IV is given after delivery, and opioids are for rescue.

Keeping the epidural catheter for a postoperative infusion is rarely worth it. It delays mobilisation and thromboprophylaxis, and neuraxial morphine does the same job without a pump. The exceptions are the opioid-tolerant woman, and the woman whose clotting is uncertain after a large bleed or with HELLP syndrome, in whom the catheter stays until it can be removed safely.

Rebound is moderate. It comes at 12-24 hours when the neuraxial morphine fades, or at 12-18 hours when a fascial plane block wears off overnight. The oral regimen has to be charted regularly rather than as needed before the woman leaves recovery. Emergency patients have more reasons for an NSAID to be withheld (bleeding, acute kidney injury, pre-eclampsia), and when it is, the opioid requirement roughly doubles and the plan needs to say so. Escalation triggers are those of the elective card, plus two: disproportionate pain with a falling haemoglobin suggests a broad ligament or rectus sheath haematoma, and pain with fever and a tender uterus in a woman who laboured with ruptured membranes is endometritis.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The category drifts. Re-ask it on arrival in theatre. A recovered trace turns a general anaesthetic into a spinal; a second bradycardia during a slow spinal turns it back.
- Local anaesthetic toxicity. A fast, large-volume top-up is the highest-dose moment in obstetric anaesthesia. Aspirate, fractionate, and keep talking to the patient.
- Spinal after a failed epidural top-up. The epidural volume compresses the dural sac and the spinal spreads higher than expected; reduce the bupivacaine dose by about 20-30%, keep her sitting slightly longer, and be ready for a high block. If 20 mL has just gone into the epidural, general anaesthesia is often the safer choice. This is the author's judgement; the evidence is case series.
- Second-stage caesarean and the impacted head. More blood loss, more uterine relaxation, more extensions, more neonatal resuscitation. Two cannulae before the start.
- Failed instrumental delivery. The spinal or top-up given for the trial of forceps is usually adequate for the caesarean; see [instrumental delivery](instrumental-delivery-manual-removal-of-placenta.html).
- Chorioamnionitis and sepsis. Atony, coagulopathy and vasodilatation. Neuraxial block is reasonable in a treated, haemodynamically stable woman and unwise in septic shock.
- Abruption and uterine rupture. Concealed loss and early coagulopathy with a low fibrinogen; general anaesthesia, blood early, and the haemorrhage card.
- Pre-eclampsia. See the [pre-eclampsia card](pre-eclampsia-eclampsia-and-the-parturient.html): spinal is preferred if the platelet count allows, and the pressor response to intubation has to be obtunded if it does not.
- Invasive monitoring and critical care. An arterial line and a second anaesthetist once loss passes 1,500 mL and is continuing; high dependency care for loss over 2,000 mL, a return to theatre, sepsis with vasopressor requirement or severe pre-eclampsia.
- The awake partner. In a category 1 case under general anaesthesia the partner waits outside; someone should be assigned to tell them what is happening.

## Key sources

The classification is a consensus instrument validated for agreement, not for outcome, and the 30 and 75 minute intervals are audit standards rather than thresholds below which babies are safe. The choice of top-up solution rests on a meta-analysis of 11 small trials. The rapid sequence spinal is supported by case series and a decision analysis, not by a trial against general anaesthesia. The analgesic plan is extrapolated from the elective PROSPECT guideline, and the advice on spinal dosing after a failed top-up is the author's judgement.
