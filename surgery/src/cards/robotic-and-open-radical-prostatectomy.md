## What the surgeon is doing and why

Radical prostatectomy removes the whole prostate, with its capsule and the seminal vesicles, for cancer confined to the gland. That is a different operation from the [transurethral procedures](transurethral-resection-of-the-prostate-and.html), which core out the middle and leave the shell. Taking the whole gland means dividing the urinary tract twice (at the bladder neck above and at the membranous urethra below), removing the segment between, and sewing the bladder back onto the urethral stump. Everything that makes the operation difficult lies within a few millimetres of the gland: the dorsal venous complex running over its anterior surface, the external sphincter at its apex, the rectum behind, and the cavernous nerves in the neurovascular bundles along its posterolateral edges. Continence depends on the sphincter and urethral length; erections depend on the bundles. The surgeon is trading cancer clearance against those two functions throughout, and decides side by side whether to spare the nerves.

The prostate sits at the bottom of a narrow bony funnel. The open retropubic operation reaches it through a lower midline extraperitoneal incision and works by feel and loupe at the depth of the pelvis, with the dorsal veins bleeding into the field. The robotic operation reaches it with long wristed instruments under tenfold magnification and uses the pneumoperitoneum to tamponade the veins, which is why blood loss fell and why most prostatectomies in Australia are now robotic. To keep the bowel out of the pelvis the patient is tipped 25-45 degrees head-down and held there, with the robot's arms docked to the ports, for the whole console time. The randomised trial from Brisbane found no difference in urinary or sexual function at 12 weeks between the two approaches, with fewer intra-operative adverse events (2% against 8%) and post-operative complications (4% against 9%) in the robotic arm.

So the anaesthetic for the robotic operation is the management of a position. Steep head-down with gas in the abdomen pushes the diaphragm up, halves compliance, moves the carina towards the tube, raises central venous, intracranial and intraocular pressure, and swells the face, conjunctiva and larynx with dependent oedema. The patient is locked to a machine and wrapped; nothing can be reached. The surgical field wants a dry pelvis, which means little urine and so little fluid until the bladder is reconnected.

The pain logic is the opposite of the physiology: this is a small operation for pain. Port sites, a 3-4 cm extraction wound, peritoneal stretch and some shoulder-tip pain make it a laparoscopic mechanism that is largely over in 48 hours. The pelvic dissection itself is nearly silent. What men complain of is the catheter: a balloon on the trigone above a fresh anastomosis, with detrusor spasm. The open operation adds a midline fascial incision and behaves like a small laparotomy for two or three days, a score of 3.

## Steps

### 1. Induction, lines and airway
**Why:** After draping and docking, the patient's arms, chest and head are under the robot and out of reach for hours.
**Anaesthesia:** Two reliable cannulae with extension lines and three-way taps brought to the head end; an arterial line now if there is any doubt (significant cardiac or lung disease, BMI over 35-40, an anticipated long node dissection), because one cannot be placed later. Tracheal tube, taped and tied, with the depth noted. Orogastric tube to deflate the stomach. Temperature probe. Eyes taped closed and padded, since conjunctival oedema and the camera arm both threaten the cornea. If intrathecal morphine is the plan it goes in before induction.

### 2. Positioning and the test tilt
**Why:** The surgeon needs the legs apart for the robot (low lithotomy or split-leg table), the arms tucked so the side arms can swing, and the bowel falling out of the pelvis.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient must not slide. Use a high-friction mattress or vacuum bean bag in contact with skin; shoulder braces compress the brachial plexus against a patient sliding into them and are the cause of the reported plexopathies. Arms neutral, padded, thumbs up; legs with no pressure on the peroneal nerve or calf, and hip flexion kept modest, because hours of elevated legs with a low perfusion pressure is the recipe for well-leg compartment syndrome (see [positioning](../a/a14-positioning.html)). Then tilt to the working angle before draping and watch: airway pressures, saturation, any slide, tube position by auscultation. A patient who cannot be ventilated in the test tilt will not improve with gas in the abdomen; negotiate a lesser angle now.

### 3. Pneumoperitoneum, ports and docking
**Why:** Gas creates the working space and compresses pelvic veins; six ports go across the upper abdomen; the robot is driven in and its arms locked to the ports.
**Anaesthesia:** Insufflation can produce vagal bradycardia or asystole; have the surgeon release the gas while you give an anticholinergic. Then the combined load arrives: plateau pressure rises, compliance halves, end-tidal carbon dioxide climbs. Recheck tube position, because the commonest cause of desaturation in the first ten minutes is an endobronchial tube. Set PEEP, lengthen inspiration, accept an end-tidal carbon dioxide in the mid 40s to low 50s mmHg in a healthy patient. Mean arterial pressure usually rises with head-down and gas; cardiac output is preserved in fit men and may not be in a poor ventricle. From docking onwards the table cannot move unless it is one linked to the robot, and neuromuscular block must be dependable (the principles are in [the laparoscopy chapter](../a/a01-laparoscopy-pneumoperitoneum.html)).

### 4. Dropping the bladder and opening the retropubic space
**Why:** The bladder is taken down from the anterior abdominal wall to expose the space of Retzius and the front of the prostate; the endopelvic fascia is opened on each side.
**Anaesthesia:** Quiet. This is when to settle the fluid plan: run a low maintenance rate, treat hypotension with a vasopressor and not volume, and tell the surgeon the running total if asked. Urine output cannot be measured for most of the case because the bladder is open.

### 5. Dorsal venous complex
**Why:** The large veins over the apex are ligated or divided and oversewn. In open surgery this is the main bleed; robotically the gas holds it until the stitch goes in.
**Anaesthesia:** The one moment of predictable blood loss. If the surgeon lowers the insufflation pressure to check haemostasis the veins declare themselves. With open venous channels under gas pressure, carbon dioxide embolism is possible: a sudden fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide and pressure is treated by stopping insufflation, 100% oxygen and support.

### 6. Bladder neck, seminal vesicles and the posterior plane
**Why:** The bladder neck is divided, the catheter is pulled up as a handle, the vasa and seminal vesicles are freed, and Denonvilliers' fascia is opened to separate the prostate from the rectum.
**Anaesthesia:** The ureteric orifices are close to the cut; some surgeons ask for a dye or simply watch for efflux, which is another reason they do not want a diuresis yet. The rectum is at risk in the posterior plane, more so after previous radiotherapy or resection; a recognised injury is repaired at once and you add metronidazole.

### 7. Pedicles, nerve-sparing and the apex
**Why:** The vascular pedicles are clipped and cut without diathermy where the nerves are being spared, the bundles are peeled off the capsule, and the urethra is divided at the apex preserving as much length as possible.
**Anaesthesia:** Athermal dissection oozes and the surgeon tolerates it. Any movement here costs function, so check the block. The specimen is free at the end of this step and is bagged.

### 8. Pelvic lymph node dissection
**Why:** For intermediate and high-risk cancer the obturator and iliac nodes are cleared from the external iliac vein to the obturator nerve on each side.
**Anaesthesia:** Adds 30-60 minutes of head-down time. The external iliac vein is the vessel that causes sudden large bleeding in this operation, and it can entrain gas. The obturator nerve is under direct vision; diathermy near it produces an adductor twitch if the block is light. Node dissection is what raises venous thromboembolism and lymphocele risk afterwards.

### 9. Vesicourethral anastomosis and leak test
**Why:** The bladder neck is sewn to the urethral stump with a running suture over a new catheter and tested by filling the bladder.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon wants a dry field until the last stitch. Once the anastomosis is watertight, give the fluid you have withheld (a litre or so of balanced crystalloid over the remainder of the case), expecting a brisk diuresis that the surgeon will want to see through the catheter. Tell recovery that early urine output was unmeasured by design.

### 10. Undocking, levelling and closure
**Why:** The robot is withdrawn, the specimen extracted through an enlarged port site, and the wounds closed.
**Anaesthesia:** Level the table as soon as the robot is clear; every minute counts for the face and eyes. Intraocular pressure in one series was a mean 13.3 mmHg above the awake baseline at the end of the head-down period, and its only independent predictors were duration and end-tidal carbon dioxide, both of which you influence. TAP blocks go in now under ultrasound, or the surgeon infiltrates the ports and places a TAP block under vision. Give paracetamol and the NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor unless the surgeon objects.

### 11. Emergence
**Why:** The surgeon wants no coughing against a fresh anastomosis and no pulling on the catheter.
**Anaesthesia:** Look at the face. After a long steep case the conjunctivae, tongue and periorbital tissues are swollen and the larynx may be too; sit the patient 30 degrees head-up, wait, confirm a cuff leak, and reverse the block fully before extubation. Stridor after a four-hour case is not rare enough to ignore. Subcutaneous emphysema in the neck and chest raises carbon dioxide for a while after the gas is out. A man who wakes agitated and trying to void has catheter-related bladder discomfort, not delirium.

### 12. Recovery and the ward
**Why:** The anastomosis is protected by the catheter for 7-10 days; a pelvic drain is left by some surgeons and not others.
**Anaesthesia:** Pain scores of 2-4 on movement on the day, mostly port-site and shoulder-tip, falling quickly. Check the eyes: a painful, gritty eye is a corneal abrasion and needs lubricant and review; painless visual loss is an emergency. New leg pain out of proportion with a tense calf after a long lithotomy case is compartment syndrome. Arm weakness or numbness is a positioning plexopathy and should be documented early.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is laparoscopic and short, so the plan is a multimodal base with a truncal block or infiltration and very little opioid. PROSPECT's 2021 update recommends paracetamol with an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor as basic analgesia for all prostatectomies and a bilateral TAP block as the first-choice regional technique for laparoscopic and robotic surgery. For open surgery it recommends wound infiltration and says intravenous lidocaine should be considered, with TAP results conflicting. Dexamethasone at induction serves nausea and analgesia together. Oral opioid is for rescue and most robotic patients need a handful of doses.

Intrathecal morphine is the alternative strategy. One randomised trial in 50 robotic patients found better pain control in recovery and less intravenous opioid, with the benefit fading by 6-24 hours once opioid use was accounted for. The author's judgement is that for a routine robotic case the price (pruritus, nausea, respiratory monitoring overnight, a spinal in an anticoagulated-tomorrow patient) outweighs a few hours of advantage, and that it is better kept for the open operation or the opioid-tolerant patient. Epidural analgesia has no place in the robotic operation and, in the author's judgement, is hard to justify for the open one now that infiltration, lidocaine and intrathecal morphine are available.

The catheter is the part of the plan most often forgotten. Bladder spasm is treated with an antimuscarinic, which the surgeon usually continues to catheter removal, and with the NSAID. A pudendal or dorsal penile nerve block reduced catheter discomfort for 8-12 hours in pooled small trials and is an option for the man who has previously tolerated a catheter badly.

Escalation triggers: severe abdominal pain with distension on day 1 is a urine leak, bleeding or bowel injury and needs the surgeon; shoulder-tip pain alone is gas and settles; calf pain is compartment syndrome or thrombosis until examined.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Open retropubic prostatectomy. Supine, extraperitoneal, 500-1,500 mL blood loss, no head-down physiology. Spinal or general anaesthesia or both; pain a band higher. Now uncommon and often chosen because the robotic route is unsuitable.
- Extraperitoneal and Retzius-sparing robotic approaches. Less tilt in some hands, more subcutaneous emphysema and carbon dioxide absorption with the extraperitoneal route.
- Who should not be tipped steeply for hours. Raised intracranial pressure, a ventriculoperitoneal shunt, advanced glaucoma, severe pulmonary hypertension or right ventricular failure, and severe obesity with poor compliance. Discuss a lesser angle, lower gas pressure, or a different treatment before the day.
- The emergency within the case. Major bleeding from an iliac vein, or cardiac arrest, with the robot docked: gas off, instruments out, undock, level, then resuscitate. The drill takes under a minute when rehearsed and several when not. This is the point at which blood, a second pair of hands and critical care are needed; the elective course needs none of them.
- Salvage prostatectomy after radiotherapy. Fused planes, rectal injury, more bleeding, longer.
- Obesity. Slides more, ventilates worse, swells more. Arterial line, a firm anti-slide plan, and an honest pre-operative conversation about the angle.
- Fluids. Restriction is right until the anastomosis and wrong after it; an unreplaced deficit plus NSAID in an older man is how creatinine rises on day 1.
- Rhabdomyolysis and compartment syndrome. Long case, heavy patient, lithotomy, hypotension. Dark urine or leg pain is checked with a creatine kinase.
- Venous thromboembolism. Cancer, pelvic surgery, node dissection; prophylaxis practice varies and should be agreed, not assumed.

## Key sources

The analgesic recommendations come from a current PROSPECT guideline built on 35 randomised trials, which is as good as procedure-specific evidence gets. The position physiology rests on small observational studies, of which the intraocular pressure series is the clearest; the rare catastrophic outcomes (ischaemic optic neuropathy, laryngeal oedema, compartment syndrome) are known from case reports, so the advice to limit time and carbon dioxide is reasoning from mechanism. The fluid restriction strategy is surgical convention without a trial behind it. The intrathecal morphine verdict is the author's judgement on a single small trial.
