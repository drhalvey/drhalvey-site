## What the surgeon is doing and why

The operation is a caesarean section or an assisted vaginal birth, and the surgical steps are those of the [elective](elective-caesarean-section.html) and [emergency](emergency-caesarean-section-by-category.html) cards. This card exists because in two groups of women the delivery is a treatment or a stress test, and the anaesthetic is built around the mother's circulation.

Pre-eclampsia is a disease of the placenta that shows itself in the mother's endothelium. A poorly implanted placenta releases anti-angiogenic factors; maternal vessels constrict and leak. The result is hypertension with a contracted plasma volume, proteinuria, and variable organ involvement: kidneys (oliguria, rising creatinine), liver (epigastric pain, raised transaminases), blood (falling platelets, haemolysis: together with the liver, HELLP syndrome), brain (headache, visual disturbance, hyperreflexia, seizures: eclampsia), lungs (oedema) and the fetus (growth restriction, abruption). The only cure is removal of the placenta. The obstetrician's sequence is therefore fixed: control the pressure, prevent seizures with magnesium, then deliver, by induction if there is time and the cervix is favourable and by caesarean if there is not. Delivery is never so urgent that it precedes stabilisation of the mother, with the single exception of a dying fetus in a mother who is otherwise controlled.

What kills women with pre-eclampsia is intracranial haemorrhage from uncontrolled systolic pressure, and pulmonary oedema from leaky capillaries meeting intravenous fluid. Both are partly anaesthetic events: one happens at laryngoscopy and extubation, the other in the 48 hours after delivery when extravascular fluid returns to a circulation that can now hold it.

The parturient with cardiac disease faces a different arithmetic. Pregnancy raises cardiac output by 40-50%, lowers systemic vascular resistance and expands blood volume; labour adds more with every contraction, and delivery returns several hundred millilitres of uterine blood to the circulation at the moment aortocaval compression is released. A heart with a fixed obstruction, a failing ventricle or a pulmonary circulation that cannot accept more flow may cope until that moment. The obstetric plan, made by a pregnancy heart team well before term, usually favours vaginal birth with an early epidural and a shortened second stage, because it avoids the fluid shifts and bleeding of surgery; caesarean is for obstetric reasons and for a short list of cardiac ones (dilated aorta, decompensated failure, labour on oral anticoagulants).

The pain mechanism is unchanged from any caesarean: abdominal wall and contracting uterus. What changes is which analgesics are permitted. Physiology is in [obstetric essentials](../a/a09-obstetric-essentials.html).

## Steps

### 1. Severity assessment in pre-eclampsia
**Why:** The obstetric team classifies the disease by pressure, symptoms and bloods to decide how fast to deliver.
**Anaesthesia:** Read the same data for different reasons. The pressure trend and what it took to control it tell you how the induction will go. Headache, visual symptoms and brisk reflexes with clonus mean the brain is irritable. The platelet count and its trend decide the block. Creatinine and urine output decide the magnesium dose and the NSAID. Oxygen saturation on air below 95% or a respiratory rate above 20 means pulmonary oedema until excluded. Facial and laryngeal oedema, a change in voice or stridor predict a difficult airway. Look at the fluid balance chart: these women arrive in theatre two litres positive more often than they should.

### 2. Blood pressure control comes first
**Why:** Antihypertensive treatment prevents maternal stroke; it does nothing for the disease.
**Anaesthesia:** Both the international and the Australasian guidelines treat a systolic of 160 mmHg or a diastolic of 110 mmHg as an emergency requiring treatment within minutes to an hour. The agents are oral nifedipine 10-20 mg, intravenous labetalol 20 mg repeated or doubled every 10 minutes to effect, and intravenous hydralazine 5-10 mg; an infusion follows if boluses do not hold it. Aim for 130-150 systolic and 80-100 diastolic, not lower, because placental flow follows pressure. Place an arterial line if the pressure has needed intravenous treatment and theatre is next. No anaesthetic is started with the systolic above 160 unless the mother's life depends on immediate delivery.

### 3. Magnesium
**Why:** Magnesium is the anticonvulsant of choice for prevention and treatment of eclampsia; it is a cerebral vasodilator and membrane stabiliser, not an antihypertensive.
**Anaesthesia:** In the Magpie trial it more than halved the risk of eclampsia (0.8% against 1.9%). Loading dose 4 g IV over 20 minutes, then 1 g/h, continued through the operation and for 24 hours afterwards. It is renally cleared: with oliguria or a raised creatinine, halve the infusion and measure levels. Toxicity is clinical first: loss of the patellar reflex, then drowsiness and a falling respiratory rate, then arrest. The antidote is calcium gluconate 10% 10 mL IV. For you it has three effects: it prolongs non-depolarising neuromuscular block substantially (reduce the dose and use a nerve stimulator), it blunts the response to vasopressors a little, and it relaxes the uterus a little, so have the second-line uterotonic ready.

### 4. Choosing the anaesthetic in pre-eclampsia
**Why:** The obstetrician needs delivery within a time set by the category; the mother needs the technique least likely to cause a pressure surge or a lost airway.
**Anaesthesia:** Spinal anaesthesia is the first choice in severe pre-eclampsia, including for most urgent cases. The constraint is the platelet count. The interdisciplinary consensus statement regards haematoma risk as very low at 70 x 10^9/L or above in hypertensive disorders, provided the count is not falling quickly and clotting is otherwise normal; a count from six hours ago is acceptable in stable disease and not in HELLP, where it can halve in that time. Between 50 and 70 the decision weighs a single fine-needle spinal against a predictably difficult airway, and often still favours the spinal. A labour epidural sited early, while the count permits, removes the question; this is the reason to offer one at the start of an induction for pre-eclampsia.

### 5. Conducting the spinal
**Why:** The surgical requirement is the same block as any caesarean.
**Anaesthesia:** Standard intrathecal doses. No fluid preload and no co-load beyond what carries the drugs. The old fear of precipitous hypotension was wrong: in the invasive haemodynamic study of severe pre-eclampsia, cardiac output did not change after the spinal, and the consensus statement on vasopressors notes less hypotension and lower vasopressor needs than in healthy women, because the high resistance is humoral rather than sympathetic. Use phenylephrine in small boluses of 25-50 micrograms, or an infusion started at half the usual rate, titrated to keep systolic above about 80% of the pre-spinal value; overshoot is as harmful here as hypotension. Continue the magnesium.

### 6. General anaesthesia when it cannot be avoided
**Why:** Platelets too low, a rapidly falling count, coagulopathy, abruption with a compromised fetus, pulmonary oedema with hypoxia, or a woman who has not regained consciousness after a seizure.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the highest-risk anaesthetic on the labour ward and it is planned as such. Arterial line before induction. Head-up, thorough pre-oxygenation. Obtund the pressor response with a drug given before the induction agent: remifentanil 1 microgram/kg or alfentanil 10-20 micrograms/kg IV, with labetalol 10-20 mg IV or a magnesium bolus of 2 g if the pressure is still high; tell the neonatal team an opioid has been given. Videolaryngoscope, a 6.0-6.5 mm tube for an oedematous larynx, and the failed intubation plan from the [general anaesthesia card](general-anaesthesia-for-caesarean-section-and.html). Reduce the non-depolarising relaxant dose and monitor it. Extubation is the second surge: treat it the same way, and extubate awake and head-up only after a leak around the cuff has been demonstrated if the face was swollen.

### 7. Delivery, uterotonic and fluid
**Why:** Once the placenta is out, the source of the disease has gone, but the uterus still has to contract.
**Anaesthesia:** Oxytocin by small slow dose (1-2 IU over a minute, then an infusion in a small volume): the haemodynamic study recorded marked transient hypotension and tachycardia after oxytocin in these women. Ergometrine is contraindicated because it can produce severe hypertension. Carboprost and misoprostol are acceptable second-line agents. Run total fluids at about 80 mL/h including the drug infusions, replace blood loss with blood, and do not chase a low urine output with fluid boluses in the first 24 hours; oliguria in pre-eclampsia is usual and self-correcting, and pulmonary oedema is not.

### 8. Eclampsia
**Why:** A generalised seizure in pregnancy or the puerperium is eclampsia until proven otherwise; a substantial proportion occur after delivery.
**Anaesthesia:** Left lateral, oxygen, protect from injury; most seizures stop within two minutes. Magnesium 4 g IV over 5-10 minutes if not already loaded, a further 2 g for a recurrent seizure, then the infusion. Benzodiazepines are second-line and depress the airway. The fetal heart slows during the seizure and recovers with maternal oxygenation: the bradycardia is not an indication for immediate caesarean under general anaesthesia in a hypoxic, hypertensive, post-ictal woman. Stabilise first, control the pressure, check the platelet count, then deliver. A woman who is awake and orientated afterwards can have a spinal. One who remains obtunded, has focal signs or keeps seizing needs her airway secured with full obtundation of the pressor response, and brain imaging after delivery.

### 9. After delivery in pre-eclampsia
**Why:** The disease resolves over days, not at the moment of delivery; pressure often peaks on day 3-5.
**Anaesthesia:** High dependency care for 24 hours on magnesium, with hourly reflexes, respiratory rate, saturation and urine output. Mobilisation of oedema fluid makes the first 48 hours the peak time for pulmonary oedema. Platelets usually reach their lowest point on day 1-2 after delivery in HELLP: an epidural catheter comes out only when the count is back above the insertion threshold, and thromboprophylaxis is timed around that. NSAIDs are commonly withheld while the creatinine is raised, urine output is low or platelets are under 70; when none of those applies, most units give them.

### 10. Cardiac disease: classification and plan
**Why:** The pregnancy heart team (cardiologist, obstetrician, anaesthetist) sets the mode, place and timing of delivery by lesion and functional state.
**Anaesthesia:** The European guideline uses the modified WHO classification: class I carries no detectable extra risk; class II and III rise through repaired and unrepaired congenital lesions, mechanical valves, moderate ventricular impairment and aortic disease; class IV (pulmonary arterial hypertension, severe systemic ventricular dysfunction, severe mitral stenosis, severe symptomatic aortic stenosis, a markedly dilated aorta) is a group in which pregnancy carries a very high risk of maternal death and delivery belongs in a centre with cardiac surgery and mechanical support. The anaesthetic review named the same four lesion groups as the main sources of mortality. Before delivery you want the most recent echocardiogram, the rhythm history, the anticoagulation plan with dates, and a written agreement on monitoring, uterotonics and where she goes afterwards.

### 11. Cardiac disease: goals by lesion and the technique
**Why:** The surgical requirement is unchanged; the route to it is slower.
**Anaesthesia:** The technique follows from what the lesion cannot tolerate.

```
 Stenosis (mitral, aortic), HOCM   keep rate slow, sinus rhythm, SVR and
                                   preload up; avoid tachycardia above all
 Regurgitation, dilated or         afterload reduction helps; avoid
 peripartum cardiomyopathy         bradycardia, depressants and fluid load
 Pulmonary hypertension, shunts    keep SVR up and PVR down: no hypoxia,
                                   hypercarbia, acidosis or pain
 Aortopathy (Marfan, bicuspid)     control pressure and rate; no surges
```

For all but the mildest lesions: arterial line first, then a slowly titrated epidural (3-5 mL increments of lidocaine 2% or ropivacaine 0.75% over 20-30 minutes) or a low-dose combined spinal-epidural (bupivacaine 5-7.5 mg with fentanyl, extended through the catheter), with a phenylephrine infusion running to hold resistance. A single-shot full-dose spinal is avoided in fixed-output lesions because the fall in resistance is abrupt. General anaesthesia is for decompensated failure, therapeutic anticoagulation that could not be stopped, or the woman who cannot lie flat: an opioid-heavy induction with an arterial line in, and transoesophageal echocardiography if the centre has it. Regional analgesia for labour follows the same slow-titration rule and is started early.

### 12. Cardiac disease: delivery and the hours after
**Why:** Delivery ends aortocaval compression and returns uterine blood to the circulation; the uterus must still contract.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the most dangerous period for a stenotic valve, a failing ventricle or a pulmonary hypertensive circulation. The uterotonic consensus statement warns that women with cardiac disease can be very sensitive to these drugs: give oxytocin as a dilute infusion or in increments of 0.5-1 IU, never as a bolus of 5 IU; avoid ergometrine (coronary and systemic vasoconstriction) and avoid carboprost in pulmonary hypertension and in shunts. A small dose of furosemide after delivery is used by some heart teams in mitral stenosis and cardiomyopathy. Monitor in coronary or high dependency care for at least 24 hours, and 48-72 for class III and IV lesions, because pulmonary oedema and arrhythmia peak after the event rather than during it.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is the ordinary caesarean wound, so the strategy is the elective one adjusted for what each disease takes away. No PROSPECT recommendation addresses these subgroups, and what follows is consensus practice and the author's judgement.

In pre-eclampsia with an adequate platelet count: spinal with intrathecal morphine 100 micrograms, regular paracetamol, dexamethasone after delivery, and an NSAID if creatinine, urine output and platelets permit. When the NSAID is withheld the woman loses the most effective drug for afterpains and will need roughly twice the rescue opioid, so chart it accordingly and review at 24 hours with fresh bloods to see whether the NSAID can start. After general anaesthesia for low platelets, fascial plane blocks carry the same bleeding caution as the spinal that was declined: superficial blocks such as TAP are reasonable at counts where a neuraxial block was not, on the grounds that the site is compressible, and surgeon wound infiltration is an alternative. Opioid patient-controlled analgesia covers the first night. Remember that magnesium is sedative and that a woman on magnesium, an opioid infusion and intrathecal morphine is observed hourly for good reason.

In cardiac disease the epidural placed for the anaesthetic can deliver morphine 2-3 mg before removal, or run as a low-dose infusion for 24 hours where pain-driven tachycardia would be dangerous (stenotic lesions, pulmonary hypertension, aortopathy); that is the group in whom a postoperative catheter is selectively justified, provided the anticoagulation plan allows for its removal. NSAIDs are avoided in heart failure and with anticoagulation.

Rebound is moderate, at 12-24 hours as neuraxial morphine fades. Escalation triggers are disease-specific: new epigastric or right upper quadrant pain after delivery in pre-eclampsia is the liver (HELLP, subcapsular haematoma) and not the wound; headache with visual symptoms is imminent eclampsia or, if postural, a dural puncture; breathlessness in either group is pulmonary oedema until examined.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Critical care thresholds. Arterial line and high dependency care for: pressure needing intravenous treatment, general anaesthesia in severe disease, pulmonary oedema, eclampsia, HELLP with platelets under 50, and every modified WHO class III or IV lesion. Intensive care for ventilation, persistent reduced consciousness or mechanical support.
- Magnesium toxicity in renal impairment presents as a sleepy woman with a slow respiratory rate on the postnatal ward; stop the infusion, give calcium, measure the level.
- Magnesium and neuromuscular block. A standard intubating dose of rocuronium can last hours; sugammadex reverses it, neostigmine may not be enough.
- Abruption. Pre-eclampsia predisposes to it; a hard tender uterus and fetal bradycardia mean concealed bleeding and early coagulopathy.
- HELLP without much hypertension. The platelet count and liver enzymes define it; the pressure may be unimpressive. Check the count again before removing an epidural catheter.
- The fluid-restricted oliguric patient. Resist the fluid challenge. A rising creatinine with a normal potassium needs time.
- Peripartum cardiomyopathy presents as breathlessness late in pregnancy or after delivery and is mistaken for pre-eclampsia, asthma or anxiety; the two can coexist. An echocardiogram is the test.
- Anticoagulated women. Mechanical valves and recent thrombosis need a dated plan for the last dose, the neuraxial window and the restart; an unplanned labour on therapeutic anticoagulation means no neuraxial block and a general anaesthetic if operative delivery is needed.
- Arrhythmia. Atrial fibrillation in mitral stenosis is poorly tolerated; cardioversion is safe in pregnancy and is done early.
- Pulmonary hypertension. Maternal mortality remains high even in expert hands; these women are delivered in centres with pulmonary vasodilators and extracorporeal support, and no single anaesthetic technique has been shown to be safer.

## Key sources

Magnesium is supported by one of the largest trials in obstetrics. The safety of spinal anaesthesia in severe pre-eclampsia rests on small haemodynamic studies and on wide experience, and the platelet threshold on a consensus built from observational data with very few haematomas to count. The opioid doses for obtunding laryngoscopy, the fluid restriction figure and the handling of NSAIDs are standard teaching, not drawn from a source in the list. For cardiac disease the guidance is expert consensus by lesion; the slow-titration neuraxial approach and the uterotonic increments are standard teaching without comparative trials.
