## What the surgeon is doing and why

The nose is a pair of narrow passages divided by a septum of cartilage and bone and lined on each lateral wall by three turbinates, which warm and humidify air and whose erectile mucosa sets nasal resistance. The paranasal sinuses drain into the nose through small openings under the middle turbinate (the ostiomeatal complex). Three related problems bring patients to this list. A deviated septum obstructs airflow mechanically. Enlarged inferior turbinates do the same by bulk. Chronic rhinosinusitis, with or without polyps, blocks the sinus ostia so that mucus stagnates behind them.

Septoplasty corrects the first. Through an incision just inside one nostril the surgeon lifts the lining (mucoperichondrium and mucoperiosteum) off both sides of the septum, removes or reshapes the bent cartilage and bone, and lays the lining back, often quilted with a suture or held with silicone splints. Turbinate reduction shrinks the inferior turbinates by diathermy, coblation, microdebrider or partial resection. Functional endoscopic sinus surgery works under a rigid endoscope held in one hand, with instruments in the other: the uncinate process is removed, the maxillary ostium widened, and the ethmoid air cells opened from front to back, with the sphenoid and frontal sinuses added as disease dictates. The aim is to restore ventilation and drainage and to let topical steroid reach the mucosa, not to strip it.

The operating corridor is bounded laterally by the lamina papyracea, a paper-thin plate over the orbit, and above by the skull base, which at the lateral lamella of the cribriform plate is among the thinnest bone in the body. The anterior ethmoidal artery crosses the roof, the optic nerve and internal carotid artery bulge into the sphenoid. The surgeon navigates by anatomy seen through a 4 mm endoscope, and a small amount of blood on the lens or pooling in the field removes that view. This is why the anaesthetic for a physiologically trivial operation is specified so closely: the mucosa is among the most vascular in the body, bleeding is capillary and venous, and it responds to venous pressure, heart rate, arterial pressure and vasoconstrictor.

The pain logic: raw mucosa and thin bone with no external wound gives a score of 2, the mucosal mechanism without the repeated provocation that makes a tonsil bed a 5. The complaints are pressure, congestion and headache from packs and crusting. Septal work with osteotomies, or a full septorhinoplasty, adds bony pain for a few days.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative review
**Why:** The surgeon is treating an airway disease that often does not stop at the nose.
**Anaesthesia:** Polyp disease travels with asthma, and the triad of polyps, asthma and aspirin sensitivity (aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease) means NSAIDs are avoided unless the patient is known to tolerate them; paracetamol and selective COX-2 inhibitors are usually tolerated. Confirm asthma control and that the preoperative oral steroid course some surgeons prescribe to shrink polyps has been taken. Obstructive sleep apnoea is common in the septoplasty population and gets worse for a few days with the nose packed. Anticoagulants, antiplatelets, fish oil and herbal supplements matter more here than their effect on measured blood loss would suggest. Know from the CT report or the surgeon whether this is revision surgery, whether the skull base or lamina is already dehiscent, and whether image guidance is planned, since it adds a headband or tracker and set-up time.

### 2. Induction and airway
**Why:** The surgeon stands at the patient's right shoulder and needs the face clear and the tube out of the way of the instruments.
**Anaesthesia:** Either a flexible reinforced laryngeal mask or a south-facing preformed tracheal tube, secured in the midline. The mask sits over the larynx like a lid and keeps blood running down the posterior pharyngeal wall out of the glottis: on fibreoptic inspection at the end of nasal surgery the glottis or trachea was blood-stained in about one in five patients with a mask and more than four in five with a tube. It also gives a smoother emergence. The tube is the better choice for reflux, obesity, long revision cases, a prone-to-move field where relaxant is wanted, and anything approaching the skull base. Throat packs are no longer recommended as routine: there is no evidence that they reduce aspiration or nausea, and retained packs have caused airway obstruction and death. If the surgeon wants one, follow a protocol: a visible tail or label, entry on the count and on the board, and a named person to confirm removal before extubation.

### 3. Position and topical vasoconstriction
**Why:** Head-up tilt lowers venous pressure in the nasal mucosa. The surgeon then decongests with pledgets soaked in vasoconstrictor and local anaesthetic, and infiltrates the septum and lateral wall.
**Anaesthesia:** Tilt the table 15 degrees head up: in a randomised trial this improved the endoscopic field and reduced blood loss without a change in arterial pressure. Surgeons vary in the vasoconstrictor: adrenaline 1 in 1,000 on pledgets, cocaine solutions or paste, phenylephrine with lidocaine spray, or oxymetazoline, followed by lidocaine 1-2% with adrenaline 1 in 80,000 to 1 in 200,000 by injection. Know what was used and how much, and count the local anaesthetic towards the maximum dose. Systemic absorption shows within minutes as hypertension and tachycardia, occasionally with ventricular ectopy. Treat by deepening anaesthesia and waiting; severe hypertension from an alpha agonist is treated with a vasodilator, not a beta-blocker, because removing the heart's ability to respond to a sudden rise in afterload has caused pulmonary oedema and cardiac arrest in reported cases. Leave the eyes untaped for sinus surgery, with lubricant, so the surgeon can see and ballot the globe.

### 4. Establishing the field
**Why:** Mucosal bleeding in this operation is capillary ooze, driven by perfusion pressure and by the time available for filling in each cardiac cycle.
**Anaesthesia:** Propofol and remifentanil TIVA is the technique with the evidence: a meta-analysis of 12 randomised trials found better visibility, less blood loss and shorter operating time than with volatile agents, with the benefit confined to trials using remifentanil. The Cochrane review is more reserved, finding an improvement of less than one grade on a six-point field scale and no reduction in total blood loss. The mechanism usually offered is that remifentanil slows the heart and reduces cardiac output without the vasodilatation that volatile agents cause in the nasal mucosa. Aim for a heart rate around 60 beats/min and a mean arterial pressure modestly below baseline (60-70 mmHg in a fit normotensive adult is the usual range), and accept less reduction in the elderly, the hypertensive and anyone with cerebrovascular, coronary or renal disease, particularly head up. Keep carbon dioxide normal: hypercapnia vasodilates. TXA 1 g IV improved the field and reduced blood loss in a meta-analysis of seven trials.

### 5. Septoplasty and turbinate reduction
**Why:** The lining is elevated off cartilage and bone in a bloodless plane, the deviated portion removed, and the turbinates reduced to open the airway.
**Anaesthesia:** Raising the flaps is mildly stimulating; fracturing or chiselling the bony septum and maxillary crest is briefly much more so. A patient who moves or coughs here tears a flap and leaves a septal perforation. Without relaxant, depth is your immobility: keep the remifentanil up ahead of the bone work.

### 6. Uncinectomy, antrostomy and ethmoidectomy
**Why:** The surgeon opens the ostiomeatal complex and removes ethmoid partitions back to the basal lamella and beyond, with the lamina papyracea as the lateral limit.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the orbital phase. Traction on orbital contents through a breached lamina, or pressure on the globe, can produce a profound bradycardia by the oculocardiac reflex: tell the surgeon, who stops, and give atropine if it persists. A microdebrider that enters the orbit can injure the medial rectus in seconds. Division of the anterior ethmoidal artery with retraction into the orbit causes a retrobulbar haematoma: a tense, proptosed eye with a rising pressure that threatens the optic nerve within an hour or so. The surgeon decompresses by lateral canthotomy and cantholysis; your part is to stop anything that raises venous pressure, avoid coughing, and have mannitol and acetazolamide available.

### 7. Sphenoid, frontal recess and skull base
**Why:** Posterior and superior extension is needed for extensive polyp disease. The margins of safety are smallest here.
**Anaesthesia:** A cerebrospinal fluid leak is recognised as clear fluid washing through the blood and is repaired at once with a graft. It changes your emergence: no positive pressure by face mask, no nasal airway, no coughing, because air can be driven intracranially. Injury to the internal carotid in the sphenoid is rare and catastrophic: the surgeon packs, you resuscitate, activate the massive transfusion protocol and arrange interventional radiology; see [major haemorrhage, cell salvage and coagulation](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html). This is the point at which a day-case operation needs an arterial line, blood and critical care.

### 8. Haemostasis and packing
**Why:** The surgeon wants a dry field at a blood pressure the patient will have in recovery, then places dissolvable dressings, packs or septal splints.
**Anaesthesia:** Let the pressure return to normal before the final inspection, as for any operation where bleeding is tested by the pressure you provide. Give the intravenous analgesia now: paracetamol 1 g IV, parecoxib 40 mg IV or another NSAID where tolerated, and a small dose of longer-acting opioid to follow the remifentanil. Packed nostrils make the patient an obligate mouth breather on waking, which some find alarming if not warned.

### 9. Emergence
**Why:** The surgeon has left raw mucosa that bleeds with a venous surge, and sometimes a repaired skull base.
**Anaesthesia:** Suction the pharynx under direct vision, looking behind the soft palate for clot, and confirm the throat pack is out. Aim for no coughing: a laryngeal mask left until the patient opens the mouth to command, or a remifentanil-based extubation, with the patient 30 degrees head up. Remifentanil ranks highest among drugs for preventing severe emergence cough, and dexmedetomidine reduces the emergence agitation that is unusually common after nasal surgery (relative risk 0.31 in a meta-analysis). Do not apply a tight face mask to a freshly operated nose; if support is needed, an oral airway and a gentle seal over the mouth.

### 10. Recovery and discharge
**Why:** The surgeon wants to know the nose is not bleeding and that the eyes are normal.
**Anaesthesia:** Sit the patient up. A trickle of blood-stained mucus is expected; a patient who keeps swallowing is bleeding posteriorly. Any eye pain, proptosis, diplopia or loss of vision is reported immediately. Nausea is driven by swallowed blood as much as by drugs. Patients with sleep apnoea and packs need longer observation with oximetry and cannot use nasal positive airway pressure until the surgeon agrees.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is mucosal and bony without an external incision, and the surgeon's own vasoconstrictor and local anaesthetic cover the first hours. The plan is paracetamol and a COX-2 inhibitor or NSAID given in theatre and continued regularly for three to five days, with a small supply of oral opioid for the first two nights after septoplasty or extensive sinus surgery. Dexamethasone 8 mg IV contributes to analgesia as well as to nausea prophylaxis. In aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease use paracetamol and, where the patient has taken it before without reaction, a COX-2 inhibitor.

A sphenopalatine ganglion block by the surgeon is the regional adjunct with trial support: a meta-analysis of six trials found lower pain scores to 24 hours and about a quarter of the rescue analgesic requirement. For septorhinoplasty with osteotomies, bilateral infraorbital and external nasal blocks cover the bony pyramid. Neither is needed for routine cases and both are the surgeon's or your choice by local habit; this is author's judgement, not evidence. There is no PROSPECT guideline for nasal or sinus surgery.

Expect worst pain of about 3 out of 10 on the first evening and day 1, described as pressure and headache, falling over the first week as packs or splints come out and crusting clears. There is no rebound. Pain out of proportion has a cause: a septal haematoma (increasing obstruction and pain on day 1-3), a pack that is too tight, or, with fever and headache after sinus surgery, an intracranial complication.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Controlled hypotension. The evidence supports TIVA and head-up tilt more clearly than a specific pressure target, and there are no outcome data on safety. The harm is cerebral or myocardial hypoperfusion in a head-up patient with unrecognised vascular disease. Lower the heart rate first, and the pressure only as far as the patient's comorbidity allows.
- Vasoconstrictor toxicity. Hypertension, arrhythmia and, with cocaine, coronary spasm. Children are at particular risk from topical phenylephrine, and an arrhythmia-prone heart from any of them.
- Orbital haematoma and visual loss. Minutes matter; do not leave recovery without a recorded check of the eyes.
- Skull base injury. Cerebrospinal fluid leak, pneumocephalus and meningitis; avoid nitrous oxide and positive-pressure mask ventilation after a recognised breach.
- Retained throat pack. A never event that obstructs the airway after extubation. If the laryngoscope is in your hand at the end, look.
- Septorhinoplasty. Longer, with external osteotomies, more swelling, periorbital bruising and more pain (nearer a 3); the nasal cast prevents a face mask being applied afterwards.
- Laryngeal mask against tube. The mask is the better protector of the glottis from above and the worse protector from below. Use the tube where regurgitation is a risk.
- Epistaxis and return to theatre. The emergency variant: a patient with a stomach full of blood, sometimes anticoagulated and hypertensive, sometimes after days of packing. Resuscitate first, rapid sequence induction, and expect ligation of the sphenopalatine artery; the principles are those of the [bleeding tonsil](bleeding-tonsil.html).
- Local anaesthesia with sedation. Feasible for polypectomy and turbinate work, but blood runs into an unprotected pharynx in a sedated patient; light sedation only.
- The shared airway is discussed in [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html) and the day-case and nausea plan in [nausea, vomiting and day-case anaesthesia](../a/a11-ponv-day-case.html). For surgery on the ear with the same field requirements see [middle ear surgery](middle-ear-surgery-tympanoplasty-mastoidectomy-cochlear.html).

## Key sources

The surgical field has been studied more than any other question here: several meta-analyses favour propofol and remifentanil TIVA over volatile anaesthesia, with heterogeneous small trials and subjective field scores, and a Cochrane review that calls the effect small. Head-up tilt and TXA each have randomised support. The laryngeal mask comparison is a single non-randomised study. Blood pressure and heart rate targets, the handling of vasoconstrictor hypertension and the emergence technique are consensus and author's judgement; the phenylephrine guidance is an advisory document written after case reports of harm.
