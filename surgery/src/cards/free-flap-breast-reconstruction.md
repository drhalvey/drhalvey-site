## What the surgeon is doing and why

An implant gives a breast shape quickly but it is a foreign body that scars, ruptures, tolerates radiotherapy badly and needs replacing. A flap of the patient's own lower abdominal skin and fat gives a soft, warm, permanent breast that ages with her. The deep inferior epigastric perforator (DIEP) flap is the standard: the same ellipse of tissue that an abdominoplasty discards, kept alive on one to three small perforating vessels that pass from the deep inferior epigastric artery and veins through the rectus abdominis muscle to the skin.

The older TRAM flap took the rectus muscle with the vessels and left a weak abdominal wall. The point of the DIEP is to leave the muscle behind. So the surgeon opens the anterior rectus sheath around the chosen perforators and follows each one down through the muscle, splitting fibres and preserving the motor nerves, until it joins the main pedicle, which is then traced to its origin from the external iliac vessels to gain length and calibre. This dissection is slow, bloodless only if it is done patiently, and planned from a pre-operative CT angiogram.

The flap then needs a blood supply on the chest. The usual recipients are the internal mammary artery and vein, exposed beside the sternum by removing a short segment of the third costal cartilage or by working in the intercostal space. The pedicle is divided, the flap is carried to the chest, and artery and vein are joined under the microscope, the vein usually with a coupler ring and the artery by hand with 9-0 suture. From division to clamp release the flap is ischaemic.

```
 abdomen (donor)                      chest (recipient)
 ellipse of skin + fat                mastectomy pocket
   | perforators through rectus       internal mammary a. and v.
   | deep inferior epigastric a./v.   (costal cartilage removed)
   +---- divide ---- ischaemia ---- anastomose ---- reperfuse
 fascia closed, skin closed flexed    flap shaped and inset
```

Two teams work at once, one raising the flap and one doing the mastectomy (immediate reconstruction) or re-opening the old scar and pocket (delayed). After reperfusion the flap is shaped into a breast and the abdomen is closed like an abdominoplasty: fascia repaired, the upper skin flap undermined to the costal margin and pulled down, the umbilicus brought out through a new hole, and the table flexed to get the edges together.

The pain logic is counter-intuitive. The reconstructed breast is denervated tissue and hurts little. The chest contributes a mastectomy wound and a cartilage resection. The dominant mechanism is the abdominal wall: a long incision, an opened and repaired rectus sheath, a split muscle and a closure under tension that is loaded by coughing, by straightening up and by getting out of bed. That is why the block for this operation is an abdominal one, and why the trajectory peaks on days 1 and 2 when the patient starts to mobilise.

## Steps

### 1. Induction, lines and a ten-hour position
**Why:** Two teams, a microscope and both donor and recipient sites need access at once, and the patient will not be moved for most of a working day.
**Anaesthesia:** Tracheal tube, two large cannulae, arterial line, urinary catheter and core temperature probe. Start warming before induction: a patient who reaches the anastomosis at 35.5 degrees with constricted peripheral vessels cannot be fixed quickly. Pad heels, sacrum, occiput and elbows, protect the eyes, apply calf compressors, and agree where the arms go. Propofol TIVA with remifentanil gives the smoothest emergence and the least nausea, which is the ERAS Society's stated aim for technique. If the mastectomy is immediate and a sentinel node biopsy is planned, the blue dye considerations in [wide local excision and sentinel node biopsy](wide-local-excision-and-sentinel-node.html) apply here too.

### 2. Flap elevation and perforator selection
**Why:** The abdominal ellipse is incised down to fascia and lifted from lateral to medial until the perforators mapped on the CT angiogram are seen; the surgeon picks the best one to three and commits to them.
**Anaesthesia:** Two to three hours of low-grade stimulation and steady insensible loss from a large open wound. This is where fluid drift happens: maintenance crystalloid at 3-5 mL/kg/h plus replacement of measured loss is enough, and the single-centre series of 354 flaps found that both extremes of crystalloid rate predicted complications. An oedematous flap is harder to inset and more prone to venous congestion. Use the arterial line and, if available, stroke volume variation to decide whether hypotension wants volume or vasopressor.

### 3. Intramuscular dissection of the pedicle
**Why:** The anterior sheath is opened and the perforators followed through rectus to the deep inferior epigastric vessels, preserving muscle and intercostal motor branches to avoid a postoperative bulge.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon wants a still, relaxed muscle: this is the one phase where neuromuscular block helps, and a twitch from diathermy near a 1 mm perforator is unwelcome. Maintain it by infusion or bolus to a train-of-four count of one or two and plan to have it gone by closure. Vasospasm of the perforators from handling is treated by the surgeon with topical papaverine or lidocaine; your part is warmth and pressure.

### 4. Mastectomy or pocket preparation
**Why:** In immediate reconstruction the second team performs a skin-sparing or nipple-sparing mastectomy; in delayed reconstruction the scar is excised and the skin flaps are re-raised to make a pocket.
**Anaesthesia:** As for [mastectomy](mastectomy-axillary-clearance-and-implant-reconstruction.html): skin flap perfusion depends on blood pressure, and the medial perforators bleed. A paravertebral or pectoral nerves block placed before incision covers this component; the author's practice is to offer one for immediate reconstruction and omit it for delayed, where the chest wound is small.

### 5. Exposure of the internal mammary vessels
**Why:** A 2-3 cm segment of the third costal cartilage is removed, or the intercostal muscles are opened in a rib-sparing approach, to expose the internal mammary artery and its vein on the pleura.
**Anaesthesia:** The pleura is immediately deep to the vessels and a small tear is recognised by the surgeon, repaired over a catheter with a sustained inflation from you, and seldom needs a drain. Report any unexplained rise in airway pressure. The cartilage resection is the sharp, localised, sternal-edge component of chest pain afterwards.

### 6. Pedicle division and ischaemia
**Why:** Once the recipient vessels are confirmed adequate, the pedicle is clipped and divided at its origin and the flap is weighed and transferred. The surgeon wants the ischaemia time as short as the anastomosis allows.
**Anaesthesia:** Some surgeons ask for unfractionated heparin IV (typically 3,000-5,000 units) before the clamps; surgeons vary, and the dose is recorded with its time. Note the ischaemia start. Use this hour to get the physiology right for reperfusion: temperature above 36.5 degrees, haemoglobin known, mean arterial pressure at the awake baseline, the patient pain-free and adequately filled.

### 7. Microvascular anastomosis
**Why:** The vein is coupled and the artery sutured under the microscope. The field is a few millimetres wide and must not move.
**Anaesthesia:** No coughing, no bucking, and no ventilator-driven excursion of the chest wall into the field: some surgeons ask for smaller tidal volumes at a higher rate for the minutes of arterial suturing. Do not let the remifentanil or the propofol run out. This is not the moment for the pressure to sag, because the surgeon will judge the anastomosis by the flow that follows.

### 8. Reperfusion and flap assessment
**Why:** Clamps come off, the flap pinks up and bleeds from its edges, and the surgeon watches the anastomosis for 15-20 minutes for spasm or thrombosis, sometimes with indocyanine green angiography to decide which zones of the flap to discard.
**Anaesthesia:** The flap vessels are denervated and maximally dilated, so flow through them is pressure-passive. A meta-analysis of 8,427 flaps found vasopressor use associated with a lower, not higher, risk of flap failure (relative risk 0.70). The defensible position is to keep mean arterial pressure at baseline with a noradrenaline or metaraminol infusion and modest fluid, and to explain to a surgeon who objects that hypotension is the alternative. A haematocrit around 30% is traditionally regarded as favourable for flow; transfuse for the patient's needs and not to a number.

### 9. Inset, shaping and abdominal closure
**Why:** The flap is de-epithelialised where it will be buried, folded and sutured into the pocket, often with the patient sat up to compare sides. The abdominal fascia is repaired (with mesh if weak), the umbilicus re-sited and the skin closed in flexion over drains.
**Anaesthesia:** Sitting up and flexing the table both drop venous return; pre-empt with vasopressor. The TAP blocks go in now, by the surgeon under direct vision through the open abdomen or by you with ultrasound before the dressings: ropivacaine 0.2-0.375%, 20 mL per side within the weight limit, or catheters if that is the plan. A tight fascial and skin closure raises intra-abdominal pressure and reduces functional residual capacity; a rise in airway pressure at closure in a patient with a large pannus removed is expected and usually tolerable. Reverse neuromuscular block fully.

### 10. Emergence
**Why:** The surgeon needs the anastomosis undisturbed, the abdominal closure unstrained and the flap visible for monitoring.
**Anaesthesia:** Wake the patient flexed at the hips, warm, comfortable and not nauseated. Coughing on the tube strains the abdominal repair; shivering multiplies oxygen consumption and constricts the recipient vessels; a hypertensive surge fills the mastectomy pocket with blood. A remifentanil-based wake-up with a long-acting opioid already on board and a forced-air blanket still running addresses all three. Check the flap with the surgeon before leaving theatre: colour, capillary refill, temperature and the Doppler signal are the baseline for the night.

### 11. The first 48 hours and the take-back
**Why:** Most anastomotic failures declare themselves within the first day or two, venous congestion more often than arterial occlusion, and a flap re-explored early is usually salvaged.
**Anaesthesia:** The flap ward continues what you started: a warm room, mean arterial pressure target written down, urine output over 0.5 mL/kg/h, analgesia that allows the patient to sit out on day 1, and hourly flap checks. A congested, blue, swollen flap or a lost Doppler signal is the emergency variant of this card. The take-back is urgent at any hour: the patient has recently been anaesthetised for many hours, may be heparinised, is often mildly hypovolaemic and nauseated, and needs the same haemodynamic targets from the moment of induction. Do not wait for results that will not change the decision.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is abdominal wall under tension, so the regional component is abdominal. Meta-analysis of 12 studies and 1,107 patients found that TAP blocks in abdominally based reconstruction reduced opioid requirement by about 134 mg of oral morphine equivalents and length of stay by a day, without changing reported pain scores, and with wide heterogeneity. That pattern (less opioid, earlier discharge, similar scores) is what an opioid-sparing block looks like inside a pathway. A randomised trial of 117 patients in an enhanced recovery pathway found that adding liposomal bupivacaine to a plain bupivacaine TAP lowered pain scores modestly but changed neither opioid use nor a stay that was already about two days.

The defensible default is therefore a multimodal pathway of the kind the ERAS Society consensus describes: paracetamol and a COX-2 inhibitor or NSAID started before surgery (microsurgeons generally accept a COX-2 inhibitor; some restrict non-selective NSAIDs for the first day on bleeding grounds), dexamethasone 8 mg IV, bilateral TAP blocks before closure, a short period of patient-controlled or oral opioid, and conversion to oral-only analgesia on day 1. Gabapentin has PROSPECT's recommendation for the breast component; the author's judgement is to use it for one to three days in patients who are not elderly or sedated. Catheters in the TAP or the rectus sheath, laid by the surgeon and run for two to three days, are worth their trouble for bilateral flaps (both hemi-abdomens dissected), for opioid-tolerant patients, and where the single shot has been seen to wear off before mobilisation begins. That selection is opinion and not trial evidence.

Thoracic epidural analgesia works but brings hypotension, a urinary catheter and a patient who cannot be mobilised early, and most units have abandoned it. Intrathecal morphine is used in some centres with the usual monitoring requirements; the author has no trial in this operation to quote for it.

Expect worst pain of 3 on the evening of surgery while the block is working, rising to 5 on days 1 and 2 with coughing, sitting out and the first walk, and falling to 2 by the end of the week. Rebound as a single-shot TAP recedes is moderate because the oral regimen is already running. Escalation triggers: severe unilateral breast pain with swelling is a haematoma or a congested flap and is a surgical call first and an analgesic problem second; calf or pleuritic pain in this population is thromboembolism until excluded; abdominal pain with a tense distended belly and rising airway or bladder pressures after a very tight closure is intra-abdominal hypertension.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Bilateral flaps. Twice the dissection, 8-12 hours, both hemi-abdomens used so there is no spare tissue, more fluid shift and more blood loss. This is the version where transfusion, a second anaesthetist for breaks and catheters for analgesia are all reasonable expectations.
- Other flaps. Muscle-sparing TRAM takes a cuff of rectus and hurts more at the donor site. The SIEA flap uses the superficial vessels and does not open the fascia at all, so donor pain is much less. Thigh-based flaps (transverse upper gracilis, profunda artery perforator) need the legs in a frog or lithotomy position and move the donor pain to the medial thigh, where a TAP is irrelevant.
- Vasopressor reluctance. The evidence is observational but consistent and points away from harm. The alternative offered by tradition, more crystalloid, is the one with a demonstrated association with complications.
- Hypothermia. The largest avoidable cause of intraoperative vasoconstriction and postoperative shivering. Two exposed surgical fields for many hours defeat a single blanket; warm the fluids and the room.
- Delayed reconstruction after radiotherapy. Fibrotic recipient vessels, a tight skin envelope and a longer anastomosis. Ischaemia time runs longer.
- Smokers, obesity and diabetes. Higher rates of fat necrosis, abdominal wound breakdown and flap loss; many surgeons will not operate on a current smoker.
- Venous thromboembolism. Long surgery, cancer, a flexed patient and a tight abdomen: the risk is real and prophylaxis starts in theatre.
- Pneumothorax. From internal mammary exposure. Usually trivial; think of it when oxygenation is unexpectedly poor in recovery.
- The long case itself. Pressure injury, corneal abrasion, compartment syndrome in a leg under a heavy drape and ulnar neuropathy are the complications of duration; reposition what you can reach every couple of hours.
- When it needs more. An arterial line is routine in the author's practice for the length of the case and the tightness of the blood pressure target. Blood is cross-matched for bilateral immediate cases and for any take-back with a haematoma. High-dependency care is for the flap, not the patient.

## Key sources

The haemodynamic advice rests on meta-analyses of observational studies (vasopressors) and a single-centre retrospective series (fluid), which agree with each other and with the physiology of a denervated flap but are not trial evidence. The TAP data are heterogeneous and mostly retrospective, with one recent randomised trial on formulation. The ERAS Society document is a consensus review. Haematocrit targets, ventilation changes during the anastomosis, heparin dosing and the selection of patients for catheters are consensus or author's judgement.
