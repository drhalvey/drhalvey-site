## What the surgeon is doing and why

The proceduralist is a radiation oncologist, and the treatment is ionising radiation delivered to a tumour while sparing what lies beside it. There are two ways to do that, and they make opposite demands.

External beam radiotherapy aims a beam from a linear accelerator, or protons from a cyclotron, at the tumour from outside. Tumour cells repair radiation damage less well than normal tissue, so the dose is split into small daily fractions, five days a week for up to six weeks, and normal tissue recovers between them. The beam is shaped to the tumour to within millimetres. That precision is only as good as the patient's position, so before treatment starts there is a planning session (simulation): the patient is placed in the treatment position, a thermoplastic mask or body mould is formed on them and fixed to the couch, and a CT is taken in it. Every fraction then reproduces that position exactly. Each fraction takes a few minutes of beam time inside 10 to 30 minutes of set-up and imaging, and during the beam nobody else can be in the room.

The treatment is completely painless. An adult lies still. A child under about five or six cannot be left alone, pinned under a mask in an empty room, and keep still to the millimetre. So the child is anaesthetised: not once but 20 or 30 times, on consecutive weekdays, by a service working in a department that was not built for anaesthesia. The radiation oncologist needs three things from you: a motionless child in the identical position every day, delivered on schedule because the machine is booked in slots, for the whole course without missed fractions, since gaps in treatment reduce cure.

Brachytherapy puts a sealed radioactive source inside or against the tumour, so the dose falls away steeply over a few centimetres. The source cannot simply be placed; hollow applicators are positioned first and the source is driven into them afterwards by a remote afterloader. For cervical cancer a tandem is passed through the cervix into the uterus with ovoids or a ring against the cervix, sometimes with interstitial needles through the perineum or vaginal wall for bulky disease. For prostate cancer, needles are passed through a perineal template under transrectal ultrasound, to leave permanent low-dose-rate seeds or to carry a temporary high-dose-rate source. Here there is something to anaesthetise: cervical dilatation, uterine instrumentation, vaginal packing and perineal needles. And the applicators stay in while the patient is moved to CT or MRI, a plan is calculated, and treatment is delivered, which takes hours, or for some interstitial implants up to two days.

The pain logic splits accordingly. External beam: none, and the plan is for immobility. Brachytherapy: visceral and perineal pain that lasts exactly as long as the applicators are in place and ends when they are removed, which is why a regional technique that outlasts insertion fits the mechanism better than a general anaesthetic that stops at the end of it.

## Steps

### 1. Planning the course with the oncology team
**Why:** The oncologist fixes the number of fractions and the start date around chemotherapy and surgery; the anaesthetic has to be sustainable for all of them.
**Anaesthesia:** Start by asking whether anaesthesia is needed at all. A structured play preparation programme allowed 82.5% of two to five year olds in one unit to complete treatment with no sedation, and children often graduate from anaesthesia during a course as the room becomes familiar. For those who need it, assess once thoroughly: the tumour (a posterior fossa tumour brings raised intracranial pressure, vomiting and bulbar weakness; a mediastinal mass brings airway and vascular compression), the chemotherapy (anthracyclines, myelosuppression), steroids, and nutrition. Central venous access is needed. Vascular device complications affect up to a quarter of children across a course, most with peripherally inserted catheters and fewest with ports. Agree the first slot of the morning so the fast is overnight and short, with clear fluids until an hour before, because a child who is fasted for six hours daily for six weeks stops growing ([the child](../a/a13-the-child.html)).

### 2. Simulation and mask making
**Why:** The mask or mould is formed from warm thermoplastic over the face or body and hardens in the treatment position; the planning CT is then taken in it. Any later change in the shape of what is under the mask makes it fit badly or changes the dose distribution.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the longest and least predictable session and the one with the most complications in the largest series, where risk rose with duration and total propofol dose. It also fixes the airway for the course: the mask is moulded round whatever is on the face, so nasal cannulae, capnography line and head position must be those you intend to use for every fraction, and a hole can be cut over the mouth and nose. An oral airway or supraglottic device under the mask changes jaw position and must then be used every day. Aim for the simplest arrangement that maintains the airway hands-off: neutral head, a small shoulder roll built into the mould if needed, natural airway.

### 3. The daily fraction: arrival and induction
**Why:** The radiation therapists need the child on the couch, in the mask and aligned to the lasers within the slot; a late start delays every patient on that machine.
**Anaesthesia:** Routine matters more here than anywhere: same team, same sequence, same words, parent present until asleep. Access the line with full asepsis, since this child will be neutropenic within weeks and the line is entered daily. Induce with propofol on the couch or on a trolley beside it and maintain with an infusion, spontaneously breathing, with nasal oxygen and capnography. Propofol alone, without opioid or other adjuncts, was associated with fewer complications in 3,833 anaesthetics, and tolerance does not develop: children given a fixed dose over a mean of 24 sessions needed no more in the last week than the first. Avoid intramuscular or ketamine-based techniques, which carry far higher complication rates in the systematic review, and avoid a daily inhalational induction if a line exists, because it teaches the child to fear the mask.

### 4. Positioning and immobilisation
**Why:** The therapists fit the mask, clip it to the couch and align skin marks to room lasers; image guidance then checks bony anatomy against the planning scan and the couch is shifted by millimetres.
**Anaesthesia:** Once the mask is clipped down you cannot lift the chin, see the lips or reach the mouth quickly. Confirm a good capnograph trace and a stable saturation after the mask is fixed and before you leave, and position the monitor and the infusion pump where the cameras see them. Prone treatment for craniospinal fields puts the face in a cut-out; the large series found no extra complications prone, but the airway check is the same. Keep the child warm: bunkers are air-conditioned for the machine. Know how to release the mask in one movement.

### 5. Beam on: watching from outside
**Why:** The room is shielded by concrete walls and a heavy door or a maze entrance, and everyone leaves before the beam is switched on. The beam can be interrupted instantly from the console and resumed without loss of dose.
**Anaesthesia:** You watch two camera feeds, one of the child's chest and face and one of the monitor, with the capnograph the most informative trace. Agree the signal beforehand: you say stop, the therapist cuts the beam, and the door starts opening. The door is slow, and reaching the child takes many seconds that feel long, so respond to a flattening capnogram and not to a falling saturation. Obstruction, apnoea after a bolus, coughing and laryngospasm are the events; all are respiratory, as the reviews confirm. Proton treatment and total body irradiation run longer, with the same isolation.

### 6. Recovery and the course over weeks
**Why:** The department needs the couch for the next patient within minutes, and the child needs to eat.
**Anaesthesia:** Move the sleeping child to a recovery bay with a nurse trained in paediatric recovery and the usual equipment ([remote site chapter](../a/a12-remote-site-sedation.html)); most wake within 15 minutes and are fed straight away. Then watch the trend across the course, not the day. Weight falls. Radiation to the head and neck produces mucositis and airway oedema from the third week, so the airway that needed nothing in week 1 may need a jaw thrust or an airway device in week 5. Counts fall, and a febrile neutropenic child is treated for sepsis first and irradiated second. Craniospinal treatment causes nausea. Dexamethasone given for tumour oedema raises glucose and appetite. The complication rate in experienced hands is about 1.3% per anaesthetic, which over 30 anaesthetics is a meaningful chance of one event per child: plan every day as if it were the day.

### 7. Brachytherapy: assessment and choice of technique
**Why:** The oncologist needs a relaxed pelvis for insertion, then a patient who lies flat and still with applicators in place through imaging, planning and treatment, sometimes repeated over several sessions in one or two weeks.
**Anaesthesia:** These are not well patients. In 1,622 brachytherapy anaesthetics more than a third were ASA III or IV and half were repeat treatments. Women with cervical cancer arrive after weeks of pelvic radiotherapy and cisplatin: anaemic, nauseated, sometimes with renal impairment from ureteric obstruction or the drug, and with platelet and neutrophil counts that must be checked before a neuraxial needle. Pelvic malignancy plus bed rest is a thrombotic combination, so heparin timing has to be planned round the block. The choice is between general anaesthesia, which covers insertion only, and a neuraxial technique that also covers the hours afterwards. The meta-analysis found less rescue analgesia with neuraxial anaesthesia and no difference in complications, and the large series used regional techniques for most pelvic work, often with a spinal catheter because total time averaged over three hours and varied widely. The defensible default is a spinal with a long-acting agent, and a catheter technique when applicators will stay overnight.

### 8. Applicator insertion for cervical and uterine tumours
**Why:** In lithotomy the bladder is catheterised, the cervix is dilated, the tandem is passed to the fundus under ultrasound guidance, the ovoids or ring are seated against the cervix, interstitial needles are added if needed, and the vagina is packed firmly to push bladder and rectum away from the source and hold everything still.
**Anaesthesia:** Cervical dilatation and traction are vagal stimuli; bradycardia is common under light general anaesthesia and less so under a spinal to T10. A tumour-distorted cervix makes uterine perforation a real risk: the oncologist checks tandem position with ultrasound, and unexplained pain or hypotension afterwards is perforation or bleeding. The packing is one of the main sources of later pain. Under general anaesthesia a supraglottic airway is usual and the plan for analgesia after waking has to be in place before emergence, since the patient wakes with the applicators in.

### 9. Imaging, treatment and removal with the applicators in place
**Why:** The patient is transferred flat to CT or MRI, the oncologist and physicist contour the tumour and organs and calculate source positions, and the afterloader delivers the dose in a shielded room over 10 to 20 minutes. Then the packing and applicators are pulled out.
**Anaesthesia:** This phase has no anaesthetist in the room for most of it and is where patients suffer. Movement displaces the applicators and invalidates the plan, so the patient must lie with legs flat and still; a spinal that is still working makes that tolerable, and a general anaesthetic that has worn off does not. If the scan is an MRI, everything about the patient and any epidural pump follows scanner rules ([MRI and CT](anaesthesia-for-mri-and-ct.html)). For implants that stay one to two days with several fractions, an epidural or spinal catheter with a dilute local anaesthetic and opioid infusion, or opioid patient-controlled analgesia, is run on the ward with thromboprophylaxis timed around it. Removal of packing and applicators is brief and painful: do it under the residual block, a top-up, or short propofol sedation with monitoring, not with reassurance alone.

### 10. Prostate brachytherapy
**Why:** In high lithotomy with a transrectal ultrasound probe on a stepper, an array of needles is passed through a perineal template into the prostate. Either permanent iodine seeds are deposited as each needle is withdrawn, or catheters are left for a high-dose-rate source and removed after treatment.
**Anaesthesia:** The oncologist needs an immobile pelvis for one to two hours, because the ultrasound grid and the template are registered to each other and a cough or a leg movement shifts the gland. A spinal or a general anaesthetic with a supraglottic airway both work. In the one comparative study propofol total intravenous anaesthesia gave the earliest voiding and discharge, and a low-dose spinal was quicker than a conventional one; pain and satisfaction were the same. The patients are older men with the usual cardiovascular disease, often on anticoagulants that decide the technique. Perineal bruising and haematuria are expected; urinary retention from prostatic swelling is the common problem afterwards and is the reason to avoid long-acting intrathecal opioid and large fluid volumes in the day-case patient. Permanent seeds emit low-energy radiation that barely leaves the patient: ordinary contact is safe, and any seed passed in urine is retrieved with forceps, not fingers.

## Pain plan

External beam radiotherapy needs no analgesia for the treatment. Pain during a course comes from the disease and from radiation mucositis and skin reaction in later weeks, and is managed by the oncology team; the anaesthetist's contribution is to avoid adding opioid to a daily anaesthetic that works better without it.

For brachytherapy the mechanism is visceral and perineal pain that is present for exactly as long as the applicators are, so the plan is matched to that duration. Single-session intracavitary treatment: a spinal with bupivacaine sufficient for about three to four hours, with or without a small dose of intrathecal fentanyl, plus paracetamol and an NSAID if renal function allows. Applicators or interstitial needles overnight or longer: a catheter technique or opioid patient-controlled analgesia, regular paracetamol, an antiemetic, and a plan for removal. Prostate: a short-acting spinal or general anaesthesia with paracetamol and an NSAID; pain afterwards is mild and perineal.

The neuraxial preference is supported by pooled observational data and a small number of trials showing less rescue analgesia; the choice between single-shot and catheter is author's judgement from the duration of the stimulus. Rebound is specific here: a spinal that recedes while the applicators are still in leaves a patient who must not move in escalating pain, so time the block to the whole schedule, not to the insertion, and have the next step charted.

Escalation: severe pain or hypotension after intracavitary insertion suggests uterine perforation or bleeding. Fever with pelvic pain after removal is infection in irradiated tissue.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The child who can do without. Reassess every week or two; play preparation, a parent's recorded voice over the intercom and a film projected in the room convert many children during a course, and each fraction done awake is a fast and an anaesthetic avoided.
- Twice-daily fractions and total body irradiation. Two anaesthetics six hours apart make fasting and nutrition harder still; some units place a nasogastric tube and feed overnight.
- The posterior fossa child early after surgery. Bulbar dysfunction, poor cough and vomiting: a natural airway under a mask may not be safe in the first weeks, and a supraglottic airway built into the mask at simulation is a reasonable alternative.
- Head and neck or mediastinal fields. Progressive mucositis and oedema; the plan made in week 1 is reviewed in week 3.
- Adults for external beam. Occasionally needed for severe claustrophobia in a mask, dementia or movement disorder. Oral anxiolysis usually suffices; general anaesthesia daily in a frail adult is a decision to make with the oncologist about whether the course is deliverable.
- Stereotactic radiosurgery. A single high-dose treatment with a head frame pinned under local anaesthesia or a frameless mask, then imaging and treatment over hours; children and uncooperative adults need general anaesthesia that travels between rooms.
- Neuraxial anaesthesia after chemoradiation. Check platelets and coagulation on the day, and the timing of low molecular weight heparin.
- Radiation exposure of staff. High-dose-rate treatment happens with everyone outside and leaves no residual activity; the anaesthetist's dose from brachytherapy is from the fluoroscopy or CT used for placement, not from the source.
- Brachytherapy at other sites. Endobronchial and oesophageal treatments are delivered through catheters placed at endoscopy ([bronchoscopy](bronchoscopy-ebus-and-interventional-pulmonology.html)); breast and head and neck interstitial implants are placed under general anaesthesia.
- Advanced cervical cancer needing surgery instead is on the [gynaecological oncology card](gynaecological-oncology.html).

## Key sources

Paediatric radiotherapy anaesthesia is supported by one large institutional series, a systematic review of nine studies and a focused review, all observational and all pointing the same way: propofol alone with spontaneous breathing is safe in organised hands, and complications are respiratory and related to duration. The absence of propofol tolerance has a small dedicated study and the large series behind it. Brachytherapy evidence is a single-centre series of 1,622 anaesthetics and a meta-analysis of heterogeneous studies favouring neuraxial techniques on analgesic use. The description of the bunker and of monitoring by camera is standard practice and is not sourced.
