## What the surgeon is doing and why

A ruptured aneurysm that reaches hospital is, by selection, a contained rupture. The aorta has torn posteriorly or laterally into the retroperitoneum, and the bleeding has slowed because three things are opposing it: the pressure of the haematoma inside a closed retroperitoneal space, the tone of the abdominal wall pressing on that space, and a low arterial pressure with intense vasoconstriction. Free intraperitoneal rupture does not usually survive to theatre. The patient in front of you is therefore in a temporary equilibrium, and everything the team does before the aorta is controlled either preserves that equilibrium or breaks it. Raising the pressure with fluid breaks it. Relaxing the abdominal wall breaks it. Abolishing sympathetic tone breaks it. Anaesthesia does the last two within a minute.

The surgeon has one aim: get control of the aorta above the hole. There are two ways.

Open repair is a rapid midline laparotomy, a hand or clamp on the aorta at the neck (or at the diaphragm if the haematoma has obliterated the infrarenal anatomy), then the same graft as the elective operation, sewn in faster and usually as a simple tube. Opening the abdomen releases the tamponade, so the interval between knife and clamp is the most dangerous two minutes of the case.

Endovascular repair treats the rupture from within. Under local anaesthesia the femoral arteries are punctured, a compliant balloon can be floated up to the supracoeliac aorta and inflated as an internal clamp if the patient is collapsing, and a stent graft is deployed across the rupture. The abdomen is never opened, the tamponade is never released, and the patient is never induced. The haematoma stays where it is, which is the source of the later problem of abdominal compartment syndrome.

The randomised evidence is IMPROVE: 613 patients with a clinical diagnosis of rupture, randomised to an endovascular strategy (stent graft if the anatomy suited, open if not) or to open repair. Thirty-day mortality was the same, 35.4% against 37.4%. At three years the endovascular strategy had lower mortality (48% against 56%), survivors went home sooner, and women appeared to do better with it. The observational analysis within the trial produced the two findings that matter most to the anaesthetist: endovascular repair under local anaesthesia alone was associated with a quarter of the odds of death compared with general anaesthesia (adjusted odds ratio 0.27), and the lowest recorded systolic pressure was strongly associated with death, with 51% mortality when it fell below 70 mmHg. These are associations, not randomised comparisons, but the first has a clear mechanism and has changed practice.

The choice between the two operations is made on CT, if the patient is stable enough to have one, and on what the unit can deliver at that hour. Roughly half to two thirds of ruptures have anatomy suitable for a standard stent graft.

The pain logic is secondary to survival and differs by operation. After open repair it is the elective laparotomy made worse: a long midline wound closed over swollen bowel, sometimes left open with a temporary dressing and closed at a second operation, in a patient who is coagulopathic and cannot have an epidural. After endovascular repair the wounds are two groin punctures, but a litre or more of retroperitoneal haematoma remains, producing back and flank pain, ileus and abdominal distension for days.

## Steps

### 1. Diagnosis, the phone call and permissive hypotension
**Why:** The surgeon needs the patient alive and uninduced at the door of theatre or the hybrid suite, with a CT if the patient's condition allows one.
**Anaesthesia:** Before control, blood pressure is a compromise between perfusing the brain and heart and not blowing off the tamponade. A patient who is conscious and talking with a systolic above the floor needs no fluid at all, however far below normal the pressure is. Animal models of aortic injury show less bleeding and better survival with hypotensive resuscitation, and there are no prospective human trials in ruptured aneurysm; within IMPROVE a lowest systolic below 70 mmHg was associated with 51% mortality, and the investigators concluded that 70 mmHg is too low a floor. The floor therefore sits above 70 mmHg: a working target of systolic 80-90 mmHg with preserved consciousness, set higher in the elderly hypertensive, is a common reading of that evidence and is judgement, since no trial defines the figure. It is achieved with small boluses of blood, not crystalloid, and without a vasopressor infusion. Consciousness alone does not excuse a systolic below 70 mmHg. Give titrated intravenous fentanyl or morphine for pain: an agitated, hypertensive, straining patient rebleeds. Activate the massive transfusion protocol, send for cell salvage, and find out which operation is planned.

### 2. Set-up in theatre: everything before induction
**Why:** For open repair the surgeon prepares and drapes the awake patient so that the interval from induction to clamp is as short as possible.
**Anaesthesia:** Two large peripheral cannulae or a rapid infusion sheath connected to a primed, warmed rapid infuser with blood in it. An arterial line if it goes in at the first attempt, otherwise after control. Do not delay for a central line. Pre-oxygenate during skin preparation. Have induction drugs, a vasopressor (metaraminol and adrenaline 10 micrograms/mL), calcium and a second anaesthetist or skilled assistant. Warm the room and put forced-air warming on the chest and arms. The surgeon scrubs, the patient is draped, and only when the surgeon says ready do you start.

### 3. Rapid sequence induction (open repair)
**Why:** The surgeon needs a relaxed abdomen to get in fast and reach the neck.
**Anaesthesia:** The same relaxation removes the abdominal wall's contribution to tamponade, and loss of sympathetic drive removes the vasoconstriction, so expect the pressure to fall precipitously within 60 s. Use ketamine 0.5-1 mg/kg IV or a small dose of etomidate where available, with fentanyl in modest dose and rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg IV or suxamethonium 1.5 mg/kg IV; the choice of agent matters less than a reduced dose and blood running as you give it. The surgeon cuts as the tube is confirmed. Treat the post-induction fall with blood and vasopressor boluses for the two minutes it takes to reach the aorta; this is the one period when a vasopressor is buying time and not causing harm.

### 4. Laparotomy and proximal control
**Why:** The surgeon opens from xiphoid to pubis and controls the aorta by the fastest route: a clamp on the infrarenal neck if it can be found, otherwise compression or a clamp at the diaphragmatic hiatus through the lesser omentum, moved down below the renal arteries once the anatomy is clear.
**Anaesthesia:** Until the surgeon says the clamp is on, transfuse to hold the same floor and no more. When it is on, the rules reverse: now resuscitate fully and quickly. A supracoeliac clamp stops the bleeding and also makes the liver, gut and kidneys ischaemic, so note the time and ask for it to be moved infrarenal as soon as possible; every 10 min of visceral ischaemia adds to the coagulopathy and acidosis that follow. Venous injury (left renal vein, inferior vena cava, iliac veins) during hurried dissection in a haematoma is common and is not controlled by the aortic clamp.

### 5. Catching up after control
**Why:** The surgeon opens the sac, oversews the lumbar arteries and sews in a tube graft, which takes 30-60 min.
**Anaesthesia:** This is your window to turn a dying physiology round before the clamp comes off. Balanced transfusion of red cells, plasma and platelets, fibrinogen concentrate or cryoprecipitate to keep fibrinogen above 2 g/L, calcium chloride 10% 10 mL IV with every four units and against the ionised calcium, viscoelastic testing if available. Place the arterial line and central access now. Blood gas every 15-20 min: lactate, base deficit, potassium, haemoglobin, ionised calcium. Warm aggressively. Reinfuse salvaged blood. Heparin is usually omitted or given in a small dose down the distal vessels by the surgeon, because the patient is already coagulopathic. Vasopressors are now a bridge only; a rising requirement after control means ongoing bleeding, inadequate volume or myocardial ischaemia.

### 6. Clamp release
**Why:** Flow is restored to one leg at a time.
**Anaesthesia:** The release is the elective event (see [open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair](open-abdominal-aortic-aneurysm-repair.html)) on a patient with no reserve: already acidotic, cold, coagulopathic and with legs that have been ischaemic since the rupture. Before release, bring the volume up, have adrenaline and calcium in hand, treat a potassium above 5.5 mmol/L with calcium, insulin 10 units IV with glucose, and hyperventilation, and ask for a slow, staged release. Cardiac arrest at clamp release from hyperkalaemia and acidosis is a recognised mode of death; partial reapplication of the clamp is the first treatment.

### 7. Haemostasis and the decision about closure
**Why:** Bowel oedema, retroperitoneal haematoma and massive transfusion mean the abdomen may not close without tension. The surgeon either closes primarily or leaves the abdomen open with a negative-pressure dressing.
**Anaesthesia:** Tell the surgeon what closure does to the ventilator: a rise in peak airway pressure of more than about 10 cmH2O, a fall in tidal volume, or a drop in pressure and urine output as the fascia comes together means intra-abdominal hypertension, and an open abdomen is the safer choice. Coagulopathic ooze continues until the patient is warm, the fibrinogen is replaced and the acidosis is clearing; those are your tasks.

### 8. The endovascular pathway: local anaesthesia and the occlusion balloon
**Why:** The operator punctures both femoral arteries under local infiltration, passes a wire, and either proceeds directly to stent graft deployment or first inflates a compliant balloon in the supracoeliac aorta if the patient is collapsing.
**Anaesthesia:** Your role is to keep an awake, frightened, hypotensive patient still without removing their compensation. Oxygen, reassurance, a hand on the shoulder, fentanyl 10-25 micrograms IV increments, and nothing that abolishes tone. No propofol sedation. Maintain permissive hypotension until the graft is deployed or the balloon is up. If general anaesthesia becomes unavoidable (loss of consciousness, uncontrollable movement), induce only after the occlusion balloon is inflated, which is the endovascular equivalent of having the surgeon scrubbed with the knife ready. Balloon occlusion above the coeliac axis is visceral ischaemia with a clock on it, as with a supracoeliac clamp. Once the graft is sealed, resuscitate fully.

### 9. Transfer to intensive care
**Why:** The aorta is repaired; the patient now has to survive the consequences of shock, ischaemia and transfusion.
**Anaesthesia:** After open repair the patient goes ventilated, with a written handover of clamp level and times, blood products given, last gas, urine output and whether the abdomen is open. Do not attempt extubation in theatre. After endovascular repair under local anaesthesia the patient is awake, and the handover stresses what has not been fixed: the haematoma is still there.

### 10. The first 72 hours
**Why:** Deaths after successful repair are from multi-organ failure, myocardial infarction, colonic ischaemia and abdominal compartment syndrome.
**Anaesthesia:** Abdominal compartment syndrome is the complication to look for after both operations, and after endovascular repair it is the one specific to the technique: the haematoma and resuscitation oedema raise intra-abdominal pressure, with oliguria, rising airway pressures and a falling cardiac output. Bladder pressure is measured 4-6 hourly; a sustained pressure above 20 mmHg with new organ dysfunction is the indication for decompressive laparotomy. Colonic ischaemia presents as bloody diarrhoea or unexplained acidosis. Acute kidney injury is common and often needs renal replacement. Lower limb ischaemia and compartment syndrome follow prolonged clamp or balloon times.

## Pain plan

The analgesic strategy is staged, because the technique that would be chosen electively is not available. A thoracic epidural is not placed during the emergency: there is no time, the patient is hypovolaemic, and a dilutional and consumptive coagulopathy is either present or about to be.

Before control, analgesia is small intravenous doses of fentanyl or morphine, titrated to a calm patient, because pain and agitation raise pressure and strain the abdominal wall. During endovascular repair it is the operator's lidocaine and the same small opioid doses.

After open repair, the first days are in intensive care on an opioid infusion (fentanyl or morphine) as part of sedation, with regular intravenous paracetamol. NSAIDs are contraindicated by kidney injury and bleeding. The author's preferred addition is bilateral rectus sheath catheters, placed by the surgeon under vision at definitive fascial closure or by ultrasound on day 1-2 once the platelet count and coagulation tests are acceptable, bolused with ropivacaine 0.2% 20 mL per side every 6 h at most. That dose is a ceiling for a well-built adult and is reduced here: the 24 h total is checked in mg/kg against the local maximum, and the volume or frequency is cut for a small patient and for kidney injury, liver injury, low albumin and acidosis after massive transfusion, all of which this patient may have. They cover the somatic midline component, carry little risk in a patient whose coagulation is still recovering compared with a neuraxial catheter, and are in place for the day the sedation is stopped, which is when wound pain first matters. Low-dose ketamine by infusion is a reasonable adjunct. A delayed epidural in a stable, extubatable patient with normal coagulation is possible but rarely worth the risk when rectus sheath catheters are available. This sequence is author's judgement; there are no trials of analgesic technique after ruptured aneurysm.

After endovascular repair, expect more pain than the groin wounds explain: back and flank ache from the retroperitoneal haematoma, lasting a week or more as it resolves. Regular paracetamol and modest oral or PCA opioid cover it. Increasing abdominal pain and distension is measured with a bladder pressure, not treated with more opioid.

The trajectory on this card is in calendar days and describes the open-repair survivor: the evening of surgery and day 1 are spent sedated on an opioid infusion, and the peak falls on days 2-3, when sedation is lightened and the wound is felt for the first time. A patient kept sedated for longer has the same peak later. Rebound in the usual sense does not apply; the equivalent hazard is the step from intensive care infusion to ward analgesia, which needs PCA and a working set of abdominal wall catheters, not an abrupt change to oral drugs.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The stable patient who is not stable. A normal pressure in a known hypertensive with a contained rupture is a low pressure. Do not be reassured into a relaxed set-up, a central line before induction, or generous sedation.
- Wrong diagnosis. About one in ten patients randomised in IMPROVE on a clinical diagnosis of rupture (55 of 613) did not have a ruptured or symptomatic aneurysm. Myocardial infarction, pancreatitis and perforated viscus are the usual alternatives, which is an argument for CT when the patient's state permits.
- Symptomatic, non-ruptured aneurysm. Pain and tenderness without blood on CT: repaired urgently on the next list with full preparation, lines and an epidural if open. It should not be run as a rupture.
- Supracoeliac control. Whether by clamp or balloon, the visceral ischaemic time drives the coagulopathy, acidosis and gut injury that kill on day 2-5. Keep the time and say it aloud.
- Anticoagulated patients. Many are on warfarin or a direct oral anticoagulant for atrial fibrillation: prothrombin complex concentrate or the specific reversal agent is given on diagnosis, not after the first litre.
- Open abdomen. The patient returns for staged closure at 24-72 h. Those anaesthetics are short, in a ventilated patient, and are the opportunity to place rectus sheath catheters.
- Conversion. Endovascular repair that fails to seal, or a patient who arrests on the hybrid table, is converted to open repair with the balloon up. The set-up for open repair is in the room from the start.
- Palliation. Some patients are not offered repair because of frailty or comorbidity, and that decision is made by the surgeon and anaesthetist together, with the patient and family, quickly. Age alone is a poor discriminator. Comfort care is intravenous opioid and a quiet room.
- Ruptured iliac and thoracic aneurysms follow the same principles; thoracic rupture is treated by endograft with the cord and arch considerations on [endovascular aneurysm repair](endovascular-aneurysm-repair.html).

## Key sources

The comparison of operative strategy rests on IMPROVE, a pragmatic randomised trial with seven-year follow-up. The two statements that drive anaesthetic conduct, local anaesthesia for endovascular repair and a systolic floor above 70 mmHg, come from prespecified observational analyses within that trial: strong associations with plausible mechanisms, not randomised comparisons. Permissive hypotension itself rests on animal models and on analogy with penetrating trauma. The transfusion approach is extrapolated from trauma and from general massive transfusion practice. The analgesic plan has no trial evidence and is the author's judgement.
