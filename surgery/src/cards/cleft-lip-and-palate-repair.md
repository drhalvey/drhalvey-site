## What the surgeon is doing and why

A cleft is a failure of the facial processes to fuse in the first trimester. It may involve the lip alone, the lip and the alveolus, the palate alone, or all three, on one side or both. The lip matters for appearance and for a seal when feeding; the palate matters for speech, for swallowing without nasal regurgitation and for the eustachian tubes, because the muscles that should sling across the soft palate and open the tubes are instead attached to the back of the hard palate and pull uselessly.

Surgery is staged to growth and function:

```
 3-6 months     lip repair (and anterior nose)
 6-12 months    palate repair, before speech develops
 5 years or so  pharyngoplasty if speech is nasal
 8-11 years     alveolar bone graft from the iliac crest
 teens          rhinoplasty, jaw surgery
```

Lip repair is a precise rearrangement of small flaps. The surgeon marks the landmarks of the Cupid's bow in ink before any injection distorts them, incises the cleft margins, frees the orbicularis oris muscle from its abnormal insertions on the nose and the cleft edge, and sutures it across the gap as a ring. Skin and mucosa are closed over it in a pattern (rotation-advancement or an anatomical subunit design) that hides the scar in the philtral column, and the nostril is reshaped.

Palate repair is done inside the mouth through a self-retaining gag. The surgeon incises along the cleft edge, lifts the mucoperiosteum of the hard palate off the bone as flaps based on the greater palatine vessels, and closes the nasal lining and then the oral lining in the midline. The key functional step is the muscle: levator veli palatini is detached from the hard palate and rotated backwards to meet its partner (an intravelar veloplasty), or the soft palate is lengthened with a double opposing Z-plasty. Relaxing incisions at the sides, needed to get the flaps to the midline, leave strips of bare bone to heal over.

The pain logic differs between the two. The lip is skin and a small muscle, supplied by the infraorbital nerve on each side: a clean target for a block and a score of 2. The palate is mucosal: raw surfaces in a mouth that must be used for feeding, a re-routed muscle that moves with every swallow, and a nerve supply (the palatine branches of the maxillary nerve) that is deeper and harder to reach. It scores 3, with the complication that the patient is an infant in whom opioid is the drug you least want to give.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative assessment
**Why:** The cleft team will have screened for associated anomalies, but the child in front of you on the day is the one with the cold.
**Anaesthesia:** Three questions. First, is this an isolated cleft or part of something: Pierre Robin sequence (small jaw, tongue falling back, U-shaped palatal cleft, obstructive symptoms), 22q11 deletion (cardiac defects, hypocalcaemia, immune deficiency), or another syndrome with a cardiac lesion that has an echocardiogram on file. Second, the airway history: snoring, apnoea, feeding difficulty, positioning needed for sleep, a nasopharyngeal airway in the neonatal period. Third, the chest: these infants have near-constant nasal discharge and frequent respiratory infections, and a perfect day is rare, so the decision is about fever, a wet cough and lower respiratory signs and not about a runny nose. Check the weight and the haemoglobin (the physiological nadir is at about three months). Keep the fast short by unit protocol.

### 2. Induction and intubation
**Why:** The surgeon needs the tube out of the way of both the lip and the palate and fixed so that it does not distort the mouth.
**Anaesthesia:** Inhalational or intravenous induction according to access and preference; maintain spontaneous ventilation until you can ventilate by mask where a difficult airway is anticipated. In a series of 985 infants, laryngoscopy was difficult in 4.8% overall and 7% in those under six months, and it was most often difficult with bilateral clefts and a small jaw. A second series found difficult intubation in none with an isolated cleft lip and in 23% with cleft palate and Pierre Robin sequence. A wide left-sided alveolar cleft can trap the blade: a gauze pack in the cleft or a lateral approach solves it. Have a videolaryngoscope with an infant blade and a supraglottic airway as a rescue and conduit. For the child with Pierre Robin sequence, plan as a difficult paediatric airway with a second experienced anaesthetist.

### 3. Tube fixation, pack and position
**Why:** The head is extended over a shoulder roll with the surgeon at the vertex, and for the palate a gag will sit on the tube.
**Anaesthesia:** Use a preformed south-facing tube taped to the centre of the chin without pulling the lower lip to either side, since asymmetry misleads the lip surgeon. Confirm bilateral air entry after extension, because the tip moves, and note the length at the lips. The surgeon places a throat pack; it goes on the count. Protect the eyes with tape and ointment, as preparation solution and instruments pass over them. The table is turned and you are at the feet with long circuits: secure every connection and keep a hand on the reservoir bag or an eye on the flow trace.

### 4. Blocks and infiltration
**Why:** The surgeon marks the landmarks, then infiltrates lidocaine with adrenaline for haemostasis and hydrodissection and waits several minutes.
**Anaesthesia:** Agree the local anaesthetic budget first. An infant of 6 kg reaches the lidocaine-with-adrenaline ceiling of 7 mg/kg at about 4 mL of 1% solution, and your block adds to it. For lip repair, bilateral infraorbital blocks with 0.5-1 mL per side of long-acting agent, by the intraoral or percutaneous route; a Cochrane review found low-quality evidence of less pain than with placebo or intravenous analgesia and slightly earlier feeding. For palate repair, bilateral suprazygomatic maxillary nerve blocks (about 0.15 mL/kg per side) reach the palatine nerves in the pterygopalatine fossa; in a randomised trial they gave better early feeding and lower pain scores than the surgeon's greater palatine blocks. Adrenaline absorbed from the infiltration raises the heart rate briefly; arrhythmia is unusual with sevoflurane at these doses.

### 5. Lip repair
**Why:** Flaps are cut, the muscle is freed and reunited, and skin, vermilion and nostril are closed in layers with fine sutures.
**Anaesthesia:** Long, quiet and bloodless. With working blocks the infant needs little opioid: paracetamol 15 mg/kg IV and a small dose of fentanyl at most. The risks are the generic ones of a small infant under drapes for two hours: temperature, glucose in the youngest, and a tube that cannot be seen.

### 6. Palate repair: the gag goes in
**Why:** A self-retaining gag with a grooved tongue blade holds the mouth open and the tube in the midline for the whole operation.
**Anaesthesia:** The moment of highest risk to the tube. As the blade is opened it can compress or kink the tube against the lower teeth or gum, push it into a bronchus, or pull it out on release. Watch the airway pressure, the capnograph and the chest as the gag is opened, and say so at once if they change: the surgeon reseats it. Extension added after intubation draws the tube outward. The tongue is compressed under the blade for the duration; long gag time is the cause of postoperative tongue swelling, and some surgeons release it periodically.

### 7. Palatal flaps and muscle repair
**Why:** Mucoperiosteal flaps are raised on the greater palatine pedicles, the nasal layer closed, the levator muscles dissected and retroposed, and the oral layer closed.
**Anaesthesia:** The blood loss of this operation is here: a steady ooze from flap edges and the bare bone, hard to measure because it is swallowed, suctioned with irrigation and soaked into the pack. Twenty to fifty millilitres is usual, which in an 8 kg infant with a blood volume of about 640 mL deserves counting swabs and watching the heart rate. Maintain with volatile or propofol and remifentanil; avoid large doses of long-acting opioid. Give dexamethasone early for oedema.

### 8. Haemostasis, pack out and the airway plan
**Why:** The surgeon checks the nasopharynx for clot, removes the throat pack, and decides whether the child needs a nasopharyngeal airway or a traction suture through the tongue for the first night.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask for the pack by name and see it come out. Suction is done by the surgeon under vision or by you along the side of the tongue, never blindly across the palate. If a nasopharyngeal airway is wanted, it is placed now, under vision, to a measured length. Ask for one for any child with pre-existing obstruction, a long gag time or a visibly swollen tongue.

### 9. Extubation
**Why:** The repair must not be struck by an airway device, and blood must not reach the cords.
**Anaesthesia:** Extubate awake: eyes open, purposeful movement, regular breathing, lateral or semi-prone with the head down a little so that blood drains out of the mouth. Laryngospasm is the common event, provoked by blood and secretions in a light infant; treat with continuous positive pressure, propofol and, if needed, suxamethonium, and avoid an oropharyngeal airway unless nothing else works. Arm splints go on in theatre in many units to keep fingers out of the mouth.

### 10. The first night
**Why:** The surgical priorities are an intact repair, no bleeding and feeding re-established by cup, syringe or breast within hours.
**Anaesthesia:** Continuous oximetry in a ward used to these children, with a high-dependency bed for Pierre Robin sequence, syndromic children and known sleep apnoea. Regular paracetamol and ibuprofen, with small doses of oral opioid for breakthrough rather than infusions. Obstruction typically appears as the child falls asleep after a dose of opioid; it is treated by positioning, the nasopharyngeal airway and the tongue suture, and by calling an anaesthetist early. Feeding is both the test of analgesia and the stimulus for pain.

## Pain plan

The mechanism decides the block. Lip repair is skin and muscle in infraorbital nerve territory, so a bilateral infraorbital block fits exactly and is supported by a Cochrane review of eight small trials, with the evidence graded low to very low. The defensible default for lip repair is that block with long-acting local anaesthetic, the surgeon's infiltration, paracetamol and ibuprofen (from three months of age), and no routine opioid after theatre.

Palate repair is mucosal pain in palatine nerve territory, re-provoked by feeding. The suprazygomatic maxillary nerve block reaches all the palatine branches at the pterygopalatine fossa and, in a 60-patient randomised trial, gave better feeding over the first 24 hours (median 80% against 60% of normal intake) and lower pain scores to 12 hours than greater palatine blocks. The trial enrolled children aged 1-10 years, so its application to infants under a year is an extrapolation, and the block is a deeper injection than the infraorbital. The author's practice is to use it for palate repair where the operator is trained in it and to accept surgeon-placed greater palatine blocks and infiltration otherwise. Regular paracetamol and ibuprofen follow, with a low-dose oral opioid in reserve and an alpha-2 agonist as an opioid-sparing adjunct in theatre. Many surgeons accept ibuprofen after palate repair; a few withhold it for the first day on bleeding grounds.

There is no PROSPECT guideline for cleft surgery. The governing principle is that analgesia and airway safety compete: the more the block and the simple analgesics do, the less opioid an infant with a newly narrowed airway receives.

Expect behavioural pain scores equivalent to 3 on the evening of surgery and 4 on day 1 after palate repair, mostly at feeds, easing over three days and near baseline at a week; lip repair runs about a point lower and settles by day 2. Rebound as the block recedes at 6-12 hours is moderate and is met by having the oral regimen already given. Escalation triggers: an inconsolable infant who will not feed may be in pain, hungry, hypoxic or bleeding, and the last two are excluded first; fresh blood from the mouth or repeated swallowing is a bleeding palate; fever with refusal to feed after day 3 raises infection or breakdown of the repair.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Bleeding palate. The emergency variant: an infant returning to theatre hours later, hypovolaemic by more than it appears, with a stomach full of blood and a swollen, bloody airway. Resuscitate first, have blood available, two suction units and an experienced assistant, and use a rapid sequence induction with a videolaryngoscope. This is the case in which cross-matched blood and a critical care bed are needed.
- Postoperative airway obstruction. The first six to twelve hours after palate repair. Risk rises with Pierre Robin sequence, syndromes, long gag time, low weight and opioid. A nasopharyngeal airway placed in theatre is far easier than one placed in a crisis.
- Pierre Robin sequence. The palate is repaired later, sometimes after mandibular distraction or a period with a nasopharyngeal airway. Difficult intubation in about a quarter in one series; obstruction after repair is the expectation and not the exception.
- 22q11 deletion. Cardiac anatomy and repair status, ionised calcium, and irradiated blood products if transfusion is needed. Medially displaced carotid arteries matter to the surgeon in pharyngoplasty.
- Upper respiratory infection. Chronic rhinitis is part of the condition. Postpone for fever, a productive cough, wheeze or a parent who says the child is unwell; proceed with a runny nose and a clear chest, accepting a higher rate of laryngospasm and desaturation.
- Bilateral cleft lip. A protruding premaxilla makes laryngoscopy awkward and the repair longer; some are done in two stages or after presurgical moulding.
- Lip adhesion and combined repairs. Some units repair the lip and anterior palate together, or the lip and soft palate; duration and the airway risk follow the palate.
- Alveolar bone graft. A school-age child, a nasal or oral tube, and cancellous bone from the iliac crest packed into the gum defect. The hip donor site is the pain of that operation and merits a regional technique or wound infusion.
- Pharyngoplasty. Narrows the nasopharynx to improve speech and can produce obstructive sleep apnoea; nasal airways and oximetry overnight, and caution with opioid.
- Local anaesthetic toxicity. Two operators and a patient of 6-9 kg. The arithmetic is done before the first injection.
- Invasive monitoring is not needed for primary repairs. Group and screen is reasonable for palate repair in small or anaemic infants; a high-dependency bed is booked for syndromic and Pierre Robin children.

## Key sources

Evidence in cleft anaesthesia is made of small trials and single-centre series. The infraorbital block has a Cochrane review that grades its own evidence as low to very low. The maxillary block recommendation rests on one recent randomised trial in older children and a retrospective series. The airway figures come from two observational cohorts with different definitions. Dosing, extubation practice, the use of nasopharyngeal airways, the approach to respiratory infection and NSAID use after palate repair are consensus and author's judgement.
