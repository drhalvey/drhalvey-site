## What the surgeon is doing and why

The lower oesophageal sphincter works because a length of oesophagus sits below the diaphragm, squeezed by abdominal pressure and pinched by the crural sling, with the stomach entering at an acute angle. Reflux disease is failure of that arrangement. A sliding hiatus hernia is the commonest anatomical cause: the gastro-oesophageal junction has migrated into the chest through a widened hiatus and all three mechanisms are lost. A paraoesophageal hernia is different in kind: the fundus, then the body of the stomach, rolls up beside the oesophagus into a peritoneal sac in the posterior mediastinum. The giant version puts most of the stomach in the chest, where it can twist on its axis (organo-axial volvulus), obstruct, bleed from mucosal erosions and occasionally strangulate.

The operation has three parts and the surgeon varies the proportions.

1. Reduce. The hernia contents are pulled down and, for a paraoesophageal hernia, the peritoneal sac is dissected out of the mediastinum, off the pleura on each side, the pericardium in front and the aorta behind. The oesophagus is mobilised high into the chest until 2-3 cm lie in the abdomen without tension, preserving both vagi.
2. Repair. The crura are sutured together behind the oesophagus to narrow the hiatus, sometimes reinforced with mesh.
3. Wrap. The fundus is freed by dividing the short gastric vessels and wrapped around the lower oesophagus: 360 degrees (Nissen), 270 degrees posterior (Toupet) or 180 degrees anterior. A full wrap controls reflux best and causes most dysphagia and gas bloat; partial wraps trade a little durability for easier swallowing. Many surgeons calibrate the wrap over a bougie that you pass.

For reflux, the patient is typically 30-60 years old and well. For giant paraoesophageal hernia the patient is typically over 70, often with anaemia, breathlessness from a stomach occupying the left chest, and cardiac disease. It is the same card with two different anaesthetics.

Three surgical facts drive yours. The dissection opens the mediastinum to pressurised carbon dioxide and runs along both pleurae, so gas enters the chest. The repair is a ring of sutures in a moving muscle with a new valve below it, and the thing most likely to tear it in the first days is a forceful retch or vomit, which is why antiemetic prophylaxis is a surgical outcome measure here. And the patient, by definition, refluxes.

The pain logic: laparoscopic. Five ports, of which the epigastric liver retractor site and any enlarged port hurt most; crural sutures that are tugged by each breath for a few days; a retrosternal and interscapular ache from the mediastinal dissection; and bilateral shoulder-tip pain from gas beneath and above the diaphragm. It peaks in 24 hours and fades over three days. Dysphagia, bloating and chest tightness for weeks are consequences of the wrap and are not analgesic problems, but patients report them as pain and should be told to expect them.

## Steps

### 1. Induction and airway
**Why:** The surgeon needs the stomach empty and flat and the airway secure for a long head-up laparoscopy.
**Anaesthesia:** Assume reflux at induction, because that is the indication: head-up position, proton pump inhibitor taken that morning, and a rapid sequence induction for symptomatic regurgitation and for every paraoesophageal hernia. An intrathoracic stomach does not empty reliably and a nasogastric tube may not pass or may coil in the chest; do not force it. In acute volvulus the stomach is obstructed and full and the patient may be shocked. Tracheal tube, TIVA by preference for nausea, dexamethasone at induction, paracetamol and NSAID early.

### 2. Positioning
**Why:** Steep reverse Trendelenburg drops the bowel and omentum away from the hiatus; the surgeon stands between the legs.
**Anaesthesia:** Head-up tilt of 30 degrees with a pneumoperitoneum and the legs dependent is a considerable reduction in preload: expect a vasopressor requirement, more in the elderly giant hernia patient who is also relatively hypovolaemic. Calf compression is mandatory. Secure against sliding with a footplate, pad the heels and peroneal nerves, and keep both arms accessible because the abdomen and chest are both under drapes for hours.

### 3. Ports, pneumoperitoneum and liver retraction
**Why:** Five ports across the upper abdomen; a retractor through the epigastrium lifts the left lobe of the liver off the hiatus.
**Anaesthesia:** The usual vagal bradycardia at insufflation. The liver retractor can lacerate the left lobe or, held for hours, raise transaminases afterwards; neither usually matters but both explain an odd blood result on day 1.

### 4. Reduction of the hernia and sac dissection
**Why:** The stomach is brought down and the sac is peeled out of the posterior mediastinum so the hernia cannot re-form into it.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the capnothorax step. The sac lies against both pleurae, the left more often breached, and a hole of any size lets pressurised carbon dioxide into the chest. The signs come together over a minute or two: end-tidal carbon dioxide rising, peak airway pressure rising, compliance falling, saturation drifting down, sometimes hypotension, and the surgeon notices the hemidiaphragm billowing down into the field. The management is not that of an air pneumothorax. Carbon dioxide is absorbed within 30-60 minutes of the insufflation stopping, the lung is not injured, and the treatment is PEEP of 5-10 cmH2O to keep the lung against the hole, a higher minute ventilation, and a request to drop the insufflation pressure to 8-10 mmHg. The surgeon can enlarge the pleural hole so it does not act as a valve, or evacuate the chest with the sucker at the end. A drain is needed only for persistent instability or a suspected lung injury. The mediastinal dissection also compresses the left atrium and irritates the pericardium: ectopy, atrial fibrillation and sudden falls in output as the surgeon retracts are common in giant hernia repair and stop when the retraction stops.

### 5. Oesophageal mobilisation
**Why:** The oesophagus is freed circumferentially up into the chest to gain intra-abdominal length; a short oesophagus that will not come down may need a lengthening gastroplasty.
**Anaesthesia:** Vagal traction produces bradycardia. Gas now tracks freely up the mediastinum: surgical emphysema of the neck, face and chest wall is frequent in long cases, raises the carbon dioxide load, and matters at extubation. If a stapled gastroplasty is planned, anything in the oesophagus (temperature probe, orogastric tube, bougie) must be out of the staple line; removing them and saying so is your job.

### 6. Division of short gastric vessels
**Why:** Frees the fundus so the wrap lies without tension or twist.
**Anaesthesia:** The one step with a real bleeding risk, from a short gastric vessel or the splenic capsule. It is usually controlled with the energy device; a splenic injury that is not is the commonest reason for conversion.

### 7. Crural repair
**Why:** Non-absorbable sutures approximate the right and left crura behind the oesophagus; mesh is added by some surgeons for large defects.
**Anaesthesia:** The crura lie on the aorta and beneath the pericardium. Deep sutures or tacks have caused cardiac tamponade and aortic injury in case reports, so unexplained hypotension in the hours after a hiatal repair with tacks is tamponade until an echo says otherwise. Keep the patient still and relaxed while these sutures are placed.

### 8. Bougie and fundoplication
**Why:** The fundus is passed behind the oesophagus and sutured to itself and to the oesophagus; a bougie in the lumen stops the wrap being made too tight.
**Anaesthesia:** Pass the bougie only when asked, well lubricated, gently, with the surgeon watching it arrive on the screen, and stop at any resistance: oesophageal and gastric perforations at this step are caused from above. Withdraw it only when asked, and confirm aloud that it is out before the last sutures. Some surgeons calibrate with a gastroscope or nothing at all.

### 9. Check, desufflation and port closure
**Why:** The wrap is inspected for twist and tension, the hiatus for bleeding; gas is released and port sites infiltrated.
**Anaesthesia:** Before the ports come out, ask the surgeon to aspirate the pleural space if it was breached, and give several sustained inflations to re-expand the lung and expel carbon dioxide from the abdomen and chest. Ask for local anaesthetic into every port, the epigastric one in particular. Feel the neck and chest for emphysema.

### 10. Emergence
**Why:** The repair must not be strained in the first minutes of its life.
**Anaesthesia:** Aim for a patient who opens their eyes without coughing on the tube: full reversal confirmed, remifentanil run to extubation or a small dose of IV lidocaine, antiemetics already given, and the head up. Extensive surgical emphysema with a high carbon dioxide is a reason to ventilate for another 15-20 minutes until it clears, not a reason to drain anything. A chest radiograph in recovery after a known pleural breach usually shows a small residual pneumothorax that needs no treatment.

### 11. Recovery and the first night
**Why:** The surgeon wants no retching, early fluids and a modified diet.
**Anaesthesia:** Treat nausea at the first report and by a different class from the prophylaxis. Regular antiemetic for 24 hours is reasonable in this operation alone. Give analgesics as liquids, dispersible tablets or intravenously: large tablets lodge above a new wrap. Chest and shoulder pain in recovery is nearly always gas, but in the older patient an ECG is cheap, and severe chest or abdominal pain with tachycardia, fever or emphysema that is increasing is an oesophageal or gastric perforation until excluded.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is ports, diaphragm and gas, peaking in the first day, with a competing priority that outranks analgesia: no retching. Those two facts point the same way, towards a non-opioid base and a small opioid total.

There is no PROSPECT guideline for fundoplication. The nearest evidence is the laparoscopic cholecystectomy guideline, whose basic regimen (paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, dexamethasone and port-site infiltration, with opioid for rescue) transfers on mechanism, and this card adopts it as the defensible default. Intraoperatively: paracetamol 1 g IV, parecoxib 40 mg IV or an equivalent, dexamethasone 8 mg IV, propofol TIVA with remifentanil, and a modest dose of a longer-acting opioid before the end. At closure: 20-30 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% across the ports within the weight limit. Afterwards: regular paracetamol and NSAID in liquid or dispersible form for five days and a small supply of liquid oral opioid.

Opioid-sparing adjuncts earn more of a place here than in cholecystectomy because of the nausea priority. A ketamine bolus of 0.25-0.5 mg/kg IV, or an IV lidocaine infusion of 1.5 mg/kg/h during the case (with the infiltration dose reduced accordingly), are reasonable; this is author's judgement extrapolated from other laparoscopic surgery. Bilateral subcostal TAP blocks are the substitute when an NSAID cannot be given and for the opioid-tolerant patient. No catheter has a role.

The elderly giant hernia patient needs the plan adjusted, not abandoned: renal function often forbids the NSAID, opioid sensitivity is higher, and the larger mediastinal dissection gives more retrosternal pain and more atrial fibrillation. Subcostal TAP blocks and regular IV paracetamol with small opioid doses are the usual answer.

Rebound is not a feature. Escalation triggers: severe pain, tachycardia and fever in the first 48 hours is a leak; sudden epigastric pain with unproductive retching and an inability to swallow saliva is acute wrap herniation or obstruction and is a surgical emergency; persistent dysphagia to liquids is oedema at the wrap and usually settles.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Giant paraoesophageal hernia. Older, frailer, anaemic, with a stomach compressing the left atrium and left lower lobe. Rapid sequence induction, arterial line, a long mediastinal dissection with near-certain pleural breach, and a higher-acuity bed. Lung function often improves afterwards.
- Acute gastric volvulus. Chest pain, retching without vomiting and a nasogastric tube that will not pass. If decompression fails or ischaemia is suspected this is an emergency reduction in a shocked patient with a necrotic stomach in the chest: full-stomach induction, invasive lines, possible gastrectomy, critical care.
- Redo surgery. Adhesions between the old wrap, liver and diaphragm; longer, bloodier, a higher rate of gastric and oesophageal perforation and of conversion to open or thoracic approaches.
- Capnothorax versus tension pneumothorax. The former is expected and benign; the latter (lung injury, air leak) is rare, does not resolve when the gas is released, and needs a drain. The distinction is the time course after desufflation.
- Bougie injury. Perforation presents in recovery or overnight with pain, tachycardia, emphysema and fever. Any difficulty passing the bougie is reported to the surgeon at the time.
- Vagal injury. Delayed gastric emptying and bloating afterwards; no intraoperative sign.
- Postoperative vomiting. Early wrap disruption or herniation follows forceful retching; this is why the antiemetic regimen is heavier than the patient's risk score alone would justify.
- Open and transthoracic approaches. A left thoracotomy or upper midline incision for the short oesophagus or the multiply recurrent hernia changes the mechanism to chest wall or abdominal wall: epidural or paravertebral catheter, and the relevant card.
- Achalasia surgery. Heller myotomy with a partial wrap shares the position and the mediastinal gas but adds an oesophagus full of retained food; it is a different aspiration problem.

## Key sources

Evidence specific to anaesthesia for this operation is thin. The surgical components of repair come from the SAGES guideline. The behaviour and management of carbon dioxide pneumothorax rest on case series and descriptive reports such as the one cited, consistent with standard teaching. There is no procedure-specific analgesic guideline, so the pain plan is extrapolated from the PROSPECT cholecystectomy review and the adjunct advice is author's judgement. The reports of tamponade and aortic injury from hiatal fixation, and the link between early retching and wrap failure, are well known in surgical practice but were not sourced during drafting.
