## What the surgeon is doing and why

Five conditions make up most of the urgent general surgery of the first weeks of life. They share a patient (a neonate, often premature, with a circulation still in transition) and they share the conduct of neonatal anaesthesia, but each has its own surgical logic and its own trap. This card sets out the shared conduct first and then takes each lesion as its own pair of steps.

The patient. A neonate has a circulating volume of 80-90 mL/kg at term and nearer 100 mL/kg if premature, so 30 mL of blood on the swabs of a 3 kg infant is 10% of the volume. Cardiac output depends on heart rate. Pulmonary vascular resistance has only just fallen and rises again with hypoxia, acidosis, hypercapnia, cold and pain, reopening the duct and the foramen ovale and shunting right to left. Glycogen stores are small. Heat is lost through a large surface and a thin skin. In the European audit of 6,542 anaesthetics in early life, a critical event needing intervention occurred in 35.2% of cases and 90-day mortality was 3.2%; hypotension, hypoxaemia and anaemia occurring together were strongly associated with death.

The lesions and their logic:

```
 Lesion               Surgical aim                The trap for you
 -------------------  --------------------------  ----------------------------
 Oesophageal atresia  divide fistula, join        ventilating the stomach
 with fistula         oesophagus                  through the fistula
 Diaphragmatic        return viscera, close       pulmonary hypertension in
 hernia               diaphragm (not urgent)      a hypoplastic lung
 Gastroschisis,       return bowel, close wall    abdominal compartment
 exomphalos           (primary or staged)         syndrome at closure
 Necrotising          resect dead bowel, stoma,   sepsis, coagulopathy,
 enterocolitis        or drain                    prematurity, liver bleed
 Malrotation with     untwist, widen mesentery    time, then reperfusion
 volvulus             (Ladd procedure)
```

Only volvulus is an emergency measured in minutes. Oesophageal atresia and gastroschisis are repaired within the first day or two; necrotising enterocolitis is operated on when medical treatment fails or the bowel perforates; diaphragmatic hernia is repaired only after days of physiological stabilisation, because the problem is the lung and not the hole.

The pain logic: these are large wounds for the size of the patient, through the abdominal wall or between ribs, and several are closed under tension. That would score 4 in anyone. The neonate differs in assessment (behavioural scores, heart rate and blood pressure) and in pharmacology (immature clearance of morphine, paracetamol and amide local anaesthetics, and apnoea with opioids unless ventilated). Most of these infants stay intubated, which makes an opioid infusion the practical default; regional catheters are for the selected term infant in whom early extubation is the aim.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment on the neonatal unit
**Why:** The surgeon has decided an operation is needed; whether it happens in theatre or in the cot is a joint decision made on the stability of the infant.

**Anaesthesia:** Establish gestation, weight today, current ventilation (mode, pressures, inspired oxygen), inotropes, the last gas, haemoglobin, platelets, clotting and glucose, what is running through which line, and which anomalies travel with the lesion. An echocardiogram matters for oesophageal atresia (associated cardiac disease, side of the aortic arch), exomphalos and diaphragmatic hernia. Confirm vitamin K has been given and that neonatal-specification blood is crossmatched. An infant on high-frequency oscillation, nitric oxide or escalating inotropes is operated on in the unit; moving them is the larger risk.

### 2. Transfer, set-up and induction
**Why:** Nothing surgical yet; the surgeon should be scrubbed or present, because several of these inductions can require an immediate surgical rescue.

**Anaesthesia:** The theatre is at 26-28 degrees C with radiant heat on before the infant leaves the unit. Continue the glucose infusion or parenteral nutrition at its current rate through a dedicated line and give everything else through another. Aspirate the nasogastric tube. For an infant not already intubated: preoxygenate, then fentanyl 1-2 micrograms/kg IV with a small dose of propofol or sevoflurane, a relaxant (rocuronium 0.6-1 mg/kg IV) unless the lesion argues against one, and gentle pressure-limited mask ventilation. One prepared laryngoscopy; note the depth at the lips and tape as if the tube were irreplaceable. Maintain with low-concentration sevoflurane or none, fentanyl in 1-2 microgram/kg increments (sick premature infants tolerate fentanyl and relaxant far better than volatile), air and oxygen to a saturation target appropriate for gestation. Avoid nitrous oxide wherever bowel is obstructed or lung is compressed.

### 3. Oesophageal atresia with fistula: airway and bronchoscopy
**Why:** In the common form the upper oesophagus ends blind and the lower oesophagus arises from the back of the trachea, usually just above the carina. The surgeon often begins with rigid bronchoscopy to locate the fistula, exclude a second one, and sometimes occlude a large one with a balloon catheter.

**Anaesthesia:** The trap is that positive pressure follows the easier path. If the lungs are stiff (prematurity with respiratory distress syndrome) and the fistula is wide, gas goes to the stomach, the stomach splints the diaphragm, ventilation worsens and more gas is diverted, ending in gastric rupture or arrest. Keep the upper pouch on continuous suction and nurse head-up. Two approaches are in use: inhalational induction preserving spontaneous ventilation until the tube is past the fistula or the fistula is controlled, or intravenous induction with relaxant and very gentle low-pressure ventilation. Both are defensible; the first is safer when the lungs are poor. Tube position is the manoeuvre that matters: advance into the right main bronchus, withdraw until breath sounds are bilateral, so that the tip lies below the fistula and above the carina, with the bevel facing forward so the tube's wall covers the posterior opening. In an audit of 106 repairs, ventilation difficulty was recorded at induction in seven. If the stomach inflates and ventilation fails, the rescue is surgical: emergency ligation of the fistula, or needle decompression of the stomach.

### 4. Oesophageal atresia: thoracotomy, fistula ligation and anastomosis
**Why:** Through a right posterolateral thoracotomy in the fourth interspace (left if the arch is right-sided), staying outside the pleura, the surgeon retracts the lung forward, ties and divides the fistula, mobilises the upper pouch and sutures the two ends of oesophagus together, usually over a fine tube you pass through the nose.

**Anaesthesia:** Lung retraction is one-lung ventilation by hand: saturation falls and the surgeon must let the lung up when you say so. Retraction also kinks the soft trachea and the tube, and secretions or blood block a 3.0 mm lumen quickly; sudden loss of the capnograph trace is obstruction or dislodgement until proved otherwise. Significant desaturation was recorded in 15 of the audited cases. Once the fistula is tied, ventilation becomes ordinary. You will be asked to advance the transanastomotic tube a measured distance: do it slowly under the surgeon's direction and never push it again once the anastomosis is complete. A tight anastomosis under tension is protected by several days of elective ventilation, sedation and sometimes neck flexion; an easy one in a term infant can be extubated early, which is the group for whom a caudally threaded epidural catheter is used in some centres. Thoracoscopic repair replaces the incision with carbon dioxide insufflation of the chest and produces marked hypercapnia and acidosis (in the pilot trial PaCO2 83 mmHg against 61 mmHg open).

### 5. Congenital diaphragmatic hernia: stabilise, then choose the day
**Why:** Bowel, stomach, spleen and sometimes liver have occupied the chest (the left in most) since early gestation, so both lungs, and the ipsilateral one most, are small with a thick-walled, reactive vascular bed. Closing the diaphragm does not enlarge the lung. Surgery is therefore delayed until the infant has shown days of stability.

**Anaesthesia:** The infant was intubated at birth without mask ventilation (which inflates the intrathoracic stomach) and has a gastric tube on drainage. The European consensus targets are the ones you inherit and keep: preductal saturation 80-95%, postductal above 70%, PaCO2 50-70 mmHg, conventional ventilation with low peak pressures as the initial mode. The randomised comparison of conventional ventilation and oscillation found no significant difference in death or bronchopulmonary dysplasia. Read the echocardiogram for right ventricular function and the direction of ductal flow. An infant still needing escalating pulmonary vasodilators or inotropes is not ready; one on extracorporeal support may be repaired on it, with the bleeding risk that heparin brings.

### 6. Congenital diaphragmatic hernia: reduction and repair
**Why:** Through a subcostal laparotomy the surgeon draws the viscera down out of the chest and closes the defect directly or with a patch; a large patch repair may need the abdominal wall left open or closed with a silo.

**Anaesthesia:** Often done in the unit on the infant's own ventilator. High-dose fentanyl (10-20 micrograms/kg IV in increments, or more) and a relaxant blunt the pulmonary vascular response to incision; a light infant is one that shunts. Keep everything that raises pulmonary vascular resistance away: hypoxia, acidosis, a rising carbon dioxide, cold, and noise at the tube. Monitor saturation on the right hand and a foot: a widening gap is right-to-left ductal shunting. Do not try to inflate the hypoplastic lung once the chest is empty; it cannot expand and the good lung ruptures first. Sudden deterioration is a contralateral pneumothorax until excluded. After reduction the abdomen is tight: peak pressures rise and venous return falls, which is the cue to discuss the closure with the surgeon.

### 7. Gastroschisis and exomphalos: protecting the bowel
**Why:** In gastroschisis the bowel has herniated through a small defect to the right of the umbilicus with no covering sac; it is thickened and matted from exposure to amniotic fluid and loses heat and fluid from birth. In exomphalos the viscera lie within a sac at the umbilicus and the infant often has other anomalies.

**Anaesthesia:** Gastroschisis is usually an isolated lesion in a small infant; the problem is evaporative loss. The bowel is wrapped in plastic, supported so its mesentery does not kink, and the infant needs fluid well above maintenance (10-20 mL/kg boluses of warmed balanced crystalloid against perfusion and lactate) and a gastric tube on free drainage. Exomphalos demands a search for cardiac disease and, with a large tongue and a large baby, Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome with its airway difficulty and profound hypoglycaemia. Venous access goes in the upper limbs, because closure may compress the vena cava. A giant exomphalos with an intact sac is often managed without early surgery.

### 8. Gastroschisis and exomphalos: reduction and closure
**Why:** The surgeon returns the bowel to an abdominal cavity that has never held it and closes the wall, or, if it will not go, places the bowel in a silo suspended above the infant and reduces it over several days. In the national cohort, primary operative closure was intended in 51% of simple gastroschisis and a preformed silo in 36%.

**Anaesthesia:** You are the pressure gauge. Full relaxation gives the surgeon the best chance of primary closure, and as the bowel goes in you report peak inspiratory pressure, tidal volume, saturation in the feet and blood pressure. A marked rise in inflation pressure, a fall in tidal volume, mottled or desaturating legs, or falling urine output means the abdomen is too tight: caval and renal vein compression, bowel ischaemia and ventilatory failure follow. Intragastric or bladder pressure can be measured; a commonly quoted limit is about 20 mmHg. Say so plainly, because a silo is a good outcome and a compartment syndrome is not. Silo placement alone is brief and is sometimes done in the cot without general anaesthesia. After primary closure most infants stay ventilated and relaxed for a day or more.

### 9. Necrotising enterocolitis: laparotomy in the septic premature infant
**Why:** Ischaemic, infected bowel in a premature infant has perforated or failed medical treatment. The surgeon resects what is dead, preserves length, and brings out a stoma; in the smallest and sickest a peritoneal drain under local anaesthetic is the alternative. Two randomised trials found no overall difference in survival or in death and neurodevelopmental impairment between the two strategies, with a signal favouring laparotomy when the preoperative diagnosis was necrotising enterocolitis.

**Anaesthesia:** The patient may weigh under 1 kg, is already ventilated, septic, acidotic, thrombocytopenic and coagulopathic, and is usually operated on in the unit. This is a fentanyl and relaxant anaesthetic (fentanyl 10-30 micrograms/kg IV in divided doses) with little or no volatile. Have platelets, plasma and red cells in the room and give each at 10-15 mL/kg against measured loss and the gas, with calcium. Opening a tense abdomen drops the blood pressure; so does bowel handling, which releases mediators. Bleeding from the surface of the liver, which lies under the incision and is fragile at this gestation, is a recognised cause of death on the table: the surgeon keeps off it and you keep the circulation from being overfilled. Oxygen is titrated to a preterm saturation target. Inotrope (dopamine or adrenaline by infusion) is often needed by the end.

### 10. Malrotation and volvulus: Ladd procedure
**Why:** A midgut that failed to rotate hangs on a narrow stalk containing the superior mesenteric vessels and can twist around it, cutting off the blood supply to everything from duodenum to transverse colon. Bilious vomiting in a neonate is this until proved otherwise. The surgeon untwists the bowel anticlockwise, divides the peritoneal bands that cross the duodenum, widens the mesenteric base, removes the appendix and lays the small bowel to the right and the colon to the left.

**Anaesthesia:** Time is bowel. Resuscitate on the way and in the anaesthetic room, not on the ward: 20 mL/kg of crystalloid, blood crossmatched, a gas, and go. The stomach is full of bile: aspirate the tube and use a controlled rapid sequence with gentle ventilation. A previously well term infant can be in shock with a rising lactate within hours. Untwisting is a reperfusion event: acid, potassium and vasoactive products return from the ischaemic gut, with hypotension that needs volume, calcium and sometimes adrenaline. Have these drawn up before the surgeon untwists and ask to be told. If viability is doubtful the abdomen is closed for a second look in 24-48 hours and the infant stays ventilated.

### 11. Closure, analgesia and the end of the case
**Why:** The wall is closed in layers, a stoma is matured, or a silo or patch is secured; the surgeon infiltrates the wound.

**Anaesthesia:** Count the local anaesthetic against the neonatal ceiling (bupivacaine or levobupivacaine 2 mg/kg in total, which is 0.8 mL/kg of 0.25%). Check a gas, haemoglobin and glucose before leaving. Decide extubation honestly: a term infant after an easy oesophageal repair or a simple Ladd procedure, warm, with a normal gas and little opioid, can be extubated awake; everyone else goes back ventilated on a fentanyl or morphine infusion. Start paracetamol at the neonatal dose and interval for gestation.

### 12. Transfer and handover
**Why:** The operation is finished but the physiology is not: the next 24 hours bring third-space losses, the effects of closure and, for some, a second look.

**Anaesthesia:** The transfer is a procedure in its own right: a tube that moves 1 cm is out or endobronchial. Hand over tube size and depth, ventilation settings that worked, total fluid and blood in mL/kg, the last gas and glucose, drugs and times, the local anaesthetic dose, and what the surgeon found. State the specific thing to watch: abdominal pressure and leg perfusion after wall closure, pulmonary hypertension after diaphragmatic repair, anastomotic tension after oesophageal repair, bleeding and inotrope need after necrotising enterocolitis.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is an incision through the abdominal wall or chest wall that is large for the patient, sometimes closed under tension. The modifiers are a ventilated infant, immature drug clearance and frequent sepsis or coagulopathy. The plan that follows is systemic for most, regional for a few.

For the infant who stays intubated, a fentanyl infusion (for example 1-3 micrograms/kg/h IV) or a morphine infusion (10-20 micrograms/kg/h IV, lower in the premature infant) with regular paracetamol at a gestation-adjusted dose is the standard, titrated against a validated neonatal pain score. The surgeon's wound infiltration covers the first hours. NSAIDs are not used.

For the term infant in whom early extubation is the aim, opioid is the obstacle because of apnoea. A caudal catheter threaded cephalad to the dermatomes of the wound, with its tip confirmed by ultrasound, allows a dilute local anaesthetic infusion and extubation without opioid; some centres use it routinely for oesophageal atresia and uncomplicated laparotomy. Amide local anaesthetics accumulate in the neonate, so infusion rates are capped (bupivacaine at about 0.2 mg/kg/h) and limited to 48 hours. This is established specialist practice, not trial-proven superiority, and it is unavailable to the septic or coagulopathic infant. The catheter decision is therefore selective and is author's judgement outside units that do it regularly.

Rebound is low in the sense used elsewhere in this book: there is seldom a dense single-shot block to wear off. The failure mode is under-recognition. Tachycardia, hypertension, desaturation with handling and a pain score that climbs are treated with opioid, after a tense abdomen, a blocked tube and a pneumothorax have been considered.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Operating in the unit. The surgeon has poor light and no table; you have the unit's ventilator, no volatile and limited access. Total intravenous fentanyl and relaxant is the technique. The gain is that an unstable infant is not moved.
- Hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia. Stopping parenteral nutrition for transfer causes the first; stress and bolus glucose the second. Keep the infusion running and measure hourly.
- Hypothermia. Evaporation from exposed bowel is the largest heat loss in neonatal surgery. It raises pulmonary vascular resistance and worsens coagulopathy.
- Blood. A 1 kg infant has a circulating volume under 100 mL. Weigh swabs, give 10-15 mL/kg aliquots, and remember potassium in stored red cells and citrate binding calcium during rapid transfusion.
- Oxygen. Hyperoxia is harmful to the preterm retina and lung; hypoxia is harmful to the pulmonary circulation in diaphragmatic hernia. Titrate to the target for the lesion and gestation.
- Associated anomalies. Oesophageal atresia with cardiac, vertebral, anorectal and renal defects; exomphalos with cardiac disease and chromosomal syndromes. The cardiac lesion often determines the outcome.
- Long-gap oesophageal atresia without a fistula. No gas in the abdomen on the film; a gastrostomy first and a delayed repair, with a different and lower-risk first anaesthetic.
- H-type fistula. Presents later with coughing on feeds; repaired through the neck after bronchoscopic cannulation.
- Thoracoscopic neonatal surgery. Hypercapnia and acidosis are greater than with open surgery and were most marked in diaphragmatic hernia in the pilot trial; see [paediatric laparoscopic and thoracoscopic surgery](paediatric-laparoscopic-and-thoracoscopic-surgery.html).
- Abdominal compartment syndrome after closure. Oliguria, rising ventilator pressures and dusky legs in the first hours: the abdomen is reopened and a silo placed.
- The reference chapter for doses, fluids and airway sizes is [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html); transfusion ratios are in [major haemorrhage](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html).

## Key sources

Neonatal surgical emergencies have little randomised evidence about anaesthesia itself. What exists concerns surgical strategy (laparotomy against drain for necrotising enterocolitis, twice, with no overall difference), ventilation strategy in diaphragmatic hernia (one trial and a European consensus whose gas and saturation targets are used here), and a pilot trial showing the carbon dioxide cost of neonatal thoracoscopy. The airway management of tracheo-oesophageal fistula rests on a review and audit data; no technique has been shown to be superior. The 20 mmHg abdominal pressure limit, the neonatal local anaesthetic infusion ceiling, opioid infusion ranges and fluid aliquots are standard paediatric teaching and are stated without a source. The observation that liver haemorrhage is a lethal complication of laparotomy for necrotising enterocolitis is from case series known to the author and was not verified against a specific paper during drafting. No PROSPECT guideline covers any of these operations.
