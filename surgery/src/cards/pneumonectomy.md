## What the surgeon is doing and why

A pneumonectomy is what remains when a tumour cannot be removed by anything smaller: it crosses the fissure, sits on the main bronchus too close to the carina for a sleeve, or wraps the main pulmonary artery. Surgeons avoid it when they can, because the operation is a disease in its own right. In a French national database of 4,498 elective pneumonectomies the operative mortality was 7.8%, and in the North American registry it was 5.6% with a major adverse event in 30%. Right-sided resection carries nearly twice the risk of left.

The surgical tasks are few and each has a physiological consequence:

```
 Step                          Consequence
 ----------------------------  ------------------------------------
 Main pulmonary artery         whole cardiac output now crosses ONE
 divided                       capillary bed; RV afterload rises
 Both pulmonary veins          left atrial cuff: bleeding, air,
 divided                       atrial irritability
 Main bronchus stapled         short stump under tracheal pressure;
 flush with the carina         heals poorly; fistula risk for weeks
 Lung removed                  empty hemithorax: mediastinum free
                               to shift; heart can herniate if the
                               pericardium was opened
```

Variants add to this. An intrapericardial pneumonectomy opens the pericardium to control the vessels centrally and leaves a defect. An extrapleural pneumonectomy for mesothelioma removes the parietal pleura, lung, hemidiaphragm and pericardium en bloc and reconstructs the last two with patches; it bleeds from the whole chest wall. A completion pneumonectomy removes what a previous lobectomy left, through scar.

The right side is worse for reasons you can name. The right lung takes a little more than half of the cardiac output, so more vascular bed is lost. The right main bronchus is short, wide and in line with the trachea, with a single bronchial artery supply, so its stump is the one that breaks down: in a series of 733 pneumonectomies bronchopleural fistula occurred in 8.2%, with the right side an independent predictor. And a herniated heart on the right twists the cavae.

The pain logic: this is a full posterolateral thoracotomy, the anchor operation for a 5. The mechanism is chest wall, with rib spreading, costovertebral strain and intercostal nerve injury, and it is evoked by every breath. There is one mitigating feature and one aggravating one. There is no lung on that side to rub a drain against pleura, and the drain is usually out within a day. But the patient has half the pulmonary reserve, so the cost of splinting, sputum retention and opioid-induced hypoventilation is doubled.

## Steps

### 1. Selection: whether the remaining lung and the right ventricle can cope
**Why:** The surgeon needs to know before opening whether a pneumonectomy is survivable, because the decision is often made on the table when a lobectomy proves impossible.
**Anaesthesia:** Predicted postoperative FEV1 and DLCO are calculated from a quantitative perfusion scan (preoperative value multiplied by the fraction of perfusion going to the lung that stays), not by counting segments, because a lung with a central tumour may already contribute little. Apply the ACCP thresholds: both above 60%, proceed; either under 30%, a cardiopulmonary exercise test, where a peak oxygen consumption under 10 mL/kg/min marks high risk of death and long-term disability. Echocardiography for right ventricular function and estimated pulmonary artery pressure, because the right ventricle will meet a sudden rise in afterload. Low albumin predicts fistula and is correctable only with time. Agree with the surgeon beforehand what will be done if resection needs a pneumonectomy that the numbers do not support.

### 2. Catheter, lines and induction
**Why:** Nothing surgical. The analgesic catheter and the monitoring go in while the patient is upright and cooperative.
**Anaesthesia:** Place and test the epidural awake, or plan a paravertebral catheter (ultrasound-guided now, or surgeon-placed at closure with a single-shot block now to cover the operation). Arterial line before induction. Central line on the operative side, so that a pneumothorax from insertion cannot threaten the only lung that will remain. Induce with attention to the right ventricle: avoid hypoxia, hypercapnia and high airway pressures, each of which raises pulmonary vascular resistance.

### 3. Lung isolation with the stump in mind
**Why:** The surgeon will staple the main bronchus within a centimetre of the carina. Anything of yours in that bronchus will be in the staple line.
**Anaesthesia:** The simplest arrangement is a double-lumen tube whose bronchial limb sits in the lung that is staying: a left tube for a right pneumonectomy. For a left pneumonectomy the choices are a right-sided tube, with its slot aligned to the right upper lobe orifice by bronchoscope, or a left-sided tube that is withdrawn into the trachea before the stapler closes, or a blocker that is deflated and withdrawn at the same moment. Each works if the withdrawal is spoken aloud and done under bronchoscopic vision. See [one-lung ventilation and thoracic essentials](../a/a03-one-lung-ventilation.html).

### 4. Lateral position and thoracotomy
**Why:** A posterolateral thoracotomy through the fifth space gives the widest access to the hilum; a length of rib may be resected or divided to let the retractor open without fracture.
**Anaesthesia:** Recheck the tube after turning. Start one-lung ventilation as you intend to continue for the rest of the patient's life: 4-5 mL/kg predicted body weight, plateau under 25 cmH2O, modest PEEP, FiO2 titrated down. In 879 resections, a high ventilatory pressure index during surgery was the strongest independent risk factor for early acute lung injury (OR 3.5), ahead of excess fluid and of pneumonectomy itself. Rib spreading is intensely stimulating; the catheter should be loaded before it.

### 5. Exploration and the decision to proceed
**Why:** The surgeon inspects the pleura, palpates the hilum, opens the pericardium if the vessels cannot be controlled outside it, and decides between sleeve lobectomy, pneumonectomy and closing.
**Anaesthesia:** Intrapericardial dissection brings atrial arrhythmia, falls in output from cardiac retraction and the possibility of a left atrial tear. Keep blood in the room. This is also the time to remind the team what the predicted postoperative values were, because the temptation in the field is always to finish the resection.

### 6. Test clamp and division of the pulmonary artery
**Why:** The main pulmonary artery is encircled and clamped or occluded before it is stapled. The clamp is both a safety step and a physiological trial of life with one lung's vascular bed.
**Anaesthesia:** Watch the right ventricle for the next few minutes: systemic pressure, central venous pressure, rhythm, and the echocardiogram if a probe is down. A fall in systemic pressure with a rising central venous pressure and a distending right ventricle is failure of the trial, and it should be said immediately, while the clamp can still be released. Oxygenation usually improves at this point because the shunt through the collapsed lung has stopped. From here on the fluid rules of the trap apply: maintenance only, replace measured blood loss, and treat epidural or anaesthetic vasodilatation with noradrenaline.

### 7. Division of the pulmonary veins
**Why:** Superior and inferior veins are stapled, sometimes with a cuff of left atrium when tumour reaches the venous confluence.
**Anaesthesia:** A stapler on the atrium shortens it and can occlude the opposite veins or provoke atrial fibrillation on the table; treat a new rapid rhythm with synchronised cardioversion if it compromises pressure, since the pads should already be on. A slipped atrial staple line is the largest bleed of the operation and is controlled by a side-biting clamp while you transfuse. Air can enter an open left atrium: have the surgeon flood the field and keep the patient slightly head-down if the cuff is opened.

### 8. Stapling the main bronchus
**Why:** The bronchus is divided flush with the carina so that no blind pouch is left to collect secretions and infect the stump.
**Anaesthesia:** Suction the operative side one last time, then withdraw the tube, blocker or suction catheter from that bronchus and say so before the stapler closes. Ventilation continues undisturbed through the lumen to the dependent lung. After firing, hand ventilation should feel unchanged; a sudden leak means the tube cuff has been cut or the staple line has failed.

### 9. Stump test and coverage
**Why:** The stump is tested under saline and, in patients at high risk of breakdown (right side, neoadjuvant therapy, diabetes, poor nutrition), buttressed with a flap of intercostal muscle, pericardial fat, pleura or pericardium. Coverage is widely practised on historical fistula rates of 6-12% without it, though only one small randomised trial supports it.
**Anaesthesia:** With the tube in the trachea or the tracheal lumen open to the stump, apply a slow sustained pressure of 20-30 cmH2O by hand while the surgeon watches for bubbles. Do not exceed what was asked. Afterwards keep peak airway pressure as low as ventilation permits, because the stump now sees every breath.

### 10. Pericardial closure, drain and chest closure
**Why:** A pericardial defect is closed or patched so the heart cannot herniate into the empty hemithorax. A single drain is placed and left clamped or on a balanced system, never on suction, so that the mediastinum sits in the midline. Some surgeons place no drain and adjust the space by needle.
**Anaesthesia:** Suction on a pneumonectomy drain pulls the mediastinum across, kinks the cavae and stops venous return: make sure the drain is labelled and the recovery staff know. The surgeon places the paravertebral catheter under vision now if that is the plan. Load the catheter or epidural before the ribs are approximated. Give nothing extra to "fill" the patient for the turn.

### 11. Turning supine and extubation
**Why:** The surgeon wants an awake, extubated patient with a central trachea and a stable circulation; positive pressure ventilation on a fresh stump is the thing to avoid.
**Anaesthesia:** Turn slowly, with the monitors on and a hand on the pulse. Abrupt hypotension, tachycardia, cyanosis of the upper body or a new bundle branch pattern at or soon after the turn is cardiac herniation until proved otherwise: return the patient to the lateral position with the operated side up, do not inject air or fluid into the empty space, keep airway pressure low, and the chest is reopened. Otherwise extubate in theatre, sitting up, fully reversed, with the block working. Avoid bucking: a smooth emergence matters more here than after any other thoracic operation. If ventilation must continue, change to a single-lumen tube under vision and keep its tip and cuff well above the carina.

### 12. The first week: oedema, rhythm, stump
**Why:** The space fills with fluid over days and the mediastinum settles; the surgeon follows the air-fluid level on the daily radiograph.
**Anaesthesia:** Three complications have a timetable and each is partly yours. Post-pneumonectomy pulmonary oedema appears on days 1-3 as rising oxygen requirement and infiltrates in the remaining lung with normal filling pressures; it was first tied to perioperative fluid load and right-sided resection, primary lung injury after resection carried 26% mortality in the largest cohort, and treatment is supportive, with non-invasive support preferred over intubation. Atrial fibrillation peaks on days 2-4 and is commoner than after lobectomy: continue beta-blockers, keep electrolytes replete, control rate, and look for the cause. Bronchopleural fistula presents early as a falling fluid level, a new air leak, or sudden breathlessness with expectoration of thin brown fluid, and later with empyema. The immediate action is positional: sit the patient up and lie them operated side down, so the remaining lung is not flooded, and drain the space.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is chest wall through a full thoracotomy, in a patient with one lung. A continuous regional technique from before the incision until the patient is mobilising and coughing freely, usually three to five days, is the core and is not optional.

PROSPECT's 2025 thoracotomy guideline recommends either a thoracic epidural or a paravertebral block as first-line, by patient and clinician preference, with paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor as basic analgesia. For pneumonectomy the choice between the two has a specific argument. The Cochrane comparison found equivalent analgesia with far less hypotension from paravertebral block (RR 0.16). Hypotension after pneumonectomy is treated with either fluid or vasopressor, and fluid is the thing this patient cannot be given. The defensible default is therefore a paravertebral catheter, placed by ultrasound before incision or by the surgeon under vision before closure, running ropivacaine 0.2% at 8-12 mL/h or as programmed boluses. An epidural at T5-T7 is equally acceptable where it is the unit's reliable technique; run it dilute (ropivacaine 0.2% or bupivacaine 0.1% with fentanyl 2 micrograms/mL at 6-10 mL/h), avoid large boluses, and meet the vasodilatation with low-dose noradrenaline. That preference is author's judgement built on the mechanism; no trial has compared the two in pneumonectomy alone. For the general comparison see [analgesia for the trunk](../a/a02-truncal-blocks-epidural.html).

Around the catheter: regular paracetamol; an NSAID when renal function and the surgeon allow, mindful that this patient is deliberately kept dry; low-dose ketamine for the opioid-tolerant; opioid by PCA as rescue in small boluses with no background, because hypoventilation and sputum retention in a single lung is how a recoverable patient becomes a ventilated one.

Rebound risk is high and the response to a failed catheter must be the same day: re-site, or add an erector spinae catheter, not simply raise the PCA. Step down when the patient is walking and coughing, with oral opioid started before the infusion stops. Escalation triggers that are not analgesic problems: new shoulder or chest pain with breathlessness and a falling fluid level is a fistula; pain with tachycardia and hypotension in the first hours is bleeding into a space that can hold the circulating volume.

The chronic tail is that of any thoracotomy: about half of patients report pain at six months. Good acute control is the only factor in your hands and its preventive effect is unproven.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Extrapleural pneumonectomy. Hours of chest wall oozing, blood loss in litres, patches on the diaphragm and pericardium, and the highest morbidity of any variant. Here the restrictive fluid rule gives way to replacing what is lost, with blood products and not crystalloid.
- Completion pneumonectomy. Scarred hilum, intrapericardial control, more bleeding, more stump failure.
- Carinal pneumonectomy and sleeve resection. The airway is open across the field and ventilation is by a sterile tube in the opposite bronchus or by jet; see [rigid bronchoscopy, stents and tracheal resection](rigid-bronchoscopy-stents-and-tracheal-resection.html) for cross-field technique.
- Anaesthesia for an established bronchopleural fistula. Positive pressure escapes through the stump and the space contents spill into the good lung. Drain the space first; keep the patient sitting and tilted operated side down; isolate the remaining lung before any positive pressure, with a double-lumen tube placed under bronchoscopic vision either awake or with spontaneous ventilation maintained, bronchial limb in the good side; then ventilate that lung alone. See also [pleural surgery](pleural-surgery-decortication-pleurodesis-and-pneumothorax.html).
- Later surgery after pneumonectomy. The mediastinum has shifted and the trachea deviates towards the empty side; one lung means a short safe apnoea time, no tolerance for a pneumothorax, and central lines placed on the pneumonectomy side.
- Emergency variants. Pneumonectomy for massive haemoptysis or trauma is a salvage operation with very high mortality, driven by acute right ventricular failure in a patient who has also bled; isolate the lung, restrict crystalloid, support the right ventricle early.
- For the smaller resection and for one-lung hypoxaemia management, see [lobectomy and wedge resection](video-assisted-and-open-lobectomy-and.html).

## Key sources

The mortality and risk-factor figures come from two large registries and are robust. The link between fluid and post-pneumonectomy pulmonary oedema rests on a 1984 case series with an animal model and on later cohort data in which fluid, ventilatory pressure and pneumonectomy were independent risks; no randomised trial of fluid restriction exists, and the 20 mL/kg figure is conventional teaching, not a tested threshold. Stump coverage is supported by pooled series with obvious selection bias. The analgesic recommendation follows PROSPECT and the Cochrane review for thoracotomy in general; the preference for paravertebral over epidural in this operation is the author's inference from the hypotension data. The management of cardiac herniation and of the fistula airway is consensus built on case reports.
