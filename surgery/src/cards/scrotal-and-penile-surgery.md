## What the surgeon is doing and why

This card collects the short operations on the external male genitalia. They share a day-case setting, a small incision in sensitive skin, and a nerve supply that is easy to block. They differ in which incision is used, and that decides which nerves matter.

Operations through the scrotum: exploration for torsion (the testis is untwisted and, if viable, fixed with sutures, and the other side is fixed too because the anatomical fault is bilateral); hydrocele repair (the tunica vaginalis is opened, drained, and everted or plicated so that it cannot refill); excision of an epididymal cyst; vasectomy (each vas is delivered through a puncture, divided and occluded); and simple orchidectomy for infarction, infection or androgen ablation. The testis is brought out of the wound on its cord for all but vasectomy.

Operations through the groin: radical orchidectomy for a testicular tumour is done through an inguinal incision so that the cord can be clamped at the deep ring before the tumour is handled and the scrotal lymphatic field is never breached. The testis is delivered upwards into the groin. Open varicocele ligation uses the same region, and the microsurgical operation a small subinguinal cut.

Operations on the penis: circumcision removes the prepuce and sutures the skin to the mucosal collar below the glans; frenuloplasty lengthens a short frenulum.

The nerve supply explains both the blocks and the reflexes.

The testis is an abdominal organ that has migrated, and its pain fibres still enter the cord at T10-L1. That is why testicular pathology presents with nausea and periumbilical pain, why traction on the cord produces a vagal response under an anaesthetic that seemed adequate for a skin incision, and why a block placed in the scrotal skin alone is not enough.

The pain logic is skin and soft tissue: a 2 on the scale. The wounds are small, but the scrotum is loose and dependent, so oedema and haematoma collect, and the day 1 ache is as much swelling as incision. The large day 1 cohort makes the general point that small operations are often under-treated. Circumcision in an adult is the outlier: the suture line is put on stretch by erections during sleep, so pain recurs nightly for one to two weeks. The trajectory and the low rebound risk in the summary box describe the scrotal operations; adult circumcision does not follow them, and its nights stay painful through the first week when the scrotal patient is nearly pain-free. A minority of men develop chronic scrotal pain after vasectomy or other cord surgery, which is a neuropathic and congestive problem managed in the first instance with cord blocks.

## Steps

### 1. Which operation, which incision
**Why:** Scrotal, inguinal and penile approaches have different nerve supplies and different haemostatic risks.
**Anaesthesia:** Match the block to the incision from the table above. Ask whether a prosthesis is planned (antibiotic), whether both sides will be done, and for tumour patients whether sperm banking and tumour markers have happened. Young men with testicular cancer may have retroperitoneal or mediastinal disease; a bulky mediastinal mass changes the anaesthetic entirely and is found by reading the staging CT, not at induction.

### 2. Choice of anaesthetic
**Why:** Most of these operations can be done under local anaesthesia; the surgeon's habit and the patient's preference decide.
**Anaesthesia:** Vasectomy is a local anaesthetic procedure. Benign scrotal surgery under local anaesthesia alone is well supported: in a series of 303 hydrocele, spermatocele and epididymal operations none needed conversion and there were no intra-operative complications. Adult circumcision is done comfortably under a penile block with or without sedation. General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway is the usual list default because it is quick, and the block is then placed asleep for post-operative analgesia. A low spinal suits the patient with bad lungs or a preference to be awake; it delays discharge by the time taken to void and walk.

### 3. Torsion: the clock
**Why:** A twisted cord occludes venous then arterial flow. The testis infarcts in hours, and no test is sensitive enough to exclude torsion, so the surgeon explores on clinical suspicion.
**Anaesthesia:** Go now. In one series duration of symptoms was the only modifiable predictor of loss: about 8.5 hours was the best cut-off for predicting a dead testis, salvage was down to a half at 15 hours, and a quarter of the testes that were fixed atrophied later. The patient is typically an adolescent, in severe pain, nauseated or vomiting, and recently fed. Rapid sequence induction with a tracheal tube, antiemetic, and no waiting for a fasting interval. Consent often includes orchidectomy and contralateral fixation.

### 4. The block
**Why:** To cover the incision and, for scrotal and inguinal work, to interrupt the visceral afferents in the cord before it is pulled.
**Anaesthesia:** Spermatic cord block: the cord is held at the scrotal neck or located by ultrasound at the external ring and 8-10 mL of long-acting local anaesthetic is placed around it, with a skin wheal along the incision; aspirate, because the testicular artery and pampiniform plexus are in the needle path. Dorsal penile nerve block: plain local anaesthetic deep to Buck's fascia on each side at the base of the penis, with a subcutaneous ventral ring for the frenulum; ultrasound guidance improves reliability, and in a prospective observational comparison in children having circumcision it gave better analgesia to 24 hours than a low-dose caudal. Inguinal incision: ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric block or a transversus abdominis plane block on that side, plus the surgeon's cord infiltration before clamping. The surgeon will often add local under vision; agree the total dose.

### 5. Incision and delivery of the testis
**Why:** The scrotal layers are opened and the testis is delivered on its cord, or in the inguinal approach the canal is opened and the cord is isolated and clamped at the deep ring before the testis is pushed up from below.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the vagal moment. A randomised trial in varicocele surgery under general anaesthesia found that a cord block placed before traction gave a smaller fall in heart rate and pressure and far fewer pauses in surgery for symptomatic bradycardia. Without a block, be at an adequate depth and have an anticholinergic to hand; ask the surgeon to release traction first. Laryngospasm on a supraglottic airway at the moment of cord traction in a young man is the same reflex arriving through a light anaesthetic.

### 6. The procedure itself
**Why:** Hydrocele: the sac is opened, the fluid drained, the redundant sac excised or plicated behind the testis. Torsion: detorsion, warm packs, assessment of colour and bleeding, then three-point fixation or orchidectomy, and fixation of the other side. Orchidectomy: the cord is divided between ligatures in two bundles. Vasectomy: the vas is delivered, a segment excised, the ends cauterised and separated by fascia.
**Anaesthesia:** Short and unstimulating once the testis is out and the cord is blocked. A prosthesis, if placed, goes in now under antibiotic cover. If the torsion patient was induced in a hurry, this is the time to give the multimodal analgesia and the second antiemetic.

### 7. Haemostasis and closure
**Why:** The scrotum has no fascial compartments to contain bleeding; a small vessel in the dartos or the edge of a hydrocele sac can fill it. Surgeons are meticulous here and some leave a small drain after large hydrocele repair.
**Anaesthesia:** Avoid a coughing, straining emergence: remove the airway deep or wake the patient smoothly with the antiemetic on board. Hypertension on waking is treated. Scrotal support goes on in theatre.

### 8. Circumcision and penile procedures
**Why:** The prepuce is excised by sleeve or guillotine technique, bleeding points on the shaft are controlled with bipolar diathermy (monopolar current through an end organ is avoided), and skin is sutured to mucosa.
**Anaesthesia:** The dorsal penile block with a ventral ring is both the anaesthetic and the analgesic. A penile erection under light general anaesthesia makes the operation difficult; deepening the anaesthetic usually resolves it, and the block prevents it. Adults should be told about night-time erections: the pain they cause is expected, peaks in the first week and is not a sign of wound failure.

### 9. Recovery and discharge
**Why:** The surgeon wants a dry dressing, a supported scrotum and a patient who will rest for a few days.
**Anaesthesia:** Standard day-case discharge with paracetamol and an NSAID taken regularly for three to five days, and a few doses of oral opioid for adult circumcision, orchidectomy and large hydrocele repair. A numb scrotum or penis for 6-12 hours is the block. A scrotum that is enlarging, tense and painful in recovery is a haematoma and goes back to the surgeon before discharge.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is superficial and local, so the plan is a block matched to the incision and simple oral analgesia. For scrotal operations: a spermatic cord block with skin infiltration, placed before the cord is handled so that it also blunts the vagal reflex. For penile operations: a dorsal penile nerve block with ventral infiltration, using plain local anaesthetic. For the inguinal approach: an ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric block or infiltration of the wound and cord. Paracetamol and an NSAID are started in theatre and continued; dexamethasone covers nausea and adds a little analgesia. This is the author's practice, built from anatomy and small trials: there is no PROSPECT guideline for scrotal or penile surgery, and the comparative evidence for the penile block is paediatric and observational.

No catheter and no neuraxial opioid has a place. Rebound when the block wears off is mild because the tissue injury is small, with the exception of adult circumcision, where the first night's erections arrive as the block fades; a dose of oral opioid at bedtime for the first two or three nights is reasonable.

Escalation triggers: increasing pain with a swelling, tense scrotum is a haematoma; pain with fever and spreading erythema after a few days is infection; severe pain out of proportion with skin discolouration or crepitus in a diabetic or immunosuppressed patient is necrotising infection. Pain that persists beyond three months after vasectomy or cord surgery should be named as chronic scrotal content pain and referred; reviews describe a stepwise approach of NSAIDs and neuropathic agents, diagnostic cord blocks, and then microsurgical denervation or reversal.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Torsion. Time-critical, full stomach, adolescent. The anaesthetic risk of proceeding is small; the cost of waiting is a testis.
- Fournier gangrene. The high-risk variant: necrotising fasciitis of the perineum and scrotum in diabetic, obese or immunosuppressed patients. Septic shock, wide debridement with real blood loss, repeated returns to theatre, and intensive care. Arterial line, central access, vasopressor, blood available, tracheal tube; no neuraxial or regional technique in infected tissue. Nothing else on this card resembles it.
- Testicular tumour. Inguinal approach, a prosthesis in some, and occasionally advanced disease with a mediastinal mass, pulmonary metastases or previous bleomycin, in whom inspired oxygen is kept as low as is safe.
- Vagal collapse under local anaesthesia. Common in vasectomy clinics: young men, cord traction, supine recovery and atropine if it persists.
- Local anaesthetic dose. A cord block, skin infiltration and the surgeon's top-up on both sides adds up; set the ceiling by weight at the start.
- Anticoagulated patients. Scrotal haematoma is the complication; hydrocele repair on antithrombotics deserves a deliberate plan.
- Children. Orchidopexy and circumcision are covered with paediatric surgery; caudal or peripheral blocks are routine there.
- Penile prosthesis, urethroplasty and priapism surgery. Longer operations with perineal incisions; pudendal blocks or a spinal are the relevant techniques.
- Chronic scrotal pain. A minority after vasectomy; warn before, document early.

## Key sources

Evidence for this group of operations is thin and mostly practical. The local anaesthesia series and the cord block trial support what many units already do. The torsion data are retrospective and show that time is the only modifiable factor. The dorsal penile block study is a prospective observational comparison in children, not a randomised trial, and is cited for the block's efficacy, not as adult evidence. Chronic post-vasectomy pain is described in narrative reviews without agreed incidence figures. The block recommendations are the author's judgement from anatomy.
