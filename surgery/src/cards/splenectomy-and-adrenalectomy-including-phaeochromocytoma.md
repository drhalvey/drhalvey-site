## What the surgeon is doing and why

Two solid organs tucked under opposite sides of the diaphragm, removed through the same kind of flank laparoscopy, grouped here because the surgery is similar and simple. What differs, and what makes the card, is the reason for removal. In both operations the surgeon's decisive act is controlling a vein, and in both the patient's disease, not the dissection, sets your plan.

Splenectomy. The spleen filters blood and makes antibody; it is removed when it is destroying blood cells (immune thrombocytopenia that has failed medical treatment, hereditary spherocytosis, autoimmune haemolysis), when it is diseased or enormous (lymphoma, myelofibrosis), as part of another resection, or when it is bleeding after trauma. Laparoscopically the patient lies right side down, the splenic flexure and the ligaments to the diaphragm and kidney are divided, the short gastric vessels are taken, and the hilum (splenic artery and vein, with the tail of the pancreas lying in it) is stapled. The spleen is bagged, broken up and extracted. The trauma operation is a different thing: a midline laparotomy in a bleeding patient, the spleen delivered into the wound and the hilum clamped by hand within minutes.

Adrenalectomy. Each adrenal sits on the upper pole of its kidney. The left drains by a long vein into the left renal vein. The right drains by a short, wide vein, a few millimetres long, straight into the back of the vena cava, and that single anatomical fact is the surgical hazard of the right side. The gland is removed for a hormone-secreting adenoma (aldosterone in Conn's syndrome, cortisol in Cushing's), for a catecholamine-secreting phaeochromocytoma, for a large or suspicious incidental mass, or for metastasis. The lateral transabdominal approach is commonest; the posterior retroperitoneoscopic approach, prone with gas insufflated directly into the retroperitoneum, avoids the peritoneal cavity altogether and suits small tumours and bilateral disease.

Phaeochromocytoma deserves its own paragraph. The tumour stores and releases noradrenaline and adrenaline. The patient lives vasoconstricted and volume-contracted, sometimes with a catecholamine cardiomyopathy. Release is provoked by anything that squeezes or stimulates the tumour: induction, laryngoscopy, positioning, pneumoperitoneum and above all surgical handling. Once the adrenal vein is ligated the source is cut off within one circulation time, leaving down-regulated receptors, residual alpha blockade and an empty circulation. The Endocrine Society guideline recommends preoperative blockade for all functional tumours with salt and fluid loading. A large observational series from one high-volume centre found only a slight difference in peak systolic pressure between blocked and unblocked patients and no major complications in either; that challenges the dogma but has not changed the guideline, and blockade remains the defensible default.

The pain logic: laparoscopic. Three or four ports under the costal margin or in the flank, an extraction site, diaphragmatic irritation with shoulder referral, and musculoskeletal flank pain from the lateral position over a broken table. Moderate, peaking in 24 hours, gone by day 3. The retroperitoneoscopic approach hurts least. Open surgery through a subcostal or upper midline incision is an abdominal wall mechanism and scores 5 on this book's scale, in line with other open upper abdominal surgery: severe on coughing and re-provoked beyond 72 hours, with a trajectory nearer [4, 6, 6, 5, 4].

## Steps

### 1. Preassessment and preparation
**Why:** The surgeon needs the endocrine or haematological disease controlled enough to operate.
**Anaesthesia:** Phaeochromocytoma: alpha blockade (phenoxybenzamine or doxazosin) for 7-14 days until the patient has a normal seated pressure, mild postural drop and a stuffy nose; a beta blocker added only afterwards for tachycardia; liberal salt and fluid; echocardiogram if there is any suggestion of cardiomyopathy; glucose. Take the last alpha blocker dose the evening before or the morning of surgery by local preference. Conn's: potassium into the normal range. Cushing's: glucose, pressure, a look at the skin and veins, and a plan for thromboprophylaxis. Splenectomy: platelet count and its response to steroids or immunoglobulin, vaccination status, and steroid history. A count of 20-30 x 10^9/L is accepted by many surgeons for laparoscopic splenectomy in immune thrombocytopenia because transfused platelets are destroyed until the artery is tied.

### 2. Induction and lines
**Why:** Nothing surgical yet.
**Anaesthesia:** For phaeochromocytoma place the arterial line awake with a little midazolam, then induce slowly and deeply with propofol and a generous opioid (remifentanil or fentanyl) and a non-histamine-releasing relaxant, and blunt laryngoscopy fully: this is the first of four surge points. Have drawn up and connected: a short-acting vasodilator (phentolamine 1-2 mg IV boluses, or glyceryl trinitrate or sodium nitroprusside by infusion), magnesium sulphate (40-60 mg/kg IV load then 1-2 g/h), esmolol, and noradrenaline for later. Avoid ketamine, ephedrine, desflurane, morphine, atracurium, metoclopramide and droperidol. Avoid glucocorticoids as well, dexamethasone for nausea included, until the tumour is out: a report of four cases with a review of seven earlier ones describes phaeochromocytoma crisis after glucocorticoid administration, one of them fatal, and concludes that these drugs should be avoided or given only if necessary and with caution in known or suspected phaeochromocytoma. Use ondansetron as the antiemetic. Where steroid replacement is unavoidable (bilateral adrenalectomy, or a patient on long-term steroid), give the hydrocortisone after the adrenal vein is ligated when that is possible, and with vasodilators connected when it is not. Central line after induction. For everyone else, routine induction with a tracheal tube; hydrocortisone 100 mg IV at induction for those who need cover (Cushing's adrenalectomy and long-term steroid users without a phaeochromocytoma).

### 3. Positioning
**Why:** Lateral decubitus with the table broken opens the flank; prone jackknife for the posterior approach.
**Anaesthesia:** Axillary roll, neutral neck, dependent arm and eye protected, upper arm supported without stretching the plexus. Patients with Cushing's syndrome have thin skin that tears with tape, osteoporotic bones that fracture with careless handling, and central obesity that makes lateral positioning awkward. Turning and flexing is the second surge point in phaeochromocytoma. In immune thrombocytopenia with a low count, avoid nasal tubes and pad everything.

### 4. Ports and insufflation
**Why:** Working space in the peritoneal cavity or, for the posterior approach, directly in the retroperitoneal fat under high pressure.
**Anaesthesia:** Pneumoperitoneum compresses the tumour and raises catecholamine levels: the third surge point, treated with depth, magnesium and a vasodilator bolus. Retroperitoneal insufflation at 20-25 mmHg is well tolerated haemodynamically but carbon dioxide is absorbed quickly through tissue planes: expect a brisk rise in end-tidal carbon dioxide, surgical emphysema up to the neck, and a need for 30-50% more minute ventilation.

### 5. Mobilisation
**Why:** Splenectomy: the colon and the lateral and diaphragmatic attachments are divided so the spleen hangs from its hilum. Adrenalectomy: the liver (right) or spleen and pancreatic tail (left) are rotated medially to expose the gland in the perinephric fat.
**Anaesthesia:** For the spleen, a capsular tear here causes most of the bleeding in the case; a massive spleen needs blood in the room. Diaphragmatic dissection can open the pleura to carbon dioxide. For phaeochromocytoma this begins the fourth and worst surge period: each time the tumour is touched the pressure climbs within seconds, with or without tachyarrhythmia. Treat peaks with short-acting drugs only, tell the surgeon when to pause, and do not chase every swing into overtreatment, because the half-life of your vasodilator must be shorter than the time to vein ligation.

### 6. Vascular control: splenic hilum or adrenal vein
**Why:** The splenic artery and vein are stapled together or separately. The adrenal vein is clipped and divided; many surgeons go for the vein early in phaeochromocytoma to stop hormone release before the tumour is handled further.
**Anaesthesia:** Splenectomy in immune thrombocytopenia: once the artery is controlled, give platelets if the field is oozing or the count is very low. A stapler misfire on the hilum is sudden, large bleeding and a conversion.

Adrenal vein ligation in phaeochromocytoma is the turning point. Ask the surgeon to announce it a minute ahead. Stop vasodilators and magnesium boluses, give 500-1,000 mL of crystalloid if not already loaded, and have noradrenaline running or ready; vasopressin is the next step when noradrenaline at rising dose is ineffective against blocked or down-regulated receptors. A pressure that does not fall after ligation suggests a second tumour or an accessory vein. On the right, a tear where the vein joins the vena cava is the bleeding emergency of adrenal surgery: venous, immediate and behind the liver.

### 7. Completion of dissection and extraction
**Why:** The gland with its surrounding fat, or the spleen, is freed, bagged and removed; a spleen is morcellated in the bag to avoid splenosis and a large wound; the tail of the pancreas is inspected.
**Anaesthesia:** For phaeochromocytoma, start checking glucose every 30-60 minutes: the loss of catecholamine suppression of insulin secretion produces hypoglycaemia in the hours after removal, and an anaesthetised or beta-blocked patient shows no signs. After unilateral adrenalectomy for Cushing's the remaining gland is suppressed and the patient is now adrenally insufficient: confirm that hydrocortisone is running.

### 8. Trauma splenectomy
**Why:** A haemodynamically unstable patient with a ruptured spleen who has failed or is unsuitable for embolisation or observation has a laparotomy, four-quadrant packing and a rapid splenectomy.
**Anaesthesia:** A major haemorrhage case: rapid sequence induction with reduced doses, haemorrhage protocol activated, TXA 1 g IV over 10 minutes if within three hours of injury, followed by 1 g IV over 8 hours, permissive hypotension until the hilum is clamped unless there is a head injury, calcium, warming. Opening the abdomen releases the tamponade. Other injuries (diaphragm, pancreas, left kidney, ribs and lung) coexist. Vaccinate before discharge.

### 9. Closure and emergence
**Why:** Ports are closed; a drain is left near the pancreatic tail by some surgeons.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask for local anaesthetic into each port and the extraction site; add the subcostal TAP or quadratus lumborum block now if planned. Extubate awake once fully reversed. After retroperitoneoscopy with extensive emphysema, ventilate until the end-tidal carbon dioxide has come down.

### 10. The first 24 hours
**Why:** The surgeon expects a quick laparoscopic recovery; the endocrine consequences run on a different clock.
**Anaesthesia:** Phaeochromocytoma: high dependency, arterial line kept, noradrenaline weaned over hours as volume is restored, hourly glucose for at least the first 12 hours and regular checks, with blood pressure and heart rate, through the first 24-48 hours, antihypertensives reviewed (most stop). Persistent hypertension is pain, fluid overload, residual tumour or underlying essential hypertension. Cushing's and bilateral adrenalectomy: hydrocortisone continued and converted to oral with an endocrinology plan; an unexplained hypotension, fever or hyponatraemia is an adrenal crisis. Conn's: stop potassium supplements and spironolactone, watch for hyperkalaemia. Splenectomy: platelets rise over days, sometimes above 1,000 x 10^9/L; fever with abdominal pain in the first weeks raises portal vein thrombosis or a subphrenic collection.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is laparoscopic with a flank component, so the plan is the standard anti-inflammatory and local regimen for upper abdominal laparoscopy, modified by three diagnoses that each remove one of its drugs. There is no PROSPECT guideline for either operation; the plan is extrapolated from the cholecystectomy guideline on mechanism and is author's judgement.

The base: paracetamol 1 g IV at induction and regularly, port-site and extraction-site infiltration by the surgeon, short-acting opioid in theatre, and oral opioid for breakthrough on days 0 to 2. A unilateral subcostal TAP block or a quadratus lumborum block on the operated side covers the ports and the extraction wound in one injection and is a reasonable addition; an erector spinae plane block suits the posterior approach because the ports are paraspinal.

The modifications:

- NSAIDs. Avoid in splenectomy for thrombocytopenia until the count has risen, and in anyone on high-dose steroid; use in Conn's, incidentaloma and phaeochromocytoma with normal kidneys.
- Dexamethasone. Pointless in a patient receiving hydrocortisone and inappropriate in Cushing's syndrome. Avoided in phaeochromocytoma and paraganglioma until the tumour is out, and in any adrenal mass in which a phaeochromocytoma has not been excluded, because glucocorticoids can trigger a catecholamine crisis; the analgesic and antiemetic benefit is not worth that risk. Acceptable in Conn's, a worked-up non-functioning incidentaloma and splenectomy.
- Opioid choice in phaeochromocytoma. Morphine releases histamine and is avoided before the tumour is out; fentanyl, remifentanil and oxycodone are used. Uncontrolled pain after surgery is a common cause of residual hypertension, so do not under-treat on the grounds that the patient is in high dependency.

For open surgery (large or malignant adrenal tumours, paragangliomas, massive spleens) the wound is subcostal or midline and needs a continuous technique: an epidural gives excellent conditions for phaeochromocytoma resection but deepens the hypotension after vein ligation, and is unusable with thrombocytopenia; subcostal TAP or rectus sheath catheters avoid both problems, and intrathecal morphine can be added to them only when the platelet count and coagulation are acceptable for a spinal needle, which excludes the thrombocytopenic splenectomy patient. If intrathecal morphine is given, the ward has to watch for delayed respiratory depression with sedation score, respiratory rate and oximetry at a frequency and for a duration matched to the dose and the patient's risk (see the [truncal blocks and epidural chapter](../a/a02-truncal-blocks-epidural.html)), with no background opioid infusion and no other long-acting opioid or sedative in that window; the obese patient with Cushing's syndrome and sleep apnoea is at the high-risk end. An epidural catheter and the low molecular weight heparin these patients need are separated by the intervals in the current ASRA guideline or local policy.

Rebound is low. Escalation triggers: left shoulder and upper abdominal pain with fever after splenectomy is a subphrenic collection or pancreatic tail leak; severe flank or back pain with hypotension after right adrenalectomy is bleeding; abdominal pain with vomiting and hypotension in a steroid-dependent patient is adrenal crisis and is treated with hydrocortisone 100 mg IV before it is investigated.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The undiagnosed phaeochromocytoma. Severe unexplained hypertension and tachycardia at induction or during handling of an "incidentaloma": deepen, give magnesium and phentolamine, ask the surgeon to stop, and consider abandoning the operation for proper preparation.
- Beta blockade before alpha blockade. Unopposed alpha stimulation produces hypertensive crisis and pulmonary oedema. Labetalol alone is not adequate alpha blockade.
- Catecholamine cardiomyopathy. Often reversible, sometimes severe; these patients tolerate neither the surges nor the fluid load and need cardiac output monitoring.
- Paraganglioma. Extra-adrenal tumours along the aorta or bladder, usually open, with multiple draining veins and no single moment of ligation.
- Bilateral adrenalectomy. Lifelong glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement; the patient must leave with sick-day rules and an emergency injection kit.
- Cushing's syndrome. Hypertension, diabetes, hypokalaemia, obesity with obstructive sleep apnoea, myopathy, poor wound healing, infection and a substantially raised risk of venous thromboembolism, which is the reason for pharmacological prophylaxis after even a laparoscopic adrenalectomy.
- Massive splenomegaly. Open or hand-assisted, litres of blood sequestered in the organ, portal hypertension in some, and a left diaphragm pushed up into the chest: left lower lobe collapse afterwards is the rule.
- Haematological malignancy and haemolysis. Anaemia, neutropenia, irradiated or phenotyped blood products, and recent chemotherapy.
- Overwhelming post-splenectomy infection. Lifelong risk, highest in the first years and in children; any fever in an asplenic patient presenting for later surgery is taken seriously and treated early.
- Pregnancy. Phaeochromocytoma in pregnancy and splenic artery aneurysm rupture are rare and lethal; both need a tertiary team.

## Key sources

The Endocrine Society guideline is the reference for preparation of phaeochromocytoma, and its recommendation for universal alpha blockade rests on observational data and physiological reasoning. The case series cited against it is observational too, from a single expert centre, and is included because the reader will meet colleagues who quote it. Drug choices for controlling surges, the avoidance list, and the management of post-ligation hypotension are consensus practice with no trial evidence favouring one vasodilator over another. The exception is the glucocorticoid entry on the avoidance list, which rests on a case series with literature review: weak evidence by design, but the harm described is severe and the drug is easily omitted. The duration of postoperative glucose and haemodynamic monitoring is conservative practice and is unsourced. The splenectomy infection guideline is cited for its existence and scope; current Australian vaccine schedules should be taken from the national immunisation handbook. Platelet thresholds for splenectomy, steroid replacement doses and the analgesic plan are standard practice and author's judgement and are unsourced.
