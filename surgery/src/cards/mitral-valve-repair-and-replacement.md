## What the surgeon is doing and why

The mitral valve is not two flaps in a ring. It is an apparatus: a saddle-shaped annulus, a large anterior leaflet that shares its hinge with the aortic valve, a smaller posterior leaflet in three scallops, and chordae that tie both to the papillary muscles and so to the ventricular wall. The leaflets close because the ventricle pulls on them correctly, and the ventricle keeps its shape partly because the chordae pull back. That two-way dependence explains the surgeon's preferences.

Degenerative regurgitation (a prolapsing or flail posterior scallop on ruptured or stretched chordae, at one end, and the billowing excess tissue of Barlow disease at the other) is a leaflet problem in a ventricle that is otherwise sound. It is repaired: the surgeon resects or folds the redundant segment or re-suspends it on artificial chordae, then sews an annuloplasty ring or band around the annulus to restore its shape and take tension off the repair. Repair is preferred because it keeps the patient's own apparatus and avoids a prosthesis; in the randomised comparison of incisions it was achieved in about 96% of patients either way.

Functional regurgitation is a ventricular problem: a dilated or infarcted ventricle pulls the papillary muscles apart and tethers normal leaflets open. A ring helps less and recurrence is common, so these patients increasingly get a chordal-sparing replacement or a [transcatheter edge-to-edge clip](transcatheter-aortic-valve-implantation-and-structural.html), which in selected heart failure patients halved hospitalisation against medical therapy. Rheumatic stenosis (fused commissures, thick calcified leaflets, shortened chordae) and destroyed endocarditic valves are replaced, mechanical or tissue, keeping the posterior leaflet and its chordae where possible so the ventricle keeps its scaffold.

Access is through the left atrium, which lies at the back of the heart. From a sternotomy the surgeon opens it behind the interatrial groove or goes through the right atrium and septum. From a right mini-thoracotomy the atrium is directly under the incision, which is the appeal of that approach; the cost is peripheral cannulation, a long pump run and a lung to manage. Recovery of physical function at 12 weeks was no better with the small incision in the one large trial.

Pain follows the incision. Sternotomy is a 4 by the mechanism described for [coronary artery bypass grafting](coronary-artery-bypass-grafting-on-and.html). The mini-thoracotomy is a chest-wall mechanism, with a spread interspace and a compressed intercostal nerve, plus a groin wound; it is in the same band, more unilateral and more often neuropathic.

## Steps

### 1. Induction against the lesion
**Why:** The lesion sets the terms before the surgeon does.
**Anaesthesia:** Regurgitation: the ventricle has been offloading into a low-pressure atrium, so it likes a faster rate, a full circulation and a low resistance. Bradycardia and vasopressor boluses both increase the regurgitant fraction; treat hypotension with a little ephedrine or a low-dose inotrope and volume before reaching for pure vasoconstriction. Stenosis: the opposite. Flow through a narrow valve needs time, so tachycardia (laryngoscopy, pain, new fast atrial fibrillation) raises left atrial pressure into pulmonary oedema within minutes; keep the rate at 60-80 per minute with opioid and a short-acting beta-blocker, hold afterload with phenylephrine because the output is fixed, and give volume cautiously. In both, long-standing disease means pulmonary hypertension and a vulnerable right ventricle: avoid hypoxia, hypercarbia, acidosis and light anaesthesia, and treat sedative premedication as a respiratory depressant in this group.

### 2. The pre-bypass echo
**Why:** The surgeon needs to know what is wrong with which segment, whether it can be repaired, and what could go wrong with the repair.
**Anaesthesia:** Describe the mechanism segment by segment (which scallop prolapses or is flail, where the jet goes), measure the annulus and the leaflet lengths, and assess the ventricle and right heart. Then the risk markers for systolic anterior motion after repair: a long posterior leaflet, a short distance from the coaptation point to the septum, a bulging basal septum, a small vigorous ventricle. Say them aloud, because they change the surgeon's choice of ring size and leaflet height. Also record mitral annular calcification, the aortic valve (it shares a hinge with the anterior leaflet and can be distorted by sutures), the atrial appendage for thrombus, tricuspid regurgitation and annular size (the surgeon may add a tricuspid ring), and a patent foramen ovale. Remember that general anaesthesia unloads the valve and downgrades regurgitation; if the pre-operative grade and yours disagree, raise afterload with phenylephrine and look again.

### 3. Incision and, for the mini-thoracotomy, the lung
**Why:** A 5-7 cm incision in the right fourth or fifth interspace with a soft-tissue retractor, a camera port and a port for the atrial retractor, or a full sternotomy.
**Anaesthesia:** For the small incision: external defibrillator pads positioned across the heart and clear of the right chest, because paddles will not fit; right lung down by double-lumen tube or bronchial blocker for the approach and after bypass for haemostasis; and a percutaneous superior caval cannula through the right internal jugular vein if the surgeon uses one, placed by you under ultrasound and echo before draping, heparinised and flushed. The patient with pulmonary hypertension tolerates one-lung ventilation poorly; if oxygenation or right ventricular function suffers, the surgeon can go on bypass earlier (see [one-lung ventilation and thoracic essentials](../a/a03-one-lung-ventilation.html)).

### 4. Bicaval cannulation and bypass
**Why:** Opening the atria needs the venous return taken from each cava separately, with snares around them, so no air enters the venous line and no blood floods the field. In the minimally invasive case the arterial cannula is femoral and flow in the aorta is retrograde.
**Anaesthesia:** Guide the femoral venous wire and cannula into the right atrium and superior cava on the bicaval echo view, and confirm the arterial wire is in the descending aorta before the surgeon dilates. Retrograde femoral perfusion through an atheromatous aorta is an embolic risk the pre-operative imaging should have excluded. When the caval snares tighten, watch the central venous pressure and the face: a superior caval cannula that is kinked or too deep obstructs cerebral venous drainage.

### 5. Cross-clamp, cardioplegia and atriotomy
**Why:** The aorta is clamped directly, through a separate stab incision with a long clamp, or from inside with a balloon; the heart is arrested and the left atrium opened and held up with a retractor.
**Anaesthesia:** An endoaortic balloon must sit in the ascending aorta between the coronaries and the innominate artery: you position it on echo and monitor both radial pressures or cerebral oximetry, since migration distally occludes the innominate and migration proximally unclamps the heart. The atrial retractor lifts the heart and distorts the aortic root, so antegrade cardioplegia repeats may leak through a pulled-open aortic valve and retrograde delivery earns its place. Otherwise this is a long, quiet pump run: depth, glucose, potassium, haematocrit.

### 6. Valve analysis and leaflet repair, or replacement
**Why:** The surgeon tests each segment with nerve hooks against a reference point, then corrects what is found: resect or plicate the excess, re-suspend on artificial chordae measured to length, close clefts. If the valve cannot be repaired it is excised with the posterior leaflet and its chordae left in place, and a prosthesis is sewn to the annulus.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing for you to do during the repair, but two anatomical neighbours of the annulus become your problems later. The circumflex coronary artery runs in the groove just outside the posterior annulus and can be caught or kinked by a deep stitch. The conduction tissue and the aortic valve sit beside the anterior annulus. In a replacement through a heavily calcified annulus, the debridement weakens the junction between atrium and ventricle.

### 7. Annuloplasty and the saline test
**Why:** A ring or band sized to the anterior leaflet restores the annular shape and reduces its front-to-back dimension so the leaflets meet with a good depth of overlap. The surgeon then fills the flaccid ventricle with saline through the valve to see whether the leaflets close symmetrically and hold.
**Anaesthesia:** The saline test is done on an arrested heart with no afterload and tells the surgeon about symmetry, not about function. The real test is yours, after bypass, on a beating heart at a proper pressure. A ring chosen small to guarantee competence pushes the coaptation point towards the septum, which is the set-up for outflow obstruction.

### 8. Atrial closure and de-airing
**Why:** The left heart has been wide open, with the atrium uppermost and the pulmonary veins draining into the field. Air collects in the pulmonary veins, the appendage and the ventricular apex.
**Anaesthesia:** The field is flooded with carbon dioxide. As the atrium is closed, inflate the lungs (both, including the collapsed right lung, with a gentle sustained breath) to flush the pulmonary veins, tip the table head down, and call the location of air on echo while the root vent runs. In the mini-thoracotomy the surgeon cannot reach the apex to shake or needle it, so de-airing takes longer and depends more on the vent, the table and time. Persistent air shows itself as right coronary ischaemia: inferior ST elevation and a sluggish right ventricle that recover with perfusion pressure.

### 9. Coming off bypass
**Why:** A newly competent valve changes what the ventricle has to do.
**Anaesthesia:** A regurgitant ventricle had a low-pressure escape route. Now every millilitre must go out through the aorta, so afterload has in effect risen and the true contractile state is revealed: an ejection fraction that looked normal before surgery commonly looks mildly or moderately impaired afterwards. Have an inodilator or low-dose adrenaline ready and keep the systemic resistance modest. After stenosis the ventricle is small and under-used and the right ventricle and pulmonary vasculature are the limit: milrinone, an inhaled pulmonary vasodilator, good oxygenation and a coronary perfusion pressure that keeps the right ventricle supplied. Pace at 80-90 per minute; atrial wires are worth having even in chronic atrial fibrillation if a maze procedure has been done. A right lung that was collapsed for a long minimally invasive pump run can develop unilateral re-expansion oedema: re-expand gently, use PEEP, and expect it if the pump run was long.

### 10. The post-bypass echo
**Why:** The surgeon will go back on bypass for a correctable fault, and this is when it is found.
**Anaesthesia:** First set the conditions: filled, systolic pressure near the patient's normal, paced or in a stable rhythm. Then report in order. Residual regurgitation: none or trivial is the target; more than mild is a failed repair. Systolic anterior motion: anterior leaflet drawn into the outflow tract, turbulence there, a posteriorly directed jet of regurgitation. Treat it before condemning the repair: stop inotropes, give volume, raise afterload with phenylephrine, slow the rate with a beta-blocker; in the reported series it occurred in a few per cent of repairs and most settled this way, and the remainder need revision or replacement. Gradient across the valve at a stated heart rate, since a small ring can produce stenosis. The lateral and inferolateral walls for a new regional abnormality from a circumflex injury. The aortic valve for new regurgitation. A prosthesis is checked for seating, leaflet motion, paravalvular leak and an unobstructed outflow tract.

### 11. Protamine, haemostasis and closure
**Why:** Suture lines here are atrial and low pressure, with one exception: the junction between atrium and ventricle after a replacement.
**Anaesthesia:** Avoid hypertension and ventricular overdistension after a mitral replacement, especially in a small elderly woman with a calcified annulus: rupture of the atrioventricular groove or posterior ventricular wall presents as sudden bright bleeding from behind the heart minutes to hours after bypass and is often fatal. Lifting the heart to inspect the back of it is itself a cause, so the surgeon will be reluctant, and your blood pressure control is the prevention. Protamine at no more than 1:1 to the heparin dose, viscoelastic-guided products, and after a mini-thoracotomy a check of the chest wall and port sites with the lung down, because intercostal bleeding into the right chest is the common reason for return to theatre.

## Pain plan

For sternotomy the strategy is the standard one and the block is the plan as a whole: paracetamol and, where kidney function and bleeding allow, an NSAID from theatre onward; parasternal block or infiltration at closure; opioid as rescue, intravenous for 24-48 hours then oral, stepping down by day 3-4. This is what PROSPECT recommends for sternotomy, with magnesium or dexmedetomidine as intra-operative adjuncts.

The mini-thoracotomy is a different wound and has no procedure-specific guideline. The pain is lateral, pleuritic, and driven by rib spreading and the intercostal nerve; the groin adds a second site. The surgeon can infiltrate two or three intercostal spaces under direct vision from inside the chest, which is quick and reliable, and some add cryoanalgesia. A right-sided erector spinae or serratus anterior plane block is the usual regional addition, but the honest position is that the pooled randomised trials in minimally invasive cardiac surgery (four trials, 252 patients) did not show a clear benefit for pain, opioid use or recovery. The author's judgement is that surgeon intercostal infiltration plus systemic multimodal analgesia is the default, with a fascial plane catheter reserved for opioid-tolerant patients or established local pathways. Neuraxial and paravertebral techniques are avoided because of full heparinisation.

Escalate when pain limits coughing, and look for a neuropathic strip below the right breast at follow-up; it responds to the usual agents and is the reason some surgeons freeze the nerve.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Acute severe regurgitation from papillary muscle rupture after infarction or from endocarditis: a small atrium with no compliance, pulmonary oedema, shock. Often arrives ventilated with a balloon pump, which is helpful here because it lowers afterload. Emergency surgery; repair is unlikely, replacement is quicker.
- Ischaemic regurgitation with grafts: a long operation in a poor ventricle; plan mechanical support with the surgeon before the start.
- Severe pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular dysfunction: the patient who needs a pulmonary artery catheter, inhaled vasodilator set up before separation, and an intensive care bed that can run it.
- Concomitant maze procedure and appendage closure: longer clamp time, junctional rhythm and heart block afterwards, pacing wires in both chambers.
- Mitral annular calcification: debridement risks groove rupture and circumflex injury; the surgeon may accept a smaller valve or an unconventional technique. Blood in the room.
- Redo mitral surgery is done either through the right chest to avoid the sternum or by repeat sternotomy, where a series with routine computed tomography planning reported re-entry injury in 1.5% against historical rates of 5-10% (see [resternotomy, tamponade and mechanical circulatory support](resternotomy-tamponade-and-mechanical-circulatory-support.html)).
- Fast-track extubation suits the isolated repair with good ventricular function. Pulmonary hypertension, a second pump run and right lung oedema are the usual reasons it fails.

## Key sources

The incision question has a good randomised trial. Systolic anterior motion, afterload mismatch and the lesion-specific haemodynamic goals rest on physiology, echo guidelines and case series. The analgesic evidence for the mini-thoracotomy is thin and, where pooled, negative for fascial plane blocks; the recommendation above is judgement.
