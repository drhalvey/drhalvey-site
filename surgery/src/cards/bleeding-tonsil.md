## What the surgeon is doing and why

A tonsillectomy leaves two open wounds on the pharyngeal wall, and they can bleed at two times for two reasons. Primary haemorrhage, within the first 24 hours and usually the first six, is a vessel that was in spasm, under-coagulated or held shut by a low blood pressure at the end of the operation and has opened since. Secondary haemorrhage comes after discharge, typically between days 5 and 10, when the fibrin slough that covers the bed separates and exposes a vessel underneath, with infection and dehydration thought to contribute. In the national audit of nearly 12,000 tonsillectomies haemorrhage occurred in 3.3%: primary in 0.5% and secondary in 2.9%. Most secondary bleeds stop on their own; in one large paediatric centre 2.9% of all tonsillectomy patients came back to theatre. Deaths after tonsillectomy are rare and almost all are from haemorrhage.

The bleeding is into the pharynx, directly above the larynx and the oesophagus. An awake patient protects the airway by swallowing, so the blood disappears into the stomach, where it cannot be measured and acts as an emetic. This shapes the whole presentation: a child who looks quiet, pale and nauseated, swallowing every few seconds, with a clot in the fossa and a deceptively small amount of blood in the bowl.

At re-operation the surgeon opens the mouth with the same gag, suctions out the clot (which often restarts the bleeding and reveals the point), and controls it with bipolar diathermy, a tie or a suture under the vessel. If the bed oozes diffusely the pillars are sutured together over a pack or haemostatic material. Bleeding after adenoidectomy comes from the nasopharynx and is controlled by re-curettage of a tag, suction diathermy or a postnasal pack left in place. Very rarely, for bleeding from a major branch, the external carotid is ligated in the neck or the vessel is embolised.

The pain logic is inherited from the first operation: see [adult and paediatric tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy](adult-and-paediatric-tonsillectomy-and-adenoidectomy.html). The patient returns in the most painful phase of healing and leaves with a freshly cauterised bed. Score 4, mucosal, with the course extended, not intensified.

## Steps

### 1. Recognise how much has been lost
**Why:** The surgeon sees a fossa with a clot and wants to know whether there is time to wait and watch or whether theatre is needed now.
**Anaesthesia:** Assess the circulation yourself. In a child the sequence is tachycardia, pallor, cool peripheries and a prolonged capillary refill, then restlessness or drowsiness, and only then hypotension, by which point a large fraction of the blood volume has gone. The blood pressure of a child who is still compensating is normal or high. In adults postural symptoms and tachycardia carry the same message. Ask how many times the patient has bled: repeated small bleeds before a large one are a recognised warning pattern. Read the first anaesthetic record for laryngoscopy grade, tube size and any difficulty, and find out whether there is a personal or family bleeding history that was missed.

### 2. Resuscitate before induction
**Why:** The surgeon cannot stop the bleeding without an anaesthetic, and the anaesthetic is the most dangerous event of the admission for a hypovolaemic patient.
**Anaesthesia:** Two cannulae, placed with topical anaesthetic if time allows and without ceremony if not; intraosseous access in a shocked child with no veins. Send a full blood count, coagulation screen and a group and crossmatch. Give balanced crystalloid in boluses of 10-20 mL/kg and reassess heart rate and perfusion after each; move to blood early if the response is transient or the haemoglobin is low. The haemoglobin taken on arrival under-reads the loss until fluid has been given. Aim to induce a patient whose heart rate has come down, whose peripheries are warm and for whom blood is in the room. The exception is torrential bleeding, where the patient goes straight to theatre and resuscitation and induction proceed together.

### 3. Temporising measures
**Why:** Slowing the bleeding buys time for resuscitation and sometimes makes surgery unnecessary.
**Anaesthesia:** Sit the patient forward or lie them on the side so blood runs out of the mouth. Give TXA 15 mg/kg IV (maximum 1 g). Nebulised TXA has been taken up in emergency departments: two before-and-after studies of protocols that included it report falls in the proportion of children needing theatre, from a half to about a quarter in one and from 44% to 15% in the other. These are uncontrolled comparisons and do not replace surgery for an actively bleeding patient. In a cooperative adult the surgeon may remove clot and apply pressure with an adrenaline-soaked swab on a clamp, or cauterise under local anaesthesia. Nil by mouth is academic.

### 4. Prepare theatre
**Why:** Everything that might be reached for during a bloody intubation has to be within reach before it starts.
**Anaesthesia:** Two wide-bore suckers, switched on, one in your hand and one with your assistant. Tracheal tubes with stylets: the size used last time and one half-size smaller, because the airway is oedematous from recent surgery and intubation. A direct laryngoscope in addition to a video laryngoscope, since blood on the camera blinds it. A bougie. The surgeon in the room, scrubbed or ready, with the gag and a headlight, able to pack the fossa or to perform a front-of-neck airway. Blood checked and at hand. Draw up drugs for a reduced-dose induction and atropine for the child.

### 5. Induction
**Why:** The airway must be secured quickly in a patient with a full stomach and blood in the pharynx.
**Anaesthesia:** Preoxygenate with the patient in the position they find easiest, with the mask held loosely enough for them to spit. Then an intravenous rapid sequence induction: this was the technique in 84% of 475 children in the largest series. Choose the agent and dose for the circulation, not the weight alone: ketamine 1-2 mg/kg IV, or propofol at a reduced and slowly given dose in the resuscitated patient, with suxamethonium 1.5-2 mg/kg IV or rocuronium 1-1.2 mg/kg IV. Gentle mask ventilation with low pressures is acceptable in a child who desaturates before the relaxant works; hypoxia is a greater risk than inflation of the stomach. The older technique of inhalational induction in the left lateral head-down position kept spontaneous breathing but meant intubating on the side in a pool of blood and has largely been abandoned.

### 6. Laryngoscopy and intubation
**Why:** Until the cuff is up, blood from the fossa runs towards the cords.
**Anaesthesia:** Suction first, then look. Expect clot at the tongue base and a swollen uvula and pillars. Intubation was difficult in 2.7% of the series, and none of those children had been difficult at the first operation, so a previous easy grade is not a guarantee. Use the stylet or bougie, pass the smaller tube if there is any resistance, inflate the cuff and confirm with the capnograph. If intubation fails, oxygenate by mask or laryngeal mask with suction, limit attempts and follow the unanticipated difficult intubation plan; the surgeon can meanwhile pack the fossa.

### 7. Surgical haemostasis
**Why:** With the gag in and the clot out, the surgeon finds and controls the point.
**Anaesthesia:** The gag can kink or displace the tube exactly as at the first operation. Removing the clot may release a brisk bleed: this is when a patient who seemed adequately filled shows that they were not. Keep the blood pressure at the patient's normal so the surgeon is not reassured by a field that is dry only because the pressure is low. Check a blood gas haemoglobin and transfuse to the result and the clinical state. If diffuse ooze persists, think about coagulation: von Willebrand disease is the usual unrecognised cause, and desmopressin, further TXA and haematology advice are the next steps.

### 8. Empty the stomach
**Why:** The stomach holds a large volume of blood and clot that will be vomited on waking, raising venous pressure in the bed and threatening the airway.
**Anaesthesia:** Pass a wide-bore orogastric tube under direct vision, keeping it in the midline away from the fossae, aspirate and remove it. Clot will not all come up a tube; the airway still needs protecting at extubation. Give ondansetron and dexamethasone.

### 9. Extubation
**Why:** The surgeon wants no coughing; you want protective reflexes.
**Anaesthesia:** Here the reflexes win. Reverse the relaxant fully, suction the pharynx under vision, turn the patient left lateral and head down, and extubate fully awake. Hypoxaemia was the commonest adverse event in the series, in about 1 in 10 children, and most of it happened at emergence and extubation, not at induction.

### 10. Recovery and the next day
**Why:** A bed that has bled twice can bleed a third time.
**Anaesthesia:** Recover on the side with oxygen and suction at the bedside, and keep the cannulae in. Repeat the haemoglobin after equilibration. Admit overnight at least. Record the laryngoscopy and tube size for whoever anaesthetises the patient next. If no surgical bleeding point was found, or bleeding was disproportionate, arrange a coagulation work-up before discharge.

## Pain plan

No block applies, and the mechanism is the mucosal wound of tonsillectomy caught at its worst point: a patient at day 5-8 was already reporting pain of 5 or 6 out of 10 before the bleed. In theatre give paracetamol 15 mg/kg IV and titrate opioid to a patient who is hypovolaemic until proven otherwise and, in a young child, possibly opioid-sensitive from sleep apnoea: fentanyl 0.5-1 microgram/kg IV in increments, or morphine in small doses once the circulation is restored. Dexamethasone adds analgesia and treats the nausea of swallowed blood. Ketamine in a sub-anaesthetic dose is a useful adjunct when opioid is unwelcome.

The question each time is the NSAID. The evidence that NSAIDs increase post-tonsillectomy bleeding is inconclusive, as set out on the tonsillectomy card, but a patient who has just bled is not the population those trials studied. The defensible default is to withhold the NSAID for the first 24-48 hours after re-operation, rely on regular paracetamol with a rescue opioid suited to the patient's age and sleep apnoea risk, and restart ibuprofen when the surgeon is content; surgeons vary, and some never restart it. This is author's judgement. Expect the course of pain to be extended by several days, not made more severe, and plan the discharge analgesia for a further week to ten days. Good analgesia matters to the bleeding risk itself: a patient who cannot swallow stops drinking, the bed dries and crusts, and dehydration is one of the proposed contributors to secondary haemorrhage.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Under-resuscitation. The recurring error. Bradycardia at induction occurred in about 4% and hypotension in 2.5% of a series from a specialist centre; in a hypovolaemic child bradycardia is a pre-arrest sign.
- Torrential haemorrhage. The emergency within the emergency: immediate transfer, activation of the massive transfusion protocol, induction with ketamine and whatever access exists, the surgeon packing the fossa with a swab on a clamp as soon as the mouth can be opened. Arterial line, critical care and possibly external carotid ligation or embolisation follow. See [major haemorrhage, cell salvage and coagulation](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html).
- The adult under local anaesthesia. A cooperative adult with a visible point may be cauterised awake. The same rules about access, blood tests and observation apply, and the threshold for theatre is low.
- Post-adenoidectomy bleeding. Blood runs from the nose and down the back of the throat; a postnasal pack may be left in for 24 hours, sometimes with the patient kept intubated or closely observed because the pack obstructs the nasal airway.
- Unrecognised coagulopathy. Suspect it with primary bleeding from both fossae, generalised ooze or a family history. Do not wait for results to give TXA.
- The second anaesthetic within hours. For a primary bleed, residual anaesthetic and opioid from the first operation are still present: reduce doses again, and expect a slower wake-up.
- Difficult airway. Oedema, clot and a small mouth opening from pain make the view worse than last time. Videolaryngoscopy helps until the lens is covered. The fallback sequence is in [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html); paediatric doses and transfusion volumes are in [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).
- Aspiration of blood. Usually well tolerated in small amounts; a persisting oxygen requirement after extubation needs a chest radiograph and observation.
- Observation without surgery. Many secondary bleeds have stopped by arrival. These patients are still admitted, cannulated and crossmatched, because a small bleed can precede a large one and inpatient observation does not by itself prevent a serious bleed.

## Key sources

The anaesthetic evidence is one retrospective series of 475 children from a single specialist hospital; it describes what was done and what went wrong and compares nothing. Incidence figures come from a national audit, and the warning value of repeated bleeds from a series of serious and fatal cases. The nebulised TXA data are two recent before-and-after quality improvement reports. The priority of resuscitation over speed, the induction drug choices and doses, tube size reduction, gastric emptying, awake lateral extubation and the withholding of NSAIDs after a bleed are consensus and author's judgement, not trial findings.
