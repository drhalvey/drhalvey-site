## What the surgeon is doing and why

Two different cancers, two different operations, one card because the same team does both and the anaesthetic problems overlap.

Ovarian, tubal and primary peritoneal cancer spreads by shedding cells into the peritoneal fluid. They implant wherever that fluid circulates: the pelvis, the omentum (which becomes a thick cake), the paracolic gutters, the surface of the bowel and its mesentery, and the underside of the diaphragm, particularly on the right. The tumour blocks peritoneal lymphatics and leaks protein, so the patient presents late with litres of ascites, a pleural effusion, weight loss hidden by abdominal swelling, and often a deep vein thrombosis. Survival tracks how much tumour is left after surgery, and the aim of cytoreduction is no visible residual disease. The surgeon therefore removes everything involved: uterus, tubes and ovaries, omentum, and then, as required, the rectosigmoid en bloc with the pelvic peritoneum, segments of small bowel or colon, the spleen, the peritoneum of the diaphragm (occasionally a full-thickness piece of it), the appendix and enlarged nodes. That needs a midline incision from pubis to xiphisternum and a fixed retractor. Surgery is either upfront (primary debulking) or after three cycles of carboplatin and paclitaxel (interval debulking), the latter in a patient with less tumour and less ascites but recent chemotherapy. Some centres finish an interval debulking by perfusing the open or closed abdomen with heated cisplatin for 90 minutes (HIPEC), which in the randomised trial lengthened median overall survival from 33.9 to 45.7 months.

Cervical cancer spreads the other way: locally into the tissue beside the cervix (the parametrium) and upper vagina, and through lymphatics to the pelvic nodes. A radical hysterectomy removes the uterus with the parametrium and a cuff of vagina, which means dissecting each ureter out of its tunnel under the uterine artery and dividing the uterosacral and cardinal ligaments close to the pelvic wall, then clearing or sampling the pelvic nodes off the iliac vessels and obturator nerve. The patient is typically younger and fitter, there is no ascites, and the incision is a lower midline or a muscle-cutting transverse one. It is done open: the randomised trial of minimally invasive against abdominal radical hysterectomy found worse disease-free survival (86.0% against 96.5% at 4.5 years) and worse overall survival with the laparoscopic and robotic approach, and practice changed.

The pain logic is the incision. A full-length midline wound stretched by a retractor for six hours is loaded by every breath and cough, and the upper half of it is what restricts ventilation. Under it lie areas of stripped peritoneum, which are raw visceral and parietal surfaces, bowel anastomoses and node beds. With a working neuraxial technique the patient is comfortable at rest on day 0, has moderate to severe movement pain on days 1 to 3, and improves over a week. A debulking confined to the pelvis and omentum, and a radical hysterectomy, sit at 4. One that includes diaphragm stripping, splenectomy or liver mobilisation is open upper abdominal surgery and behaves like a 5. The analgesic plan has to be chosen with one eye on what else it does: an epidural's sympathetic block in a patient with large fluid shifts, and any catheter in a patient who may become coagulopathic.

## Steps

### 1. Preoperative assessment and optimisation
**Why:** The surgeon decides operability on imaging and sometimes a staging laparoscopy, and times surgery against chemotherapy cycles.
**Anaesthesia:** Look for the six things the disease does. Ascites and effusions reduce lung volumes and are worth draining a day or two ahead if symptomatic, with the hypotension that follows large-volume drainage in mind. Albumin is low and so are drug-binding and oncotic pressure. Nutrition is poor and prehabilitation is part of the enhanced recovery guideline. Venous thromboembolism is common before surgery: ask about leg swelling and breathlessness, and establish how any anticoagulant will be bridged around a neuraxial technique. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy leaves anaemia, thrombocytopenia, peripheral neuropathy (document it before a block) and difficult veins. Bowel obstruction or a large mass means a full stomach. For cervical cancer the assessment is usually simpler; check renal function for ureteric obstruction.

### 2. Neuraxial block, induction and lines
**Why:** The surgeon needs four to eight hours of a relaxed, still abdomen and wants the patient mobile the next day.
**Anaesthesia:** Place the epidural or the spinal awake, before induction (choice in the pain plan). Induce head up with full pre-oxygenation; tense ascites shortens the time to desaturation and pushes gastric contents up, so use a rapid sequence technique when in doubt. Arterial line before or straight after induction, a second large cannula, and a central line when the plan includes HIPEC, vasopressor infusion or there is no reliable peripheral access. Urinary catheter, temperature probe, forced-air and fluid warming, calf compression. Antibiotics and TXA before incision. Give dexamethasone 8 mg IV unless the patient is a brittle diabetic.

### 3. Positioning
**Why:** Lloyd-Davies gives a second assistant room between the legs and access to the anus for a circular stapler if the rectosigmoid is resected.
**Anaesthesia:** This is a six-hour position: heels and calves free in the boots, hips not hyperflexed, arms out at less than 90 degrees with the hands visible. A pre-existing chemotherapy neuropathy makes a postoperative deficit harder to interpret, so record both the neuropathy and the padding. Head-down tilt for the pelvic phase and head-up for the upper abdominal phase will each be asked for.

### 4. Laparotomy and drainage of ascites
**Why:** The midline is opened, ascites is aspirated for cytology and volume, and the abdomen is explored to decide whether complete cytoreduction is achievable.
**Anaesthesia:** Draining 3-8 L of ascites removes the abdominal tamponade on the splanchnic bed and unloads the vena cava at the same time: the pressure may rise briefly with improved venous return and compliance, then fall as the splanchnic vessels fill and fluid re-accumulates over hours. Have noradrenaline running or ready and replace with albumin 20% 100 mL per 2-3 L drained, a convention carried over from paracentesis practice, plus balanced crystalloid to stroke volume. Ventilation becomes suddenly easier; reduce the pressures. If the disease is judged unresectable the operation ends here with biopsies, and the full-length incision may not have been needed: ask the surgeon to explore through the lower half first if resectability is in question.

### 5. Omentectomy and pelvic clearance
**Why:** The omental cake is detached from the transverse colon and stomach. The pelvis is then cleared retroperitoneally: the ureters are found at the pelvic brim, the ovarian vessels and uterine arteries divided, and the uterus, ovaries and pelvic peritoneum removed in one block, with the rectosigmoid if it is encased.
**Anaesthesia:** The first sustained blood loss, from the gastroepiploic arcade, the pelvic side walls and the presacral veins. Loss is steady and hidden in packs; weigh swabs and take a blood gas with haemoglobin, lactate and ionised calcium hourly. Transfuse to symptoms and trend, treat coagulopathy on viscoelastic testing, and keep the patient warm with the abdomen open, which is the hardest of the three.

### 6. Bowel resection and anastomosis
**Why:** Rectosigmoid, ileocaecal or small bowel segments carrying tumour are resected and joined, with a defunctioning stoma when the anastomosis is low or the patient is frail.
**Anaesthesia:** An anastomosis wants perfusion pressure without oedema. Hold the mean arterial pressure with noradrenaline once fluid responsiveness has gone, and stop chasing urine output with crystalloid. The surgeon may ask for the rectal stapler or an air leak test, which is done from below by the assistant; no nitrous oxide. Re-dose antibiotics at four hours.

### 7. Upper abdomen: diaphragm, spleen and liver
**Why:** The right hemidiaphragm is stripped of peritoneum after mobilising the liver; tumour through the muscle is excised full thickness and the defect sutured; the spleen is removed if its hilum is involved.
**Anaesthesia:** Mobilising and retracting the liver kinks the inferior vena cava and drops the cardiac output abruptly; tell the surgeon when it happens, because releasing the retractor fixes it. Entering the pleura gives a pneumothorax that shows as a rising airway pressure and falling saturation: the surgeon closes the diaphragm over a suction catheter during a held inflation, or places a chest drain. Expect a right pleural effusion afterwards either way, and diaphragm work is the main reason for postoperative respiratory support. Splenectomy adds a brisk bleeding risk at the hilum and a vaccination schedule later. This phase is done head up.

### 8. Lymphadenectomy
**Why:** Bulky pelvic and para-aortic nodes are removed in ovarian cancer; in cervical and endometrial cancer the pelvic nodes are cleared or sentinel nodes are taken after injection of indocyanine green or blue dye into the cervix.
**Anaesthesia:** Nodes lie on the iliac veins, the vena cava and the aorta; a torn vein here is sudden, large-volume bleeding in a deep hole, controlled by pressure while you catch up. Patent blue dye causes anaphylaxis and a spurious fall in the pulse oximeter reading; indocyanine green can produce a brief false low reading. The obturator nerve is at risk in the pelvic dissection (thigh adductor weakness afterwards).

### 9. Radical hysterectomy: ureteric tunnel and parametrium
**Why:** For cervical cancer the ureters are unroofed from the parametrium, the uterine arteries divided at their origin, the parametrium and uterosacral ligaments taken laterally and the vagina transected below the cervix. Nerve-sparing techniques try to preserve the hypogastric and pelvic splanchnic fibres to the bladder.
**Anaesthesia:** Bleeding comes from the venous plexus around the ureter and the vaginal angles and is typically 300-1,000 mL. The autonomic injury is the surgeon's long-term concern: the bladder is slow to recover, a catheter stays for several days, and retention is expected, so intrathecal morphine's effect on voiding is irrelevant here. The patient is usually young and well and can be extubated and sent to the ward.

### 10. HIPEC
**Why:** After cytoreduction, inflow and outflow cannulae are placed and cisplatin in several litres of carrier fluid is circulated at 40-42 degrees for 90 minutes, with the abdomen open under a cover or closed, to treat microscopic residual disease.
**Anaesthesia:** Cool the patient to about 36 degrees beforehand and turn the warmers off: core temperature climbs 1-2 degrees during perfusion, with tachycardia, vasodilation, a rise in oxygen consumption and end-tidal carbon dioxide, and a higher minute ventilation requirement. A closed perfusion raises intra-abdominal pressure like a pneumoperitoneum. Cisplatin is nephrotoxic: maintain a brisk diuresis through and after the perfusion, give sodium thiosulfate at the times the protocol specifies, avoid NSAIDs and other nephrotoxins, and replace magnesium and potassium. Fluid and protein loss into the abdomen is large. Coagulopathy and thrombocytopenia worsen over the next days, which is the argument against leaving an epidural catheter in these patients. Staff exposure precautions for cytotoxics apply to the suction, the drapes and the body fluids for 48 hours.

### 11. Closure and emergence
**Why:** Mass closure of a long midline, drains by indication, rectus sheath catheters if that is the plan.
**Anaesthesia:** Decide extubation on the usual criteria plus four specific ones: temperature above 36 degrees, lactate falling, noradrenaline at a low dose, and no large diaphragm resection. Most are extubated in theatre. Test the epidural before waking, or have the surgeon place rectus sheath catheters under vision before the sheath is closed (bolus ropivacaine 0.2% 20 mL each side, within the maximum dose). Restart nothing nephrotoxic. Hand over the fluid balance, the transfusion total and the anticoagulation timing explicitly.

### 12. The first three days
**Why:** The surgeon wants sitting out on day 0 or 1, early diet, drains and catheter out as soon as they can be, and chemotherapy to restart in three to four weeks.
**Anaesthesia:** Hypotension on the ward is the daily question, and the differential is the epidural, bleeding, under-filling, sepsis from an anastomotic leak (from day 3), and pulmonary embolism at any time. An epidural should not be blamed until the others are considered, and a low-dose noradrenaline infusion in a monitored bed is better than litres of crystalloid. Ascites re-accumulates and leaks through the wound and drains, taking protein with it. Ileus is common after bowel resection and HIPEC. Low molecular weight heparin continues for 28 days and its timing governs catheter removal.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a long abdominal wall incision with widespread peritoneal stripping beneath it, severe on movement for three to four days. The enhanced recovery guideline for this specialty asks for multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia and leaves the regional technique to be matched to the operation; the choice is between an epidural, intrathecal opioid, and catheters in the abdominal wall.

For a midline incision that stays below or just above the umbilicus (staging laparotomy, pelvic debulking, radical hysterectomy), the defensible default is intrathecal morphine 200 micrograms before induction, paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor when the kidneys and the surgeon allow, dexamethasone, and a PCA. The evidence is direct: in a sham-controlled trial of 68 women having laparotomy for gynaecological malignancy, 200 micrograms halved 24-hour intravenous opioid (18 against 36 mg) and improved satisfaction to 48 hours without more complications; in a second trial of 105 women already receiving long-acting wound infiltration, adding an intrathecal opioid cut the proportion needing any intravenous opioid from 71% to 26%. The intrathecal dose lasts a day. To cover days 2 and 3, the author's practice is to add surgical rectus sheath catheters at closure, which is judgement extrapolated from other midline laparotomy practice.

For a full-length incision with diaphragm, liver or splenic surgery, a thoracic epidural at T8-T10 is the technique that covers the upper wound and protects ventilation, and it has the strongest claim in the patient with poor respiratory reserve or opioid tolerance. Its costs are specific to this operation: vasodilation on top of large fluid shifts, which pushes the team towards either vasopressor infusion in a monitored bed or excess fluid; and a catheter that has to come out through a window in 28 days of anticoagulation, sometimes with a falling platelet count. Where HIPEC is planned, or coagulopathy is likely, the author's choice is intrathecal morphine with rectus sheath catheters instead.

The claim that an epidural improves cancer survival should not drive the decision. A meta-analysis of eight retrospective cohorts (2,036 patients) found longer overall survival with epidural plus general anaesthesia (HR 0.75) but no consistent effect on recurrence, and the best-known single cohort found the association only with more than 48 hours of epidural use. These are observational data open to selection bias: fitter patients with more resectable disease get epidurals.

Adjuncts: ketamine 0.25-0.5 mg/kg IV then a low-dose infusion for the opioid-tolerant; intravenous lidocaine infusion when no neuraxial or wall technique is possible; gabapentinoids only where already established. NSAIDs are withheld after cisplatin HIPEC, with a fresh anastomosis at the surgeon's request, and when urine output has been marginal, which removes the anti-inflammatory arm in exactly the biggest operations.

Rebound is the step-up when the intrathecal morphine fades at 18-24 hours or the epidural is stopped on day 3; make sure oral or PCA opioid and wall catheters overlap it. Escalation triggers: pain that breaks through a previously working epidural with a rising heart rate is a leak or a bleed until excluded; new shoulder-tip pain after diaphragm surgery is the effusion or a subphrenic collection; calf or pleuritic pain is thromboembolism.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Unresectable at laparotomy. An "open and close" in a patient with an epidural sited for an eight-hour operation. Keep the plan flexible and the incision staged.
- Tense ascites and effusions at induction. Rapid desaturation, aspiration risk and cardiovascular collapse with positive pressure if a large effusion is tamponading venous return. Drain symptomatic collections beforehand.
- Bleomycin exposure. Young women with germ cell tumours may have had bleomycin: use the lowest inspired oxygen that maintains saturation and avoid fluid overload.
- Recent bevacizumab. Impaired wound and anastomotic healing and bleeding; surgery is scheduled several weeks clear of the last dose, and the surgeon will know the date.
- The frail patient with advanced disease. The trade-off between complete cytoreduction and surviving it is made before theatre; if the plan is a limited operation, match the monitoring and the analgesia to that and not to the maximal version.
- Massive haemorrhage. Presacral veins, iliac veins and the splenic hilum. Activate the protocol early, pack and wait, and correct calcium and temperature with the blood.
- Emergency presentations. Bowel obstruction or perforation from tumour, and torsion or rupture of a malignant mass: a septic or obstructed patient having a laparotomy without optimisation, covered by the principles on the [emergency laparotomy](emergency-laparotomy.html) card.
- Bowel work shared with colorectal surgeons. The anastomotic and stoma considerations are on the [colectomy](laparoscopic-and-open-colectomy.html) and [anterior resection](anterior-resection-and-abdominoperineal-resection.html) cards.
- Pelvic exenteration. Removal of bladder, rectum or both with the pelvic organs for recurrent cervical cancer, usually in an irradiated pelvis: eight to twelve hours, several litres of blood loss, urinary and faecal diversion, and intensive care.
- Radical vulvectomy and groin node dissection. A perineal and groin wound in an elderly woman, done in lithotomy under spinal or general anaesthesia: skin-and-soft-tissue pain, wound breakdown and lymphoedema, not an abdominal operation.
- Endometrial cancer. Mostly staged laparoscopically or robotically in obese patients and covered on the [laparoscopic hysterectomy](laparoscopic-and-robotic-hysterectomy.html) card.

## Key sources

The perioperative framework is the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery Society guideline, now in its third update, which is consensus graded by GRADE. The intrathecal opioid recommendation rests on two randomised trials in exactly this population and is the best-evidenced statement on the card. The epidural and survival data are retrospective and are cited to explain why they should not decide the technique. HIPEC and the open approach to radical hysterectomy each rest on a single landmark randomised trial that changed surgical practice; neither trial addressed anaesthesia. The conduct of HIPEC, ascites replacement, the response to caval compression and the choice of rectus sheath catheters over an epidural when coagulopathy is expected are standard teaching or the author's judgement and are unsourced.
