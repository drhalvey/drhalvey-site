## What the surgeon is doing and why

Functional neurosurgery changes how a circuit behaves without removing a mass. Two families reach theatre, and they share one feature that sets them apart from every other cranial operation: the surgeon's target is defined partly by electrical recording or by the patient's response, and your drugs act on exactly the cells being recorded.

Deep brain stimulation places a permanent electrode in a deep nucleus, usually both sides. The subthalamic nucleus or the globus pallidus are the targets for Parkinson's disease and dystonia, and the ventral intermediate thalamus for tremor. The target is a few millimetres across and 8 cm from the surface. The surgeon reaches it stereotactically. A frame is pinned to the skull under local anaesthetic and the patient is scanned in it, or a frameless or robotic system is registered to earlier imaging; coordinates are calculated; a burr hole is made on each side in front of the coronal suture; and a cannula is passed along a planned track that avoids sulci, vessels and the ventricle. In the traditional operation the target is then confirmed physiologically. Microelectrodes are advanced in fractions of a millimetre while the characteristic firing of each nucleus is listened to and displayed, and a test stimulus is delivered through the lead while the patient, awake and deliberately off medication, is examined for loss of tremor and rigidity and for side effects such as tingling, pulling of the face or double vision. The leads are fixed at the skull. In a second stage, the same day or a week later and under general anaesthesia, extension wires are tunnelled behind the ear and down the neck to a pulse generator in a pocket below the clavicle.

The alternative is to place the leads under general anaesthesia and confirm position with intraoperative CT or MRI. A meta-analysis of 145 studies found no difference in targeting error or motor outcome between awake and asleep surgery, fewer lead passes, haemorrhages and infections asleep, and a somewhat better side effect profile awake. Units have moved in both directions, and the anaesthetic you give depends entirely on which method the surgeon uses.

Epilepsy surgery is a staged search followed by a removal. In drug-resistant focal epilepsy the team first tries to localise where seizures begin. When scalp recording and imaging do not agree, electrodes are implanted: depth electrodes passed through a dozen or more small drill holes with a robot or frame (stereo-EEG), or less often subdural grids through a craniotomy. The patient is then monitored for one to two weeks with medication reduced until enough seizures have been captured. The second operation removes or disconnects the focus: an anterior temporal lobectomy with removal of the hippocampus and amygdala is the commonest, alongside lesion resections, laser ablation, and in children hemispheric disconnection. During resection the surgeon may record directly from the cortex (electrocorticography) to define the margin, or map function awake as in an [awake craniotomy](awake-craniotomy.html). A vagal nerve stimulator is the palliative device for those without a resectable focus.

The complication that matters most in stimulation and electrode surgery is bleeding along a track. In a systematic review the pooled incidence of haemorrhage was 5%, symptomatic in 2.1% and causing permanent deficit or death in 1.1%, with hypertension and age the main patient factors and microelectrode recording and the number of passes the technical ones. Blood pressure is therefore yours to control, tightly, for the whole time anything is being passed into the brain.

The pain logic: the head part is punctures, pins and small scalp wounds, covered completely by local anaesthetic. The generator stage adds a subcutaneous tunnel and a chest pocket, which is where the soreness is. A resection is a temporal craniotomy and is planned as one.

## Steps

### 1. The patient with Parkinson's disease, off medication
**Why:** The surgeon needs the symptoms present in order to see them disappear with test stimulation, so dopaminergic drugs are stopped the night before.
**Anaesthesia:** Meet a patient who is rigid, tremulous or dystonic, slow to speak, uncomfortable lying still, and at higher risk of aspiration and chest wall rigidity than their on-state suggests. Autonomic dysfunction gives labile pressure and orthostatic hypotension. Know the contraindicated drugs: dopamine antagonists (metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, droperidol, haloperidol) worsen parkinsonism, and pethidine and tramadol risk serotonin toxicity with selegiline or rasagiline. Explain the day in detail and agree a hand signal for distress. Rapport replaces sedation: in a large series of awake functional procedures a third of patients had no sedative at all.

### 2. Frame application and imaging
**Why:** Four pins fix the base ring to the skull so that image coordinates and patient coordinates are the same rigid system.
**Anaesthesia:** Local anaesthetic at four pin sites or a scalp block, with a brief period of sedation if needed (a small propofol bolus or a remifentanil infusion) that is fully worn off before the patient lies in the scanner. From now on the frame's front bar crosses the mouth and nose. Keep the key or spanner that releases it taped to the frame, know how the front piece comes off, and have a supraglottic airway immediately available, since laryngoscopy in a fixed frame is not realistic. Transfer to imaging and back is a monitored transfer.

### 3. Positioning and burr holes
**Why:** The frame is locked to the table with the head elevated, which reduces cerebrospinal fluid loss through the burr hole. Loss of fluid lets air in and shifts the brain, moving the target away from its planned coordinates.
**Anaesthesia:** Let the patient settle into position before the frame is locked, with knees flexed, heels and sacrum padded, and the off-state limb supported; a comfortable patient is a still one. Drapes are arranged so the face, arms and legs are visible. Nasal oxygen with capnography. Scalp infiltration covers the incision; drilling is noise and vibration, and a short remifentanil infusion at 0.03-0.05 micrograms/kg/min or dexmedetomidine helps the anxious. The semi-sitting position with an open burr hole can entrain air: a sudden cough, chest discomfort and a fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide in a spontaneously breathing patient is venous air embolism until shown otherwise, and the surgeon floods the hole and lowers the head.

### 4. Blood pressure before the first pass
**Why:** A cannula and then microelectrodes are about to traverse 8 cm of brain several times.
**Anaesthesia:** Hypertension is common: withheld medication, anxiety, a full bladder, discomfort, and the disease's own autonomic instability. Treat the cause first, then the number, to a systolic under about 140 mmHg before the first pass: labetalol 5-10 mg IV increments, hydralazine 5 mg IV, or a low-dose clevidipine or glyceryl trinitrate infusion. Avoid agents that sedate. An arterial line is justified when control is difficult. The same target holds until the last electrode is out.

### 5. Microelectrode recording
**Why:** Each nucleus has a recognisable firing pattern, and the length of track that shows it tells the surgeon how well the trajectory crosses the target.
**Anaesthesia:** Propofol and benzodiazepines act at GABA receptors that are dense in these nuclei and reduce or abolish the firing being recorded, so propofol is stopped 15-20 minutes beforehand and midazolam is not given at all. Dexmedetomidine at a low rate (0.1-0.4 micrograms/kg/h, patient rousable to voice) and remifentanil are the compatible options: in a retrospective comparison, adding dexmedetomidine to remifentanil left the population measures used for targeting and the firing rates unchanged. The room is quiet and electrically clean for this step, and unnecessary equipment is unplugged at the surgeon's request.

### 6. Macrostimulation and clinical testing
**Why:** Current through the lead should abolish tremor and rigidity at a low threshold and produce side effects only at a high one. That margin is the measure of a well-placed lead.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient must be fully awake and cooperative, so nothing sedative is running. Sedatives also suppress tremor by themselves and give a false result. Your role is observation: a sudden change in speech, conscious level or limb power is a haemorrhage until a scan says otherwise, and seizures occur occasionally with stimulation and are treated with a small propofol dose and airway support. In the awake series, intraoperative events occurred in 16%, chiefly seizures, neurological change, hypertension and airway obstruction. If the airway is lost in the frame, the sequence is jaw thrust and oxygen, the front bar off, a supraglottic airway, and the frame released from the table to lay the patient flat.

### 7. Asleep lead placement with imaging
**Why:** The surgeon relies on intraoperative CT or MRI to confirm the lead against the plan, and does not need the patient's report.
**Anaesthesia:** A conventional general anaesthetic with a tracheal tube. If microelectrode recording is still wanted, use remifentanil with low-dose volatile or dexmedetomidine and avoid propofol infusion during recording, by agreement with the neurophysiologist. Dopaminergic drugs can be continued, which makes the patient easier to manage. Blood pressure control is the same. Intraoperative MRI brings its own environment: compatible equipment, long circuits and restricted access, as described in [remote site anaesthesia](../a/a12-remote-site-sedation.html). Children and patients with dystonia or severe anxiety are done this way from the outset.

### 8. Generator implantation and tunnelling
**Why:** The lead ends are exposed behind the ear, an extension is tunnelled down the neck, and the generator is placed in a subcutaneous pocket below the clavicle or in the abdomen.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with the head turned. If the frame is still on from an awake first stage, secure the airway before it is removed only if the airway is safe; otherwise remove the frame first. Tunnelling is a brief intense stimulus: give a remifentanil or fentanyl bolus beforehand. Restart the patient's dopaminergic medication through a nasogastric tube or as soon as they can swallow, because a prolonged off period after a long day produces rigidity, poor cough and aspiration. Surgeons vary on diathermy near the new system; bipolar is preferred.

### 9. Stereo-EEG electrode implantation
**Why:** Ten to twenty depth electrodes are placed through small drill holes along robot-planned trajectories to sample the suspected network.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia, head in pins or a frame, two to four hours with little stimulation and many passes through brain: the same blood pressure discipline as stimulation surgery. No recording is done in theatre, so the choice of agents is free. Continue anticonvulsants on the day. Wake promptly for a neurological check and a scan. The patient returns one to two weeks later for removal and often resection, by then on reduced medication, sleep-deprived and after several seizures.

### 10. Resection with electrocorticography
**Why:** With the cortex exposed, the surgeon records spontaneous spikes from a grid or strip to confirm the focus, resects, and may record again.
**Anaesthesia:** The craniotomy itself follows the [supratentorial craniotomy](craniotomy-for-supratentorial-tumour.html) card. For the recording period: no benzodiazepines at any stage, volatile at or below 0.5 MAC or off, no propofol boluses, and nitrous oxide avoided; opioid infusion and dexmedetomidine maintain the anaesthetic. In a randomised trial dexmedetomidine did not suppress electrocorticography with either propofol or isoflurane. Light anaesthesia without relaxant recovery is an awareness risk, so say when recording starts and ends and deepen again immediately. If spikes are sparse the surgeon may ask for a provoking agent, such as a small dose of alfentanil or methohexital. Long-term enzyme-inducing anticonvulsants shorten the action of aminosteroid relaxants and increase opioid requirement: monitor the block and expect to redose.

## Pain plan

The mechanism differs by stage, and the analgesic strategy is local anaesthetic at each site plus simple systemic analgesia. For awake lead placement the scalp block or infiltration is the anaesthetic, and pain that breaks through is treated by the surgeon with more local anaesthetic within the dose you have calculated, not with sedation. Back and limb discomfort from lying still in the off state is often the patient's main complaint and is helped by position, paracetamol and reassurance.

After the generator stage the tunnel and pocket ache for two to three days. Infiltration of both by the surgeon, regular paracetamol and a few doses of oral oxycodone are sufficient for most. A superficial cervical plexus block or pectoral plane infiltration is a reasonable addition as author's judgement; there is no trial to cite. NSAIDs are withheld until the post-operative scan has excluded a tract haemorrhage, and many units withhold them for the first week.

After a resection the plan is the craniotomy plan: scalp block, paracetamol, cautious opioid, and a temporal flap that hurts on chewing. Tramadol is avoided in epilepsy because it lowers seizure threshold, and pethidine for the same reason and for its interaction in Parkinson's disease.

Escalation: headache with a new deficit, confusion or drowsiness after any electrode surgery is a haemorrhage and is scanned. Increasing pain, redness or swelling over the generator or along the track in the following weeks is infection, and the hardware is at stake.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Pneumocephalus and brain shift. Coughing, a long open burr hole and the head-up position let air in and move the target. A calm, non-coughing patient improves accuracy.
- Venous air embolism in the awake semi-sitting patient presents as cough.
- The patient who cannot continue awake. Convert by agreement: either complete under light general anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway and accept less testing, or stop and return for an asleep procedure.
- Later anaesthesia with a stimulator in place. Bipolar diathermy only where possible, the device switched off if it interferes with the ECG, MRI only under the manufacturer's conditions, and no short-wave diathermy. Abrupt loss of stimulation can cause severe rebound parkinsonism.
- Dystonia and Tourette syndrome. Violent movements make frame-based awake surgery unsafe; asleep placement.
- Vagal nerve stimulator. A left neck dissection with a chest pocket; test stimulation can cause bradycardia or asystole, and the device later causes hoarseness and worsens sleep apnoea when it fires.
- Laser ablation. Done partly in the MRI scanner with a long, immobile anaesthetic and a bolted probe.
- Hemispherectomy and large resections in children. Major blood loss relative to size, coagulopathy and electrolyte shifts: arterial and central access, blood in theatre, and paediatric intensive care, with the dosing rules in [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).
- Emergency variant. A symptomatic tract haemorrhage after either operation returns as an emergency craniotomy in a patient with fresh hardware; the principles are those of [traumatic brain injury](traumatic-brain-injury.html).
- Seizures on the monitoring unit. Patients with electrodes are deliberately under-medicated; a return to theatre for removal after a cluster means a post-ictal, possibly aspirated patient.

## Key sources

The evidence here is observational almost throughout. Awake against asleep stimulation surgery rests on a meta-analysis of case series with very few asleep cohorts. The haemorrhage figures come from a systematic review that showed wide variation between techniques. The drug effects on microelectrode recording come from small retrospective studies and from pharmacological reasoning, and the single randomised trial cited for electrocorticography is small. The blood pressure threshold is common practice derived from the association with haemorrhage, not a tested target. The pain plan, the infusion ranges and the airway drill in a frame are author's judgement and consensus.
