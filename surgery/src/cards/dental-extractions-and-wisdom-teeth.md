## What the surgeon is doing and why

A tooth is held in its socket by the periodontal ligament. An erupted tooth is removed by tearing that ligament: the surgeon expands the socket with an elevator and delivers the tooth with forceps. The wound is a bony socket lined with torn ligament that fills with clot. That is the whole operation for a child's carious primary molars or an adult's periodontally loose teeth, and it takes a minute a tooth.

An impacted lower third molar is a different operation. The tooth lies partly or wholly inside the mandible, often tilted against the second molar, with its roots close to the inferior alveolar canal and the lingual nerve running in the soft tissue on its inner side. To get it out the surgeon raises a mucoperiosteal flap, removes buccal and distal bone with a bur under irrigation to expose the crown, sections the tooth so that it comes out in pieces along a path the bone allows, irrigates the socket and closes the flap. Upper third molars sit in thin maxillary bone and usually come with an elevator alone.

Most extractions are done under local anaesthesia in a dental chair. General anaesthesia is used for four impacted third molars in one sitting, for children too young or too frightened to cooperate, for adults with intellectual disability or severe phobia, and for the patient with a medical reason to do everything once. The children's list is the high-volume end: healthy, anxious, often fasted poorly, each case over in five minutes.

The pain logic is bone. Removing bone from the mandible and leaving a raw socket produces an inflammatory response that peaks at 48 hours with swelling and trismus, which is why third molar removal is the standard model for testing oral analgesics. It is NSAID-responsive, short and predictable. Simple extractions without bone removal sit at the bottom of the scale. The anaesthetic problems are not pain: they are a shared airway full of blood, irrigation and tooth fragments, and a day-case population that goes home an hour later.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment and day-case selection
**Why:** The surgeon needs a still, open mouth and a patient who can go home; the list is built on throughput.
**Anaesthesia:** Most patients are young and well. The exceptions carry the risk: the adult with a learning disability who cannot be examined or cannulated awake and may have congenital heart disease, epilepsy or reflux; the patient having a clearance before cardiac surgery or radiotherapy; the obese adult with sleep apnoea. Ask about mouth opening. Trismus from pericoronitis is pain and spasm and usually relaxes after induction; trismus with swelling of the floor of the mouth or the neck is a different card ([dental and neck abscess](dental-and-neck-abscess.html)). Anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents are generally continued for extractions because local measures control socket bleeding; the surgeon decides, and you need to know the decision. For children see [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html): a parent at induction, topical local anaesthetic cream, and a plan for the child who will not cooperate.

### 2. Induction and the choice of airway
**Why:** The surgeon wants the tube out of the way of the side being operated on, the mouth opened wide with a prop, and the tongue retracted.
**Anaesthesia:** Three workable choices. A nasal tracheal tube gives the surgeon the whole mouth, protects the trachea from blood and irrigation, and is the default for bilateral lower third molars with bone removal and for long restorative cases. A reinforced supraglottic airway is quicker, avoids the nose and suits short extractions, but it occupies space in the mouth, can be displaced when the prop is opened or the tongue retracted, and gives a seal that depends on the surgeon not moving it. A nasal mask or nasopharyngeal airway with spontaneous ventilation survives in some paediatric extraction lists for cases under five minutes. Agree with the surgeon which it is before induction. If a supraglottic device is used, watch the capnograph and the bag at the moment the mouth prop is inserted and each time the surgeon changes sides.

### 3. Nasal intubation
**Why:** Routing the tube through the nose clears the mouth and lets the surgeon check the bite.
**Anaesthesia:** The nose bleeds and the turbinates tear, so preparation is the technique: ask which nostril is clearer, apply a vasoconstrictor (xylometazoline or co-phenylcaine spray), choose a small preformed nasal tube (6.0-6.5 mm for an adult), soften it in warm water and lubricate it. Pass it along the floor of the nose, directed back and not up. Resistance is a turbinate or a septal spur: withdraw and try the other side instead of pushing. A videolaryngoscope and Magill forceps guide the tip through the cords; hold the forceps away from the cuff. Epistaxis is the common complication and usually stops with the tube in place acting as a tamponade. Secure the tube so that it does not press on the nostril rim, because alar necrosis from a tube pulled upwards under drapes is a pressure injury you cause. Avoid the nasal route in a patient with a coagulopathy or on full anticoagulation, where an oral reinforced tube moved from side to side is safer.

### 4. Throat pack
**Why:** A pack was traditionally placed to absorb blood and catch fragments above the cords.
**Anaesthesia:** A consensus statement from the Difficult Airway Society and the maxillofacial and ENT surgical associations found no study showing benefit from routine anaesthetist-inserted throat packs, and many reports of harm, including postoperative airway obstruction from retained packs and at least one death. They no longer recommend routine insertion. If you or the surgeon judge one necessary (a supraglottic airway that will not catch debris, heavy bleeding expected), follow a protocol: one named person inserts it, the tail is left outside the mouth where possible, it is written on the board and stuck on the airway device or the patient's forehead as a label, it is part of the swab count, and its removal is confirmed aloud before reversal. A cuffed tube with a careful pharyngeal suction under vision at the end does the same job with no object to forget.

### 5. Mouth prop and local anaesthetic
**Why:** The surgeon injects lidocaine or articaine with adrenaline as inferior alveolar nerve blocks and buccal and palatal infiltration, for haemostasis during the operation and analgesia after it.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask what and how much. Dental cartridges are 1.8-2.2 mL of lidocaine 2% with adrenaline 1:80,000 or articaine 4% with adrenaline 1:100,000; six cartridges of lidocaine is over 250 mg, which matters in a 20 kg child and not in an adult. Intravascular injection into the pterygoid plexus or the inferior alveolar vessels produces tachycardia and hypertension within a minute; it passes, and it is not light anaesthesia. The value of the blocks for postoperative pain in children is uncertain: a Cochrane review could not pool the trials, and a numb lip that a small child bites or finds frightening is a real cost. In adults having third molars the block carries the first hours and is worth extending with bupivacaine.

### 6. Lower third molars: flap, bone removal and sectioning
**Why:** The flap exposes the bone over the crown; the bur removes enough bone to create a path of withdrawal and divides the tooth so that it can be delivered in pieces without levering against the second molar or the canal.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the stimulating step and the dirty one. The drill runs with saline irrigation that pools in the pharynx; the assistant's suction is the airway protection for a supraglottic device. The surgeon pushes down on the mandible with an elevator, which closes the mouth against the prop, moves the head and can dislodge the airway: support the jaw or ask the assistant to. Watch for the tube being kinked by the prop or pushed down by a retractor. The nerves at risk are the inferior alveolar and the lingual; injury to either gives numbness or dysaesthesia that the patient notices when the block has worn off, and it is worth documenting that the lip and tongue were numb from local anaesthetic at discharge.

### 7. Upper teeth and simple extractions
**Why:** Upper third molars and erupted teeth are elevated and delivered with forceps through the thin bone of the maxilla.
**Anaesthesia:** Fast and briefly stimulating. An extracted tooth or a fragment of crown can vanish over the back of the tongue. If the surgeon is not sure where a tooth went, the case does not end until it is found: look in the pharynx with a laryngoscope, check the pack and the suction canister, and take a chest radiograph before waking the patient if it is still missing. A tooth in the right main bronchus is an avoidable bronchoscopy. An upper molar root can also open the maxillary sinus, which the surgeon closes; it changes nothing for you.

### 8. Haemostasis and closure
**Why:** Sockets are compressed, the flap is sutured and gauze is placed over the sockets for the patient to bite on.
**Anaesthesia:** Give the NSAID and paracetamol now if not given orally before. The gauze packs the surgeon leaves for biting on are foreign bodies in an unconscious mouth: long tails outside the lips, or left out until the patient is awake. Suction the pharynx under direct vision with a laryngoscope, behind the soft palate as well, because a clot sitting in the nasopharynx above a nasal tube falls onto the cords when the tube comes out. Remove the throat pack if one was placed and say so.

### 9. Emergence
**Why:** The surgeon wants no clenching on fresh sutures and no bleeding from coughing.
**Anaesthesia:** The choice is between awake extubation with reflexes intact above a bleeding mouth, and deep removal in the lateral head-down position with the blood draining out. For a tracheal tube in an adult the author's practice is awake extubation, turned to the side as soon as the tube is out. For a supraglottic airway in a child, removal deep in the lateral position by a recovery team who do it daily is equally sound. Laryngospasm is the complication of this list, triggered by blood on the cords in a child at a light plane: have propofol and suxamethonium drawn up, and do not stimulate a child who is neither deep nor awake.

### 10. Recovery and discharge
**Why:** The patient goes home the same day with oozing sockets and a numb face.
**Anaesthesia:** Bleeding in recovery is managed by biting on gauze for 20 minutes with the patient sitting up; persistent bleeding goes back to the surgeon. Nausea comes from swallowed blood more than from the drugs. Discharge needs a written analgesic plan: ibuprofen and paracetamol taken together and regularly from before the block wears off, for three to four days. Warn that swelling and jaw stiffness peak on day 2. See [nausea, vomiting and day-case anaesthesia](../a/a11-ponv-day-case.html) for discharge criteria.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is bone and an inflamed socket, so the plan is anti-inflammatory and the opioid is a spare. The surgeon's local anaesthetic covers the first two to four hours with lidocaine or articaine and six to eight hours with bupivacaine. Rebound when the block recedes is real after surgical third molars, because the patient goes from a numb jaw to an inflamed bony wound in an hour, usually at home. The answer is timing: the first oral doses go in before the numbness fades.

The oral evidence is unusually good because this operation is the standard analgesic model. A Cochrane review found high quality evidence that ibuprofen is superior to paracetamol at six hours, and that the combination of the two outperformed either alone. Regular ibuprofen 400 mg with paracetamol 1 g is therefore the regimen, with a few doses of a weak or low-dose strong opioid for the first two nights after bony third molar surgery, and none after simple extractions. Dexamethasone reduces pain, swelling and trismus after third molar surgery; a network meta-analysis of 34 trials found most routes and doses better than placebo on day 1 with nothing to choose between them, at low certainty. Dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction is the practical version, and it is already being given for nausea.

In children having forceps extractions, paracetamol and ibuprofen before or during the case are enough, with fentanyl 1 microgram/kg IV if several teeth are removed. Whether to ask for local anaesthetic blocks in small children is a judgement the evidence does not settle; infiltration for haemostasis without a lip-numbing nerve block is a reasonable middle course.

Escalation triggers. Pain that worsens on day 3 to 5 instead of improving, with a foul taste and an empty socket, is alveolar osteitis (dry socket) and needs the surgeon to dress it, not more opioid. Increasing swelling with fever, trismus and difficulty swallowing after the first three days is infection spreading into the fascial spaces and is assessed urgently.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The lost tooth or fragment. Count teeth out against the plan. A missing piece is looked for before emergence.
- The retained throat pack. It kills by obstruction after extubation. No pack, or a protocol with a visible label and a spoken check.
- Supraglottic airway displacement. Happens when the prop goes in, when the surgeon changes sides and when the mandible is pushed down. A rising airway pressure or a flattening capnograph during any of those is the device, not bronchospasm.
- Nasal bleeding and trauma. Vasoconstrictor, a small warmed tube and no force. A turbinate avulsed into the tube blocks it: if ventilation is impossible after a difficult nasal passage, pass a suction catheter and be ready to replace the tube.
- The anxious or uncooperative child. Premedication (oral midazolam 0.5 mg/kg) and a calm inhalational induction beat a struggle. Repeat general anaesthetics for dental disease are common in this group, so the experience matters.
- The adult with intellectual disability. The assessment may only be possible once the patient is asleep. Premedication, a familiar carer present, and consent arrangements settled in advance. Use the anaesthetic for everything that needs doing: bloods, examination, radiographs, the whole dental treatment plan.
- Anticoagulated and antiplatelet patients. Usually continued. Expect oozing, more swallowed blood and more nausea; avoid the nasal route when the anticoagulation is full.
- Cardiac patients. A dental clearance before valve surgery is done on the sickest hearts on the list: severe aortic stenosis with adrenaline-containing local anaesthetic and a head-up position needs an arterial line and a vasopressor running, in a main theatre and not a day unit.
- Head and neck radiotherapy or bisphosphonate therapy. The surgical concern is necrosis of the jaw; for you the previously irradiated patient may have a fixed, difficult airway.
- Emergency and high-risk variant. Bleeding sockets returning to theatre: a stomach full of blood, so a rapid sequence induction with a tracheal tube. Extractions in a patient with a spreading infection are covered in [dental and neck abscess](dental-and-neck-abscess.html). Invasive monitoring, blood and critical care are not part of routine dental surgery; they enter with the cardiac patient and the abscess.

## Key sources

The analgesic evidence is strong because third molar surgery is the model on which oral analgesics are tested. The throat pack recommendation is a consensus statement built on a systematic review that found reports of harm and no evidence of benefit. The dexamethasone evidence is plentiful but low in certainty and says little about dose. The airway choices, emergence technique and the management of a lost tooth are consensus and author's judgement, as is the pain trajectory.
