## What the surgeon is doing and why

A tracheostomy is an opening made through the front of the neck into the trachea, usually between the second and fourth rings, held open by a tube. It is done for one of four reasons, and the reason defines the patient in front of you. In the ventilated critically ill patient it replaces a translaryngeal tube to allow less sedation, easier weaning and care of the mouth and larynx. In major head and neck cancer surgery it covers the days when swelling, a bulky flap or a divided mandible would make the natural airway unreliable. In upper airway obstruction from tumour, infection or bilateral cord palsy it bypasses the narrowing. And in chronic neurological disease it gives access for secretion clearance and long-term ventilation.

The surgeon makes a transverse incision midway between cricoid and sternal notch, separates the strap muscles in the midline, and meets the thyroid isthmus lying across the second and third rings, which is retracted or divided between clamps. The pretracheal fascia is cleared and the trachea steadied with a cricoid hook. The opening itself varies by surgeon: a window excised from the anterior wall, a vertical slit, or an inferiorly based flap of the anterior wall sutured to the skin edge (a Bjork flap), which keeps the track open if the tube comes out. Stay sutures through the tracheal wall on each side, taped to the chest, serve the same purpose and are routine in children. The tube is inserted, the cuff inflated and the flange sutured and tied.

The percutaneous dilatational technique, done at the bedside in intensive care with a needle, wire and dilators under bronchoscopic vision, makes the same hole with less dissection. Open surgery in theatre is preferred when the anatomy is unfavourable (obesity, a short neck, a goitre, previous neck surgery, an unstable cervical spine), in children, and whenever the airway above is obstructed.

The pain logic is minor: skin, a midline plane between muscles and a cartilage window, with nothing under tension. Score 2. The card is about a sequence in which the airway is deliberately opened, shared and then exchanged. The general approach is in [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html).

## Steps

### 1. Deciding which tracheostomy this is
**Why:** The surgeon's steps are the same for every indication; the patient's reserve and the state of the upper airway are not.
**Anaesthesia:** Three situations. The intubated intensive care patient arrives on a ventilator with an airway already secured; the risks are transfer, oxygen dependence and the exchange. Ask about inspired oxygen and PEEP: a patient needing 60% oxygen and 10 cmH2O will desaturate during any leak, and the operation can wait a few days. The head and neck cancer patient is having it as one step of a longer operation and is covered in [laryngectomy, neck dissection and free flap reconstruction](laryngectomy-neck-dissection-and-free-flap.html). The obstructed patient with stridor has no secured airway, and the choice is between awake tracheal intubation, where the pathology allows a tube to pass, and tracheostomy under local anaesthesia. Review the nasendoscopy and CT with the surgeon, and agree it outside the room. Check coagulation and platelet count, anticoagulant timing and, in the ventilated patient, that enteral feed has been stopped and the stomach aspirated.

### 2. Awake tracheostomy under local anaesthesia
**Why:** When the glottis or supraglottis is nearly closed by tumour or oedema, the surgeon bypasses it without anyone abolishing the patient's own breathing.
**Anaesthesia:** Your role is monitoring, oxygen, reassurance and restraint. The patient sits as upright as the surgeon can tolerate. Sedation is minimal or none: the patient is using accessory muscles and conscious effort to breathe, and a dose that takes the edge off anxiety can take away the airway. Small increments of remifentanil or midazolam are used by some in the calmer patient; the defensible default is none until the trachea is open. High-flow nasal oxygen buys comfort and saturation but fills the drapes with oxygen, and an airway fire has been reported in exactly this setting: keep diathermy off or bipolar only, tent the drapes, and tell the surgeon the flow. The surgeon infiltrates generously; lidocaine injected through the tracheal wall just before the window is cut blunts the cough. In published series emergency awake tracheostomy secures the airway with immediate survival around 97%. Once the cuffed tube is in and capnography is confirmed, induce general anaesthesia through it for whatever follows.

### 3. Setting up the asleep case
**Why:** Extension draws the trachea out of the chest and stretches the skin over it; the surgeon needs the chin, the notch and the midline exposed.
**Anaesthesia:** Before draping, loosen the tapes on the existing tube and make sure you can reach it, the pilot balloon and the circuit under the drapes: you will have to move it under vision of the capnograph alone. Have a laryngoscope, bougie, a second tracheal tube and the same means of reintubation used originally. Check the tracheostomy tube with the surgeon: type, size, cuff tested, obturator in, inner cannula available, one size smaller on the trolley, and a sterile catheter mount. Preoxygenate with 100% oxygen during the dissection, deepen anaesthesia and give a relaxant. Extension in the patient with an unstable neck or rheumatoid disease is limited to what is safe, which makes the surgery harder.

### 4. Incision and dissection to the trachea
**Why:** The surgeon stays in the midline, where there are no named vessels except the anterior jugular veins and the isthmus.
**Anaesthesia:** Infiltration with adrenaline-containing local anaesthetic gives a brief pulse rate rise. Bleeding from the isthmus or a high innominate artery is usually modest but runs into the trachea once it is open. Positive end-expiratory pressure and a high-riding pleura in the hyperinflated or paediatric patient put the lung apices in the field: a pneumothorax is possible.

### 5. Preparing to open the trachea
**Why:** The surgeon has the rings exposed and is about to cut into the airway directly over your cuff.
**Anaesthesia:** Two actions on the surgeon's word. First, reduce the inspired oxygen to 30% or less if the patient tolerates it, or to the lowest tolerated, and wait for the expired figure to follow; the surgeon uses a blade, not diathermy, to enter the trachea. Tracheostomy is listed among the highest-risk operations for airway fire because the plastic tube and cuff lie directly under the point of entry. Second, protect the cuff: advance the tube so the cuff lies below the incision site (accepting it may be endobronchial for a minute), or deflate it as the blade goes in. A punctured cuff means an unsealed airway and falling tidal volumes at the moment the trachea is open.

### 6. Tracheal window and withdrawal of the tube
**Why:** The opening is made and the surgeon looks in to see the tracheal tube lying in the lumen.
**Anaesthesia:** Ventilation now leaks through the window. Return to 100% oxygen only if saturation requires it and diathermy is finished. Suction the tube. On the surgeon's instruction deflate the cuff and withdraw the tube slowly until the surgeon sees its tip rise just above the upper edge of the window, and stop there. The tip is then in the subglottis: the larynx is still intubated, and the tube can be advanced again past the stoma in seconds if the next step fails. Holding the tube at that mark is the single task you must not delegate.

### 7. Insertion and confirmation
**Why:** The surgeon passes the tracheostomy tube on its obturator into the lumen, removes the obturator and inflates the cuff.
**Anaesthesia:** Connect the circuit by the sterile catheter mount and look for three things in order: a capnograph trace over several breaths, chest movement on both sides, and a normal airway pressure. The failure to fear is a false passage, the tube lying in the pretracheal space in front of the trachea: high pressure, no carbon dioxide, and surgical emphysema if you persist in ventilating. If the trace is absent, do not squeeze harder. Disconnect, readvance the oral tube beyond the stoma, reinflate its cuff, ventilate, and let the surgeon start again. Only once the capnograph is unequivocal does the oral tube come out.

### 8. Securing the tube
**Why:** A new stoma has no track. The tube is held by sutures through the flange and by ties, with the neck flexed back to neutral before the ties are tightened.
**Anaesthesia:** Removing the shoulder bolster changes the depth of the tube in the trachea; re-check ventilation after the head is neutral. Note for the handover what kind of opening was made (window, slit or flap), whether stay sutures were placed and which is which, the tube type and size, the grade of laryngoscopy and whether the upper airway is usable. That information goes on the bedhead sign.

### 9. Transfer and the first hours
**Why:** The surgical concerns are bleeding around or into the tube and its position.
**Anaesthesia:** Transfer with capnography, a spare tube of the same size and one smaller, tracheal dilators, suction and the means to reintubate from above. Early bleeding is usually from the wound edge or thyroid and settles with pressure or a return to theatre. Surgical emphysema of the neck comes from a tight closure around a leaking tube or from a false passage. A chest radiograph is taken only for a clinical reason.

### 10. The first week
**Why:** The track from skin to trachea takes several days to mature; until then a displaced tube may not go back.
**Anaesthesia:** You are the person called. The national audit and confidential enquiry found the same failures repeatedly: unrecognised displacement, no capnography, and staff unsure what to do. The emergency algorithm is built for first responders: call for airway help, assess breathing at both face and stoma, apply oxygen to both, use capnography, remove any speaking valve and inner cannula, pass a suction catheter to test patency, deflate the cuff, and if the patient is not improving take the tube out. Then oxygenate by whichever route works, covering the stoma for mask ventilation from above or ventilating the stoma with a small face mask or laryngeal mask, before attempting oral intubation with the tube advanced beyond the stoma or reinsertion of a smaller tube through the stoma over a bougie. In the first days oral intubation is generally the safer rescue if the upper airway allowed it at the start.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a small skin and soft tissue wound that the surgeon has already infiltrated. Paracetamol, with an NSAID where renal function and bleeding risk allow, is sufficient; most ventilated patients are in any case on an analgesic infusion for other reasons. Expect wound pain of about 3 out of 10 on the first evening and little by day 3. There is no rebound and no role for a block beyond the infiltration, though bilateral superficial cervical plexus blocks are a reasonable supplement for a planned awake procedure; this is author's judgement.

What needs treating is the tube. It provokes coughing with every movement and suction pass for the first day or two, which a small opioid dose or a low-rate remifentanil or dexmedetomidine infusion in critical care settles better than more wound analgesia. Humidification from the start prevents the crusting that blocks tubes. The loss of voice with the cuff up is a source of real distress and of unrecognised pain: writing materials, a communication board and, as soon as the cuff can be deflated, a speaking valve are part of the plan.

Pain that increases after day 2 is infection of the stoma or pressure from the flange and ties, and is managed by wound care.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Laryngectomy is different. After a total laryngectomy the trachea ends at the skin and nothing connects it to the mouth. Oxygen by face mask does nothing and oral intubation is impossible. The emergency algorithm for these patients starts and ends at the neck.
- Displacement and obstruction. The two emergencies of the first week. A tube that has come partly out can sit in the pretracheal tissue with the patient still breathing around it; the signs are a new voice, a cuff needing more air, and a flat capnograph.
- Airway fire. Reduce oxygen, avoid diathermy at tracheal entry, protect the cuff. If it happens: disconnect and stop gas flow, remove the burning tube, flood with saline, ventilate, reintubate, bronchoscope.
- The hypoxic ventilated patient. High oxygen and PEEP requirements make both the fire precautions and the leak hazardous. Defer if possible; if not, accept a higher oxygen concentration, have the surgeon enter with a cold blade, and move fast through steps 5 to 7.
- Obesity and the short neck. A standard tube is too short for the depth of tissue and its tip sits against the posterior wall or comes out; adjustable-flange or extended-length tubes are chosen before the start.
- Percutaneous tracheostomy. The anaesthetist manages the upper tube and bronchoscope while the operator dilates; the commonest serious errors are cuff puncture, loss of the airway on withdrawal and posterior wall injury. The trial evidence comparing it with open surgery is of low quality and excludes many of the patients sent to theatre.
- Children. Smaller margins everywhere: uncuffed or small cuffed tubes that block easily, stay sutures as routine, no window excised, and accidental decannulation that is rapidly fatal. Specialist centres.
- Emergency front-of-neck access. In the adult who cannot be intubated or oxygenated the rescue is a scalpel cricothyroidotomy, which is quicker and shallower than a tracheostomy; a formal tracheostomy follows once the patient is oxygenated. The decision tree for the obstructed airway is in [airway foreign body, epiglottitis and the obstructed airway](airway-foreign-body-epiglottitis-and-the.html) and for infection in [dental and neck abscess](dental-and-neck-abscess.html).
- Late bleeding. A small herald bleed days to weeks later, in a patient with a low-lying or over-inflated tube, can precede erosion into the innominate artery. Over-inflate the cuff, apply digital pressure through the stoma against the sternum, and call for theatre and blood: see [major haemorrhage, cell salvage and coagulation](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html).
- Invasive monitoring and blood are determined by the underlying illness, not by the operation. Critical care or an equivalent ward with trained staff is required for every new tracheostomy.

## Key sources

The emergency management of a new tracheostomy is a multidisciplinary consensus guideline, supported by a national audit and a confidential enquiry that show where care fails. Awake tracheostomy outcomes come from retrospective single-centre series. The fire precautions are an advisory based on case reports and expert opinion. The intraoperative sequence (oxygen reduction, cuff protection, withdrawal to just above the stoma, capnographic confirmation before removing the oral tube) is standard teaching and author's practice, not trial evidence, and the oxygen and PEEP thresholds for deferral are author's judgement.
