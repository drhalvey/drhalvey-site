## What the surgeon is doing and why

The oesophagus is a muscular tube with no serosa and a rich longitudinal lymphatic network, running through three compartments: neck, posterior mediastinum and upper abdomen. Cancer of its lower third or of the gastro-oesophageal junction spreads along those lymphatics early, so a curative operation removes most of the oesophagus with the surrounding nodes and replaces it. The replacement is nearly always the stomach, refashioned into a tube and pulled up into the chest or neck. Most patients have had chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy first, and arrive deconditioned, sometimes malnourished, with whatever those treatments did to the heart, lungs and marrow.

The surgery has four jobs, and every named variant is a different arrangement of them.

```
 Job                    Where            What it costs you
 ---------------------  ---------------  -------------------------------
 1 Mobilise stomach     abdomen          laparotomy or laparoscopy
 2 Make the conduit     abdomen          stomach now on ONE artery
 3 Resect oesophagus    right chest      one-lung ventilation, azygos,
   and nodes                             airway, thoracic duct, heart
 4 Join conduit to      chest (Ivor      leak into mediastinum, or
   oesophageal stump    Lewis) or neck   leak into neck + laryngeal
                        (McKeown)        nerve at risk
```

Job 2 is the one that defines your intraoperative priorities. To reach the chest the stomach is detached from the left gastric, short gastric and left gastro-epiploic vessels and stapled into a tube about 4 cm wide. What remains is perfused by the right gastro-epiploic arcade alone, and the tip of the tube, which is the part that will be joined to the oesophagus, lies furthest from that inflow, across a watershed of submucosal collaterals. It is denervated and cannot autoregulate. Its perfusion follows arterial pressure and cardiac output passively and is worsened by venous congestion and tissue oedema. Anastomotic leak is the complication that determines survival, and the anaesthetist controls two of its inputs.

The Ivor Lewis operation does the abdomen first (supine), then the right chest (lateral or prone), and makes the anastomosis high in the chest. The McKeown operation does the chest first or second and finishes with a left neck incision for a cervical anastomosis: a leak there drains to the skin and is survivable, but the recurrent laryngeal nerves are at risk. The transhiatal operation avoids opening the chest altogether, with the surgeon's hand dissecting blind through the hiatus behind the heart. Each phase can be open, laparoscopic, thoracoscopic or robotic. In the MIRO trial, simply replacing the laparotomy with laparoscopy, while keeping the open thoracotomy, cut major complications from 64% to 36%, almost entirely by reducing pulmonary complications. The abdominal wound matters to the lungs as much as the chest wound does.

The pain logic: this is the anchor operation for a score of 5. The dominant mechanism is chest wall. A thoracotomy spreads ribs, stretches costovertebral joints and crushes or stretches an intercostal nerve; thoracoscopy levers ports against the same nerves; chest drains lie across the pleura and move with each breath for days. An upper abdominal incision adds abdominal wall pain that is loaded by the same coughs. A neck wound adds little. The intensity is severe, it is evoked by exactly the breathing and coughing the patient must do, it lasts well beyond 72 hours, and thoracotomy leaves a proportion with chronic neuropathic chest wall pain. Minimally invasive approaches lower the peak but not the need for a continuous technique.

## Steps

### 1. Preassessment and prehabilitation
**Why:** The surgeon is selecting patients who can survive a pulmonary complication and a leak.
**Anaesthesia:** Functional capacity, lung function, nutrition and the effects of neoadjuvant therapy (anthracycline or fluoropyrimidine cardiotoxicity, radiation to the lung fields, anaemia). Smoking and alcohol cessation, inspiratory muscle and exercise training, and feeding by jejunostomy or stent where dysphagia is severe all happen in the weeks before. Explain the epidural or catheter, the critical care stay and early mobilisation.

### 2. Regional technique and induction
**Why:** Nothing surgical; the analgesic catheter is placed awake while the patient can report a paraesthesia.
**Anaesthesia:** Thoracic epidural at T6-T8, tested, before induction for open and hybrid cases. Treat the oesophagus as a reservoir: tumours obstruct, and food and saliva pool above them, so use a head-up rapid sequence induction when dysphagia is more than mild. Left double-lumen tube, position confirmed with a bronchoscope. Arterial line, central line in the right internal jugular (the left neck is the surgeon's in three-stage surgery), cardiac output monitor, warming. A nasogastric tube is passed now and will be withdrawn and re-advanced on the surgeon's instruction several times.

### 3. Abdominal phase: gastric mobilisation
**Why:** The stomach is freed along the greater curve, preserving the right gastro-epiploic arcade, the left gastric pedicle is divided with its nodes, and the hiatus is widened. A pyloric drainage procedure and a feeding jejunostomy are added by some surgeons.
**Anaesthesia:** Laparoscopic in most centres now: pneumoperitoneum with head-up tilt, and once the hiatus is opened carbon dioxide passes into the mediastinum and pleura, so a rising end-tidal carbon dioxide and airway pressure in this phase is capnothorax. Bleeding risk is the spleen and the left gastric artery. Start as you mean to go on with fluid: maintenance at 2-3 mL/kg/h with boluses only against a stroke volume response, and a low-dose noradrenaline infusion to hold mean arterial pressure near baseline when the epidural is running.

### 4. Conduit formation
**Why:** The stomach is stapled into a tube along the lesser curve, either in the abdomen or later through the chest; many surgeons assess the tip with indocyanine green fluorescence.
**Anaesthesia:** From this moment the tip of the conduit is the most pressure-dependent tissue in the patient. Withdraw the nasogastric tube and any oesophageal probe above the staple line when asked and say that it is done. Indocyanine green, if used, is given IV on request (typically 5-7.5 mg) and briefly makes the pulse oximeter read low. Avoid epidural boluses of concentrated local anaesthetic here; run a dilute infusion and treat pressure with noradrenaline.

### 5. Repositioning for the chest
**Why:** The right chest gives access to the whole thoracic oesophagus with only the azygos vein in the way.
**Anaesthesia:** Left lateral for thoracotomy, semi-prone or prone for thoracoscopy. Every line, the epidural and the tube are at risk during the turn; recheck the double-lumen tube with the bronchoscope once the patient is positioned, because a 1 cm shift is enough to lose isolation. Pad and check pressure points for a three-hour stay. Prone positioning lets the lung fall forward and some units use a single-lumen tube with a low-pressure capnothorax of 6-8 mmHg instead of lung isolation; that gas compresses the mediastinum and reduces venous return.

### 6. One-lung ventilation and thoracic dissection
**Why:** The right lung is collapsed, the azygos arch is divided, and the oesophagus with its nodes is dissected off the aorta, pericardium, trachea and both main bronchi. The thoracic duct is usually ligated.
**Anaesthesia:** The longest and most demanding phase. Protective one-lung ventilation: 4-6 mL/kg predicted body weight, PEEP 5 cmH2O, permissive hypercapnia, the lowest FiO2 that holds the saturation, and CPAP or intermittent re-inflation of the right lung by agreement if hypoxaemia persists. The surgeon is working on the back of the heart and the great veins: retraction causes sudden falls in output and runs of atrial ectopy that stop when the retractor moves. The membranous trachea and left main bronchus lie in the field and a tear presents as a gas leak and loss of tidal volume; the tube may need advancing beyond it. Bleeding from the azygos vein or an aortic oesophageal branch is abrupt and large.

### 7. Intrathoracic anastomosis (Ivor Lewis)
**Why:** The conduit is pulled up through the hiatus, the specimen is removed, and the conduit is joined to the oesophageal stump with a circular or linear stapler or by hand, high in the chest.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon will ask for the nasogastric tube to be advanced across the new join under direct vision; do it slowly and stop the moment they say so. Then fix it as if it were an arterial line, because a tube that falls out on the ward cannot be blindly replaced across a fresh anastomosis. Hold mean arterial pressure at baseline. A leak test with air or dye through the tube may be requested.

### 8. Chest closure, drains and re-expansion
**Why:** One or two chest drains are placed, the lung is re-expanded under vision and the ribs are approximated.
**Anaesthesia:** A paravertebral catheter is placed by the surgeon under direct vision now if that is the plan, or intercostal blocks are added. Recruit the right lung gently with a sustained inflation while the surgeon watches for an air leak. Return to two-lung protective ventilation. Send a gas.

### 9. Neck phase (McKeown)
**Why:** A left neck incision along sternocleidomastoid exposes the cervical oesophagus; the conduit is delivered up through the posterior mediastinum and anastomosed there.
**Anaesthesia:** Supine again, head turned to the right, which is why the central line belongs on the right. The left recurrent laryngeal nerve lies in the groove being dissected and is bruised or stretched in a meaningful minority: a hoarse voice and a weak cough after extubation mean silent aspiration risk. Traction on the carotid sheath causes bradycardia. Some units change the double-lumen tube to a single-lumen tube before this phase; with a swollen airway after hours of surgery, do it over an exchange catheter with a videolaryngoscope or not at all.

### 10. Emergence and extubation
**Why:** The surgeon wants a patient breathing, coughing and moving on the day of surgery.
**Anaesthesia:** Extubation in theatre is the expected course and the enhanced recovery standard: warm, fully reversed against a quantitative monitor, the epidural or catheter working and tested, sitting up. Suction the oropharynx and the tubes carefully and never pass a suction catheter blindly down the oesophagus. Reasons to delay are hypothermia, a long difficult one-lung phase with poor gas exchange, major transfusion, or a swollen airway.

### 11. Critical care and the first week
**Why:** The surgeon is watching the drains, the conduit and the chest radiograph, and wants the patient out of bed on day 1.
**Anaesthesia:** Three problems arrive on a timetable. Days 1-3: hypotension from the epidural meets a team that wants the conduit perfused and the patient mobilised; a low-dose vasopressor on the ward or high-dependency unit is better than fluid boluses or switching the epidural off. Days 2-4: atrial fibrillation, in about one in six, which is associated with leak and pneumonia and should prompt a search for both. Days 3-7: pneumonia and anastomotic leak, the first often caused by pain-limited coughing or aspiration from a paralysed cord. The catheter or epidural stays until the chest drains are out, usually day 4-6, with a planned step-down to oral or jejunal analgesia.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is chest wall with an abdominal wall component, severe and prolonged, and directly coupled to the complication that matters most: a patient who cannot cough gets pneumonia. A continuous regional technique covering the chest for as long as the drains are in is not optional. The choice is between an epidural and a paravertebral catheter, and it depends on the approach.

For open and hybrid surgery (a thoracotomy, a laparotomy, or both), thoracic epidural at T6-T8 covers both wounds with one catheter and has the largest body of evidence. A 2025 meta-analysis of epidural against other techniques in oesophagectomy found lower pain scores, fewer pulmonary complications and fewer severe complications, with slightly less anastomotic leakage, at the price of more catheter-related problems and more vasoactive drug use. That last finding is the whole difficulty: the technique that best protects the lungs produces the hypotension that threatens the conduit. The answer is to run it dilute (ropivacaine 0.2% or bupivacaine 0.1-0.125% with fentanyl 2 micrograms/mL at 6-10 mL/h), avoid large boluses, and treat the resulting vasodilatation with noradrenaline and not with volume.

For thoracoscopic and robotic surgery the balance shifts. The abdominal wounds are ports, the chest wounds are ports and drains on one side, and a unilateral paravertebral catheter covers the side that hurts without bilateral sympathetic block. Two cohort studies cited here found paravertebral catheters non-inferior to epidural for pain after Ivor Lewis surgery, with less hypotension, less vasopressor use, lower opioid consumption and a shorter intensive care stay. They are observational and single-centre, and the defensible reading is that paravertebral analgesia is a reasonable first choice for minimally invasive oesophagectomy, not that it has beaten the epidural. The author's practice for minimally invasive cases is a surgeon-placed or ultrasound-guided paravertebral catheter at T5-T6, intrathecal morphine 150-300 micrograms before induction to cover the first 24 hours and the visceral component, port-site infiltration in the abdomen, and a PCA.

Around either technique: regular paracetamol IV then via the jejunostomy; an NSAID is avoided in most units for the first days because of the anastomosis and the kidneys, and this caution is convention more than evidence; low-dose ketamine infusion for the opioid-tolerant patient; gabapentinoids only for established neuropathic features.

Rebound is high. A failed or dislodged epidural on day 1 leaves a thoracotomy with nothing, and the response must be the same day: re-site, or place a paravertebral or erector spinae catheter, not simply escalate the PCA. Step-down when the drains come out should be staged, with oral or jejunal opioid started before the infusion stops. Escalation triggers: new severe chest or interscapular pain with tachycardia, fever, atrial fibrillation or a change in drain fluid is a leak or conduit necrosis; shoulder pain on the operated side is usually the drain or the position on the table.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Transhiatal oesophagectomy. No thoracotomy and no one-lung ventilation, so less pain and a single-lumen tube, but the surgeon's hand in the posterior mediastinum compresses the heart: abrupt profound hypotension and arrhythmia that resolve when the hand comes out. Agree a spoken signal. Bleeding and airway tears occur out of sight.
- Emergency oesophagectomy or repair for perforation (Boerhaave, iatrogenic). A septic patient with mediastinitis and a hydropneumothorax: drain the chest before positive pressure if possible, rapid sequence induction, invasive lines, and expect intensive care ventilation.
- Vasopressors and the conduit. Hypotension harms the conduit; whether noradrenaline at modest dose harms it is not established, and consensus has moved towards using it to maintain pressure in a euvolaemic patient. High doses to mask hypovolaemia are a different matter.
- Fluid. A strongly positive balance is associated with pulmonary complications and conduit oedema; a dry patient on an epidural is hypotensive. Aim for near-zero with flow monitoring, and accept a low urine output for some hours if lactate and flow are satisfactory.
- Recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy. Commoner with cervical anastomosis and upper mediastinal node dissection. A weak, breathy voice after extubation needs a swallow assessment before anything by mouth.
- Chyle leak. Milky drain output once feeding starts; a nutritional and immunological problem that sometimes needs re-operation.
- Colon or jejunal interposition. Used when the stomach is unavailable: three anastomoses, a longer operation, and a conduit even more sensitive to perfusion.
- The double-lumen tube and the difficult airway. A bronchial blocker through a single-lumen tube is an acceptable alternative and avoids a tube exchange at the end.
- Post-thoracotomy chronic pain. A proportion of thoracotomy patients have persistent neuropathic chest wall pain; good acute control is the only modifiable factor in your hands, and its effect is uncertain.

## Key sources

The ERAS Society guideline is the framework and is graded, but many of its anaesthetic sections rest on evidence borrowed from lung resection and major abdominal surgery. The MIRO trial is the strongest single piece of evidence in the card and makes the case that the size of the wound drives pulmonary outcome. The analgesic comparison is weaker than it looks: the epidural meta-analysis pools heterogeneous comparators, and the paravertebral studies are retrospective or before-and-after cohorts, so the approach-based recommendation above is author's judgement. The statements on conduit perfusion, vasopressors and fluid balance reflect physiological reasoning and consensus; no adequately powered trial settles them. Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury rates and chronic post-thoracotomy pain rates are deliberately given without numbers because none was verified during drafting.
