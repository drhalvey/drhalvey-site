## What the surgeon is doing and why

The proceduralist is a psychiatrist, and the operation is a generalised tonic-clonic seizure produced on purpose. Electrodes on the scalp (both temples, or the right temple and the vertex for right unilateral treatment) deliver a train of brief electrical pulses for a few seconds. The current depolarises enough cortex to start a seizure that generalises and then stops itself. A course is typically two or three treatments a week for six to twelve treatments, and some patients continue with maintenance treatments weeks apart. The indications are severe or treatment-resistant depression, catatonia, mania and some psychoses, often in patients who are not eating or drinking, are suicidal, or are elderly and frail.

Why the seizure treats the illness is not established. What is known empirically is that the seizure has to be adequate: generalised, of reasonable length on the EEG, with well-formed high-amplitude activity and abrupt suppression afterwards. The psychiatrist determines the seizure threshold at the first session by stepwise stimuli and then treats at a multiple of it. The threshold rises during a course, because the treatment is itself anticonvulsant, so the electrical dose climbs over the weeks. Higher electrical dose is what costs the patient memory.

That is where the anaesthetic enters the treatment itself. Every induction agent except ketamine and etomidate is an anticonvulsant at induction doses. The anaesthetic therefore raises the threshold and shortens the seizure in proportion to dose and inversely to the time elapsed since it was given. An inconsistent anaesthetic makes the psychiatrist's dosing uninterpretable: a short seizure may mean the threshold has risen or may mean the patient had 40 mg more propofol than last time. Consistency, session to session, matters more than which agent.

The original, unmodified treatment broke vertebrae and long bones. Muscle relaxation solved that, and general anaesthesia was added to cover the relaxant. The anaesthetic has three jobs and no more: unconsciousness for about five minutes, enough relaxation to turn the convulsion into a flicker, and oxygenation through a period of apnoea and a sharply raised cerebral oxygen demand.

The physiological event is large and stereotyped. The stimulus and the tonic phase bring a parasympathetic discharge lasting seconds: bradycardia, sometimes a few seconds of asystole, and salivation. The clonic phase brings a sympathetic surge: heart rate and systolic pressure rise sharply, peak within a few minutes and return towards baseline within about ten. Cerebral blood flow and intracranial pressure rise with it. Intragastric and intraocular pressure rise briefly. A fit heart ignores all of this; a heart with a fixed stenosis, poor ventricle or recent infarct may not.

The pain logic: no tissue is cut. Patients report headache, jaw ache and muscle soreness, which follow from the seizure, the direct stimulation of the jaw muscles, and suxamethonium. All respond to simple analgesia and are usually worst after the first treatment.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment before the course and before each session
**Why:** The psychiatrist prescribes a course, not a single treatment, and needs to know at the outset whether the patient can have ten or more brief anaesthetics and at what risk.
**Anaesthesia:** One thorough assessment before the course and a brief check at each session. There are no absolute contraindications; the conditions that need a plan are those the autonomic sequence stresses: raised intracranial pressure, a recent myocardial infarct or stroke, severe aortic stenosis, an untreated aneurysm, phaeochromocytoma. Across nearly 800,000 treatments, major cardiac events occurred after about 1 in 200 to 500 treatments, chiefly arrhythmia, heart failure and pulmonary oedema, and mortality is about 2 per 100,000 treatments, no worse than brief general anaesthesia for anything else. Read the medication chart for drugs that change the seizure or the anaesthetic: benzodiazepines and anticonvulsants raise the threshold; lithium prolongs neuromuscular block and adds to post-ictal confusion; theophylline prolongs seizures. Capacity and consent are the psychiatrist's domain, but a patient treated under mental health legislation is still fasted and still has reflux. Depressed, catatonic patients are often dehydrated and may not have taken their cardiac drugs: antihypertensives and antianginals are given on the morning.

### 2. The room, the record and the plan
**Why:** The suite is laid out for a conveyor of short treatments; the psychiatrist needs each patient asleep, treated and out in about ten minutes.
**Anaesthesia:** This is a remote site in the full sense ([remote site chapter](../a/a12-remote-site-sedation.html)): confirm oxygen, suction, a self-inflating bag, airway equipment, emergency drugs, dantrolene access and a defibrillator before the list, and know how help is summoned. Monitoring is ECG, non-invasive pressure, oximetry and capnography; the psychiatrist's machine records a two-channel EEG. Then read the record of the previous session: agent, dose in milligrams, suxamethonium dose, seizure length and quality, peak pressure, time to wake, and any agitation. That record is the plan. Changes are deliberate and discussed, one variable at a time.

### 3. Pre-oxygenation and the isolated limb
**Why:** The psychiatrist wants to see a motor seizure as well as the EEG; a cuff inflated above systolic pressure on one ankle or forearm before the relaxant keeps suxamethonium out of that limb so it convulses visibly.
**Anaesthesia:** Pre-oxygenate properly: the seizure raises cerebral oxygen consumption during apnoea, and a desaturating patient has a shorter seizure and a worse heart. Inflate the limb cuff after the induction agent and before the suxamethonium, on the right for right unilateral treatment so the observed seizure proves generalisation to the left hemisphere. Do not put it on a limb with a fracture risk, fistula or thrombosis. Intravenous access is sometimes the hardest part of the session by treatment eight; a reliable vein matters more than its site.

### 4. Induction agent
**Why:** The psychiatrist needs unconsciousness without losing the seizure, and needs the same conditions each time so the EEG can guide the electrical dose.
**Anaesthesia:** Methohexital is the historical standard and is not generally available in Australia. The practical choices are propofol 0.75-1 mg/kg IV, thiopentone 2-4 mg/kg IV where it can be obtained, and etomidate 0.15-0.3 mg/kg IV. Propofol gives the shortest seizures and the best haemodynamic control and recovery; etomidate and ketamine give the longest; pooled data confirm longer motor and EEG seizures with etomidate than propofol. Nobody has shown that the choice changes the antidepressant outcome, so the defensible default is propofol at the lowest dose that works, with a switch to etomidate when seizures become inadequate despite a rising electrical dose. Three adjustments lengthen the seizure without changing agent. Reduce the propofol and add remifentanil 1 microgram/kg IV: the meta-analysis shows the seizure lengthens only when the opioid is used to lower the induction dose, and the pressor response is smaller. Wait: seizure quality is better the longer the interval between induction and stimulus, so do not let the stimulus follow the syringe by 30 seconds. And hyperventilate (step 6). Ketamine does not improve depression outcomes or cognition as the induction agent and causes more confusion; its place is as a seizure-lengthening rescue, not a default.

### 5. Suxamethonium
**Why:** The psychiatrist wants the convulsion reduced to movement that cannot injure, while still being able to see that a seizure occurred.
**Anaesthesia:** Give it once consciousness is lost. The traditional 0.5 mg/kg is too little for many patients: in a crossover dose-finding trial, acceptable conditions needed 0.85 mg/kg IV in half of patients and 1.06 mg/kg in 90%, with more than 90% twitch suppression. About 1 mg/kg is the defensible starting dose, more for the patient with osteoporosis, a recent fracture or a spinal fusion, adjusted from the previous session's record. Wait for fasciculations to finish, about 60-90 seconds, which also serves the interval in step 4. Suxamethonium is contraindicated here for the usual reasons, and psychiatric units have their own versions of them: prolonged immobility in catatonia and neuroleptic malignant syndrome both raise the risk of hyperkalaemia. The alternative is rocuronium 0.4-0.6 mg/kg IV with neuromuscular monitoring and sugammadex after the seizure. Myalgia after the first session is common in ambulant patients and lessens with later treatments.

### 6. Hyperventilation and the bite block
**Why:** The electrical stimulus depolarises the temporalis and masseter directly, bypassing the neuromuscular junction, so the jaw clamps shut with full force however much relaxant was given. Teeth, tongue and lips are what get injured in modern practice.
**Anaesthesia:** Hand-ventilate with oxygen to mild hypocapnia for the minute before the stimulus: pooled trials show a few seconds' longer seizure and no harm, and it tops up the oxygen store. Then place a soft bite block yourself. It must sit between the molars on both sides so the force is spread across the strongest teeth, with the tongue behind it and the lips free; an oropharyngeal airway is not a bite block and concentrates the force on the incisors. Check for loose teeth, crowns and dentures beforehand (dentures usually come out; a few well-fitting ones are left to protect isolated teeth). Hold the chin up against the block and say that the patient is ready.

### 7. The stimulus and the vagal phase
**Why:** The psychiatrist delivers the pulse train over up to eight seconds. At the first session several stimuli of increasing dose may be given to find the threshold, and a stimulus that fails to produce a seizure produces only the vagal discharge.
**Anaesthesia:** Bradycardia and brief asystole occur in the seconds after the stimulus, and they are most marked and least opposed after a subconvulsive stimulus, because no sympathetic phase follows. For titration sessions, and for patients who have shown a pause before or are on beta blockers, glycopyrrolate 200 micrograms IV or atropine 300-600 micrograms IV before induction is reasonable; routine use for every session adds tachycardia to the surge and is not needed. Have atropine drawn up regardless. If the stimulus fails, the psychiatrist will restimulate at a higher dose after about 20-30 seconds: keep ventilating, and consider whether the induction agent is the reason.

### 8. The seizure and the sympathetic surge
**Why:** The tonic phase lasts about 10 seconds and the clonic phase 20-60 seconds; the psychiatrist times the motor seizure in the cuffed limb and reads the EEG for duration, amplitude and post-ictal suppression.
**Anaesthesia:** Heart rate and pressure climb as the clonic phase begins and peak within a few minutes; systolic pressures well above 200 mmHg are seen in untreated hypertensives. In a healthy patient nothing is needed. In coronary disease, heart failure, aneurysm or after a stroke, blunt it: remifentanil at induction, or esmolol 0.5-1 mg/kg IV or labetalol 5-20 mg IV given after the seizure has started, since beta blockade before the stimulus deepens the vagal phase and may shorten the seizure. Resume gentle ventilation with oxygen as soon as the tonic phase passes; the apnoeic patient is consuming oxygen at seizure rates. Ventricular ectopy and ST change during the surge are common and usually resolve with it.

### 9. Seizure end, the prolonged seizure and emergence
**Why:** The psychiatrist wants a seizure that ends itself with clear EEG suppression. One that continues is doing harm, and one that was too short has not treated the patient.
**Anaesthesia:** A seizure that passes about two minutes on the EEG is terminated: propofol 0.5-1 mg/kg IV or midazolam 1-2 mg IV, repeated. The motor seizure can stop while the EEG seizure continues, so the psychiatrist's trace, not the limb, defines the end. Ventilate until suxamethonium wears off, usually three to five minutes; a patient still apnoeic at ten minutes has a butyrylcholinesterase problem and needs sedation, ventilation and time. Remove the bite block when the jaw relaxes, check the teeth and tongue, and turn the patient lateral.

### 10. Recovery and post-ictal agitation
**Why:** The post-ictal state is part of the treatment's effect and lasts minutes to an hour; the unit needs the trolley back.
**Anaesthesia:** Most patients are drowsy, disoriented and cooperative. A minority emerge agitated, frightened and combative, and they do it reliably at every session. Midazolam 1-2 mg IV or a small propofol dose at the end of the seizure, or a calm low-stimulus recovery with a familiar nurse, usually prevents it; record what worked. Headache is treated with paracetamol or an NSAID and prevented at the next session by giving it beforehand. Chest pain, breathlessness or a new arrhythmia in recovery gets a 12-lead ECG and a troponin, because the cardiac events cluster here. Note the doses, intervals, seizure length and recovery in the record for whoever does the next session.

## Pain plan

No block is relevant. The mechanism is muscle: direct electrical contraction of the jaw closers, the seizure itself and suxamethonium fasciculation, plus a vascular-type headache from the haemodynamic surge. The plan is paracetamol 1 g orally with or without an NSAID, given after treatment for those who need it and before the next session for those who had a headache after the last one. Opioids are not needed and add nausea and sedation to a post-ictal patient.

Because this is a course, the plan is adjusted between sessions. Severe myalgia after the first treatment argues for checking that the suxamethonium dose was adequate, not for reducing it; inadequate relaxation hurts more than fasciculation. Jaw pain and a bitten tongue mean the bite block was wrong.

Escalation: a severe, sudden or persistent headache with neurological signs is not post-ECT headache and is imaged. Back pain after a poorly modified seizure in an osteoporotic patient is a compression fracture until examined.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The inadequate seizure. In order: check the induction dose and interval, hyperventilate, reduce propofol with remifentanil, change to etomidate, consider ketamine, and review anticonvulsant and benzodiazepine prescriptions with the psychiatrist. Flumazenil before the stimulus is used for benzodiazepine-dependent patients in some units.
- The first session. Threshold titration means several stimuli, more vagal effect and a longer anaesthetic: a slightly larger induction dose, an anticholinergic, and a second dose of induction agent ready.
- Implanted cardiac devices. The current path is across the scalp and little reaches the chest. Pacemakers are generally left as they are with a magnet available; a defibrillator has its tachyarrhythmia therapy suspended for the treatment so that muscle artefact is not read as fibrillation, with external pads on. Cardioversion suites raise the same questions: see [electrophysiology ablation and cardioversion](electrophysiology-ablation-and-cardioversion.html).
- Pregnancy. ECT is used when drugs are unwanted or ineffective. After the first trimester add left lateral tilt, antacid prophylaxis, a low threshold for a tracheal tube late in pregnancy, and fetal heart monitoring before and after by the obstetric team.
- The elderly, frail patient. Thresholds are higher, the heart is less tolerant and confusion lasts longer. Smaller induction doses, a blunted surge and a slower recovery plan.
- Intracranial pathology. A mass with oedema is the classical high-risk case because of the rise in cerebral blood flow; treatment has been given after steroid cover and with tight haemodynamic control, and that decision belongs to a multidisciplinary discussion.
- Etomidate over a course. Repeated doses raise the question of adrenal suppression; it has not been shown to matter clinically in ECT, and the uncertainty is a reason to reserve etomidate for patients who need it.
- Tardive seizures and prolonged confusion. A seizure recurring in recovery is treated as status until it stops; cumulative confusion across a course is reported to the psychiatrist, who can change electrode placement, pulse width or spacing.
- Different anaesthetist each session. The commonest cause of variable seizures is variable anaesthesia. The record is the continuity.

## Key sources

The safety figures are from two large pooled analyses and are reassuring. The suxamethonium dose comes from a well-conducted crossover trial. Everything about induction agents is evidence about seizure duration and EEG quality, which are surrogates: the systematic reviews say plainly that antidepressant efficacy and cognitive outcome have not been compared between agents in adequate trials, so the agent recommendation here is author's judgement built on mechanism and consistency. The autonomic sequence and the bite block are standard teaching and are not individually sourced.
