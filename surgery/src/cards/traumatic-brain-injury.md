## What the surgeon is doing and why

The skull is a closed box and a traumatic haematoma is new volume inside it. Once cerebrospinal fluid and venous blood have been displaced, each further millilitre raises pressure steeply, the brain shifts across the midline and down through the tentorial notch, the third nerve is compressed (the dilated pupil), and the brainstem follows. The operation removes volume, or enlarges the box.

Three lesions come to theatre. An extradural haematoma is arterial blood, usually from a middle meningeal artery torn by a temporal fracture, stripping the dura from the bone. The brain underneath is often uninjured, which is why a patient can talk and then deteriorate, and why evacuation within the hour gives an excellent result and delay gives a death. An acute subdural haematoma is venous blood from torn bridging veins or a burst lobe, spread over the hemisphere, and it marks a brain that has itself been injured: after the clot is removed the brain may swell rather than fall back. Contusions and intracerebral haematomas are bruised brain that enlarges over the first days.

For all three the surgeon turns a large trauma flap, a question-mark incision from the zygoma round to the midline, strips temporalis, and removes a bone flap at least 11-12 cm across. The dura is opened for a subdural, the clot is washed out, and bleeding points are controlled. Then comes a decision: replace the bone (craniotomy) or leave it out and close the scalp over a dural patch (decompressive craniectomy). In RESCUE-ASDH, 450 patients with acute subdural haematoma were randomised at that moment, and outcome at 12 months was no different, so the surgeon decides on how the brain looks. A swollen brain that will not sit inside the bone edge gets a craniectomy.

Craniectomy is also done later, for intracranial pressure that medical treatment cannot control. The two trials of that use explain the surgeon's caution. DECRA, early bifrontal craniectomy for diffuse injury, lowered pressure and gave worse functional outcome. RESCUEicp, craniectomy as a last-tier treatment, cut deaths at six months from 48.9% to 26.9%, and the survivors gained were distributed across vegetative state, severe disability and moderate disability, with no more good recoveries. The operation saves lives and not necessarily function.

Your part is defined by the difference between primary and secondary injury. The impact cannot be undone. What follows is ischaemia from low perfusion pressure, hypoxia, hypercapnia, hypocapnia, hyperglycaemia, fever and coagulopathy, each of which is in your hands from the emergency department to intensive care. In the Traumatic Coma Data Bank, hypotension occurred in a third of severe head injuries and was associated with a 150% increase in mortality. The physiology is in [neuroanaesthesia essentials](../a/a08-neuroanaesthesia-essentials.html); this card is about timing.

The pain logic matters least here of any craniotomy, because most patients remain sedated. It matters for the minority woken after an extradural haematoma, and for the other injuries.

## Steps

### 1. The handover and the clock
**Why:** The surgeon has decided from the scan and the pupils that minutes count. An expanding extradural haematoma with a dilating pupil is the most time-critical operation in neurosurgery.
**Anaesthesia:** Take four facts: the Glasgow Coma Score and pupils before sedation, the mechanism and other injuries, anticoagulant and antiplatelet drugs, and what has been given. Then move. Nothing that can be done in theatre should be done in the emergency department: an arterial line, a central line, a second scan and a tidy cannula are not reasons to wait. If a pupil has blown, give mannitol 0.5-1 g/kg IV or hypertonic saline (for example 3% at 3-5 mL/kg IV) now, and hyperventilate to about 30 mmHg as a bridge of minutes. Tranexamic acid is given if the injury was within 3 hours: in CRASH-3 it reduced head injury deaths in mild to moderate injury and was safe.

### 2. Induction and intubation
**Why:** A patient with a Glasgow Coma Score of 8 or less, a falling score, or an unprotected airway needs the airway secured before the scan or the operation.
**Anaesthesia:** Rapid sequence induction with manual in-line stabilisation and a videolaryngoscope. The drug choice matters less than the dose: hypotension at induction is the injury you can cause. Use a reduced dose of propofol, or ketamine 1-2 mg/kg IV (the old concern about intracranial pressure does not hold in a ventilated patient with controlled carbon dioxide), with fentanyl 2-3 micrograms/kg and rocuronium 1-1.2 mg/kg, and have metaraminol or noradrenaline running. The targets are the Brain Trauma Foundation's: systolic at least 110 mmHg for ages 15-49 and over 70, at least 100 mmHg for ages 50-69. Pre-oxygenate fully; a saturation below 90% is a second insult. Hypertension with bradycardia before induction is the Cushing response and is left alone: it is the brain defending its own perfusion.

### 3. Ventilation, position and neck
**Why:** The surgeon needs the side of the head uppermost and the brain as slack as the injury allows.
**Anaesthesia:** Ventilate to an arterial carbon dioxide of 35-40 mmHg confirmed on a gas; prolonged prophylactic hyperventilation is specifically not recommended because it reduces flow to a brain that is already ischaemic. Head up 15-30 degrees provided the pressure tolerates it. The cervical collar compresses the jugular veins: once the head is fixed, open the front or replace it with blocks and tape. Turn the trunk with a shoulder roll, not the neck. Maintain with propofol infusion or a volatile at less than 1 MAC plus opioid; propofol is preferred when the brain is tight. No nitrous oxide, since pneumocephalus and pneumothorax are both possible.

### 4. Blood, coagulation and anticoagulants
**Why:** Injured brain releases tissue factor, and the scalp, bone and dura bleed throughout. The surgeon cannot achieve haemostasis in a coagulopathic patient.
**Anaesthesia:** Send a gas, full blood count, coagulation screen, fibrinogen and a viscoelastic test on arrival and treat the result, using the [major haemorrhage](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html) pathway early in polytrauma. Reverse anticoagulants before or during the opening, not afterwards. Warfarin: four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate 25-50 units/kg IV with vitamin K 5-10 mg IV; in the INCH trial the concentrate normalised the INR faster than plasma and faster normalisation went with less haematoma expansion. Dabigatran: idarucizumab 5 g IV. Factor Xa inhibitors: prothrombin complex concentrate, or andexanet where available and approved. Antiplatelet drugs: platelet transfusion for a patient going to craniotomy is common practice, and is a surgical and haematological decision.

### 5. Scalp flap and bone flap
**Why:** Speed. For an extradural haematoma the surgeon may make a single burr hole or a small temporal craniectomy first to let clot out, then complete the flap.
**Anaesthesia:** The scalp bleeds briskly until clips are on. Place the arterial line now if it was deferred. Keep the pressure target; intravascular volume is restored with isotonic crystalloid and blood, never hypotonic or glucose-containing fluid. Check glucose and temperature: treat hyperglycaemia and avoid fever, but do not cool deliberately, since early prophylactic hypothermia is not recommended.

### 6. Decompression: the moment the bone comes off
**Why:** Lifting the flap and opening the dura releases the pressure that has been compressing the brain and driving the patient's hypertension.
**Anaesthesia:** Expect abrupt hypotension. The sympathetic drive of the Cushing response disappears at decompression, revealing hypovolaemia from other injuries, mannitol diuresis and anaesthetic vasodilatation together. Anticipate it: have volume in, the vasopressor infusion running and blood checked before the bone is lifted, and tell the surgeon if the pressure is not ready. A dilated pupil often comes down within minutes, which is worth saying aloud. Bradycardia or asystole can occur at dural opening.

### 7. Clot evacuation and haemostasis
**Why:** The clot is removed by suction and irrigation, bleeding cortical vessels and bridging veins are coagulated, and a torn sinus is packed or repaired.
**Anaesthesia:** Sinus bleeding is fast and brings a risk of air embolism in a head-up patient. Blood loss is easy to underestimate under the drapes: use the haemoglobin, the suction and the swab count. If the brain swells progressively out of the craniotomy after the clot is out, tell the surgeon what you have already corrected (neck veins, airway pressure, carbon dioxide, pressure, depth) and give a further dose of osmotic agent and a propofol bolus. Unexplained swelling can mean a new haematoma on the other side, released when the tamponade came off, and needs an immediate scan after closure.

### 8. Bone flap decision, monitor and closure
**Why:** The surgeon replaces the flap if the brain is slack, or leaves it out, enlarges the dura with a patch and stores the bone. An intracranial pressure monitor or a ventricular drain is placed.
**Anaesthesia:** Record the opening intracranial pressure once the monitor is in and work to a cerebral perfusion pressure of 60-70 mmHg from then on, treating intracranial pressure above 22 mmHg. With the bone out, the brain is under scalp only: the side of the craniectomy is marked on the dressing and never lain on. Give paracetamol 1 g IV; ask for the incision to be infiltrated if a wake-up is planned.

### 9. Wake or keep asleep
**Why:** The surgeon wants an examination where one is possible, and a protected brain where it is not.
**Anaesthesia:** Wake and extubate only the patient with an isolated extradural haematoma who was talking shortly before surgery, has a slack brain, normal gases and temperature and no other injury requiring ventilation. Everyone else is transferred sedated and ventilated with full monitoring, a vasopressor running and the transport ventilator set to the same carbon dioxide. Transfer is a period of secondary injury risk equal to induction: the same targets apply in the lift and the scanner.

### 10. Intensive care and the return to theatre
**Why:** Swelling peaks over the first days. Refractory intracranial hypertension brings the patient back for a secondary decompressive craniectomy, and months later for a cranioplasty to replace the bone.
**Anaesthesia:** The secondary craniectomy patient is on maximal medical therapy: deep sedation, osmotic agents, sometimes a barbiturate infusion and high-dose vasopressors. Move them without interrupting any of it, keep the ventricular drain transduced and clamped only for the move (see [ventriculoperitoneal shunt and external ventricular drain](ventriculoperitoneal-shunt-and-external-ventricular-drain.html)), and expect the same hypotension at decompression. Phenytoin or levetiracetam for the first week reduces early seizures. Steroids are contraindicated in severe head injury.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a large craniotomy through temporalis plus the injuries of the accident, and for most patients the analgesic strategy is the sedation regimen: an opioid infusion with propofol in intensive care, where analgesia also blunts the rises in intracranial pressure that come with suctioning and turning. No block is needed for that patient, and the surgeon's infiltration of the incision with adrenaline-containing local anaesthetic is for haemostasis as much as pain.

For the patient who is woken, the plan is that of any craniotomy: infiltration or a scalp block at closure, regular paracetamol and small titrated doses of opioid, with conscious level and pupils checked before each dose. NSAIDs are avoided in the first days while the risk of haematoma expansion and the coagulation picture are unsettled; that is common practice and author's judgement, not a trial result.

Associated injuries often hurt more than the head. Rib fractures in a patient who needs to breathe and be examined are a strong case for a regional technique once coagulation is normal and the surgical team agrees; neuraxial catheters wait until intracranial pressure and coagulopathy are no longer in question.

Escalation: a woken patient with increasing headache, vomiting or a falling Glasgow Coma Score has a recurrent or new haematoma and needs an immediate scan. Agitation in a head-injured patient is hypoxia, a full bladder, pain or the injury itself, in that order of exclusion, and is not treated with sedation until the first three are dealt with.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Polytrauma with competing priorities. Uncontrolled bleeding in the chest, abdomen or pelvis is treated first or simultaneously: permissive hypotension for the torso conflicts with the head's need for perfusion pressure, and with a significant head injury the head's target wins unless haemorrhage is immediately life-threatening.
- The talk-and-deteriorate patient. A lucid interval followed by drowsiness and a dilating pupil is an extradural haematoma until proven otherwise, and the scan and the theatre are booked at the same time.
- The elderly anticoagulated patient with an acute subdural. A low-energy fall, a large clot and an atrophic brain that tolerated it for a while. Reversal is the first anaesthetic intervention. The chronic version is a different operation: see [burr hole drainage of chronic subdural haematoma](burr-hole-drainage-of-chronic-subdural.html).
- Children. Extradural haematomas occur with trivial-seeming falls; blood loss into the scalp and haematoma is proportionally large, and pressure targets scale with age: see [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).
- Cervical spine. Unknown until cleared. Every positioning move assumes instability.
- Suxamethonium and ketamine. Both are acceptable at induction; neither should be withheld on intracranial pressure grounds if they are the safest choice for the circulation and the airway.
- Diabetes insipidus and sodium. Polyuria after severe injury is diabetes insipidus or mannitol; measure sodium and osmolality before treating.
- Cranioplasty. Months later, an elective operation in a patient with a sunken flap, often on anticonvulsants, with infection and sudden post-operative swelling as the hazards.

## Key sources

The physiological targets come from the Brain Trauma Foundation's fourth edition, where most are level II or III recommendations built on observational data: they are the best available numbers and not strongly evidenced ones. The surgical decisions have three good randomised trials. Tranexamic acid has one very large trial with benefit confined to mild and moderate injury treated early. Anticoagulant reversal draws on one small randomised trial for warfarin and on guideline consensus for the rest; the doses given are standard and should be read against local protocol. The induction drug advice and the priority rules in polytrauma are consensus and author's judgement.
