## What the surgeon is doing and why

The pituitary sits in a bony saddle at the centre of the skull base. Directly in front of it is an air-filled room, the sphenoid sinus, which opens into the back of the nose. That anatomy gives a route to the gland that crosses no brain: through a nostril, through the back of the septum, through the front and floor of the sphenoid sinus, and through the thin bone of the sellar floor. On either side of the gland, a few millimetres away, are the cavernous sinuses and the internal carotid arteries. Above it, separated by a diaphragm of dura, are the optic chiasm and the subarachnoid space.

Adenomas come to surgery for two reasons. Non-functioning macroadenomas grow upward, press on the chiasm and cause a bitemporal field loss, and compress the normal gland into hypopituitarism. Functioning adenomas, often small, are removed for what they secrete: growth hormone (acromegaly), adrenocorticotrophic hormone (Cushing's disease), rarely thyroid-stimulating hormone. Prolactinomas are treated medically and seldom operated on. Each endocrine syndrome is a different anaesthetic patient with the same operation, and the pre-operative work is mostly about which one you have.

The surgeon, usually working with an ENT colleague and an endoscope, decongests the nose, displaces the turbinates, opens the sphenoid sinus widely, removes the sellar floor with a drill and rongeurs, incises the dura and removes the tumour with curettes and suction. Adenoma is soft and comes away from the firmer normal gland. Tumour above the sella is encouraged to descend. If the diaphragm tears, cerebrospinal fluid appears, and the surgeon repairs the defect in layers with fat or fascia from the abdomen or thigh, a synthetic patch and often a vascularised flap of septal mucosa. The nose is packed or splinted.

Several consequences follow from that sequence. The nose is the most stimulating part of the operation and the sella the least, so the haemodynamic profile is front-loaded. Topical vasoconstrictors are absorbed. Bleeding is venous and depends on head elevation and venous pressure. The airway is shared, soiled with blood, and afterwards obstructed at the nose by packs. A repaired skull base must not be exposed to positive pressure from below. And the posterior pituitary and its stalk have been handled, so water balance is unstable for a week.

The pain logic: mucosa and thin bone, with dura at the sella. There is no muscle and no flap. Patients describe a frontal and retro-orbital headache and facial pressure, moderate for a day or two, and an uncomfortable blocked nose. It scores as mild to moderate, with the caution that a severe or changing headache after pituitary surgery is a complication and is not treated as pain.

## Steps

### 1. Endocrine assessment
**Why:** The surgeon and endocrinologist have defined what the tumour secretes and what the remaining gland has lost.
**Anaesthesia:** Read the hormone profile before the airway. Cortisol axis: if deficient or untested, steroid cover is given; common practice is hydrocortisone 100 mg IV at induction with a tapering course until the axis is retested. In Cushing's disease many units deliberately withhold glucocorticoid and measure cortisol on the first mornings to prove remission, so ask what the protocol is and do not give dexamethasone as an antiemetic. Thyroid: replacement should be established, and cortisol is replaced before thyroxine. Cushing's disease brings hypertension, diabetes, obesity, sleep apnoea, fragile skin and veins, hypokalaemia and a thrombotic tendency. Acromegaly brings hypertension, cardiomyopathy, diabetes, sleep apnoea and the airway. Record visual fields and acuity as documented by the surgeon.

### 2. The acromegalic airway
**Why:** Growth hormone enlarges the mandible, tongue, epiglottis and soft tissues of the pharynx and larynx, and may narrow the glottis and subglottis.
**Anaesthesia:** In a prospective series of 128 acromegalic patients, laryngoscopy was grade III in 26% and intubation was difficult in 10%, and bedside tests missed a significant number. Treat every acromegalic as a potentially difficult mask ventilation and laryngoscopy: videolaryngoscope as the first device, a smaller tube than the patient's size suggests, a long blade, and an awake flexible technique where hoarseness, stridor or severe sleep apnoea suggest laryngeal involvement. The principles are in [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html). The extubation is as much a concern as the intubation.

### 3. Induction, tube and throat pack
**Why:** The surgeon works through both nostrils with the endoscope and instruments, and blood and irrigation run backwards into the pharynx.
**Anaesthesia:** An oral reinforced or pre-formed tube fixed at the left corner of the mouth, away from the surgeon's hand. A throat pack is placed and announced, recorded where the team will see it, and its removal is a counted item at the end. Protect the eyes carefully, because the surgeon's hands rest on the face. Maintain with propofol and remifentanil or a volatile with remifentanil: the aim is controlled pressure for a dry field and a fast, clear wake-up. Head up 15-30 degrees. A lumbar drain is placed by some surgeons for large suprasellar tumours or anticipated leaks.

### 4. Nasal preparation
**Why:** The mucosa is shrunk with topical vasoconstrictor and injected with local anaesthetic and adrenaline to reduce bleeding and open the corridor.
**Anaesthesia:** Know what has been applied and how much: cocaine, adrenaline in various dilutions, oxymetazoline or phenylephrine, alone or in combination. Systemic absorption causes hypertension, tachycardia and occasionally arrhythmia or myocardial ischaemia, and it happens in the first few minutes. Treat a hypertensive surge by deepening anaesthesia and with remifentanil; avoid a beta-blocker alone after topical phenylephrine or cocaine, since unopposed alpha stimulation with a depressed ventricle has caused pulmonary oedema. In a patient with Cushing's disease or acromegalic cardiomyopathy, ask for the smallest effective dose.

### 5. Nasal and sphenoid phase
**Why:** The turbinates are displaced, the posterior septum is taken down, a septal mucosal flap may be raised and stored, and the front wall of the sphenoid is removed with punches and a drill.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the most stimulating part of the operation, in trigeminal territory, and it comes early. Run remifentanil high (0.2-0.3 micrograms/kg/min) and expect to halve it once the surgeon is in the sinus. A dry field depends on a mean pressure at the low end of the patient's normal range, a slow heart rate, head-up position and unobstructed venous return; deliberate hypotension is not needed and is unwise in a patient whose optic chiasm is already compressed.

### 6. Opening the sella and removing the tumour
**Why:** The sellar floor is drilled away between the two carotid prominences, the dura is opened in a cross, and tumour is curetted out. The surgeon wants the suprasellar part to descend into the sella where it can be reached.
**Anaesthesia:** Stimulation is now minimal and pressure falls: lighten the opioid and support with a vasopressor. Requests at this stage are to help the tumour down: allow the carbon dioxide to rise to 45-50 mmHg, apply a sustained inflation, or inject saline through the lumbar drain, each raising intracranial pressure a little from above. Venous bleeding from the intercavernous sinuses is managed by the surgeon with packing and haemostatic agents and by you with a little more head-up tilt. An arterial jet is a carotid injury: the surgeon packs, you resuscitate with blood through large access, hold pressure up for collateral flow, and the patient goes to the angiography suite for endovascular control. It is rare, and it is the reason for a second large cannula and a group and screen in every case.

### 7. Cerebrospinal fluid leak and reconstruction
**Why:** A tear in the diaphragm is closed with fat or fascia, sealant and often the septal flap, sometimes supported by a balloon or packing. The surgeon may ask for a Valsalva manoeuvre to test the repair.
**Anaesthesia:** Give the sustained inflation on request, to a stated pressure and time. A leak changes the rest of your plan in three ways. Diabetes insipidus is more likely: in a series of 881 patients a visible intraoperative leak raised transient diabetes insipidus from 18.3% overall to 33.3%. The repair must be protected from pressure, so emergence has to be free of coughing and no positive pressure is applied to the nose or face afterwards. And nitrous oxide, if anyone still uses it, is off. A graft site on the abdomen or thigh is infiltrated with long-acting local anaesthetic.

### 8. Packing, pharyngeal toilet and emergence
**Why:** The nose is packed or splinted and the surgeon wants no rise in venous or intracranial pressure that would start bleeding or lift the repair.
**Anaesthesia:** Suction the pharynx under vision, remove the throat pack and say so. The stomach often contains swallowed blood: pass an orogastric tube and empty it, since blood is a potent emetic. Extubate fully awake, sitting up, with the patient warned beforehand that the nose will be blocked and they will breathe through the mouth. Keep remifentanil at a low rate until the tube is out to prevent coughing. If the airway obstructs after extubation, use an oral airway and jaw thrust, and reintubate early if that fails; mask continuous positive pressure and nasal airways are avoided after a skull base repair because air forced through the defect causes tension pneumocephalus. The acromegalic or cushingoid patient with sleep apnoea who normally uses a machine at night cannot use it now, and needs monitored care and a semi-sitting position for the first nights.

### 9. First 48 hours: vision, urine and cortisol
**Why:** The surgeon is watching for a sellar haematoma compressing the chiasm, a cerebrospinal fluid leak, and loss of posterior and anterior pituitary function.
**Anaesthesia:** A deterioration in vision in recovery is a haematoma or an over-packed sella and goes back to the surgeon at once. Diabetes insipidus presents within 24-48 hours as urine output above about 250 mL/h for two or three consecutive hours, dilute urine and a rising sodium; a diuresis of intraoperative fluid, hyperglycaemia and mannitol are the mimics. An awake patient with intact thirst drinks to keep up and is treated with desmopressin 0.5-1 microgram subcutaneously or IV only when the diagnosis is confirmed with paired sodium and osmolalities; 12.4% needed it in the large series, and 2% had permanent disease. Hypotension, nausea and a low sodium or glucose suggest cortisol deficiency and are treated with hydrocortisone without waiting for a result.

### 10. Day 5 to 7: the sodium falls
**Why:** Damaged posterior pituitary axons release stored vasopressin in an uncontrolled way about a week after surgery, sometimes after an initial phase of diabetes insipidus (the triphasic response).
**Anaesthesia:** Delayed hyponatraemia presents after discharge with headache, nausea and lethargy, and is the usual cause of readmission. The discharge instructions therefore include a sodium check at about one week and advice to drink to thirst, not to a target. Treatment is fluid restriction for mild cases and hypertonic saline for symptomatic ones.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is mucosal and thin-bone surgery with a small dural opening, so there is no nerve block to centre the plan on and the strategy is systemic: regular paracetamol, the surgeon's topical and infiltrated local anaesthetic, and small doses of opioid for the first day. Most patients need little.

Paracetamol is started in theatre and continued regularly. NSAIDs are usually withheld for the first days, as author's judgement shared with most surgeons: the cavity is closed, a small haematoma compresses the chiasm, and the nose bleeds. Opioid rescue is oral oxycodone 5 mg or small intravenous increments, with particular restraint in sleep apnoea, where the nasal route is blocked and the patient's own treatment is unavailable. Sphenopalatine ganglion and infraorbital blocks are described and used in some units; the evidence is limited and they are an option, not a default. The fat graft site is infiltrated and is often the site the patient notices most.

Saline nasal care, humidified air and sleeping propped up help the pressure headache more than drugs do. Nose blowing, straining and drinking through a straw are prohibited for weeks because they load the repair.

Escalation: severe headache with visual loss or an ophthalmoplegia is a sellar haematoma or apoplexy of residual tumour. Headache worse on sitting up, with clear salty fluid from the nose or down the throat, is a cerebrospinal fluid leak. Headache with fever and neck stiffness is meningitis. Headache with nausea and lethargy at a week is hyponatraemia. None of these is treated with analgesia first.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Pituitary apoplexy. Haemorrhage or infarction of an adenoma with sudden headache, visual loss, ophthalmoplegia and acute cortisol deficiency. This is the emergency variant: hydrocortisone 100 mg IV before anything else, correction of sodium, and urgent decompression if vision is threatened.
- Cushing's disease. Fragile veins and skin, osteoporotic bones on the table, glucose control, potassium, and a thrombotic risk that continues for weeks after cure. Cortisol falls abruptly after successful surgery and the patient feels unwell until replaced.
- Acromegaly. Airway, sleep apnoea and cardiomyopathy as above. Soft tissue changes regress slowly after cure, so a later anaesthetic is not automatically easier.
- Thyrotroph adenoma. Rendered euthyroid before surgery with somatostatin analogues or antithyroid drugs.
- Extended endonasal approaches for craniopharyngioma, meningioma and clival lesions. Longer, with a certain cerebrospinal fluid leak, a lumbar drain, more diabetes insipidus and closer proximity to the carotids and basilar artery: arterial line and blood available.
- Microscopic sublabial approach. An older route under the upper lip with a nasal speculum, with more facial and dental numbness and the same anaesthetic.
- Transcranial approach. Large tumours with lateral extension are approached by [craniotomy](craniotomy-for-supratentorial-tumour.html).
- Throat pack retention. The classic never event of nasal surgery.
- Raised intracranial pressure is unusual with pituitary tumours; where it exists, the principles in [neuroanaesthesia essentials](../a/a08-neuroanaesthesia-essentials.html) apply.

## Key sources

The anaesthetic approach rests on narrative reviews from high-volume units and on physiology, not on trials. The diabetes insipidus figures come from one large single-centre retrospective series, and the airway figures from one prospective series of 128 patients using direct laryngoscopy, so both are best read as orders of magnitude. Steroid cover follows endocrine guideline practice, and the dose quoted is the common one, not a trial result. There is no PROSPECT guideline for this operation; the analgesic plan, the avoidance of NSAIDs and the prohibition of positive pressure after skull base repair are consensus and author's judgement.
