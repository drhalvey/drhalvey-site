## What the surgeon is doing and why

The appendix is a narrow blind tube off the caecum. When its lumen obstructs (a faecolith, lymphoid swelling, occasionally a tumour) it distends, its wall becomes ischaemic and infected, and over a day or two it progresses from inflamed to gangrenous to perforated. The first pain is visceral and midline, carried by midgut afferents to T10; the pain localises to the right iliac fossa only when the inflamed organ touches parietal peritoneum. A perforation either walls itself off as an abscess or mass, or spills pus through the pelvis and paracolic gutters as generalised peritonitis. The operation removes the source before or after that happens. Nothing is reconstructed.

The laparoscopic operation has become standard in adults and children because it lets the surgeon look first (a normal appendix with an ovarian cyst, a Meckel's diverticulum or terminal ileitis changes the plan), wash every quadrant under vision, and leave wounds that hurt less and infect less. Three ports: umbilical for the camera, and two working ports, usually suprapubic and left iliac fossa. The patient is tilted head down and left side down so the small bowel falls out of the pelvis and off the caecum. The mesoappendix with its artery is divided with diathermy, an energy device or clips; the base is secured with loop ligatures or a stapler; the appendix is bagged and delivered through the umbilicus. Pus is aspirated and the pelvis and right gutter are washed or suctioned.

The open operation survives for the patient who cannot have a pneumoperitoneum, in some pregnancies, in resource-limited settings and as a conversion. A transverse (Lanz) or oblique (gridiron) incision over McBurney's point splits external oblique, internal oblique and transversus along their fibres, the caecum is delivered into the wound and the appendix removed. Muscle is split, not cut, so the wound is less painful than its site suggests, but it is one somatic wound in the territory of T10 to L1 that a unilateral block covers completely.

What changes the operation is the pathology, and the booking form rarely says which. Simple appendicitis is 30-45 minutes. A gangrenous retrocaecal appendix stuck to the lateral wall takes longer and bleeds more. A perforated appendix with four-quadrant pus is a septic patient, a long washout, a drain and several days in hospital. A friable base or a caecal mass may need a stapled partial caecectomy or, rarely, a right hemicolectomy, at which point this is no longer this card.

The pain logic: before surgery the dominant mechanism is visceral and peritoneal, and removing the source improves it, which is why many patients with simple appendicitis feel better the next morning than they did on admission. The operation adds a laparoscopic mechanism (ports, gas, shoulder tip) that peaks in the first 24 hours. Perforated disease leaves inflamed peritoneum behind, an ileus and often a drain, and the pain course is longer and more visceral. The 179-procedure cohort placed appendicectomy among the 25 most painful operations on day 1, which reflects how little analgesia a "minor" emergency case booked at midnight tends to get.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment and resuscitation before induction
**Why:** The surgeon has made a clinical or imaging diagnosis and wants the source out within hours; in simple appendicitis a delay to the next morning's list does not worsen outcome, in generalised peritonitis it does.
**Anaesthesia:** Sort the patient into one of three cases. The well patient with a day of pain: a litre behind, fasted on paper, a full stomach in practice. The sick patient with perforation: febrile, tachycardic, possibly hypotensive, raised lactate, and needing 20-30 mL/kg of crystalloid, antibiotics already given and a vasopressor ready before induction. The special patient: the child, the pregnant woman, the elderly patient in whom the "appendicitis" may be a perforated caecal cancer. Pain relief should not wait for theatre; opioid given on the ward does not obscure the diagnosis.

### 2. Rapid sequence induction and airway
**Why:** The abdomen is inflamed, the gut has stopped and the patient has had opioid.
**Anaesthesia:** Rapid sequence induction with a cuffed tube in adults; in children a controlled rapid sequence with gentle mask ventilation avoids the desaturation that classical apnoea produces. Reduce the induction dose in the septic patient and have the vasopressor drawn up. Rocuronium 1-1.2 mg/kg IV gives intubating conditions and, with sugammadex available, does not commit you to a long block in a 30-minute case; suxamethonium remains reasonable where it is not. An orogastric tube decompresses the stomach for the umbilical port. Give dexamethasone and the antibiotic now if not already given.

### 3. Bladder, ports and pneumoperitoneum
**Why:** The umbilical port goes in by open cut-down; the suprapubic port sits just above the bladder, so the bladder must be empty.
**Anaesthesia:** A catheter or a documented void immediately before theatre. Insufflation produces the same vagal bradycardia as any laparoscopy, more marked in the young and fit: stop the gas, give an anticholinergic, restart slowly. In a hypovolaemic or septic patient the pneumoperitoneum drops venous return before you have finished resuscitating; keep fluid running and treat pressure with a vasopressor in preference to turning down the volatile to nothing.

### 4. Head-down, left-tilt positioning
**Why:** Gravity clears the small bowel from the right iliac fossa and pelvis so the caecum and appendix can be seen.
**Anaesthesia:** Trendelenburg plus gas pushes the diaphragm up: airway pressures rise, the tube can slip endobronchial (recheck by auscultation or capnography shape after the tilt), and end-tidal carbon dioxide climbs. The left arm is tucked, so secure the cannula and its extension where you can reach them. The steep tilt is brief, which is the only reason it is tolerated in the obese and the pregnant; ask for the minimum that gives a view.

### 5. Diagnostic look
**Why:** The surgeon confirms the diagnosis and inspects the pelvis, terminal ileum, ovaries and the rest of the peritoneal cavity; a meaningful minority of clinically diagnosed cases have another or no pathology.
**Anaesthesia:** The duration of the case is decided here. A normal appendix with a gynaecological finding means a short case or a different surgeon. Free pus in four quadrants means an hour more, a drain, a sicker patient in recovery and no day-case discharge. Ask what they have found and adjust opioid, fluid and relaxant to the answer.

### 6. Mesoappendix division and control of the base
**Why:** The appendicular artery is sealed, the appendix is freed to its base on the caecum and the base is closed with loops or a stapler so the stump does not leak.
**Anaesthesia:** A still abdomen while loops are tightened on a friable base. Bleeding is small. If the base is necrotic the surgeon staples across healthy caecum, which adds a few minutes and little else; a decision to resect bowel or convert to a midline incision is the point at which the analgesic plan, the lines and the bed all need revising.

### 7. Extraction, washout and drain
**Why:** The specimen is bagged to keep pus off the wound and delivered through the umbilicus; pus is aspirated and the pelvis irrigated, and a drain is left for an abscess cavity.
**Anaesthesia:** Mobilising pus can produce a transient septic response: a pressure drop, a tachycardia and a temperature rise 10-30 minutes later. Warm the irrigation where possible, because children cool quickly. This is the moment for the surgeon's intraperitoneal local anaesthetic (PROSPECT recommends instillation for laparoscopic appendicectomy) and for port-site infiltration with the fascia included; agree the total dose in mg/kg aloud, particularly in a child, because two operators are now giving local anaesthetic.

### 8. Open or converted operation
**Why:** A right iliac fossa muscle-splitting incision, or a lower midline incision when the laparoscopic view is lost or a resection is needed.
**Anaesthesia:** For a planned open operation PROSPECT recommends a unilateral TAP block before incision, with pre-incisional infiltration as the fallback; an ilioinguinal-iliohypogastric block is the long-standing paediatric equivalent. After an unplanned conversion to a right iliac fossa incision, place the block before waking. A midline conversion is an emergency laparotomy: rectus sheath catheters or an epidural, a PCA and a higher-acuity bed.

### 9. Emergence and recovery
**Why:** The surgeon wants a patient who is comfortable, eating and, for simple disease, home within 24 hours.
**Anaesthesia:** Extubate awake and protect the airway: the stomach was full at the start and may be again. Reverse fully. Recovery pain is ports plus residual peritonitis plus shoulder tip; titrate IV opioid, make sure the NSAID has been given unless the patient is septic with a rising creatinine, and start regular oral paracetamol and NSAID. Children do well on weight-based paracetamol and ibuprofen with oral opioid only for the first day. A persistent tachycardia in recovery is hypovolaemia, sepsis or bleeding from a port site, not pain, until examined.

### 10. The first days
**Why:** Simple appendicitis needs no further antibiotics; complicated disease needs a short course and observation for ileus and collection.
**Anaesthesia:** Simple disease: regular paracetamol and NSAID for three to five days, a small opioid supply for the first 48 hours, home the same or next day. Perforated disease: IV then oral antibiotics, an ileus that makes oral analgesia unreliable for a day or two (IV paracetamol and a PCA or nurse-given opioid), and NSAIDs only when renal function and perfusion are sound. Pain or fever that returns around day 5-7 is a pelvic collection.

## Pain plan

Two mechanisms and two matching plans. For the laparoscopic operation the mechanism is ports, gas and inflamed peritoneum, short-lived and inflammatory, so the plan is anti-inflammatory and local. PROSPECT's 2024 guideline, covering adults and children, recommends paracetamol and an NSAID perioperatively, a conventional three-port laparoscopic approach in preference to open or single-port surgery, instillation of intraperitoneal local anaesthetic, and opioid for rescue only. It found limited evidence for TAP blocks in laparoscopic appendicectomy, for ketamine and for dexamethasone as an analgesic, which is not the same as evidence against them: dexamethasone still earns its place for nausea. Note the contrast with laparoscopic cholecystectomy, where intraperitoneal local anaesthetic is not recommended; the difference is in what the trials for each operation showed, not in the pharmacology.

For the open operation the mechanism is a single muscle-splitting wound in the right lower quadrant, and a unilateral block covers it. PROSPECT recommends a preoperative unilateral TAP block, with pre-incisional wound infiltration as the alternative, on the same paracetamol and NSAID base.

The block for this operation is therefore the strategy: paracetamol 1 g IV (15 mg/kg in children) and an NSAID at induction or as soon as resuscitation allows, dexamethasone 8 mg IV for nausea, short-acting opioid intraoperatively with a modest longer-acting dose before the end, port-site infiltration and intraperitoneal instillation by the surgeon within a stated mg/kg maximum, and oral paracetamol and NSAID afterwards with a few doses of oral opioid. No catheter and no neuraxial technique has a role unless the operation has become a laparotomy.

The NSAID question deserves a deliberate answer in this population. The simple appendicitis patient is young with normal kidneys and should get one. The septic, hypotensive or oliguric patient with perforation should not until perfusion and creatinine are secure; the substitute is a TAP block on both sides, IV paracetamol and a PCA, and this is author's judgement.

Rebound is not a feature. Escalation triggers: pain that is worse on day 2 than day 1, a rigid or distending abdomen, or a heart rate that will not settle is a stump leak, a bleed or a missed alternative diagnosis; pain and fever returning at the end of the first week is a collection.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Perforation with septic shock. This is an emergency laparotomy patient having a laparoscopy: arterial line, noradrenaline, lactate-guided resuscitation, a reduced induction dose, and a critical care discussion before the start. Do not let the name of the operation set the level of care.
- The child. Fluid deficit is proportionally larger; give 10-20 mL/kg boluses before induction. Insufflation pressure 8-10 mmHg, a warming plan for the washout, weight-based local anaesthetic maximum shared between surgeon and anaesthetist, and dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV with ondansetron 0.1 mg/kg IV.
- Pregnancy. The commonest non-obstetric surgical emergency in pregnancy, and a perforation carries a far higher fetal loss than the operation does. Left uterine displacement from 20 weeks, rapid sequence induction, port positions moved up by the surgeon, low insufflation pressure, normocapnia, limited head-down tilt, and NSAIDs avoided in the third trimester. Fetal heart check before and after, with obstetric input on anything more.
- The elderly patient. Later presentation, more perforation, more alternative pathology (caecal carcinoma, diverticulitis), and frailty that turns a short operation into a week in hospital.
- Appendix mass or abscess. Often managed with antibiotics and radiological drainage first; an operation in this setting is longer, bloodier and more likely to convert or resect.
- Non-operative management. Antibiotics alone are an accepted option for uncomplicated appendicitis in adults, with a substantial recurrence rate; the patient who arrives for surgery after failing it has had more days of illness than the history suggests.
- Negative laparoscopy and gynaecological findings. A normal appendix is usually still removed; an ovarian torsion or ectopic pregnancy changes the surgeon and occasionally the urgency.
- Suxamethonium myalgia and anaphylaxis. A young muscular adult mobilising the same day is the patient most troubled by suxamethonium pains; rocuronium with sugammadex avoids them at the cost of its own anaphylaxis risk.
- Day-case appendicectomy. Feasible for simple disease operated in daylight hours with a multimodal regimen and antiemetic prophylaxis; the limiting factors are the time the operation finishes and nausea.

## Key sources

The PROSPECT appendicectomy guideline (2024) is the anchor and is recent, but it rests on fewer and smaller trials than the cholecystectomy guideline, and several interventions that are plausible (TAP block for laparoscopic surgery, dexamethasone as an analgesic) sit in its "limited evidence" category. The advice on the septic patient, on withholding NSAIDs until perfusion is restored, and on pregnancy and children is standard practice and author's judgement and is unsourced. No trial tells you whether rapid sequence induction is necessary for every appendicectomy; treating the stomach as full is consensus.
