## What the surgeon is doing and why

Minimal access surgery in children uses the same idea as in adults (a working space made with gas, a camera and long instruments) in a much smaller box. The reasons for doing it are the adult ones: smaller wounds, less pain, less adhesion formation and, in the chest, avoidance of a thoracotomy that in a growing child carries a risk of scoliosis and chest wall deformity. The list of operations is long: fundoplication and gastrostomy (often in a neurologically impaired child with reflux and poor lungs), pyeloplasty, nephrectomy, splenectomy, cholecystectomy, pull-through for Hirschsprung disease, appendicectomy and herniotomy in the abdomen; decortication for empyema, lung biopsy, resection of congenital lung lesions, mediastinal cysts, and in the neonate repair of oesophageal atresia and diaphragmatic hernia in the chest.

What is common to all of them is how the space is made. In the abdomen, carbon dioxide is insufflated through an umbilical port placed by open cut-down, to a pressure the surgeon keeps as low as the view allows. In the chest there are two ways to move the lung out of the way: stop ventilating it (lung isolation), or push it down with carbon dioxide at low pressure (a capnothorax) while both lungs continue to be ventilated; in infants the second is often the only practical one, and the two are frequently combined.

The physics is unfavourable in a small child. The abdominal wall is thin and compliant, so a low pressure gives a large dome, but the diaphragm is the main muscle of breathing, the functional residual capacity is small and close to closing volume, and oxygen consumption per kilogram is twice an adult's. A few mmHg of intra-abdominal pressure therefore has a proportionately larger effect on tidal volume and on venous return. The peritoneal and pleural surfaces are large relative to body mass and thin, so carbon dioxide is absorbed quickly. The trachea is 4-5 cm long in an infant, so a tube that is correctly placed can be endobronchial after a 1 cm shift of the carina.

```
 Gas in the abdomen or chest of a small child
        |
        +--> diaphragm splinted --> tidal volume and compliance fall
        |
        +--> carina moves cephalad --> tube tip enters right bronchus
        |
        +--> CO2 absorbed --> end-tidal CO2 rises, minute volume must rise
        |
        +--> vena cava and heart compressed --> stroke volume falls,
        |                                       resistance rises
        +--> open vein (umbilical in neonates) --> gas embolism
```

The pain logic: ports and gas, a 3 with infiltration and simple analgesia, worst on day 1 and largely gone by day 3. Thoracoscopy adds intercostal nerve pressure at each port and a chest drain, which is what the regional technique is for. An operation that is converted to open, or that needs a sizeable extraction wound, is re-scored as an abdominal-wall or chest-wall incision.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment and the plan with the surgeon
**Why:** The surgeon chooses ports, pressure, position and whether lung collapse will be by gas, isolation or both; those choices are the anaesthetic.

**Anaesthesia:** Ask four things: the position, the expected duration, the chance of conversion, and for the chest whether lung isolation is wanted or carbon dioxide alone will do. Look for the conditions that make gas dangerous: congenital heart disease with a shunt (paradoxical embolism, and a pulmonary vascular resistance that rises with carbon dioxide), pulmonary hypertension, raised intracranial pressure or a ventriculoperitoneal shunt, and severe lung disease in the ex-premature infant. The child for fundoplication is frequently a child with cerebral palsy, seizures, recurrent aspiration and scoliosis: optimise the chest and plan postoperative respiratory support. A recent upper respiratory infection matters more before one-lung ventilation than before a hernia.

### 2. Induction, airway and lines
**Why:** Nothing surgical yet.

**Anaesthesia:** A cuffed tracheal tube for all cases: it seals at the higher airway pressures that insufflation demands and can be adjusted without changing the tube. Place it deliberately high (tip at mid-trachea), note the depth, and tape for a patient who will be turned and tilted. Two cannulae before draping, because the limbs disappear. Pass an orogastric tube to deflate the stomach before the first port, and empty the bladder for pelvic work. An arterial line for neonatal thoracoscopy, for one-lung ventilation expected to last over an hour or two, and for any child with cardiac or significant lung disease. Neuromuscular block is maintained throughout: a compliant, relaxed wall is what allows a low insufflation pressure.

### 3. Positioning
**Why:** The surgeon stands where the instruments point: at the feet for upper abdominal work with the infant across the table, at the side for the chest, with screens over the head.

**Anaesthesia:** After the final position and before drapes: listen to both lungs, confirm the capnograph and the tube depth, check that the circuit is supported and that you can still reach a cannula and see the face or at least the tube. In the lateral position use an axillary roll and protect the dependent ear and eye. Small children slide on tilting tables; they are strapped and padded, and the tilt is tested before preparation. Forced-air warming is applied to whatever skin remains.

### 4. First port and insufflation
**Why:** Open (Hasson) entry at the umbilicus is standard in children because the viscera, aorta and bladder lie millimetres under the wall; gas then goes in at low flow to 6-12 mmHg according to size.

**Anaesthesia:** Watch four things as the gas goes in. Heart rate: peritoneal stretch causes vagal bradycardia, treated by desufflation and atropine 20 micrograms/kg IV. Capnograph: an abrupt fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide with hypotension and desaturation is gas embolism; in neonates the umbilical vein and ductus venosus may still be patent and the umbilical port sits next to them. Ask for immediate desufflation, give 100% oxygen, tilt head-down and left side down, and support the circulation. Airway pressure and tidal volume: in infants at maximal pressure, tidal volume fell by a third, compliance halved and peak pressure rose by 18%, and nine in ten needed a ventilator change. Breath sounds: the carina has moved up, so listen on the left.

### 5. Established pneumoperitoneum
**Why:** The surgeon works for one to three hours at the lowest pressure that gives a view, and will ask for more relaxation before asking for more pressure.

**Anaesthesia:** Raise the respiratory rate, not the tidal volume, to hold end-tidal carbon dioxide; absorbed gas accounted for 10-20% of expired carbon dioxide within 10 minutes in children. Use pressure-controlled ventilation with PEEP of about 5 cmH2O to oppose basal collapse. Circulation: in healthy infants at 10 mmHg, aortic flow and stroke volume fell to about two-thirds of baseline and vascular resistance rose by 60%, with no change in arterial pressure, all reversing on desufflation. A healthy heart tolerates this; an infant with a cardiac lesion or hypovolaemia may not, and blood pressure will not warn you. Give a fluid bolus before insufflation in the fasted infant, and agree a pressure ceiling with the surgeon. Urine output falls with raised intra-abdominal pressure and recovers afterwards. Subcutaneous emphysema with a climbing carbon dioxide means a port has slipped into the wall.

### 6. Tilt
**Why:** Head-up for the hiatus, spleen and gallbladder; head-down for the pelvis; lateral roll for the kidney. Gravity is the retractor.

**Anaesthesia:** Head-down adds to the diaphragmatic splinting and pushes the tube towards or into the right main bronchus: a slow fall in saturation with a rise in airway pressure after tilting is endobronchial intubation, and the treatment is to withdraw the tube 0.5-1 cm, not to increase oxygen. Head-up in a small child with a pneumoperitoneum reduces venous return further. Pressure areas and straps are rechecked after every change.

### 7. Thoracoscopy: making the space
**Why:** With the child lateral, the first port goes in below the scapular tip and the lung must fall away. In infants the surgeon insufflates carbon dioxide at 4-6 mmHg and about 1 L/min, which compresses the lung even while it is ventilated; in older children the lung is isolated and gas is used briefly or not at all.

**Anaesthesia:** The infant in the lateral position behaves differently from the adult. The soft chest wall cannot hold the dependent lung open against the weight of the mediastinum, functional residual capacity is near closing volume, and the hydrostatic gradient that directs blood to the dependent lung in an adult is small in a small chest. So ventilation and perfusion match poorly with the healthy lung down, and desaturation comes earlier (see [one-lung ventilation](../a/a03-one-lung-ventilation.html)). A capnothorax adds positive intrapleural pressure: venous return and cardiac output fall as in a tension pneumothorax if pressure or flow is too high. Introduce the gas slowly, watch the arterial pressure, and treat hypotension by asking for the pressure to be reduced before reaching for a vasopressor.

### 8. Lung isolation by size
**Why:** For lobectomy, long mediastinal dissection or a wet lung (empyema, bronchiectasis), the surgeon needs a still, collapsed lung and the other lung needs protection from pus and blood.

**Anaesthesia:** No device fits every age.

```
 Age or size            Usual method
 ---------------------  ------------------------------------------------
 Under about 2 years    mainstem intubation of the ventilated side, or a
                        5 French blocker passed outside the tracheal tube
 About 2-8 years        bronchial blocker beside or inside the tube
 About 6 years upward   Univent tube (large outer diameter limits its use)
 From 8 years or 30 kg  double-lumen tube, 26 French the smallest
```

Mainstem intubation is simple and gives no access to the operated lung for suction or oxygen, and the right upper lobe is easily occluded. An extraluminal 5 French blocker placed under bronchoscopic vision isolated the lung in 14 of 15 children under two in one series, with hypercarbia in most. Every method is positioned with a fibrescope small enough for the tube (2.2 mm for the smallest) and checked again after turning. Blockers dislodge into the trachea with surgical traction: sudden loss of ventilation with high pressures is a blocker across the carina, and the treatment is to deflate it. During one-lung ventilation use 100% oxygen to start, pressure control, small tidal volumes and a higher rate; accept an end-tidal carbon dioxide in the 50s to 60s mmHg in a child without cardiac disease or pulmonary hypertension.

### 9. Working in the chest: carbon dioxide, pH and communication
**Why:** The surgeon dissects for one to three hours with the lung held down by gas, a retractor or isolation.

**Anaesthesia:** Carbon dioxide is absorbed across the pleura and eliminated through a reduced lung volume, so arterial carbon dioxide climbs and the end-tidal value underestimates it. In the neonatal pilot trial, thoracoscopic repair produced an intraoperative PaCO2 of 83 mmHg against 61 mmHg open and a pH of 7.13 against 7.24, worst in diaphragmatic hernia. Measure a gas where you have an arterial line; where you do not, treat a rising end-tidal trend as the tip of it. Agree in advance that you will ask for pauses: desufflation and two-lung ventilation for a few minutes restores saturation and clears carbon dioxide. Severe acidosis that does not respond to pauses is a reason to convert, and it is yours to raise.

### 10. Bleeding and conversion
**Why:** A torn splenic vessel, pulmonary artery branch or azygos vein cannot always be controlled through ports, and the surgeon opens.

**Anaesthesia:** Conversion happens fast and the child is already tilted, cold-prone and without easy access. Blood should have been grouped and, for splenectomy and lung resection, crossmatched. Give fluid and blood by mL/kg (see [major haemorrhage](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html)), return to two-lung ventilation if the surgeon can tolerate it, and re-plan analgesia for a thoracotomy or laparotomy: this is when the selective catheter in the block plan is placed, at the end of surgery.

### 11. Desufflation, re-expansion and closure
**Why:** Gas is released, ports are removed under vision, and in the chest a drain is placed and the lung re-expanded under the camera.

**Anaesthesia:** Ask the surgeon to empty the abdomen fully: residual carbon dioxide under the diaphragm is the source of shoulder-tip pain in older children. Re-expand the collapsed lung with sustained manual inflations under vision and suction the tube. Remove or deflate a blocker before the stump or bronchus is tested. The surgeon infiltrates ports to a shared ceiling of 2 mg/kg levobupivacaine and, in the chest, can place intercostal blocks under direct vision at and around the port spaces. End-tidal carbon dioxide stays high for some minutes after desufflation as tissue stores wash out; keep ventilating until it settles before reversing the relaxant.

### 12. Emergence and the first night
**Why:** The surgeon expects early feeding and mobilisation, which is the point of the approach.

**Anaesthesia:** Extubate awake once warm, reversed and normocapnic. Infants after long pneumoperitoneum or one-lung ventilation may need high-flow oxygen for some hours; neonates after thoracoscopic repair return to intensive care ventilated. Regular paracetamol and ibuprofen, with oral or intravenous opioid as rescue; a chest drain is the main reason for opioid after thoracoscopy and its removal is the point at which requirement falls.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is port wounds with peritoneal or pleural irritation: moderate, front-loaded, and largely over in three days. A 3 on the scale, with the plan to match: local anaesthetic at the ports, paracetamol 15 mg/kg, an NSAID from three months of age if renal function and the surgery allow, dexamethasone, and small doses of opioid titrated in recovery.

For laparoscopy, port-site infiltration by the surgeon is the default. The one paediatric randomised trial that added transversus abdominis plane blocks to infiltration (in laparoscopic appendicectomy) found no clinically important benefit, and there is no reason to expect a different answer for other laparoscopic operations with small ports. A fascial plane block earns its place when there is an extraction incision (splenectomy through a Pfannenstiel wound, nephrectomy) and a caudal is useful for pelvic and lower abdominal laparoscopy in infants. These are author's judgements by extension; there is no PROSPECT guideline for any paediatric laparoscopic procedure.

For thoracoscopy the intercostal nerve is the target. Options are a single-shot paravertebral or erector spinae plane block before incision, or intercostal blocks by the surgeon under vision; the choice between them is local skill and not evidence. A catheter (paravertebral, erector spinae, or thoracic epidural in the older child) is selective: for a planned or converted thoracotomy, for decortication of empyema where the pleura is inflamed and a drain will stay for days, and for long resections. In the registry of more than 100,000 paediatric blocks, serious complications were rare and catheter problems were mostly benign failures. Doses are by weight and infants are the group in whom toxicity occurs. Pectus surgery, which is the most painful thoracoscopic operation in children, has its own card ([pectus repair](pectus-repair.html)).

Rebound is moderate after a single-shot paravertebral or erector spinae block for thoracoscopy, when it wears off on the first night with a drain in place: chart regular simple analgesia from recovery and an opioid rescue. Escalation triggers: disproportionate pain with fever and tachycardia after fundoplication or pyeloplasty (leak), and increasing oxygen requirement with pain after thoracoscopy (retained collection, pneumothorax after drain removal).

## Pitfalls and variants

- Endobronchial intubation. The commonest cause of desaturation after insufflation or head-down tilt in a small child. Confirm by auscultation and withdraw.
- Gas embolism. Rare, mostly neonatal and at the start of insufflation; sudden loss of end-tidal carbon dioxide with collapse. Desufflate first.
- Capnothorax during laparoscopy. Gas tracks through the hiatus or a diaphragmatic defect during fundoplication: rising airway pressure, falling saturation, and a bulging diaphragm on the screen. Usually resolves with desufflation and PEEP because carbon dioxide absorbs quickly; a drain is seldom needed.
- Hypothermia and hyperthermia. Cold, dry insufflation gas cools infants over a long case; a draped toddler under forced air with little exposed skin can overheat. Measure core temperature.
- The neurologically impaired child for fundoplication. Poor cough, scoliosis, seizures, baclofen pumps and long operations: the pulmonary complication rate is determined by the patient, and postoperative high-dependency care is planned in advance.
- Congenital heart disease. Raised intra-abdominal or intrathoracic pressure and hypercapnia raise pulmonary vascular resistance and reduce preload; single-ventricle circulations with passive pulmonary flow tolerate this worst. These children are done in a cardiac centre at the lowest pressures or open.
- Ventriculoperitoneal shunt. Pneumoperitoneum raises intracranial pressure; a functioning shunt usually tolerates low pressure, and a child with a recent shunt problem does not.
- Empyema. The diseased lung is wet: protect the good lung by isolation or by position, and expect bleeding from decortication.
- Neonatal thoracoscopy. Hypercapnia and acidosis exceed those of open repair; see [neonatal emergencies](neonatal-emergencies.html). The general physiology of pneumoperitoneum is in [laparoscopy, robotics and the pneumoperitoneum](../a/a01-laparoscopy-pneumoperitoneum.html) and weight-based detail in [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).

## Key sources

The physiology in this card comes from small, careful observational studies in infants and children: measured pulmonary mechanics at stated pressures, echo-Doppler haemodynamics at 10 mmHg, and isotopic measurement of carbon dioxide absorption. They agree with each other and with adult physiology scaled down. The only randomised trial is the neonatal pilot of thoracoscopic against open repair, with gas tensions as its outcome. Lung isolation methods by age come from a descriptive review and a case series; the age and size thresholds in the table are conventional and overlap. Insufflation pressures by age, the capnothorax pressures, and the management of embolism and endobronchial intubation are standard paediatric practice and are stated without a source. The explanation of why infants desaturate in the lateral position is textbook physiology. Block recommendations are author's judgement.
