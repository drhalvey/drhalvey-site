## What the surgeon is doing and why

The disease is the same as on the bypass card: an artery narrowed or blocked by atheroma, and a leg that is either short of flow on walking (claudication) or short of flow at rest (chronic limb-threatening ischaemia, with night pain, ulcers or gangrene). The endovascular solution is to reopen the patient's own artery from inside instead of building a new route around it. A wire is passed through or around the lesion, a balloon is inflated to crack the plaque and stretch the wall, and where the artery recoils or dissects a stent is left to hold it open. Nothing is cut. The whole procedure goes through a needle hole.

That simplicity is why endovascular treatment is the first approach for most patients, and the trial evidence broadly supports it in the frail. In BASIL-2, patients with limb-threatening ischaemia needing below-knee revascularisation did better with endovascular treatment first than with vein bypass first, mainly because fewer died. In BEST-CLI, patients fit for surgery with a good vein did better with bypass. The patients sent for angioplasty therefore include the least fit of the vascular population: those considered too high-risk for a four-hour bypass. The procedure is minor. The patient is not.

The operator's steps determine what the patient feels and what you have to provide.

Access is usually the common femoral artery, either the opposite groin with the wire taken up and over the aortic bifurcation, or the same groin pointing down the leg (antegrade). Brachial, radial and retrograde pedal or tibial punctures are used when the groin route fails. The puncture is the only wound.

Crossing the lesion can take two minutes or two hours. A short stenosis is crossed with a wire directly. A long occlusion may need the wire to be steered within the wall (subintimal) and brought back into the true lumen below, or approached from the foot as well as from above. During this phase the operator needs road-map imaging: a contrast image is stored and live fluoroscopy overlaid on it, which only works if the leg has not moved by a millimetre since the image was taken.

Treatment is balloon inflation for 1-3 minutes at a time, repeated along the lesion, with or without drug coating, atherectomy to debulk calcified plaque, or stents. Balloon inflation stretches the adventitia, where the artery's nociceptors are, and causes a deep, aching, well-localised pain for as long as the balloon is up. Mild pain is expected and tells the operator the balloon is sized to the vessel; severe pain is a warning of impending rupture, which is one reason operators prefer a patient who can report it.

Closure is by a collagen plug or suture device, or by manual pressure for 10-20 minutes followed by hours of flat bed rest.

The pain logic is unlike any surgical card. The procedure itself produces almost none: a local anaesthetic sting, a few episodes of balloon ache, and a bruised groin afterwards. The pain problem is what the patient brings. Ischaemic rest pain is relieved by hanging the leg down and made worse by lying flat with the leg elevated to heart level, which is the position the procedure demands for hours. Add a hard narrow table, kyphosis, heart failure, a chronic cough and often delirium or dementia, and the task is to keep a frail person in pain motionless without stopping them breathing. The anaesthetic plan is built for that problem.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment and the choice between sedation, block and general anaesthesia
**Why:** The operator wants a still leg and, ideally, a patient who can report pain during ballooning and hold a breath for iliac imaging.
**Anaesthesia:** Three questions decide the technique. Can the patient lie flat with the leg straight for the expected time: ask them to do it on the trolley for five minutes. How long will it take: an iliac stent is 45 minutes, a long tibial recanalisation is 3 hours. Can the patient cooperate. Where the answers are yes, short and yes, operator local anaesthesia with minimal sedation is sufficient and many such cases need no anaesthetist. Where rest pain prevents lying flat, a single-shot popliteal sciatic block with ropivacaine 0.2% 20 mL, without adjuncts, removes the foot and calf pain for the procedure and the first hours of bed rest and usually converts a restless patient into a still one; it is a superficial, compressible site, compatible with antiplatelets and procedural heparin, and it is the author's preferred solution. The low concentration is deliberate. The block is placed immediately before a long occlusion is reopened, and a dense block lasting 12 hours or more would sit across the whole reperfusion window and hide both distal embolisation and a compartment syndrome; this is the same rule as on the [embolectomy and fasciotomy](embolectomy-and-fasciotomy-for-acute-limb.html) card. A higher concentration is kept for the patient in whom the weaker block has failed, and that decision is handed over. A limb that is acutely ischaemic, with sensory loss or weakness, gets no block. Where the patient is confused, cannot lie flat for cardiorespiratory reasons, or the case is long, a planned general anaesthetic with a supraglottic airway is safer than deep sedation at a distance. A registry comparison in emergency infrainguinal procedures found no difference in mortality or limb outcomes between local and general anaesthesia, so the choice can be made on these practical grounds.

### 2. Preparation: kidneys, antithrombotics and the room
**Why:** The operator will use iodinated contrast and heparin, and the patient is usually on antiplatelets with an anticoagulant in a proportion.
**Anaesthesia:** Know the eGFR. The measures with a physiological basis are avoiding hypovolaemia and hypotension and limiting contrast volume; routine intravenous hydration did not reduce contrast nephropathy in the AMACING trial of patients with eGFR 30-59, and caused complications in 5.5%, so do not fluid-load a patient with heart failure for the kidneys' sake. Below an eGFR of 30 the trial does not apply and cautious hydration remains usual. Carbon dioxide angiography is the operator's option in severe renal impairment and is painful in the leg, which may change the sedation plan. Insulin-treated diabetic patients are fasted and often late on the list: check glucose on arrival. Set up so that you can reach the airway: the image intensifier, the lead screens and the length of the table place you at the patient's head with the operator at the hip, and the lines need extensions.

### 3. Position and sedation
**Why:** The patient lies flat with legs straight, feet sometimes taped in internal rotation to separate the tibial vessels on the image.
**Anaesthesia:** Pad the heels, the sacrum and the occiput; ischaemic heels ulcerate from a few hours on a hard table. A pillow under the knees is usually refused because it changes the image geometry, so ask. Sedation aims at a calm, rousable patient who breathes without help: remifentanil 0.02-0.05 micrograms/kg/min suits balloon pain and rest pain well and stops quickly; dexmedetomidine 0.2-0.7 micrograms/kg/h suits the agitated patient at the cost of bradycardia and hypotension; small doses of midazolam alone often produce disinhibition in the elderly. Capnography is mandatory. The patient's usual opioid should have been given that morning, since withdrawal of a regular dose is a common cause of restlessness on the table.

### 4. Arterial puncture and sheath
**Why:** The common femoral artery is punctured over the femoral head, under ultrasound, so that it can be compressed against bone afterwards. A puncture too high enters the external iliac above the inguinal ligament, where bleeding tracks into the retroperitoneum and cannot be compressed.
**Anaesthesia:** Lidocaine 1% 10-15 mL by the operator. Heparin 3,000-5,000 units is given once the sheath is in. An antegrade puncture in an obese patient, with the pannus retracted, is uncomfortable and slow. Vasovagal bradycardia and hypotension at puncture are common in anxious patients: atropine 300-600 micrograms IV and a fluid bolus.

### 5. Crossing the lesion
**Why:** A hydrophilic wire and support catheter are worked through the stenosis or occlusion under road-map imaging.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the long, quiet phase and the one in which movement costs most, because each lost road map means more contrast and more radiation. It is also when sedation drifts deeper as stimulation is low. Keep verbal contact. If the patient is becoming unmanageable at 60 minutes with the lesion uncrossed, say so and convert deliberately to general anaesthesia; the worse option is repeated boluses in the dark.

### 6. Balloon angioplasty, atherectomy and stenting
**Why:** The balloon is inflated to its nominal pressure for 1-3 minutes, sometimes several times along a long lesion. Stents are placed for flow-limiting dissection or recoil, and primarily in the iliac arteries. Atherectomy devices shave or sand calcified plaque and shed debris downstream.
**Anaesthesia:** Warn the patient before each inflation and give a small opioid bolus or increase the remifentanil a minute ahead for long inflations. Pain that is severe, sharp or persists after deflation should be reported to the operator at once: it suggests rupture or dissection. Iliac rupture is the dangerous one, because bleeding is retroperitoneal and brisk: hypotension, back or flank pain and an urge to move. The operator re-inflates the balloon across the tear and places a covered stent, while you call for help, blood and large-bore access. Femoropopliteal and tibial perforations usually seal with balloon tamponade and heparin reversal with protamine. Atherectomy and thrombectomy devices can cause distal embolisation with sudden foot pain, and some cause haemolysis and bradycardia.

### 7. Completion angiogram and closure
**Why:** Final images confirm flow to the foot. The sheath is removed and the puncture sealed with a closure device or manual pressure.
**Anaesthesia:** Manual compression for 10-20 minutes over the femoral artery is painful and vagal. Lighten sedation toward the end so that the patient can be told to keep the leg straight, and avoid coughing or straining on emergence from general anaesthesia: a smooth removal of the supraglottic airway protects the puncture. Ask the operator for the contrast volume and the heparin and antiplatelet plan for the handover.

### 8. Recovery: flat, still and observed
**Why:** The artery has a hole in it and the patient is anticoagulated.
**Anaesthesia:** Flat for 2-6 hours depending on sheath size and closure method. The complications arrive in this window and present to recovery staff and to you. Groin haematoma is visible. Retroperitoneal haemorrhage is not: unexplained hypotension, tachycardia (absent in beta-blocked patients), flank, back or lower abdominal pain, and a falling haemoglobin after a femoral puncture is retroperitoneal bleeding until a CT says otherwise. A newly painful, pale foot is acute thrombosis or embolism and goes back to the operator. Reperfusion of a previously ischaemic foot causes burning pain, warmth and swelling for a few days and is a sign of success.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a needle puncture, so the analgesic strategy for the procedure is the operator's local anaesthetic and nothing more is required afterwards beyond paracetamol. The plan that needs thought is for the pre-existing ischaemic pain, during the procedure and for the hours of flat bed rest that follow.

For the patient without rest pain (the claudicant, the day case), minimal or no sedation and paracetamol. For the patient with rest pain, continue the regular opioid on the morning of the procedure, and choose at the start between a popliteal sciatic block and titrated remifentanil. The block is the author's default when the pain is in the foot or calf and the patient is unlikely to tolerate the table: it treats the cause of the restlessness, has no respiratory effect, and covers the post-procedure bed rest. Its cost is that the patient cannot report foot pain from distal embolisation or the calf pain of a compartment syndrome, and that reperfusion cannot be judged by symptoms until it wears off, so use the low concentration without adjuncts, tell the operator, rely on the angiogram and Doppler, and hand over hourly checks of the calf for tense swelling and pain on passive toe movement after a long occlusion has been reopened. This recommendation is author's judgement; there are no comparative trials.

NSAIDs are avoided on the day because of the contrast load and the anticoagulation. Opioid requirement after the procedure is the patient's baseline; successful revascularisation reduces rest pain over the following day or two, and the opioid can be weaned from there.

Rebound in the block sense is low: when the sciatic block wears off, the leg has usually been reperfused and the rest pain is less than before. Escalation triggers are diagnostic. Groin, flank or back pain with haemodynamic change is bleeding. Sudden foot pain with pallor is embolism or acute occlusion. A tense, painful calf after reopening a long-occluded vessel is compartment syndrome until the surgeon has examined it; simple reperfusion swelling is the commoner explanation and is the diagnosis only after that examination.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Deep sedation by increments. The commonest route to a critical incident in the angiography suite: no single dose is unreasonable, and the total produces obstruction or apnoea in a dark room with the patient's head under an image intensifier. Decide the ceiling at the start.
- Remote site. Angiography suites are often distant from theatres, with unfamiliar equipment and no anaesthetic assistant unless one is brought. Check suction, oxygen, the tilting function of the table and where the difficult airway equipment is before starting.
- Iliac work. Iliac angioplasty and stenting carry the rupture risk and need breath-holds for imaging; group and screen, and two cannulae.
- Brachial and radial access. Used when both groins are hostile. A brachial sheath haematoma compresses the median nerve within its fascial compartment: hand pain or numbness after a brachial puncture is a surgical problem, not an analgesic one.
- Pedal and tibial retrograde access. A puncture at the ankle or dorsum of the foot is painful and reflexly withdrawn from; a sciatic block or an ankle block makes it possible.
- Catheter-directed thrombolysis. For acute or subacute occlusion, an infusion catheter is left in the clot and the patient returns for check angiograms over 24-48 hours while on alteplase and heparin. All neuraxial and deep blocks are contraindicated, intramuscular injections are avoided, and any new headache or neurological sign is an intracranial bleed until imaged.
- Hybrid procedures. Angioplasty combined with femoral endarterectomy or bypass is planned as the open operation; see [lower limb bypass and femoral endarterectomy](lower-limb-bypass-and-femoral-endarterectomy.html).
- Dementia and delirium. Common, and predictive of failure of sedation. A short general anaesthetic with a supraglottic airway and a low-concentration sciatic block is kinder and safer than restraint and repeated midazolam.
- Dialysis patients. The contrast is removed at the next session and fluid volume is the main concern, but a dialysing patient who still passes urine has residual function worth protecting, as on the [arteriovenous fistula](arteriovenous-fistula-formation.html) card, so the contrast volume is limited and hypotension avoided all the same. Protect the fistula arm.
- The emergency variant. Acute limb ischaemia treated endovascularly (aspiration thrombectomy or thrombolysis) follows the same technique in a sicker patient with a threatened limb; if the limb is paralysed and insensate the patient needs open surgery and the considerations on [embolectomy and fasciotomy for acute limb ischaemia](embolectomy-and-fasciotomy-for-acute-limb.html). The emergency within an elective case is iliac rupture, managed as above, with conversion to open repair if a covered stent fails.

## Key sources

The trials tell you who is on the table: BASIL-2 and BEST-CLI between them direct the frailer patient with limb-threatening ischaemia toward endovascular treatment. The evidence on anaesthetic technique is limited to registry comparisons, which find no outcome difference between local and general anaesthesia and are confounded by case complexity. The contrast advice rests on a single-centre randomised trial that excluded the highest-risk kidneys. Nothing has been published of any quality on analgesia for peripheral angioplasty; the sciatic block recommendation and the warning about incremental sedation are the author's judgement from the mechanism of rest pain.
