## What the surgeon is doing and why

The open operation survives for the uterus that will not come out any other way: one enlarged by fibroids to the size of a 16-week pregnancy or more, one fixed by endometriosis or adhesions, one in a woman who cannot tolerate hours of head-down pneumoperitoneum, or one in a unit without the laparoscopic skills. The anatomy is as on the [laparoscopic hysterectomy](laparoscopic-and-robotic-hysterectomy.html) card: the uterus is freed by dividing its attachments from the top down (round ligaments, adnexal pedicles, broad ligament, uterine arteries, cardinal and uterosacral ligaments) and then cutting around the top of the vagina. A subtotal hysterectomy stops at the uterine arteries and amputates the body from the cervix, which is quicker and spares the bladder dissection.

What differs is the way in. A Pfannenstiel incision is a transverse cut two fingers above the pubis through skin and rectus sheath, with the sheath lifted off the muscles and the recti separated in the midline. It heals well and hurts less than a vertical incision, but it gives a limited window, and it divides or stretches the iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves at its lateral ends. A uterus too large for it needs a midline incision, sometimes carried above the umbilicus. A self-retaining retractor then holds the wound open under tension for the duration, the bowel is packed into the upper abdomen with large swabs, and the table is tilted head down.

Myomectomy is the uterus-preserving alternative for a woman who wants to keep her fertility or her uterus. The surgeon incises the myometrium over each fibroid, finds the plane between the fibroid and its pseudocapsule, shells it out and closes the cavity in layers. Nothing is devascularised first: the uterine arteries stay intact, and each enucleation bed bleeds until it is sutured. So the surgeon reduces inflow temporarily, by injecting dilute vasopressin into the myometrium, by tying a tourniquet around the lower uterus and sometimes the ovarian pedicles, or both, and works against the clock. The more fibroids, the more incisions, the more blood, and occasionally the operation ends as a hysterectomy, which is in the consent for that reason.

The pain logic is the abdominal wall. Skin, sheath and peritoneum have been cut and then stretched by a retractor, and that wound is loaded by coughing, sitting up, getting out of bed and straining for days. It is somatic, movement-evoked and well covered by anything that blocks the T10-L1 segments. Underneath is a visceral wound (pedicles, vault, or a sutured uterus that cramps) that truncal blocks do not reach and intrathecal morphine does. That split is the whole argument for the plan: the incision earns a 4, a Pfannenstiel sits at the lower end of it and a long midline at the upper end, and no recent PROSPECT publication was found for the open operation, so the plan rests on individual trials and meta-analyses.

## Steps

### 1. Before the day: haemoglobin, size and incision
**Why:** Women come to this operation because they bleed. The surgeon chooses the incision on uterine size and mobility and may have pre-treated with a GnRH analogue to stop menstruation and shrink the fibroids.
**Anaesthesia:** Iron-deficiency anaemia is the modifiable risk: a haemoglobin below 100 g/L going into a myomectomy that may lose a litre is a reason to treat with intravenous iron and move the date, not to cross-match more. Ask which incision is planned, because it changes the analgesic plan, and how many fibroids are on the imaging, because it predicts the blood loss. A very large uterus behaves like a mid-trimester pregnancy: it can compress the vena cava when supine, obstruct the ureters and splint the diaphragm.

### 2. Spinal, then induction
**Why:** The surgeon needs a relaxed abdominal wall, an empty bladder and a still field for one to three hours.
**Anaesthesia:** The author's default is a spinal before induction with intrathecal morphine 150-200 micrograms, with or without bupivacaine, then general anaesthesia with a tracheal tube. In the dose-finding trial for this operation, 200 micrograms reduced 24-hour PCA morphine more than 100 micrograms and 300 micrograms added nothing. Adding hyperbaric bupivacaine 0.5% 2-2.5 mL gives a dense intraoperative block and a lower anaesthetic requirement at the price of more hypotension at induction; omit it in the patient likely to bleed. Paracetamol 1 g, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor (withheld at myomectomy until haemostasis is secure, by many surgeons' preference), dexamethasone 8 mg IV, antibiotics and TXA go in before incision. Site the second cannula now.

### 3. Incision, retractor and packing
**Why:** Pfannenstiel or midline entry, a self-retaining retractor to hold the wound open, wet packs to lift the bowel out of the pelvis, and head-down tilt.
**Anaesthesia:** Peritoneal traction and bowel packing are vagal stimuli: bradycardia here is common and stops when the surgeon pauses. Give the relaxant before the request for it. The lateral blades of a self-retaining retractor in a transverse incision can compress the femoral nerve against the pelvic side wall, more so in thin patients and long cases; it is the surgeon's responsibility to check blade depth, and a postoperative weak quadriceps is that, not the spinal. Tilt reduces functional residual capacity; add PEEP.

### 4. Upper pedicles and the broad ligament
**Why:** Round ligaments, then tube and utero-ovarian ligament or the infundibulopelvic ligament if the ovaries are being removed, are clamped, cut and tied; the broad ligament is opened.
**Anaesthesia:** Steady venous ooze and the first measurable loss. Traction on the uterus and the infundibulopelvic ligament produces visceral stimulation that a truncal block does not cover and a spinal does; without neuraxial block expect to need opioid here.

### 5. Bladder reflection and uterine arteries
**Why:** The bladder is pushed off the cervix, the ureters are kept lateral by upward traction on the uterus, and the uterine vessels are clamped at the level of the internal os and tied.
**Anaesthesia:** Once both uterine arteries are tied, bleeding at a hysterectomy largely stops; a case that is still losing blood after this point has a slipped pedicle or a venous injury on the pelvic side wall. Blood in the urine bag is the bladder. A sudden request for more relaxation usually means the surgeon is working deep on a pedicle with poor access.

### 6. Vaginal entry, specimen out and vault closure
**Why:** The cardinal and uterosacral ligaments are divided, the vagina is opened and cut around, the uterus is removed and the vault is closed and suspended. Subtotal hysterectomy stops above this and oversews the cervical stump.
**Anaesthesia:** The contaminated step, which is why anaerobic cover is given. The vaginal angles bleed; allow the pressure to return to normal before the surgeon inspects, because a dry field at a systolic of 85 mmHg is not a dry field. Give the long-acting opioid if no intrathecal morphine was used.

### 7. Myomectomy: vasopressin and tourniquet
**Why:** Before incising the uterus the surgeon injects dilute vasopressin into the myometrium around each fibroid (a common recipe is 20 units diluted in 100 mL or more of saline, with a few millilitres per site) and may tie a catheter or suture tightly around the lower uterus. In the Cochrane review vasopressin reduced blood loss by about 250 mL and the need for transfusion.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask for a warning before the injection and for the total dose afterwards. Systemic absorption or intravascular injection produces, within one to five minutes: marked hypertension, skin pallor with loss of the pulse oximeter trace, then reflex bradycardia, sometimes with ST change from coronary vasoconstriction, pulmonary oedema, or arrest. The published arrest occurred two minutes after injection in a healthy 31-year-old. The pattern can be misread as light anaesthesia (the pressure) or as haemorrhage (the pallor and lost trace). Do not give a beta blocker into it. Treat profound bradycardia with atropine 600 micrograms IV, ischaemia or severe hypertension with glyceryl trinitrate, and arrest conventionally; the drug's effect fades over 20-30 minutes, and when it does the uterus may bleed again. A pericervical tourniquet is released every 20-30 minutes in some hands, with a small bleed each time.

### 8. Myomectomy: enucleation and closure of the uterus
**Why:** Each fibroid is shelled out through the smallest number of incisions and the defect closed in two or three layers to obliterate dead space and restore a wall that can carry a pregnancy.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the bleeding phase and it is continuous, not sudden: swabs, suction and blood pooling behind the uterus. Have the scrub team weigh swabs and call the total every 15 minutes, check a haemoglobin and blood gas at about 500 mL and every 500 mL after, and start cell salvage collection early in a multiple myomectomy. Uterotonics are of limited use (the Cochrane review found no good evidence for oxytocin), so the tools are TXA already given, volume, blood and the surgeon's speed. Agree in advance the loss at which conversion to hysterectomy will be discussed. If the endometrial cavity is opened the patient will be advised to deliver future pregnancies by caesarean; that is the surgeon's note to make.

### 9. Haemostasis, closure and the wound
**Why:** Packs out, pelvis washed, peritoneum often left open, sheath closed, then skin. An adhesion barrier may be placed over a myomectomy scar.
**Anaesthesia:** If no spinal was given, this is the moment for bilateral truncal blocks or for the surgeon to infiltrate the sheath and subcutaneous layers; the network meta-analysis of 36 trials found all single-shot techniques effective for this operation, with erector spinae plane and quadratus lumborum blocks ranking above TAP and infiltration on limited direct comparison. Count the retractor time. Reverse the relaxant against a monitor, warm the patient, and aim for an awake, comfortable extubation without coughing against a fresh sheath closure.

### 10. Recovery and the first night
**Why:** The surgeon wants early drinking, sitting out the same evening and the catheter out the next morning.
**Anaesthesia:** With intrathecal morphine working, pain at rest is low and pain on movement moderate; breakthrough is treated with oral oxycodone 5-10 mg or small IV increments, not a background infusion. Respiratory rate and sedation are observed to the unit's intrathecal opioid protocol for 18-24 hours, and no other long-acting opioid or sedative is charted in that window. Pruritus responds to naloxone 40 micrograms IV or ondansetron. Without a spinal, start a PCA in recovery. Hypotension with a rising heart rate and a tense abdomen in the first six hours is a slipped pedicle or, after myomectomy, bleeding into the uterus or broad ligament.

### 11. Day 1 to day 3
**Why:** Mobilisation, diet, bowel function and discharge on day 2 or 3.
**Anaesthesia:** The intrathecal morphine fades between 18 and 24 hours, at the point the patient is first asked to walk, and the wound declares itself. That step-up is this operation's rebound, and it is managed by making sure regular paracetamol, the NSAID and a charted oral opioid are in place before it happens, not by reacting to a pain score of 8 on the ward round. Opioid needs fall quickly after day 3. Ileus is uncommon after benign surgery and more likely with heavy opioid use.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is an abdominal wall incision with a visceral wound beneath it, worst on movement for three days. The plan needs something for the wall, something for the viscera, and an anti-inflammatory base, and it needs to cover the first mobilisation.

Intrathecal morphine does the first two for a day. The randomised dose-finding trial in 144 women having abdominal hysterectomy through a low transverse incision found that 100 micrograms reduced PCA morphine against placebo, 200 micrograms reduced it further, and 300 micrograms added nothing but itch; no serious adverse effects were seen. The defensible default is therefore 150-200 micrograms, with paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor and dexamethasone, and oral opioid for breakthrough. It costs a night of respiratory observation, pruritus in a substantial minority and more nausea, all of which are affordable in a patient staying two or three nights anyway.

Truncal blocks are the alternative when a spinal is declined, contraindicated or has failed. The network meta-analysis of 36 trials supports single-shot blocks as a class, ranks erector spinae plane and quadratus lumborum blocks highest, and a later pairwise meta-analysis of eight trials found lower pain scores with quadratus lumborum than TAP, with heterogeneity too high to rely on the size of the effect. The practical reading: a posterior block that may reach some visceral fibres (quadratus lumborum or erector spinae plane) is preferable to a lateral TAP block, any of them is better than nothing, and none lasts beyond the first night. Combining intrathecal morphine with a truncal block for a Pfannenstiel incision is unnecessary in most patients.

Catheters are selective. A midline incision carried above the umbilicus, an opioid-tolerant patient, or severe respiratory disease justifies an epidural or bilateral rectus sheath catheters for 48-72 hours; this is the author's judgement, borrowed from the evidence for other midline laparotomies, since the hysterectomy trials are dominated by transverse incisions.

Adjuncts: ketamine 0.25-0.5 mg/kg IV at induction for the opioid-tolerant; gabapentinoids are not recommended routinely because sedation on top of intrathecal morphine is the wrong direction. After myomectomy the retained uterus cramps for a day or two, which is prostaglandin-mediated and is the reason to restart the NSAID as soon as the surgeon is content with haemostasis.

Chronic pain: a third of women in the Danish cohort had pain a year after benign hysterectomy, predicted by preoperative pelvic pain and pain elsewhere and not clearly by the route of surgery. Spinal anaesthesia was associated with less (OR 0.42), which is observational but points the same way as the acute plan. Burning or numbness at the lateral end of a Pfannenstiel scar that persists is ilioinguinal or iliohypogastric neuralgia.

Escalation triggers: pain uncontrolled at rest on day 0 despite intrathecal morphine is a failed spinal or a complication; pain with distension, hypotension or a falling haemoglobin is bleeding; unilateral loin pain or fever from day 2 is a ureter.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Multiple myomectomy. The highest blood loss on this card and the least predictable. Arterial line, cross-matched blood, cell salvage, TXA, and an agreed threshold for hysterectomy.
- Vasopressin. Dilution and total dose vary between surgeons; ask each time. Hypertension, pallor and bradycardia in the minutes after injection are the drug.
- The very large uterus. Aortocaval compression supine (a wedge under the right hip helps), difficult ventilation, hydronephrosis, a midline incision and a bigger analgesic problem. Decompression when the uterus is lifted out of the abdomen drops the pressure.
- Anaemia and the patient who declines blood. Preoperative iron and hormonal suppression, TXA, cell salvage if acceptable to her, meticulous surgical inflow control, and a clear documented plan.
- Emergency hysterectomy for haemorrhage. After delivery it belongs to the [major obstetric haemorrhage](major-obstetric-haemorrhage-and-placenta-accreta.html) card; after gynaecological surgery it is a relook laparotomy in a hypovolaemic patient: no spinal, rapid sequence induction, massive transfusion readiness and critical care.
- Spinal as the sole anaesthetic. Feasible for a Pfannenstiel hysterectomy with a block to T4-T6 and useful in severe respiratory disease; visceral traction, packing and tilt are poorly tolerated awake and conversion must be planned for.
- Anticoagulation and timing. The first low molecular weight heparin dose is given no sooner than the unit's interval after a spinal; do not let a ward protocol chart it at a fixed evening time without checking.
- Femoral neuropathy from the retractor. Weak knee extension and a numb anterior thigh on day 1; usually recovers over weeks.
- Malignancy found or suspected. Staging laparotomy, omentectomy and node dissection are on the [gynaecological oncology](gynaecological-oncology.html) card.
- The minimally invasive alternative. Laparoscopic myomectomy and hysterectomy trade this wound for ports and a long head-down case; see the [laparoscopic hysterectomy](laparoscopic-and-robotic-hysterectomy.html) card.

## Key sources

The intrathecal morphine dose rests on one well-conducted randomised dose-finding trial in exactly this operation, which is unusually direct evidence. The comparison of truncal blocks rests on a network meta-analysis with few head-to-head trials and a pairwise meta-analysis with high heterogeneity: enough to say the blocks work, not enough to rank them with confidence. The myomectomy haemostasis data are a Cochrane review of 18 mostly small trials that explicitly lacked adverse-effect data, which is why the vasopressin warning is supported by a case report. The catheter advice for midline incisions, the timing of NSAIDs after myomectomy and the haemoglobin threshold for deferring surgery are the author's judgement. No recent PROSPECT publication for open hysterectomy was found during drafting.
