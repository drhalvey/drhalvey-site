## What the surgeon is doing and why

Orthognathic surgery moves the tooth-bearing parts of the facial skeleton to where they should have grown. The patient is usually 17 to 30 years old, has worn orthodontic braces for a year or two to line the teeth up within each jaw, and has a jaw relationship that orthodontics cannot correct: a mandible too far forward or back, a maxilla that is short, long, narrow or retruded, an open bite or an asymmetry. A second group has maxillomandibular advancement for obstructive sleep apnoea. The operation is planned to the millimetre on a three-dimensional model, and printed acrylic splints record the intended bite at each stage.

There are three building blocks, all done through the mouth with no skin incisions.

1. Le Fort I osteotomy. An incision in the upper vestibule, subperiosteal dissection up to the infraorbital nerves and back to the pterygoid plates, and a horizontal saw cut through the maxilla above the tooth roots from the piriform rim to the tuberosity on each side. The nasal septum and the lateral nasal walls are divided with osteotomes, the maxilla is separated from the pterygoid plates behind, and the whole upper jaw is levered down (the down-fracture) on its soft tissue pedicle of palatal mucosa. It is then mobilised, moved to the planned position in the intermediate splint and plated.
2. Bilateral sagittal split osteotomy. On each side the ramus is split lengthwise in the plane of the inferior alveolar nerve, so that the tooth-bearing segment can slide forward or back against the segment carrying the condyle, with a broad overlap of bone to heal. The nerve lies in the split and is stretched or handled in most cases.
3. Genioplasty. A horizontal cut through the chin below the tooth roots and mental nerves, to slide the chin point.

To set the bite the surgeon wires or bands the teeth together through the splint (intermaxillary fixation) while plating. With rigid plates the fixation is usually released before the patient wakes and replaced by light guiding elastics; some surgeons leave the jaws firmly banded.

The pain logic is bone, tempered by nerve. These are clean osteotomies with rigid fixation, which hurt like fractures that have been fixed: moderately, for two or three days, and responsive to anti-inflammatories. The infraorbital, inferior alveolar and mental nerves have been dissected, stretched or lie in the cut, so the lip, chin, cheeks and palate are numb for weeks, and that takes the edge off. What patients describe is pressure and swelling that peaks at 48 hours, a nose blocked by clot and oedema that forces mouth breathing through a mouth they cannot open properly, and a sore throat. The anaesthetic difficulty of the operation is elsewhere: a nasal tube in the surgical field, a request for a dry field in a vascular area, three to five hours of surgery, and an emergence with a bloody, swollen, partly closed airway in a patient at high risk of vomiting.

## Steps

### 1. Preoperative assessment
**Why:** The surgeon has a fixed plan and a young patient with high expectations of a cosmetic and functional result.
**Anaesthesia:** Most patients are healthy. Look for the airway that the deformity itself creates: marked retrognathia or micrognathia, a syndrome (Treacher Collins, hemifacial microsomia, repaired cleft, Pierre Robin sequence) or temporomandibular ankylosis, any of which can make laryngoscopy difficult, and obstructive sleep apnoea in the advancement group. Ask about nasal patency side to side and previous septal surgery. A group and screen is enough for bimaxillary surgery in most units. Explain what waking will be like: a numb swollen face, a blocked nose, elastics on the teeth, and suction in the hand.

### 2. Induction, nasal intubation and securing the tube
**Why:** The surgeon must close the teeth together to set the occlusion, so nothing can pass between them; the tube goes through the nose and must leave the field upwards.
**Anaesthesia:** Vasoconstrictor to both nostrils, a warmed, lubricated preformed north-facing nasal tube (6.0-7.0 mm) passed along the floor of the nose, and a videolaryngoscope to see it through the cords. Fix it over the forehead with padding under the connector and no upward pull on the alar rim: three to five hours of pressure there causes necrosis. Tape the join between tube and connector, because it is the commonest disconnection under the drapes. Protect the eyes with ointment, tape and pads or shields; the surgeon works directly over them with saws and retractors. If a throat pack is requested, apply the protocol in the consensus statement: named inserter, visible label, written on the board, counted out. Where the nose cannot be used (previous pharyngeal flap for cleft, blocked nasal passages) or a simultaneous rhinoplasty is planned, the alternative is submental intubation, which in a systematic review of 2,229 patients (15% orthognathic) had a complication rate of 7%, mostly minor wound problems; see [facial fracture fixation](facial-fracture-fixation.html).

### 3. Positioning, lines and the bleeding plan
**Why:** The maxilla and its surrounding venous plexus are highly vascular and cannot be controlled with a tourniquet or clamps; a dry field shortens the operation and lets the surgeon see the cuts.
**Anaesthesia:** Head up 10-15 degrees, arms tucked, long lines, table turned. Second cannula; arterial line if you intend deliberate hypotension, which you measure at the level of the head. Give tranexamic acid before incision: a meta-analysis of nine randomised trials and two cohort studies found a mean reduction in blood loss of 171 mL and a lower odds of transfusion (0.33), without more thromboembolism, and a network meta-analysis restricted to bimaxillary trials found the effect significant only at 20 mg/kg. Dexamethasone goes in now for swelling and nausea. Maintain with propofol and remifentanil: it gives a controllable pressure, a still patient without repeated relaxant, the lowest emetic burden and a quick, calm wake. Warm the patient and catheterise for a bimaxillary case.

### 4. Infiltration and incision
**Why:** Adrenaline-containing local anaesthetic is infiltrated along the upper and lower vestibules and the rami for haemostasis and analgesia, then the mucosa and periosteum are incised and stripped.
**Anaesthesia:** Know the total dose of local anaesthetic and adrenaline; a transient tachycardia and pressure rise a minute after infiltration is absorbed adrenaline. Subperiosteal stripping is stimulating. Deepen with remifentanil instead of chasing pressure with vasodilators.

### 5. Le Fort I osteotomy cuts
**Why:** A reciprocating saw divides the anterior and lateral maxillary walls; osteotomes divide the nasal septum and lateral nasal walls, working backwards inside the nose, and a curved osteotome separates the tuberosity from the pterygoid plates.
**Anaesthesia:** The nasal tube runs along the floor of the nose, exactly where the septal and lateral wall osteotomes travel. The tube, its cuff inflation line or the pilot balloon can be cut or the tube pinned by a chisel. Signs are a sudden leak, bubbling of blood in the field with each breath, a falling tidal volume, or a cuff that will not hold pressure. A partial cut can be managed by advancing the tube so the defect lies below the cords, packing around it and continuing with higher flows; a complete transection or an unmanageable leak means changing the tube over an airway exchange catheter, through a nose that is now bleeding and unstable, with the surgeon holding the maxilla. Have the exchange catheter and a spare tube in the room for every case. This is the period when modest hypotension earns its place: a mean arterial pressure of 55-65 mmHg in a healthy young adult, achieved with remifentanil and head-up tilt, reversed once the maxilla is plated. The evidence that it reduces blood loss and improves the field is low certainty, and no trial has been large enough to measure harm, so the target is relaxed for anyone with hypertension, vascular disease or age against them.

### 6. Down-fracture and mobilisation
**Why:** The maxilla is levered downwards with hooks or disimpaction forceps and stretched free of the pterygoid plates and soft tissue until it moves passively to its new position.
**Anaesthesia:** Two events. First, bleeding: the descending palatine arteries run in the posterior medial wall and are stretched, clipped or torn here, and the pterygoid venous plexus lies just behind the cut. Most bleeding is venous ooze controlled with packing, head-up tilt and pressure; a brisk arterial bleed is the reason for the second cannula. Second, the trigeminocardiac reflex: traction on the maxilla and the branches of the maxillary nerve can produce sudden bradycardia or a pause. It stops when the surgeon lets go, so tell them; give atropine 0.6 mg or glycopyrronium 0.2-0.4 mg IV if it recurs, and expect it again with further traction. Watch the tube as the maxilla moves: an advancement or impaction pulls or kinks it, and airway pressures change.

### 7. Positioning and plating the maxilla
**Why:** The mobile maxilla is seated in the intermediate splint against the uncut mandible, the jaws are wired together, the complex is rotated up until bone contact is right, and four small plates fix it.
**Anaesthesia:** The teeth are now wired shut around the splint; your access to the mouth is gone until release. With impaction the nasal floor rises and can compress or kink the tube against the septum; rising airway pressure at this step is mechanical and is reported to the surgeon, who can trim the septum. Once the plates are on, bring the blood pressure back to normal so that bleeding declares itself before the mucosa is closed, not in recovery.

### 8. Bilateral sagittal split osteotomy
**Why:** Cuts through the inner cortex of the ramus above the lingula and the outer cortex at the molar region are joined along the anterior border and the mandible is split with osteotomes and spreaders, keeping the nerve in the tooth-bearing segment. The distal segment is moved into the final splint, the jaws are wired together again and each side is fixed with a plate or screws.
**Anaesthesia:** Less bleeding than the maxilla, with the occasional brisk bleed from the inferior alveolar or facial vessels. Retractors on the ramus swell the cheek and the floor of the mouth. The split can go wrong (a bad split), which adds time and plates. No relaxant is needed if remifentanil is adequate, and masseter tone does not prevent the split. The operation is now three hours old: check temperature, pressure areas, urine output and blood loss measured from suction minus irrigation.

### 9. Genioplasty, final occlusion check and closure
**Why:** If planned, the chin is cut and plated. The fixation is released, the surgeon checks that the mandible closes passively into the planned bite, and the mucosa is closed. Guiding elastics or, in some hands, firm intermaxillary fixation are applied.
**Anaesthesia:** Before the mouth is closed for the last time: throat pack out and said aloud, pharynx suctioned under vision, and an orogastric tube passed to empty the stomach of swallowed blood, then removed. In an 11-year series of 667 cases a nasogastric tube left in after surgery was associated with four times the odds of nausea and vomiting, so empty and remove. Ask the surgeon exactly what will hold the jaws: light elastics that a patient can open against, or tight elastics or wires that must be cut to reach the airway. If the latter, the scissors or wire cutters travel with the patient and the recovery nurse is shown which bands or wires to cut. Give paracetamol and the NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor if the surgeon accepts it, and a long-acting opioid before remifentanil stops.

### 10. Emergence and extubation
**Why:** The surgeon wants no coughing against fresh mucosal closures, no bleeding from a pressure surge and no vomiting through closed teeth.
**Anaesthesia:** Sit the patient up, suction the nose and nasopharynx gently, and extubate awake and obeying, on a low remifentanil infusion to suppress coughing. The upper airway is now narrower than at induction: swollen tongue and floor of mouth, a nose full of clot, and a mouth that opens a little. Facemask ventilation is harder and reintubation harder still. Withdrawing the nasal tube until its tip sits in the pharynx and leaving it as a nasopharyngeal airway through the first hour is a useful half-step. Oxygen by face tent, a Yankauer sucker in the patient's hand, and no nasal continuous positive airway pressure through a fresh Le Fort without the surgeon's agreement.

### 11. Recovery and the first two nights
**Why:** Swelling increases until about 48 hours and then recedes; the surgeon wants ice, head elevation, steroids and a clear liquid diet.
**Anaesthesia:** Nurse sitting up. Oozing from the nose is expected; a steady bleed is not. Treat nausea early and with something different from what was given in theatre. Sleep apnoea patients have a better pharyngeal airway after advancement but a blocked nose and opioids, and are monitored overnight. Dexamethasone is often continued for 24-48 hours; a randomised trial of a single 40 mg preoperative dose showed less oedema than placebo, and a trial of 5 mg against 15 mg showed no difference, so the dose question is open.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is bone with a numb overlay, so the plan is systemic, anti-inflammatory and short. There is no PROSPECT guideline for this operation.

The defensible default is paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor from the end of surgery (surgeons who worry about bleeding accept a COX-2 inhibitor more readily), dexamethasone at induction, surgeon infiltration of every incision with bupivacaine or ropivacaine with adrenaline, remifentanil intraoperatively with a transition dose of a longer-acting opioid, and oral or sublingual opioid as required for two to three days. A patient-controlled opioid pump is seldom needed past the first night and works against the nausea plan. Everything must be liquid, dispersible or intravenous, because the patient cannot open the mouth or chew. Maxillary and inferior alveolar nerve blocks with long-acting local anaesthetic are reasonable opioid-sparing adjuncts where someone is practised at them; they are author's judgement and not established by large trials.

Expect worst pain of 3-4 out of 10 on days 1 and 2, described as pressure and aching, falling by day 3 as the swelling turns. Rebound is not a feature because the numbness is neuropraxia, not local anaesthetic, and lasts weeks. Ice and head elevation are analgesic. Nasal decongestant and saline sprays, if the surgeon allows them, treat the complaint the patient minds most.

Escalation triggers. Rapidly increasing unilateral cheek or floor-of-mouth swelling in the first hours is a haematoma and is an airway question before it is a pain question. Severe pain in one jaw joint or a bite that does not meet is a displaced condyle or failed fixation. Pain rising after day 4 with fever is infection.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Tube damage at the Le Fort I cut. Recognise the leak, tell the surgeon, and choose between advancing, packing and exchanging. An exchange catheter and spare tube are in the room before incision.
- Bleeding at down-fracture. Usually controlled by packing and patience. Persistent arterial bleeding from the descending palatine or maxillary artery occasionally needs interventional radiology; it can also present as a delayed epistaxis at one to two weeks.
- Trigeminocardiac reflex. Bradycardia or a pause with maxillary traction. Stop the stimulus first.
- Deliberate hypotension. A tool for the maxillary phase in fit young patients, not a standing order. The supporting evidence is low certainty and harm has not been measured.
- Intermaxillary fixation at emergence. Rare with rigid plates, but when the jaws are wired the airway plan changes: awake extubation only, antiemetics maximal, cutters at the bedside and staff who know which wires to cut.
- Nausea and vomiting. The risk profile is high (young, female, non-smoking, opioids, swallowed blood). Reported rates vary widely with technique; with propofol-based anaesthesia and prophylaxis, the 667-case series recorded 7.9%. See [nausea, vomiting and day-case anaesthesia](../a/a11-ponv-day-case.html).
- Sleep apnoea advancement. Older, heavier patients with hypertension: no deliberate hypotension, a difficult airway plan at induction (see [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html)), and monitored care overnight.
- Syndromic and cleft patients. Difficult laryngoscopy, a scarred nasal passage and velopharyngeal surgery in the way of a nasal tube; plan an awake or asleep flexible bronchoscopic intubation or the submental route.
- Surgically assisted maxillary expansion and single-jaw surgery. Shorter (60-120 min), less blood, the same airway issues; some units discharge within 24 hours.
- Nasal tube pressure injury and corneal abrasion. Both are yours to prevent at set-up and neither can be checked once the drapes are on.
- Emergency variant. Return to theatre for bleeding or haematoma: a swollen face, blood in the stomach and possibly banded jaws. Cut the elastics, sit the patient up, and plan as a difficult airway with the surgeon present.
- Invasive monitoring, blood and critical care. Arterial line for induced hypotension and long bimaxillary cases; blood is rarely needed; high dependency for sleep apnoea, syndromic airways, heavy swelling or a difficult intubation.

## Key sources

Tranexamic acid has the best evidence in this operation, from two meta-analyses that agree on direction and disagree on dose. Hypotensive anaesthesia is supported only at low certainty and its safety is assumed from small trials in healthy young patients. The dexamethasone trials show an effect on swelling against placebo and none between moderate doses. The nausea figures come from one retrospective series. The management of a cut tube, the trigeminocardiac reflex, the extubation technique and the pain trajectory are consensus and author's judgement; the blood pressure target is the author's.
