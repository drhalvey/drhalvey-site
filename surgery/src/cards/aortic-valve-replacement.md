## What the surgeon is doing and why

The aortic valve fails in two ways and the two are opposite problems for the ventricle and for you.

Stenosis is a fixed obstruction: calcified, immobile cusps (degenerative in the elderly, bicuspid a decade or two earlier) leave an orifice under 1 square centimetre. The ventricle answers pressure overload by thickening. A thick ventricle generates the pressure, but it is stiff, so it fills poorly without atrial contraction and a generous preload; and it is hungry, with more muscle to feed through coronaries that fill only in diastole against a high cavity pressure. Its perfusion depends on aortic diastolic pressure and time. Lower the pressure or shorten diastole and the subendocardium becomes ischaemic, the ventricle weakens, the pressure falls further.

Regurgitation is a leak: dilated root, prolapsing or perforated cusps, endocarditis. The ventricle answers volume overload by dilating. Each beat ejects a large stroke volume, part of which falls back in diastole, so diastolic pressure is low and the longer diastole lasts the more comes back. This ventricle likes to be fast, full and working against a low resistance.

The operation is the same for both. Through a full sternotomy, an upper mini-sternotomy or a right anterior thoracotomy, the surgeon goes on bypass, clamps and arrests, opens the aorta just above the valve, cuts out the cusps, debrides the calcium from the annulus, and sews a prosthesis into the annulus with a ring of sutures: a mechanical valve (durable, lifelong warfarin), a stented tissue valve, or a sutureless or rapid-deployment tissue valve that is positioned and expanded with only a few guiding stitches to shorten the clamp time. Small roots may be enlarged with a patch so that an adequate valve fits. The aorta is closed, the left heart is de-aired because it has been open to the room, and the heart is restarted. Where the anatomy and the patient suit it, the same end is reached without any of this by [transcatheter implantation](transcatheter-aortic-valve-implantation-and-structural.html), which in low-risk patients had fewer deaths, strokes and readmissions at one year than surgery; what remains for the surgeon is the younger patient, the bicuspid valve, the dilated root, endocarditis and the patient who needs grafts or another valve as well.

Pain is the sternotomy, at a 4 on the book's scale, by the mechanism set out for [coronary artery bypass grafting](coronary-artery-bypass-grafting-on-and.html) without the mammary bed or the leg. The smaller incision does not change the band: in the randomised comparison a mini-sternotomy did not speed recovery or shorten stay.

## Steps

### 1. Induction against the lesion
**Why:** The surgeon has not started, but the valve lesion dictates the first 30 minutes more than anything they will do.
**Anaesthesia:** For stenosis: arterial line awake, vasopressor infusion attached and running at a low rate before the first drug, a slow induction, and immediate treatment of any fall in pressure with phenylephrine or noradrenaline, not with fluid alone and not with ephedrine-driven tachycardia. Keep sinus rhythm at 60-80 per minute. New atrial fibrillation removes the atrial contribution to a stiff ventricle and is cardioverted, so external pads go on before induction. For regurgitation: avoid bradycardia and sudden rises in afterload, accept a lower diastolic number than you would like but watch the ST segments, and do not use a balloon pump, which inflates in diastole into a leaking valve. Mixed lesions are managed as stenosis, because that is the one that kills at induction.

### 2. Sternotomy or mini-sternotomy
**Why:** A full sternotomy exposes everything. An upper mini-sternotomy, a J into the third or fourth right interspace, exposes the aorta and right atrial appendage and little else; a right anterior thoracotomy through the second or third space avoids the sternum altogether.
**Anaesthesia:** Through a small incision the surgeon cannot get paddles around the heart or easily place epicardial wires after the heart is full, so external pads are the defibrillator and pacing wires are placed on an empty heart before the clamp comes off. Venous drainage may be femoral with a long cannula that you guide into the superior cava on echo. A thoracotomy approach may need the right lung down briefly; a bronchial blocker is enough.

### 3. The pre-bypass echo
**Why:** The surgeon is choosing a valve and a strategy and wants numbers they cannot get by looking.
**Anaesthesia:** Confirm the lesion and its mechanism, measure the annulus and the root, and look for what changes the plan: a septal bulge under the valve that predicts outflow obstruction once the stenosis is relieved, mitral regurgitation that may or may not be functional, a calcified or dilated ascending aorta at the planned cannulation and clamp site, a patent foramen ovale, and ventricular function. The severity of regurgitation determines how cardioplegia will be given, so say it before the clamp goes on.

### 4. Heparin, cannulation and bypass
**Why:** Aortic cannulation is high on the ascending aorta or arch to leave room for the aortotomy; venous drainage is a single two-stage cannula because the right heart stays closed.
**Anaesthesia:** Heparin, clotting time and the going-on-bypass routine are as for any pump case (see [cardiopulmonary bypass and cardiac essentials](../a/a07-cardiopulmonary-bypass.html)). The stenotic patient tolerates atrial handling badly, so the period between pericardiotomy and bypass is one for a steady hand on the vasopressor. With significant regurgitation the ventricle distends as soon as it stops ejecting effectively on bypass, particularly if it fibrillates as it cools: tell the surgeon, who will clamp or vent promptly.

### 5. Cross-clamp, vent and cardioplegia
**Why:** The heart must be arrested and the ventricle kept empty for a still, dry field. A vent through the right superior pulmonary vein into the left ventricle drains what returns from the bronchial circulation.
**Anaesthesia:** Antegrade cardioplegia into the root relies on a competent aortic valve to direct it into the coronaries. With more than mild regurgitation it falls into the ventricle, distends it and protects nothing, so the surgeon either opens the aorta and gives it directly into each coronary ostium with a hand-held cannula or gives it retrograde through the coronary sinus, and usually both. Echo shows the coronary sinus catheter position and any distension. The hypertrophied ventricle is the hardest muscle to protect, so expect repeat doses every 20 minutes or so and expect the potassium and the haemodilution that come with them.

### 6. Aortotomy, excision and debridement
**Why:** A transverse or oblique aortotomy a centimetre or so above the right coronary gives a view down onto the valve. The cusps are excised and the annular calcium is nibbled away until sutures will seat a prosthesis without a gap.
**Anaesthesia:** Two consequences reach you later. Calcium fragments fall into the ventricle and the coronary ostia unless caught, and are one source of post-operative stroke and of unexpected regional dysfunction. And the conduction tissue runs just beneath the commissure between the right and non-coronary cusps, so deep debridement or a suture there produces atrioventricular block when the heart restarts; the heavier the calcium, the more likely you are to be pacing.

### 7. Sizing and seating the prosthesis
**Why:** The largest valve that fits is chosen, because too small a valve leaves a gradient in a large patient. Twelve to twenty pledgeted sutures are placed around the annulus, passed through the sewing ring, and the valve is slid down and tied; a sutureless valve is expanded in place instead.
**Anaesthesia:** A quiet period for you, used for rewarming strategy, blood gas, glucose and potassium correction. If the surgeon decides on a root enlargement or finds the root unsuitable and converts to a root replacement, clamp time and bleeding risk both rise and the blood and product plan should move with it.

### 8. Aortotomy closure and de-airing
**Why:** The left heart has been open to air. Closing the aorta traps air in the ventricle, the left atrium and the pulmonary veins, and the first ejections would send it to the brain and the right coronary.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon floods the field with carbon dioxide during the open phase, fills the heart before the last stitch, and leaves a root vent on suction. Your part is the echo and the lungs: put the table head down, ventilate by hand with sustained inflations to push blood and air out of the pulmonary veins, and report where the air is (pulmonary veins, atrial appendage, along the septum and the apex) while the surgeon shakes and needles the heart. The clamp comes off with the vent running. Do not agree to come off bypass while pooled air is still visible, and expect a late shower when the heart first ejects fully.

### 9. Clamp off: rhythm and pacing
**Why:** Reperfusion restarts the heart, often through ventricular fibrillation.
**Anaesthesia:** A hypertrophied ventricle fibrillates readily and resists defibrillation; correct potassium and magnesium, give lidocaine or amiodarone if repeated shocks are needed, and keep perfusion pressure up, because a fibrillating thick ventricle is poorly perfused even on bypass. Then look at the conduction. Complete or high-grade block gets atrioventricular sequential pacing through atrial and ventricular wires at 80-90 per minute; ventricular pacing alone gives away the atrial contribution this ventricle depends on. Most early block recovers over days as oedema settles, and a minority of patients go on to a permanent system (see [pacemaker, defibrillator and lead extraction](pacemaker-defibrillator-and-lead-extraction.html)).

### 10. Coming off bypass with a thick ventricle
**Why:** The obstruction has gone but the muscle that grew to overcome it is still there.
**Anaesthesia:** Fill before you squeeze. The usual failure is not poor contractility but an underfilled, stiff, hyperdynamic ventricle: the echo shows a small cavity, walls nearly touching, and sometimes turbulence in the outflow tract with systolic anterior motion of the mitral leaflet. Inotropes make that worse. The treatment is volume, a vasopressor, a slower rate, and stopping the adrenaline. The dilated ventricle of chronic regurgitation is the opposite case: it has lost its low-resistance runoff, now faces a competent valve, and often needs an inotrope and afterload kept modest. Right ventricular long-axis function falls after any pericardiotomy and is not by itself a reason for treatment.

### 11. The post-bypass echo
**Why:** This is the only chance to find a correctable problem with the chest open.
**Anaesthesia:** Report in a fixed order: the prosthesis is seated and stable; leaflets or discs move (both discs of a mechanical valve, all three cusps of a tissue valve); any regurgitation is central and trivial washing jets or paravalvular, and if paravalvular how much and where; the gradient at a known cardiac output; flow in both coronary ostia and no new regional wall motion abnormality, because a low-sitting prosthesis or a sewing ring can obstruct an ostium; mitral valve unchanged; no residual air. More than mild paravalvular leak, a stuck disc or an obstructed coronary means going back on.

### 12. Protamine, haemostasis and closure
**Why:** The aortotomy is a suture line in a thin, often friable, high-pressure vessel, and it is the bleeding point specific to this operation.
**Anaesthesia:** Keep systolic pressure in a band the surgeon is comfortable with, commonly 100-120 mmHg, through protamine, haemostasis and transfer: a hypertensive surge on emergence can tear the suture line. Protamine is given slowly and dosed at no more than 1:1 to the heparin given; viscoelastic testing directs products and red cells follow a threshold of 75 g/L. The stiff ventricle tolerates sternal closure poorly if it is underfilled, so watch the trace as the wires tighten. Hand over with the pacing mode, rate, thresholds and underlying rhythm stated.

## Pain plan

The analgesic strategy is that of any sternotomy and the "block" is the plan as a whole. PROSPECT recommends paracetamol and an NSAID from the operating theatre onward unless contraindicated, parasternal block or infiltration, and opioid for rescue, with magnesium or dexmedetomidine as intra-operative adjuncts. The valve patient is on average older than the coronary patient and often has reduced kidney function, so the NSAID is the element most often left out; there are no coronary grafts to argue against it otherwise.

The defensible default is regular paracetamol, a bilateral parasternal block or surgeon infiltration at closure, short-acting opioid for 24-48 hours and oral opioid stepping down by day 3-4. Pain climbs on days 1 and 2 with coughing and mobilisation, as after any sternotomy. A right anterior thoracotomy approach moves the pain to the right second or third interspace, with rib spreading and sometimes a divided costal cartilage: it is sharper, more unilateral and more amenable to a single-sided erector spinae or serratus block, and the author's judgement is that a catheter is reasonable there if the unit has a pathway for it. Neuraxial techniques are avoided because of full heparinisation.

Escalate if pain limits coughing. Mechanical valve patients will be anticoagulated within days, which closes the door on late interventional analgesia.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Critical stenosis with a poor ventricle (low-flow, low-gradient): the highest-risk induction in elective cardiac surgery. Surgeon scrubbed, perfusionist ready, femoral vessels identified.
- Cardiac arrest in severe stenosis before bypass: compressions generate little flow across the valve. The response is immediate cardioversion of any shockable rhythm, vasopressor, and emergency institution of bypass.
- Acute regurgitation from endocarditis or [dissection](surgery-of-the-thoracic-aorta-and.html): a normal-sized ventricle with no time to dilate, very high end-diastolic pressure, pulmonary oedema, premature mitral closure on echo. Keep the rate up, avoid vasopressor surges, operate without delay. Root abscess in endocarditis predicts heart block and a longer, bloodier operation.
- Bicuspid valve with a dilated ascending aorta: the operation may become a root or ascending replacement, with the longer clamp time and bleeding of an [aortic procedure](surgery-of-the-thoracic-aorta-and.html).
- Combined valve and grafts: the pump run is long, the vein grafts allow cardioplegia down them, and the bleeding risk is that of the longer operation.
- Septal hypertrophy: decide on the pre-bypass echo whether a myectomy is needed, because outflow obstruction discovered afterwards means a second pump run.
- Fast-track is appropriate for the isolated valve in a patient with preserved function. Heart block, bleeding and the elderly patient with delirium risk are the usual reasons it fails; keep sedatives and long-acting opioids low for the last of these.

## Key sources

The haemodynamic goals for stenosis and regurgitation are physiology and consensus, not trial data. Incision choice has one adequately sized randomised trial, which found no recovery advantage for the smaller sternotomy. The blood, protamine and extubation evidence is shared with the rest of cardiac surgery and comes from mixed-procedure trials; the tranexamic acid dose trial included valve patients, the largest placebo-controlled trial did not.
