## What the surgeon is doing and why

A large incisional hernia is not a hole so much as a failed abdominal wall. The rectus muscles, no longer joined in the midline, are pulled sideways by the three flat muscles on each flank, which shorten and stiffen over time. The viscera move out into the sac. If more than about a fifth to a quarter of the abdominal contents lives outside the fascia, the abdominal cavity itself contracts around what is left: this is loss of domain, and it means the contents no longer have a home to return to. The patient has back pain from a wall that cannot brace, paradoxical breathing, skin problems over the sac, and often a history that includes several failed repairs, a wound infection, a stoma or a fistula.

The aim of reconstruction is to bring the rectus muscles back to the midline and restore a continuous, innervated, load-bearing wall, reinforced by a very large mesh in a well-vascularised plane away from both bowel and skin. Simple closure is impossible because the gap is 10-20 cm, so the surgeon gains length by releasing one layer of the lateral wall and letting the others slide (component separation).

- Anterior component separation divides the external oblique aponeurosis lateral to the rectus. It gains length but needs wide skin flaps, with wound complications to match.
- Posterior component separation with transversus abdominis release is now the usual operation. Through the old midline scar the surgeon enters the abdomen and frees all bowel from the wall, opens each rectus sheath and lifts the muscle off its posterior sheath out to the lateral edge, then divides the posterior lamella of internal oblique and the transversus abdominis muscle just medial to the nerves entering the rectus, and continues in the plane in front of the peritoneum out to the psoas, up behind the costal margin and down to the pubis. That releases the posterior layer, which is closed in the midline to exclude the bowel; a mesh as large as 30 by 30 cm is laid on it; and the rectus muscles and anterior sheath are closed in front. Mesh ends up sandwiched between vascular layers with no fixation or very little.

Often there is more: a stoma taken down or re-sited, a fistula resected, old infected mesh removed, or a panniculectomy. Each adds time, contamination and blood loss.

Three physiological facts drive the anaesthetic. First, the operation finishes by raising intra-abdominal pressure, in a patient whose respiratory mechanics have adapted to a slack wall; pulmonary complications followed a third of repairs in a recent series. Second, the patients are selected by their history for obesity, diabetes, smoking, lung disease and previous critical illness. Third, the wound is the whole abdominal wall.

Pain logic. This is the abdominal-wall mechanism at its largest: a long midline closure under tension, bilateral muscle dissection from costal margin to pubis, divided transversus, drains, and a tight binder. Pain on coughing and getting out of bed is severe for three to four days and the tight, strapped feeling persists for weeks. It scores 4, and patients with chronic pain or opioid use before surgery, who are common in this group, behave like a 5.

## Steps

### 1. Preparation in the weeks before
**Why:** Recurrence and wound failure track modifiable factors, so most surgeons will not list a patient who is still smoking, has poorly controlled diabetes or is above a body mass index threshold, and may inject botulinum toxin into the lateral abdominal muscles about four weeks ahead to lengthen them. Progressive pre-operative pneumoperitoneum is used in some centres for loss of domain.
**Anaesthesia:** Pre-assessment is where you earn the outcome: spirometry or exercise testing for those with lung disease, sleep apnoea screening and treatment, anaemia, nutrition, and a plan for existing opioids. Botulinum toxin lengthens the lateral muscles by about 4 cm on average in observational series, though matched comparisons have not shown higher fascial closure rates; from your side it means flank muscles that are paralysed for three months, a weaker cough, and no interaction with neuromuscular blockers that matters clinically. Estimate from the CT how much is outside the abdomen, because that predicts the airway pressure problem at closure.

### 2. Induction, lines and the analgesic decision
**Why:** A long open operation with the whole torso prepared.
**Anaesthesia:** If an epidural is the plan, site it awake at T7-T9. Tracheal tube, arterial line, two large cannulae, urinary catheter, warming above the nipples and below the thighs only, since everything between is surgical field. After induction and before incision, at fixed volume-controlled settings with the patient paralysed, record the plateau and peak airway pressures; this is the baseline for step 7. Risk factors for prolonged intubation in a 4,378-patient registry were severe COPD, ASA 3-4, current smoking and body mass index above 40; a patient with two or more of these has a high dependency bed booked.

### 3. Laparotomy and adhesiolysis
**Why:** The old scar and often an ulcerated or thinned area of skin are excised, the sac is opened and every loop of bowel is freed from the anterior wall and from old mesh, so that nothing is caught in the closure.
**Anaesthesia:** The slowest and least predictable part, one to three hours in a multiply operated abdomen. Enterotomies change the wound class and the mesh choice and may lead the surgeon to stage the repair. Explanting old mesh bleeds. Evaporative loss from an open abdomen is large; give maintenance fluid with attention to stroke volume, and keep the patient warm.

### 4. Retrorectus dissection
**Why:** The posterior rectus sheath is incised near the midline and the muscle is swept off it laterally to the linea semilunaris, preserving the epigastric vessels on the muscle and the segmental nerves entering at its lateral edge.
**Anaesthesia:** Bleeding is from perforators and the epigastric vessels, steady and usually modest. Deep neuromuscular block makes the plane easier to develop. Nothing here is covered by a block yet; opioid and adjuncts (ketamine, dexamethasone, magnesium by the author's practice) carry the intraoperative analgesia unless an epidural is running.

### 5. Transversus abdominis release
**Why:** Dividing the transversus muscle and entering the plane in front of the transversalis fascia and peritoneum lets the posterior sheath advance 8-10 cm a side towards the midline and creates the pocket for the mesh.
**Anaesthesia:** The dissection runs up under the costal margin to the diaphragm's insertion and down into the space in front of the bladder. A hole in the peritoneum is repaired; a breach of the diaphragm or pleura high up is uncommon and shows as a rise in airway pressure or a fall in saturation. The plane being opened is the TAP block plane: once it is dissected, a percutaneous ultrasound-guided TAP block or catheter has nowhere reliable to go, which is why the block here is placed by the surgeon under vision, lateral to the dissection, or replaced by another technique.

### 6. Posterior layer closure and mesh
**Why:** The posterior sheaths and peritoneum are sutured together to seal the bowel away, the mesh is spread across the whole retromuscular space, and drains are placed on it.
**Anaesthesia:** A quiet step. Use it to give the surgeon-delivered TAP block if that is the plan (count the local anaesthetic dose), to re-dose antibiotics, check a gas and haemoglobin, and prepare for closure: patient fully paralysed, stomach emptied through the orogastric tube, bladder draining, bowel not distended by nitrous oxide.

### 7. Anterior fascial closure and the pressure check
**Why:** The rectus muscles and anterior sheath are brought together in the midline over the mesh, restoring the linea alba. This is the purpose of the operation and the point of maximum tension.
**Anaesthesia:** As the fascia comes together, watch compliance. With the same ventilator settings as at baseline, read the plateau and peak pressures again and tell the surgeon the change. A rise in peak pressure was independently associated with pulmonary complications in a recent series, with no clean threshold; a rise of a few cmH2O is expected, and a rise near 10 cmH2O or a plateau approaching 30 cmH2O is a patient who should not be extubated immediately and whom the surgeon may choose to close differently, by bridging part of the gap with mesh or resecting omentum. Falling urine output and rising ventilator pressures at this point are the early signs of abdominal compartment syndrome.

### 8. Skin, panniculectomy and drains
**Why:** Redundant, poorly perfused skin and the old sac are excised; a formal panniculectomy is sometimes added. Subcutaneous drains and a binder follow.
**Anaesthesia:** Panniculectomy adds an hour, a transverse wound, further blood loss and a higher thrombosis risk. The binder goes on before emergence and adds a little more to chest wall restriction; check ventilation again with it on.

### 9. Extubation or not
**Why:** The surgeon wants no coughing against a new closure and no reintubation.
**Anaesthesia:** Extubate in theatre when the pressure rise was modest, gas exchange is good on 40% oxygen or less, the patient is warm, fully reversed and comfortable enough to breathe deeply, and do it head-up and smoothly. Otherwise ventilate for 12-24 hours while the abdominal wall stretches and the bowel decompresses: this is planned care, not failure, and the patient should have been warned. Obese patients and those with sleep apnoea go to non-invasive ventilation or high-flow oxygen directly after extubation.

### 10. First days
**Why:** The surgeon is watching the wound, the drains, bowel function and breathing.
**Anaesthesia:** Bladder pressure measured through the catheter is the monitor if urine output falls or ventilation worsens. Ileus is common and is where the choice of analgesic technique shows. Chest physiotherapy and sitting out from day 1 depend on pain on movement being under control. Thromboprophylaxis continues; these patients are immobile, obese and have had a long operation.

## Pain plan

Two reasonable strategies exist and the evidence does not settle between them. A thoracic epidural gives the best dynamic analgesia for a wound of this size and protects the cough; in one large centre, abandoning routine epidurals after transversus abdominis release was followed by a ninefold rise in postoperative ileus, with the epidural predicting less ileus and less uncontrolled pain. Against that, a comparative study of 135 patients found that a surgeon-performed TAP block gave similar pain scores (slightly better on day 2), a stay shorter by a day and a half, and earlier diet. Both are retrospective. There is no PROSPECT guideline for this operation.

The author's reading: the epidural earns its place where the consequence of poor analgesia is respiratory failure or where opioids will work badly, meaning severe COPD, a marked expected pressure rise from loss of domain, morbid obesity with sleep apnoea, and opioid-tolerant patients. For the fitter patient with a moderate defect, a surgeon-delivered TAP block under vision with long-acting local anaesthetic, intrathecal morphine before induction for the first day, and PCA with a ketamine infusion gives comparable comfort with less hypotension and easier mobilisation. Whichever is chosen, add regular paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor from day 0 or 1 if renal function and bleeding allow (there is often no anastomosis), and a bowel regimen.

Many of these patients arrive on opioids, gabapentinoids or both after years of pain and repeated surgery. Continue the baseline, expect double the usual requirements, and involve the pain service before the day.

Rebound is moderate: a single-shot TAP block fades on the first night, which is when the PCA and ketamine do their work, and the epidural's removal on day 3-4 needs oral opioid in place first. Pain that escalates with distension, oliguria and breathlessness is abdominal hypertension, not inadequate analgesia.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Loss of domain. The highest-risk subgroup for ventilatory failure and compartment syndrome; expect planned postoperative ventilation and discuss it beforehand.
- Contaminated cases. Stoma takedown, fistula resection or infected mesh removal at the same operation adds bowel anastomoses (which changes the NSAID and vasopressor thinking), longer surgery and a higher wound failure rate.
- Robotic transversus abdominis release. No midline wound, hours of pneumoperitoneum with the arms docked on both sides in turn, operative times around five hours in an early series, less blood loss and a shorter stay. Pain is lower and the epidural argument weakens.
- Anterior component separation. Shorter and more superficial, with large skin flaps: wound ischaemia and seroma are the problems, and vasoconstrictor infusions are unwelcome.
- Bridged repair. When the fascia cannot be closed, mesh spans the gap; less tension and less rise in pressure, more recurrence.
- Severe COPD and current smokers. The group most likely to stay intubated; many surgeons will not operate until smoking has stopped.
- Diabetes. Dexamethasone and stress raise glucose in a patient whose wound outcome depends on it; treat with insulin and do not omit the antiemetic.
- Thromboembolism. Long surgery, obesity, a tight binder and reduced mobility; mechanical prophylaxis from induction and pharmacological prophylaxis as soon as the surgeon agrees.
- Smaller defects closed without component separation belong on the [incisional hernia](umbilical-epigastric-and-incisional-hernia-repair.html) card; the same operation in a patient with bowel obstruction starts on the [small bowel obstruction](small-bowel-obstruction-and-adhesiolysis.html) card.

## Key sources

The evidence here is retrospective throughout. The two analgesic studies point in different directions on length of stay and ileus and neither is randomised, so the recommendation is explicitly the author's judgement. The airway pressure check at closure is supported by observational data showing an association with pulmonary complications but no reliable threshold; the figures quoted for when to keep a patient intubated are working rules, not validated cut-offs. Botulinum toxin has a plausible mechanism and a measured effect on muscle length, without proof of better closure rates.
