## What the surgeon is doing and why

Here the proceduralist is a radiographer running a machine, with a radiologist deciding the protocol, and nothing is done to the patient except that they must not move.

A magnetic resonance scanner has three fields, and each one produces an anaesthetic hazard. The static field, 1.5 or 3 tesla, comes from a superconducting coil in liquid helium and is on day and night whether or not a scan is running. It aligns hydrogen nuclei in the patient, and it pulls ferromagnetic objects towards the bore with a force that rises steeply over the last metre: the projectile hazard. It also acts on implants and disturbs electronics. The radiofrequency field is pulsed to tip the nuclei, and the signal they emit as they relax is the image. Radiofrequency energy is deposited in the patient as heat and induces current in any conductor lying on or in them: the burn hazard. The gradient fields are switched rapidly to encode position, which makes the coils vibrate: the noise, and the artefact on your ECG.

An image is built over minutes, one sequence at a time, and movement during a sequence ruins that sequence. A brain protocol is five or six sequences over 20 to 40 minutes; spine plus brain, or cardiac imaging with breath-holds, runs past an hour. The patient lies inside a tube about 60 cm wide with a receiver coil clamped over the part of interest. Most adults manage. The patients who are referred to you cannot: children from about three months to five or six years, people with intellectual disability, movement disorders, severe claustrophobia or pain on lying flat, and intensive care patients who are already ventilated.

CT is the opposite in almost every respect. It uses ionising radiation, the gantry is a short open ring, and a modern scanner acquires a whole chest in a few seconds. Far fewer patients need help, the equipment restrictions vanish, and the problems are those of transfer, contrast and a brief period when nobody is in the room.

Three consequences shape the MRI anaesthetic. Everything that enters the scan room must be known to be safe there, including you. The patient is deep inside the bore with the head furthest in, so the airway chosen at the start is the airway you have. And you are in the control room behind a window for the whole scan, relying on monitoring that is delayed and degraded by the scanner itself.

There is no pain logic on this card. Nothing is injured, and the plan is for immobility, not analgesia.

## Steps

### 1. Screening the patient, the staff and the equipment
**Why:** The radiographer is responsible for what crosses the scan room door and uses a written checklist for every person every time, because the magnet does not distinguish a patient from a helper.
**Anaesthesia:** The suite is laid out in zones of increasing restriction, from public areas through a screened holding area to the control room and finally the magnet room, with the field strength at which pacemakers may malfunction marked on the floor. Devices are labelled MR Safe, MR Conditional or MR Unsafe; conditional means safe only at a stated field strength and with stated settings, and the conditions must be read. For the patient: cardiac devices (many modern systems are conditional and need reprogramming before and after), aneurysm clips, cochlear implants, neurostimulators, programmable shunts (re-check the setting afterwards), intrathecal pumps, metal fragments in the eye, medicated patches with metallic backing. For you: empty every pocket, remove the pager, phone, watch, scissors, stethoscope and identity badge clip. For equipment: the laryngoscope, the oxygen cylinder, the drip stand and the trolley are the classic missiles. If an unscreened object is needed urgently, the patient comes out to it.

### 2. Choosing the technique
**Why:** The radiologist needs every sequence free of movement; how deep the patient must be to deliver that depends on age, temperament and scan length.
**Anaesthesia:** Work up a ladder and stop at the lowest rung that will succeed. Infants under about three months often sleep through a scan after a feed and firm swaddling: in 279 such scans, 79% answered the clinical question in full and 1% were inadequate, with preterm infants and spine imaging doing less well. Children from about five years can frequently manage with preparation, a mock scanner and a film to watch. Between those ages, and for those who cannot cooperate, the choice is sedation or general anaesthesia. Dexmedetomidine preserves airway tone and breathing; intranasal doses of 2-3 micrograms/kg with oral midazolam achieved first-attempt success in about 80-88% of young children in one trial, with onset around 25 minutes and recovery around an hour, which is the price. A propofol infusion with a natural airway is faster at both ends, and pooled trials show no difference from dexmedetomidine in success or adverse events. General anaesthesia with an airway device is the most reliable and suits long, multi-region or breath-hold studies. The defensible default for the typical two-year-old having a 40-minute brain scan is a propofol-based technique run by an anaesthetist, and that is author's judgement. Parents ask about the developing brain: the one large randomised trial found that just under an hour of sevoflurane in infancy did not change neurodevelopmental outcome at five years. The honest addition is that a scan repeated every few months is a different exposure from one hernia repair. See [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html) for doses and fasting.

### 3. Induction outside the magnet room
**Why:** The scanner is booked in slots, and the radiographer needs the patient delivered asleep, screened and on a non-ferrous trolley.
**Anaesthesia:** Induce in an adjoining bay with ordinary equipment, a proper table height, a familiar machine and an assistant, then transfer. That bay is also the place you will return to in an emergency, so leave it set up. Secure the airway decision here: a child with a natural airway on propofol gets nasal cannulae with a capnography line and a shoulder roll; a supraglottic airway or tube is taped as if for a prone case, because nobody will be able to adjust it. A tracheal tube is the choice for neonates and ex-premature infants, significant reflux, intensive care patients and cardiac or abdominal studies needing controlled breath-holds. Tape the eyes. Inhalational induction with sevoflurane and then conversion to a propofol infusion is common in children; maintenance on sevoflurane through an MR Conditional machine is equally acceptable.

### 4. Transfer, positioning and cables
**Why:** The radiographer positions the patient on the table, fits the receiver coil, lands the region of interest at the magnet's centre and sends the table into the bore.
**Anaesthesia:** Switch to the MR Conditional monitor and pump before crossing the door, or run infusion lines through the waveguide from pumps outside. Burns are the injury that reaches incident reports: the reviews attribute most to monitoring cables and electrodes. Run every lead straight out of the bore with no loops and no crossing, keep cables off bare skin with padding, use the manufacturer's ECG electrodes placed close together, use fibre-optic oximetry, and stop limbs touching each other or the bore wall, since skin-to-skin contact makes a conductive loop too. Remove anything with a wire in it that is not needed: temperature probes, pulmonary artery catheters, depth of anaesthesia sensors. Fit ear plugs or ear defenders to every patient; the anaesthetised ear is not protected by being unconscious. Wrap infants and cover the head: the room is kept cold for the magnet and a small infant on a long scan cools, while a larger patient on a long high-field scan warms from radiofrequency deposition.

### 5. The scan: watching from the control room
**Why:** Sequences run for two to eight minutes each, with a pause between them in which the radiographer plans the next. Any intervention on the patient should happen in a pause, because entering mid-sequence wastes it.
**Anaesthesia:** You watch through a window and a camera. Know what each monitor can still tell you. The ECG is distorted by blood flowing in the static field, which produces T wave and ST changes that are artefact, and by gradient switching, so rate is usable and morphology is not. The oximeter is your most reliable monitor. The capnogram arrives several seconds late down a long sampling line and is damped, but its presence and shape are the airway monitor for a patient you cannot see breathe. Non-invasive pressure works. With a long circuit, compressible volume and time constants are larger: small tidal volumes in infants are partly lost in the tubing, and a change in vaporiser setting takes minutes to reach the patient. An infusion change takes as long to arrive down three metres of line. A child who starts to move is usually light; ask the radiographer to pause, bolus through the port nearest the patient, and wait for it to arrive before restarting.

### 6. Contrast
**Why:** Gadolinium-based contrast shortens relaxation times in tissue with abnormal vessels or a leaky blood-brain barrier and is given part-way through, followed by repeat sequences.
**Anaesthesia:** It is given through your cannula in a pause, by hand or by a power injector whose line must be compatible with the cannula's pressure rating. Acute reactions are uncommon and treated as any anaphylaxis, outside the room. The radiologist screens renal function because of the association between older agents and nephrogenic systemic fibrosis in severe renal impairment. For the anaesthetist the practical point is that contrast extends the scan by 10 to 20 minutes, so ask at the start whether it is planned.

### 7. Emergencies in the scanner
**Why:** The radiographer can stop a sequence at once and bring the table out in seconds, but cannot switch the magnet off without destroying its helium charge.
**Anaesthesia:** The rule is evacuation: stop the scan, table out, patient onto the trolley and into the anaesthetic bay, door closed behind you. Laryngospasm, loss of airway, anaphylaxis and arrest are all treated there, with standard equipment that could not have come in. Rehearse it, because the arrest team arriving with a defibrillator, cylinders and pockets full of steel is the foreseeable second accident; someone is stationed at the door to stop them. A quench vents the helium to atmosphere and collapses the field over a minute or more; it is for a person pinned to the magnet by a heavy object, not for an arrest. If the vent fails and cold gas fills the room it displaces oxygen: evacuate.

### 8. The critically ill and the difficult adult
**Why:** Intensive care patients are scanned for brain injury, spinal cord compression and sepsis sources; other adults are referred because they cannot tolerate the bore.
**Anaesthesia:** The ventilated patient needs a transfer in which every device is exchanged: MR Conditional ventilator or long circuit, infusions rationalised to those that matter and extended, arterial transducer moved to the compatible monitor, pacing wires and oesophageal probes removed or declared unsafe. Allow 30 minutes for the exchange and decide beforehand what level of support is too much to move; a patient on high-dose vasopressors with a marginal airway pressure gains little from an image and risks a great deal for it. For the claustrophobic or learning-disabled adult, sedation with a natural airway in the bore combines an obese or obstructing patient with an invisible airway. A general anaesthetic with a supraglottic airway is often the safer and quicker option. Adults in pain from spinal pathology need analgesia before positioning, not sedation to tolerate it.

### 9. CT: what is different
**Why:** The radiographer needs seconds of stillness and, for many studies, a timed bolus of iodinated contrast through a power injector; everyone leaves the room for the exposure.
**Anaesthesia:** No magnet, so ordinary equipment, and fast enough that many children can be scanned awake, swaddled or with intranasal sedation. Anaesthesia is for the child who needs a breath-hold, a contrast study through a secure line, or a CT-guided procedure ([interventional radiology](interventional-radiology.html)). You step behind the screen during the exposure, so set the monitor where it can be seen through the window. Arms go above the head for chest and abdomen, which pulls on lines and the airway: re-check both after positioning. Iodinated contrast is the commonest cause of anaphylaxis in the department; flushing and a warm sensation are expected, hypotension and bronchospasm are not. The dangerous CT is the trauma or intensive care patient mid-resuscitation: the scanner is a place where bleeding continues unobserved for ten minutes, and the decision to go there belongs to the team leader with the anaesthetist's voice in it.

### 10. Emergence and recovery
**Why:** The radiographer checks the images before the patient is woken, because a failed sequence is cheaper to repeat now than at a second anaesthetic.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask whether the images are adequate before stopping the infusion. Wake the patient in the anaesthetic bay or a staffed recovery area, not in the scan room. After propofol, children are awake within 10 to 20 minutes and are fed early; after sevoflurane expect more emergence delirium, which settles with time, a parent, and if needed a small dose of propofol or dexmedetomidine. Remote recovery areas need the same staff and equipment as any other ([remote site chapter](../a/a12-remote-site-sedation.html)). In nearly 50,000 propofol sedations of children outside theatre there were no deaths and two resuscitations, but airway obstruction or apnoea occurred in more than 1 in 20: the outcome was good because the systems could rescue.

## Pain plan

There is none to make. The scan is painless, so analgesia is given only for the patient's underlying condition: a child with a spinal tumour or an adult with cord compression may need opioid to lie flat, and that is prescribed for the disease. Topical local anaesthetic for the cannula is the single analgesic intervention most children need.

The decision that takes the place of the block decision is depth: no drug, sedation or general anaesthesia. It follows from the scan's demand (duration, breath-holds, regions) and the patient (age, airway, comorbidity), and the ladder in step 2 sets it out. Opioids have no routine role and add apnoea and vomiting.

Escalation is of depth, not of analgesia: a sedation that produces movement artefact twice should be converted, in the anaesthetic bay, to a general anaesthetic with an airway, and not deepened by increments inside the bore.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The ex-premature infant. Post-anaesthetic apnoea risk applies to a scan exactly as to a hernia repair: tracheal tube, apnoea monitoring afterwards, and a strong case for feed and wrap first.
- The child with a difficult airway, craniofacial syndrome or mucopolysaccharidosis. These children are scanned often, and the airway worsens with age. Secure it definitively outside the room; a natural-airway technique in the bore is the wrong economy.
- Repeated scans. Children with tumours are scanned every few months for years. Record what worked, protect veins, and keep trying the non-drug option as the child grows.
- Cardiac MRI. Long, with repeated breath-holds and sometimes adenosine or dobutamine stress, in patients with the worst hearts. Controlled ventilation, an arterial line when the ventricle demands it, and the ECG artefact at its most misleading.
- Intraoperative and interventional MRI. A theatre built round a magnet: every count, instrument and line follows scanner rules, and the checklist is longer.
- Pregnancy. MRI without contrast is used freely; left tilt in later pregnancy, and gadolinium is avoided unless essential.
- Implanted devices that are conditional. The cardiology or device team programmes them before and after, and someone who can manage the rhythm stays for the scan.
- Tattoos, eyeliner and transdermal patches. Conductive pigments and foil backings heat; remove patches and warn about tattoos.
- The helper in the room. A parent or nurse who stays is screened exactly like the patient and wears ear protection.

## Key sources

Safety practice rests on two professional guidelines, British and American, whose recommendations are consensus built on incident reports and physics, not trials. Paediatric sedation safety comes from a very large prospective registry. The comparison of dexmedetomidine with propofol is a meta-analysis of small trials, and feed and wrap has single-centre series. The neurodevelopmental reassurance is from one randomised trial of a single short exposure and should not be stretched to cover repeated long ones. Field strengths, noise and heating effects are standard physics and are not individually sourced.
