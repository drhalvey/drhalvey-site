## What the surgeon is doing and why

Acute limb ischaemia is the sudden loss of arterial flow to a limb that has not had time to grow collaterals. There are two causes and they make two different operations.

An embolus is a clot from elsewhere, most often the left atrium in atrial fibrillation or the left ventricle after a myocardial infarction, that lodges where the artery narrows: the femoral bifurcation, the popliteal trifurcation or the brachial bifurcation. The arteries are otherwise healthy. The limb goes from normal to white, cold, painful and pulseless within minutes. The fix is mechanical and quick. Through a groin or antecubital incision the artery is opened, a balloon-tipped (Fogarty) catheter is passed beyond the clot, inflated and withdrawn, dragging the embolus out. Flow returns and the arteriotomy is closed.

Thrombosis in situ is clot forming on existing atheroma, a popliteal aneurysm or a failed bypass graft. The limb has some collaterals, so the onset is less abrupt and the ischaemia often less profound, but the artery is diseased and passing a balloon through it does not solve the problem. The surgeon needs imaging, and then some combination of thrombectomy, angioplasty, endarterectomy or bypass, which is a long operation in an unplanned patient. Catheter-directed thrombolysis is the alternative for the limb that is not immediately threatened.

Which patient is in front of you is decided by the state of the limb. A limb that is painful but has normal sensation and movement is viable and can wait for imaging. A limb with sensory loss beyond the toes and any weakness is immediately threatened and goes to theatre now. A limb that is paralysed, insensate, rigid and mottled with fixed staining is dead, and revascularising it would deliver its contents to the circulation; the operation for that limb is amputation. Muscle and nerve tolerate about six hours of complete warm ischaemia, which is why the anaesthetic assessment is done in the time it takes to open the instruments.

The second half of the title follows from the first. Ischaemic muscle that is reperfused swells. Capillaries made leaky by hours of hypoxia are suddenly exposed to arterial pressure, and the muscle expands inside a fascial envelope that cannot stretch. Compartment pressure rises above capillary perfusion pressure and the muscle becomes ischaemic again, this time with a palpable pulse at the ankle. The treatment is to open the envelope: a four-compartment fasciotomy of the lower leg through two long incisions, one anterolateral for the anterior and peroneal compartments and one medial for the superficial and deep posterior compartments, each 15-25 cm, left open. Surgeons do it prophylactically at the time of revascularisation when ischaemia has been prolonged or profound, and therapeutically when a reperfused calf becomes tense.

The systemic effect of reperfusion is the reason this is not a minor case. The limb beyond the occlusion has been accumulating potassium from dying cells, lactate and hydrogen ions, and myoglobin from muscle necrosis. Restoring flow returns all of it to the circulation over minutes. The consequences run from a brief dip in pressure to hyperkalaemic arrest on the table, with myoglobinuric kidney injury over the following days. Severity scales with the mass of muscle involved and the duration of ischaemia: an arm is a small event, a leg from the groin after eight hours is a large one, and an aortic saddle embolus involving both legs is the largest.

The pain logic has three layers. The surgical wound of an embolectomy is a small groin incision and would rate a 1 on its own. The limb itself is the main source: ischaemic pain before surgery is severe, and after revascularisation it is replaced by the aching, tight pain of reperfusion oedema and the burning dysaesthesia of recovering nerve. Fasciotomy adds two long open wounds over exposed muscle, which are painful at rest for days and very painful at each dressing change until they are closed or skin-grafted. The complicating fact is that escalating pain is also the cardinal symptom of a compartment syndrome, so analgesia has to be effective without removing the warning.

## Steps

### 1. Diagnosis, heparin and the ten-minute assessment
**Why:** The surgeon gives heparin immediately to stop clot propagating into the collaterals and distal vessels, grades the limb, and decides between theatre now, imaging first, or thrombolysis.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient is unfasted, in severe pain, and the cause of the embolus is your comorbidity: fast atrial fibrillation, a recent infarct with a ventricular thrombus, endocarditis, malignancy. Get an ECG, potassium, creatinine, a venous gas and a creatine kinase, and ask how long the limb has been ischaemic. Give analgesia now, titrated intravenous morphine or fentanyl; nobody benefits from waiting. Heparin 5,000 units IV has usually been given, which settles the neuraxial question. Do not delay a threatened limb for echocardiography or rate control beyond what can be done on the way; rate control and volume can be managed on the table.

### 2. Choosing local or general anaesthesia
**Why:** A femoral embolectomy needs only a 6 cm groin incision and is well tolerated under infiltration. The surgeon cannot always say in advance whether the case will stay that simple.
**Anaesthesia:** Local infiltration with an anaesthetist present is the usual plan for a clear embolus in a frail patient: it avoids induction in someone with a recent infarct or uncontrolled atrial fibrillation, and leaves a patient who can report symptoms. Its limits should be agreed before the start. Fasciotomy cannot be done under infiltration. Bypass and prolonged popliteal exploration cannot either. A patient in severe ischaemic pain will not lie still for 2 h. If the limb has been ischaemic for more than 4-6 h, if thrombosis on chronic disease is likely, or if the patient is agitated, start with general anaesthesia: a rapid sequence induction with a tracheal tube in the unfasted patient, with an arterial line first if it takes no more than a few minutes. Sedation under local anaesthesia is kept minimal for the same aspiration reason.

### 3. Exposure and arteriotomy
**Why:** The common femoral artery and its bifurcation are exposed and slung, and a transverse arteriotomy made in a healthy artery or a longitudinal one in a diseased artery that will need a patch.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon infiltrates lidocaine 1% up to 20 mL and tops up around the femoral sheath. Dissection around the femoral nerve and traction on the artery are felt despite infiltration: small doses of fentanyl or a low-rate remifentanil infusion cover it. A further dose of heparin is requested before clamps if more than an hour or two has passed since the first.

### 4. Balloon embolectomy
**Why:** The catheter is passed distally down the superficial femoral and profunda arteries and proximally up the iliac, inflated and withdrawn repeatedly until no more clot comes and there is brisk back-bleeding from below and pulsatile inflow from above.
**Anaesthesia:** Inflating and dragging a balloon through an artery is painful under local anaesthesia, a deep cramping pull along the thigh and calf; warn the patient and give opioid ahead of each pass. Each pass loses 20-50 mL of blood through the arteriotomy, and with many passes this adds up. Use this period to prepare for reperfusion: repeat the potassium and gas if ischaemia has been long, treat a potassium above 5.5 mmol/L now with insulin 10 units IV and glucose, give a fluid bolus, and have calcium chloride 10% 10 mL and a vasopressor drawn up. Tell the surgeon the potassium.

### 5. Completion imaging and adjuncts
**Why:** An on-table angiogram shows whether the tibial vessels are clear. Residual clot is treated with further passes, intra-arterial thrombolytic, or a below-knee popliteal exploration; underlying stenosis with angioplasty; an occluded diseased segment with bypass.
**Anaesthesia:** This is where a 45 min case becomes a 3 h case. If the operation is extending under local anaesthesia, convert to general anaesthesia deliberately, before reperfusion and not during it. Intra-arterial alteplase adds to the bleeding from wounds and puncture sites and firmly excludes any regional technique afterwards.

### 6. Reperfusion
**Why:** The arteriotomy is closed, clamps are released and the surgeon looks for a pink foot and a pulse or Doppler signal.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask for the release to be announced and, after long ischaemia, staged: a few seconds of flow, reclamp, observe, repeat. Watch the ECG for peaked T waves and broadening of the QRS complex, the arterial pressure for a fall, and the end-tidal carbon dioxide for a rise. Treat hypotension with fluid and a vasopressor; treat ECG change immediately with calcium chloride 10% 10 mL IV, then insulin and glucose, hyperventilation if ventilated, and sodium bicarbonate 50-100 mmol for severe acidosis. Repeat the potassium and gas at 5 and 15 min. After prolonged ischaemia of a whole leg, venting the first 200-300 mL of venous effluent from the femoral vein is practised by some surgeons to reduce the load.

### 7. The fasciotomy decision
**Why:** The surgeon feels the calf after reperfusion. Prolonged or severe ischaemia, a tense calf, or poor venous return leads to fasciotomy now, since a prophylactic fasciotomy is far less morbid than a late one through necrotic muscle.
**Anaesthesia:** If the case has been under local anaesthesia this is the point of conversion to general anaesthesia: in a heparinised, unfasted patient, with reperfusion products circulating, so the relaxant for this rapid sequence induction is rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg IV, with sugammadex available, and not suxamethonium. Potassium is rising minute by minute after reperfusion, muscle that has been ischaemic for hours releases more of it when depolarised, and a value measured a few minutes earlier does not describe the potassium the heart will see, so a normal result is no licence for suxamethonium. Repeat the gas after induction. If the surgeon decides against fasciotomy, the limb is at risk for the next 24-48 h and the analgesic plan must preserve the ability to detect a compartment syndrome.

### 8. Four-compartment fasciotomy
**Why:** Two full-length incisions decompress all four compartments. Dead muscle that does not contract or bleed is excised. The wounds are left open under a dressing or negative-pressure system.
**Anaesthesia:** Bleeding is steady and deceptive: anticoagulated, hyperaemic muscle oozes across a large raw area both in theatre and into the dressings afterwards, and transfusion is common over the following day. Opening the compartments releases a second, smaller wave of potassium and myoglobin. Start the renal protection: maintain a urine output above 1 mL/kg/h with crystalloid, look at the urine colour, and send a creatine kinase. Give long-acting opioid before emergence; the patient will wake to two large open wounds.

### 9. Emergence and handover
**Why:** The surgeon wants an anticoagulated patient with a perfused foot and no bleeding wound.
**Anaesthesia:** A heparin infusion usually starts within hours. Hand over the ischaemic time, the potassium trend, the creatine kinase, urine output and colour, and whether fasciotomy was done. If it was not, hand over explicitly that the limb needs hourly checks for pain on passive toe movement, tense swelling and altered sensation. Extubate the patient who is stable; keep ventilated the one with ongoing acidosis, rising potassium or a large vasopressor requirement.

### 10. The first 48 hours
**Why:** The embolus has been removed but its source has not, and the limb is still declaring its final state.
**Anaesthesia:** Hyperkalaemia and acidosis can recur for hours as perfusion of the muscle bed improves. Rhabdomyolysis peaks at 24-72 h, with creatine kinase as the marker and acute kidney injury as the consequence; early renal replacement is needed in a minority. Re-embolism and rethrombosis bring the patient back. The underlying cause, usually cardiac, now needs its work-up, and death in this population is more often from the heart than from the limb. Fasciotomy wounds return to theatre at day 3-7 for closure or split-skin grafting, a short general anaesthetic in an anticoagulated patient.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is ischaemic and then swollen muscle, with or without open fasciotomy wounds, in a patient who is fully anticoagulated and whose pain is also the monitor for compartment syndrome. Those three facts leave a systemic, multimodal analgesic strategy as the default, and remove most of the regional options that the pain intensity would otherwise justify.

Before surgery: intravenous opioid titrated to comfort. Ischaemic pain is severe and under-treated in the rush to theatre.

For embolectomy without fasciotomy: surgeon infiltration of the groin, regular paracetamol, and oral or PCA opioid in modest dose. Pain should fall after successful revascularisation. The plan must not obscure a compartment syndrome, so no dense or long-acting block of the leg is placed. The Association of Anaesthetists' guidance on the analogous problem in lower leg trauma is the nearest formal statement: avoid dense blocks whose duration greatly exceeds the surgery, and accept that low-concentration blocks have not been associated with delayed diagnosis where surveillance is effective. Systematic review finds no good evidence that PCA or regional analgesia delays the diagnosis, and no adequate prospective data either, so the caution is consensus and mechanism, not proof. In practice the reperfused, un-fasciotomised limb gets opioid by PCA, structured hourly limb observations, and a low threshold for compartment pressure measurement. Escalating opioid requirement is itself treated as a sign.

After fasciotomy the compartments are open and the masking argument largely falls away, while the wounds are painful. PCA opioid with regular paracetamol is the base; low-dose ketamine by infusion (0.1-0.2 mg/kg/h) is the author's preferred adjunct for the first days and for dressing changes. A low-concentration sciatic or femoral block or catheter (ropivacaine 0.1-0.2%) becomes reasonable at this stage if no thrombolytic has been given and anticoagulation and the puncture site permit, on the author's judgement that a superficial compressible site in a heparinised patient is acceptable where a deep or neuraxial one is not; the thigh compartments and the foot remain at risk and are still observed. Neuraxial techniques stay excluded throughout by therapeutic heparin and any thrombolysis. NSAIDs are withheld because of the kidney and the bleeding risk. Gabapentinoids are reasonable for the burning dysaesthesia of nerve recovery from day 2-3.

The score of 4 and the trajectory on this card are for the patient with a fasciotomy. After embolectomy alone, with a limb that reperfuses well, the pain is a 2: the ischaemic pain goes within hours, the groin wound is minor, and pain that does not fall is a finding. Expect pain to be highest on day 0-1 and to settle slowly, more slowly with fasciotomy, with spikes at dressing changes that need pre-emptive opioid or ketamine. Escalation triggers: pain rising after an initial improvement, pain on passive stretch of the toes, or a tense compartment in a limb without fasciotomy is compartment syndrome and needs the surgeon within minutes. Return of the original cold, white, painful limb is re-occlusion. Pain with dark urine and a rising creatinine is rhabdomyolysis.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Brachial embolectomy. The arm tolerates ischaemia better and has less muscle, so reperfusion is mild. Done under local infiltration at the antecubital fossa in most patients; a brachial plexus block is possible but is being placed in an anticoagulated patient, which favours an axillary approach or infiltration alone.
- Aortic saddle embolus. Both legs, bilateral groin embolectomy, the largest reperfusion load and a high mortality. General anaesthesia, arterial line, intensive care, and release of one leg at a time.
- Thrombosed popliteal aneurysm and failed grafts. Not embolectomy cases: expect thrombolysis, bypass and a long anaesthetic; see [lower limb bypass and femoral endarterectomy](lower-limb-bypass-and-femoral-endarterectomy.html). Endovascular thrombectomy and lysis are on [peripheral angioplasty and stenting](peripheral-angioplasty-and-stenting.html).
- Recent myocardial infarction. A common source of the embolus. The limb cannot wait for the heart to recover; local anaesthesia where the surgery allows, and invasive monitoring where it does not.
- Uncontrolled atrial fibrillation. Rate control with a beta-blocker or amiodarone alongside the surgery, not before it. Hypovolaemia and pain are usually part of the rate.
- Malignancy and hypercoagulable states. Arterial thrombosis without an embolic source or atheroma; re-thrombosis is common.
- Late presentation. A limb ischaemic for more than 12-24 h with fixed mottling is managed by primary amputation, and revascularisation attempted against advice is the scenario for fatal reperfusion. Resuscitate first, and be part of that decision.
- Compartment syndrome under sedation or ventilation. In the intensive care patient who cannot report pain, the diagnosis depends on examination and pressure measurement; prophylactic fasciotomy is used more readily for that reason.
- Fasciotomy for other causes. Trauma, crush, prolonged lithotomy and reperfusion after elective bypass produce the same operation; the anaesthetic considerations for the wound are the same, without the embolic comorbidity.
- Returns to theatre. Second-look debridement and delayed closure are short procedures in an anticoagulated patient; general anaesthesia, with the heparin infusion paused according to the surgeon's instruction. Suxamethonium is avoided at these returns as well, because damaged and immobile muscle exaggerates its potassium release.

## Key sources

The evidence base for the anaesthetic management of acute limb ischaemia is thin. The surgical pathway follows the ESVS 2020 guideline. The reperfusion management on this card is physiology and consensus, extrapolated from aortic unclamping. The advice on regional analgesia and compartment syndrome rests on a consensus guideline written for lower leg trauma and on systematic reviews that found mostly case reports; applying it to a reperfused ischaemic limb is the author's extrapolation. There are no trials of analgesic technique after embolectomy or fasciotomy, and the pain scores and trajectory given are the author's estimate.
