## What the surgeon is doing and why

The middle ear is a small air-filled cleft between the tympanic membrane and the inner ear, bridged by three ossicles and continuous behind with the air cells of the mastoid. It fails in a small number of ways. A perforated drum lets water and infection in and loses the area ratio that amplifies sound. A broken or fixed ossicular chain fails to transmit. A cholesteatoma, a sac of keratinising skin growing where skin does not belong, erodes whatever it meets: ossicles, the bony facial canal, the lateral semicircular canal, the tegmen under the temporal lobe. And in profound sensorineural deafness the hair cells of the cochlea are lost, so no amount of middle ear repair helps and the auditory nerve must be stimulated directly.

Myringoplasty patches the drum with a graft, usually temporalis fascia or tragal cartilage, laid under the remnant through the canal or through a postauricular incision. Tympanoplasty adds reconstruction of the ossicular chain with a reshaped ossicle or a prosthesis. Stapedectomy replaces a stapes fixed by otosclerosis with a piston through a small opening into the vestibule. Mastoidectomy for cholesteatoma drills away the mastoid air cells with a high-speed burr under constant irrigation to expose and remove the sac, either keeping the posterior canal wall (combined approach) or taking it down to leave an open cavity. A cochlear implant uses a cortical mastoidectomy and a posterior tympanotomy, a window drilled between the facial nerve and the chorda tympani, to reach the round window; the electrode array is fed into the scala tympani and the receiver is seated in a pocket or well on the skull behind the ear.

All of this happens under an operating microscope or endoscope at high magnification, in a space where the facial nerve runs across the medial wall and down through the mastoid in a bony canal that disease may have eroded or development left open. Two requirements follow and they shape the anaesthetic: a field without blood, because one drop obscures a 2 mm structure, and a facial nerve that can announce itself, because the monitor detects contraction of the facial muscles when the nerve is touched or stimulated.

The pain logic is modest. The incision is skin, periosteum and fascia with nothing under tension: score 2. The reasons patients stay overnight or return are vomiting and vertigo, since the operation works next to, and sometimes opens, the labyrinth.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative review
**Why:** The surgeon is operating on an organ of hearing and balance, often in a patient who cannot hear well in the other ear either.
**Anaesthesia:** Establish how the patient communicates and whether a hearing aid in the other ear can stay in until induction and go back in recovery. Ask about vertigo and previous postoperative vomiting. Cochlear implant candidates include infants around a year old and children with syndromic deafness: Jervell and Lange-Nielsen syndrome couples congenital deafness with a long QT interval, so a deaf child needs an ECG on file; others have craniofacial, cardiac or renal associations. Adults for implantation are often elderly, and many units now implant them as day cases or under local anaesthesia with sedation. Stapes surgery and transcanal myringoplasty are also done under local anaesthesia in cooperative adults, which lets the surgeon test hearing on the table.

### 2. Induction and airway
**Why:** The head will be turned away, draped and inaccessible, and the surgeon will be drilling beside it for hours.
**Anaesthesia:** A reinforced tracheal tube is the default for mastoid surgery and implants; a flexible laryngeal mask is reasonable for shorter transcanal work and gives the quieter emergence. Secure it to the side away from the surgery. If you intubate with a relaxant use a single dose of rocuronium 0.3-0.6 mg/kg and note the time, or intubate with remifentanil and propofol alone. Reversal with sugammadex before the nerve monitor is tested removes the uncertainty. Start propofol and remifentanil TIVA.

### 3. Position and facial nerve monitor
**Why:** The head is rotated to bring the mastoid uppermost. The surgeon places needle electrodes in orbicularis oculi and orbicularis oris and tests the system.
**Anaesthesia:** Rotate the head only within the range the neck allowed awake, and check that the jugular veins on the dependent side are not kinked, since venous congestion shows up as ooze in the ear. Tilt 10-15 degrees head up. The table is turned away, so lines and circuit need extensions and the cannula and blood pressure cuff must be reachable. The surgeon confirms the monitor with a tap test; your confirmation is a train-of-four count. Facial electromyographic responses are unreliable at low counts and were present in every patient once the count exceeded two in the one study that mapped it, so four twitches before the drill is the safe rule.

### 4. Infiltration, incision and graft harvest
**Why:** The surgeon infiltrates the postauricular skin and the canal with lidocaine and adrenaline for haemostasis, incises behind the ear, lifts the periosteum and takes a sheet of temporalis fascia for the graft.
**Anaesthesia:** Adrenaline absorption gives a brief tachycardia and hypertension. Local anaesthetic infiltrated around the stylomastoid foramen or into a dehiscent canal can block the facial nerve itself, producing a silent monitor and a temporary palsy in recovery; it resolves with the local anaesthetic, and knowing what was injected saves an anxious afternoon.

### 5. Establishing the field
**Why:** Bleeding in the middle ear is mucosal and capillary. The surgeon cannot tie or pack it without obscuring the anatomy.
**Anaesthesia:** The same logic as sinus surgery. Remifentanil with propofol lowers pressure and middle ear blood flow and produces good conditions without a second hypotensive drug; it outperformed dexmedetomidine for this purpose in a direct comparison. Aim for a heart rate of 50-60 beats/min and a mean arterial pressure modestly below baseline (60-70 mmHg in a fit adult), less of a reduction in the elderly and the vascular patient, who is head up. Normocapnia. Avoid nitrous oxide from the start.

### 6. Mastoid drilling
**Why:** The burr removes air cells to expose the antrum, the sac and the landmarks: the lateral semicircular canal, the facial nerve, the sigmoid sinus behind and the dura above.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the long phase, and the patient must not move at all: a cough with a burr spinning beside the facial nerve or the sigmoid sinus is the injury. With no relaxant, immobility is remifentanil at an adequate rate (commonly 0.15-0.3 microgram/kg/min, or an effect-site target of 4-6 ng/mL) with processed EEG monitoring to keep hypnosis adequate under TIVA. Irrigation fluid cools the patient over hours; warm from the start. A breach of the sigmoid sinus bleeds briskly and, with the head up, can entrain air; the surgeon packs it and you level the table. Drilling is loud enough to threaten hearing in the operated ear, which is the surgeon's concern, but it also means a lightly anaesthetised patient has a stimulus you might not expect.

### 7. Middle ear dissection and ossicular work
**Why:** Disease is lifted off the ossicles, the stapes footplate and the facial nerve; the chain is rebuilt or the stapes replaced.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon may stimulate the facial nerve deliberately to confirm it; the monitor's sound is your reassurance too. Manipulation near the stapes and oval window stirs the perilymph and is the origin of postoperative vertigo and vomiting. This is the most delicate microsurgery of the case: steady depth, no changes of rate or position.

### 8. Graft placement
**Why:** The fascia is laid under the drum remnant on a bed of absorbable sponge and the middle ear is closed as a sealed air space.
**Anaesthesia:** This step is the historical reason for avoiding nitrous oxide. It diffuses into a closed middle ear faster than nitrogen leaves, raising the pressure and tending to lift an underlay graft; when it is stopped the reverse occurs and the drum is drawn in. Two randomised trials found no difference in graft take or hearing with and without nitrous oxide, so the effect on outcome is unproven. The author's judgement is that there is still nothing to gain from it here: TIVA is already running for the field and for nausea.

### 9. Cochlear implant: electrode insertion and testing
**Why:** The array is advanced slowly through the round window to preserve residual hearing, and the audiologist then tests the device by neural response telemetry and sometimes by the electrically evoked stapedius reflex.
**Anaesthesia:** Insertion takes a minute or more of complete stillness. Stapedius reflex thresholds are raised by volatile anaesthetics in a dose-dependent way and abolished by relaxants, another reason for TIVA without paralysis; neural response telemetry is unaffected by anaesthesia. Once the implant is in, monopolar diathermy is not used on the head. In small children the blood loss from the scalp flap and bone well is proportionally greater and the case is long: temperature, glucose and fluid need attention as in [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).

### 10. Closure and head bandage
**Why:** The wound is closed in layers, the canal packed with dressing, and a mastoid pressure bandage wound around the head.
**Anaesthesia:** The bandage needs the head lifted and turned repeatedly, which moves the tube in the trachea. Keep the remifentanil running until it is on. Give paracetamol 1 g IV, an NSAID or parecoxib 40 mg IV, a small dose of long-acting opioid and the second antiemetic. The surgeon may infiltrate bupivacaine at closure.

### 11. Emergence and recovery
**Why:** A cough or Valsalva transmits pressure through the Eustachian tube to the graft and prosthesis and through the perilymph after stapes surgery.
**Anaesthesia:** Extubate on a remifentanil infusion tapering to a low rate, or remove a laryngeal mask awake and undisturbed, operated ear uppermost. Remifentanil ranks highest for preventing severe emergence cough. In recovery the surgeon wants to see the face move: ask the patient to smile and close the eyes, and record it. Vertigo and nystagmus mean labyrinthine irritation; treat with an antiemetic that has vestibular action (cyclizine 50 mg IV or prochlorperazine 12.5 mg IM) and keep the head still.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a skin and periosteal wound behind the ear, in the territory of the great auricular and lesser occipital nerves, plus a canal incision supplied by the auriculotemporal nerve and the auricular branch of the vagus. The surgeon's infiltration covers all of it for the first hours and long-acting local anaesthetic at closure extends that into the evening. The systemic plan is paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, regular for three to five days, with a few doses of oral opioid available for the first night after mastoid surgery. A great auricular nerve block is a reasonable addition for a large postauricular wound in a patient who cannot have an NSAID; that is author's judgement, and there is no PROSPECT guideline for ear surgery.

Keep intraoperative long-acting opioid small, because nausea is the outcome that matters. Middle ear surgery is one of the operations with the highest baseline risk of postoperative nausea and vomiting: the surgery stimulates the labyrinth directly, the patients are often young and the anaesthetic is long. The consensus guideline approach is multimodal prophylaxis: TIVA, dexamethasone, a 5-HT3 antagonist, and a third agent for those with a history. One trial of hypotensive agents is worth knowing: patients given magnesium as the hypotensive adjunct had less pain, shivering and nausea afterwards than those given remifentanil, a reminder that high-dose remifentanil leaves the patient with nothing on board and possibly hyperalgesic. Bridge it.

Expect worst pain of about 3 out of 10 on the first evening and day 1, falling quickly. The pressure bandage causes as much complaint as the wound and comes off the next day. Pain that increases after day 2 with swelling behind the ear is a haematoma or infection.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Facial nerve palsy in recovery. Immediate and complete is local anaesthetic or an injured nerve; the surgeon decides whether to wait for the local anaesthetic to wear off or re-explore. Delayed palsy over days is oedema.
- Relaxant and the monitor. Partial block allows monitoring in studies, with amplitude falling as block deepens, but a reduced signal in a nerve already compromised by disease is a weak safety margin. The defensible default is no relaxant after intubation and a documented train-of-four of four before drilling.
- Controlled hypotension. No outcome data define a safe floor. Head-up position plus hypotension in an elderly implant recipient is the combination to avoid; accept a wetter field.
- Local anaesthesia with sedation. Suits stapes surgery, transcanal myringoplasty and, increasingly, cochlear implantation in frail adults. Sedation must be light enough for the patient to keep still and report vertigo; drilling noise and the sensation of irrigation are the limits of tolerance.
- Children. Cochlear implants around the first birthday, sometimes bilateral in one sitting (four hours or more). Recent otitis media is common. Emergence delirium with a bandaged head and no hearing is distressing: the parent in recovery early.
- Cholesteatoma complications. A fistula of the lateral semicircular canal gives severe vertigo afterwards; an eroded tegmen can leak cerebrospinal fluid; intracranial extension (abscess, sigmoid sinus thrombosis) is the emergency variant and is managed as a neurosurgical case with an arterial line and a critical care bed.
- Revision surgery. Landmarks are gone and the facial nerve may lie exposed: the monitor matters most here.
- Bone-anchored hearing aid. A short scalp procedure with a titanium fixture, often under local anaesthesia; none of the middle ear considerations apply.
- For the same field requirements in the nose see [septoplasty, turbinate surgery and functional endoscopic sinus surgery](septoplasty-turbinate-surgery-and-functional-endoscopic.html); for facial nerve monitoring outside the temporal bone see [parotidectomy and submandibular gland excision](parotidectomy-and-submandibular-gland-excision.html); for the antiemetic strategy see [nausea, vomiting and day-case anaesthesia](../a/a11-ponv-day-case.html).
- Invasive monitoring, blood and critical care are not needed for routine ear surgery. They enter with intracranial complications, sigmoid sinus injury and the patient's own disease.

## Key sources

The field technique rests on small randomised trials with surrogate outcomes (laser Doppler flow and surgeon-rated field scores); none measures harm from hypotension. The facial nerve monitoring studies are small and physiological. The nitrous oxide trials are small and probably underpowered, so the absence of an effect on graft take is not proof of safety; the physiology of pressure change is undisputed. Remifentanil rates, pressure targets, the train-of-four rule, the effect of volatile agents on stapedius reflex testing and the association of deafness with long QT syndromes are consensus statements or author's judgement and are unsourced here.
