## What the surgeon is doing and why

A supratentorial tumour is a mass inside a rigid box. The three common ones behave differently. A glioma infiltrates brain, has no capsule, and is removed from the inside out until the surgeon reaches tissue that looks or functions like brain; the aim is maximal safe resection, because extent of resection buys survival and a new deficit costs it. A metastasis is a discrete ball with a great deal of surrounding oedema and usually shells out. A meningioma grows from the dura, is outside the brain, takes its blood supply from dural and scalp arteries, and may sit on or invade a venous sinus: the surgeon devascularises it at its base first, then debulks it and peels the capsule off the cortex. The first two are short of blood loss and long on oedema. The third can bleed like a vascular case.

The exposure is the same for all of them. The head is fixed in a three-point pin clamp so that navigation stays registered and the microscope has a still target. The scalp is opened as a flap, the muscle and pericranium are stripped from the bone, burr holes are drilled and joined with a craniotome, and the bone flap is lifted off the dura. From that moment the surgeon needs one thing from you above all: a brain that sits slack inside the bone edge when the dura is opened, so that it does not herniate through the durotomy, strangle its own cortical veins on the bone margin, and need hard retraction to reach the tumour. Brain retraction is ischaemia by another name, and a tight brain converts a clean operation into a swollen one.

The physiology you control is the content of the box: brain tissue water, blood volume (arterial via carbon dioxide and anaesthetic agents, venous via head position, neck rotation, airway pressure and coughing) and cerebrospinal fluid. The background is in [neuroanaesthesia essentials](../a/a08-neuroanaesthesia-essentials.html). The patient who arrives drowsy, with midline shift on the scan and already on dexamethasone, has no compliance left: for that patient, induction, laryngoscopy and pinning are each capable of producing a pressure wave, and the plan is built around avoiding all three.

At the other end, the surgeon wants an examination. A patient who is awake, moving all four limbs and speaking ten minutes after the dressing is evidence that the operation went as intended. A patient who is not is a diagnostic problem that costs a CT scan and sometimes a return to theatre. The anaesthetic therefore has to be deep and still for hours and then gone in minutes, with a blood pressure that does not surge into a fresh resection cavity.

The pain logic is easily underestimated. Brain has no nociceptors, but scalp, temporalis, pericranium and dura do, and up to two-thirds of patients report moderate to severe pain after craniotomy. It is a somatic, superficial pain in the territory of six named nerves on each side, which is why a scalp block works, and it is front-loaded: worst on the day of surgery and day 1, largely settled by day 3. Flaps that divide temporalis or the suboccipital muscles hurt more than a vertex flap. The constraint is that the analgesia must not sedate, constrict pupils to the point of uselessness, or depress ventilation and raise carbon dioxide in a patient with a swollen brain.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative assessment and induction
**Why:** The surgeon has chosen the approach from the scan. The same scan tells you how little reserve there is: midline shift, effaced ventricles and oedema mean the compliance curve is on its steep part.
**Anaesthesia:** Record the Glasgow Coma Score, pupils, speech and limb power yourself, because you will be comparing against them in recovery. Continue dexamethasone and anticonvulsants on the morning of surgery. Avoid sedative premedication in a patient with mass effect: hypoventilation raises carbon dioxide and intracranial pressure before you have control of either. Induction is a matter of protecting cerebral perfusion pressure while preventing the laryngoscopy surge: propofol titrated, remifentanil or fentanyl in a dose that covers intubation, a full dose of non-depolarising relaxant confirmed with a nerve stimulator before the blade goes in, and a vasopressor in hand. Coughing on the tube is a large, avoidable rise in intracranial pressure.

### 2. Lines, tube security and eye protection
**Why:** After pinning and draping the patient is turned away, the table is rotated, and nothing can be re-sited without stopping the operation.
**Anaesthesia:** Arterial line before induction when the brain is tight, after it otherwise; transduce at the external auditory meatus if you are thinking in terms of cerebral perfusion pressure. Two reliable cannulae with extensions, a urinary catheter (mannitol will produce a diuresis of a litre or more), temperature probe and neuromuscular monitor. Tape the tube rather than tie it, since a tie around the neck obstructs jugular drainage. Eyes are taped and padded, then waterproofed against skin preparation running into them.

### 3. Pin fixation
**Why:** Three pins are driven through scalp and periosteum into the outer table under a set spring force to hold the skull rigid for navigation and microsurgery.
**Anaesthesia:** Pinning is the most intense stimulus of the operation and it arrives when the surgical team is busy and you may be distracted by lines. Unblunted, it produces an abrupt rise in heart rate and arterial pressure which, in a non-compliant brain, becomes a rise in intracranial pressure. Three methods work and are usually combined: a remifentanil bolus of 0.5-1 microgram/kg IV or a propofol bolus 30-60 seconds before the pins, local anaesthetic infiltrated at the pin sites, or a scalp block placed after induction that covers the pin sites as well as the incision. Tell the surgeon to wait for your word. Confirm full neuromuscular block: a patient who moves in pins risks scalp laceration and cervical injury.

### 4. Final head position and scalp block
**Why:** The head is rotated, flexed or extended to bring the lesion to the top of the field and let the brain fall away from the approach under gravity.
**Anaesthesia:** Check what the position has done to the neck. Extreme rotation or flexion kinks the internal jugular veins and the tube: keep two finger breadths between chin and sternum, and a shoulder roll under the ipsilateral shoulder when the head is turned beyond 45 degrees. Venous obstruction at the neck is the commonest correctable cause of a tight brain and it is set at this step. Recheck tube position and airway pressure after the final move, then 15-30 degrees of head up. If a scalp block was not done before pinning, do it now: supraorbital and supratrochlear, zygomaticotemporal, auriculotemporal, greater and lesser occipital nerves, with ropivacaine 0.5% or levobupivacaine 0.375% to a total within the weight-based limit. The scalp is vascular and absorption is fast, so calculate the dose including what the surgeon will add to the incision line with adrenaline.

### 5. Scalp flap, burr holes and bone flap
**Why:** The scalp is incised and reflected, muscle is stripped from bone, burr holes are placed and the craniotome cuts between them. The dura is separated from the inner table as the saw passes.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the time to deliver brain relaxation, so that it has taken effect when the dura opens 20-30 minutes later. Mannitol 0.5-1 g/kg IV over 15-20 minutes, or hypertonic saline (for example 3% at 3-5 mL/kg IV), given as the skin is incised. The Cochrane review of trials in craniotomy found fewer tense brains with hypertonic saline than with mannitol (risk ratio 0.60), on low-quality evidence and with no outcome data, so either is defensible: mannitol brings a diuresis, a transient rise in intravascular volume and later hypovolaemia; hypertonic saline brings a sodium and chloride load without the diuresis. Scalp bleeding is controlled with clips and is usually modest. The craniotome crossing a venous sinus is a moment of possible sudden bleeding and of venous air embolism in a head-up patient: watch end-tidal carbon dioxide during the cut when the flap is near the midline.

### 6. Dural opening: the brain is inspected
**Why:** The surgeon tests dural tension with a finger before opening. A slack, pulsating dura is opened; a tense one is a request to you.
**Anaesthesia:** If the brain is tight, work through the list in order of speed and harm. Head position and neck veins. Airway pressure, tube patency and adequate neuromuscular block. Depth of anaesthesia and blood pressure. Arterial carbon dioxide: lower it to 30-35 mmHg and confirm with a blood gas, because the end-tidal gap is unreliable in a long case. If a volatile is running, turn it off and change to propofol, which reduces cerebral blood volume where sevoflurane above 1 MAC increases it. A second dose of osmotic agent. A propofol bolus. After that the options are surgical: ventricular or cisternal drainage of cerebrospinal fluid, or a larger decompression. Hyperventilation below 30 mmHg trades volume for ischaemia and is a short bridge only.

### 7. Corticotomy and tumour resection
**Why:** Under the microscope, with navigation, ultrasound or fluorescence, the surgeon enters the tumour and debulks it with suction, bipolar and ultrasonic aspirator, working outward to the margin. For glioma near eloquent cortex, motor mapping may be done asleep, or the whole operation is done as an [awake craniotomy](awake-craniotomy.html).
**Anaesthesia:** A long, steady phase with almost no surgical stimulus, since brain is insensate. Remifentanil requirements fall and blood pressure drifts down: support it with a vasopressor infusion to keep cerebral perfusion pressure, particularly under retractors. Maintain normothermia, normoglycaemia (dexamethasone pushes glucose up) and normovolaemia with isotonic crystalloid; avoid hypotonic and glucose-containing fluids, which add brain water. If motor evoked potentials or cortical mapping are in use, the neuromuscular blocker is allowed to wear off or is omitted after intubation and the technique is intravenous. A seizure under anaesthesia appears as unexplained tachycardia, hypertension and a rising carbon dioxide; during cortical stimulation the surgeon treats it with iced saline on the cortex and you add a propofol bolus.

### 8. The vascular meningioma and the venous sinus
**Why:** A convexity or parasagittal meningioma is fed by the middle meningeal and scalp arteries and may be attached to the superior sagittal sinus. The surgeon coagulates the dural base first, and may have had the tumour embolised beforehand.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the variant that needs blood. Bleeding is continuous from the moment the flap is turned until the base is controlled, and a sinus tear is sudden, large and accompanied by air entrainment. Crossmatched blood in theatre, two large cannulae, a rapid infuser available, and early use of the [major haemorrhage](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html) pathway. Flood the field, lower the head and ask for bone wax or packing if end-tidal carbon dioxide falls abruptly. Coagulopathy after a large meningioma resection is recognised: measure it rather than assume it.

### 9. Haemostasis and closure
**Why:** The cavity is lined with haemostatic material and inspected at a normal pressure. The dura is closed watertight, the bone flap plated back and the scalp closed in two layers, often over a subgaleal drain.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon may ask for a Valsalva manoeuvre or for the pressure to be raised to the patient's normal to test haemostasis: do it deliberately, to a named figure. Stop neuromuscular block dosing after the dura is closed but keep the patient still until the pins are out, using remifentanil. Give paracetamol 1 g IV and the antiemetics now. Bone flap replacement and scalp closure are stimulating again after a long quiet period. If the scalp block was placed more than four or five hours earlier, ask the surgeon to infiltrate the wound at closure or repeat the block, since post-closure blocks gave the longest benefit in the pooled trials. A small dose of longer-acting opioid (fentanyl 50-100 micrograms or morphine 2-5 mg IV) before the remifentanil is stopped prevents the abrupt pain that the NeuroMorfeo remifentanil arms recorded.

### 10. Pin removal and emergence
**Why:** The pins come out, the head dressing goes on, and the surgeon waits for a neurological examination.
**Anaesthesia:** Pin removal and the head bandage move the tube and are the usual trigger for coughing. Keep remifentanil at a low rate (0.05-0.1 micrograms/kg/min) through bandaging, reverse neuromuscular block fully with sugammadex or neostigmine against a train-of-four ratio above 0.9, and extubate as soon as the patient obeys. Volatile and intravenous maintenance gave equivalent wake-up times in the randomised comparison, so the agent matters less than how it is run. Treat emergence hypertension promptly, because sustained hypertension in the first hours is linked in case-control data to post-operative haematoma and a ceiling of about 160 mmHg systolic is the usual working limit: labetalol 5-10 mg IV increments or esmolol, drawn up before the drapes come down. Do not extubate a patient who was obtunded before surgery, has had a long operation with major blood loss, or shows a swollen brain at closure: transfer ventilated and wake later.

### 11. Recovery and the first night
**Why:** The surgeon's complications declare themselves in the first six hours: haematoma in the cavity or under the flap, oedema, seizure and venous infarction.
**Anaesthesia:** Hourly neurological observations, the head up 30 degrees, systolic pressure limits written down with a named drug. A fall in conscious level, a new deficit or a new pupil is a scan, not a wait. Pain is treated with the regular non-opioids and small titrated opioid doses; an opioid-induced rise in carbon dioxide is a rise in intracranial pressure, so the dose is small and the observation close. Sodium is measured daily: both the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone and cerebral salt wasting occur, and dexamethasone drives glucose.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is scalp, muscle, periosteum and dura, all somatic and all supplied by nerves that can be blocked at the scalp, so the analgesic strategy is regional plus regular non-opioids, with opioid as rescue. That is also the PROSPECT recommendation for craniotomy: paracetamol, NSAIDs, an intravenous dexmedetomidine infusion and a regional technique, either incision-site infiltration or a scalp nerve block, with opioids for breakthrough.

The scalp block is the centre of the plan. In the meta-analysis of seven small trials it lowered pain by about 1.6 points at one hour, with benefit to 6-8 hours when placed before surgery and to 12 hours when placed after closure, and it reduced opioid use in the first 24 hours. It also covers pinning and incision, which is the argument for placing it after induction, and the argument for topping it up or infiltrating at closure in a long case. There is no catheter option, and rebound is not a clinical problem because the pain is already falling when the block wears off.

Paracetamol is regular from the end of surgery. NSAIDs are recommended by PROSPECT and are effective for this somatic pain, but most neurosurgeons will not accept them in the first 24 hours because a post-operative haematoma is catastrophic in a way that a wound haematoma elsewhere is not. The defensible default, as author's judgement, is to withhold non-selective NSAIDs until the first post-operative scan or the first uneventful 24 hours and then add a short course, or to use a COX-2 selective agent early where the surgeon agrees. Dexmedetomidine as an intraoperative infusion (0.2-0.5 micrograms/kg/h) is supported for analgesia and also smooths emergence; it is optional and costs some bradycardia and hypotension. Dexamethasone given for oedema contributes.

Opioid rescue is small doses of oral oxycodone or intravenous fentanyl or morphine, titrated against conscious level. Codeine has a long tradition here and little to recommend it. Avoid long-acting opioids and gabapentinoids in the first 24 hours, when sedation is a diagnostic problem.

Escalation: headache that is severe, rising, or accompanied by drowsiness, vomiting or a new sign is intracranial until imaging says otherwise, and is not treated as a pain problem. Wound pain with a boggy flap is a subgaleal collection.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The tight brain before induction. Drowsy, vomiting, with shift on the scan: arterial line first, no sedative premedication, osmotic therapy started early, intravenous maintenance, and a plan to stay intubated.
- Emergency variant. The patient with a tumour who deteriorates acutely from haemorrhage into the tumour or obstructive hydrocephalus comes as an emergency with a full stomach and a failing conscious level; the principles are those of the [traumatic brain injury](traumatic-brain-injury.html) card and the surgeon may place an [external ventricular drain](ventriculoperitoneal-shunt-and-external-ventricular-drain.html) first.
- Lateral and park bench positions for temporal, parietal and occipital approaches: see [positioning](../a/a14-positioning.html). Posterior fossa tumours are a different operation with different risks: see [posterior fossa surgery](posterior-fossa-surgery.html).
- Seizures. Anticonvulsant prophylaxis is surgeon-dependent; levetiracetam 500-1,000 mg IV at induction is common. A patient who fails to wake may be in non-convulsive status.
- Venous air embolism. Not confined to the sitting position: any head-up craniotomy near a sinus can entrain air.
- Delayed emergence. Work through residual drug, carbon dioxide, temperature, glucose and sodium quickly, then scan. The threshold for imaging is low and the time allowed is short, typically 15-30 minutes.
- Pneumocephalus. Nitrous oxide is avoided after dural closure and in any re-operation within weeks.
- Frontal lobe tumours. Disinhibition and agitation on emergence are common and are managed with patience and small doses of propofol or dexmedetomidine, not with opioids.
- Steroid effects. Hyperglycaemia needs treating intraoperatively; long courses of dexamethasone mean adrenal suppression and a fragile skin and airway.

## Key sources

The analgesic recommendations rest on a PROSPECT systematic review of 53 randomised trials, most of them small, and on a meta-analysis of scalp block trials totalling 320 patients: the direction is consistent and the precision is low. The volatile against intravenous question has one good multicentre equivalence trial, in patients without raised intracranial pressure, and no outcome data in patients with a tight brain, where the preference for propofol is physiology and consensus. The osmotic agent comparison is low-quality evidence on a surrogate. The blood pressure threshold on emergence, the timing of NSAIDs and the extubation criteria are author's judgement and common practice, not trial results.
