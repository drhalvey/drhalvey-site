## What the surgeon is doing and why

The facial skeleton is a set of thin plates held between thicker buttresses: vertical pillars beside the nose, through the zygoma and behind the maxilla, and horizontal bars at the frontal bone, orbital rims, palate and mandible. Fractures follow the weak lines between the buttresses, and repair means restoring the buttresses with small titanium plates so that the face regains its height, width and projection, the eyes sit at the right level and depth, and the teeth meet as they did.

The teeth are the surgeon's reference. If the upper and lower teeth are brought into their old bite and held there with arch bars, wires or screws and elastics (intermaxillary fixation), then the mandible and the tooth-bearing maxilla must be in the right place, and plating proceeds from that known position outwards. This is why the surgeon wants the mouth closed with nothing between the teeth for any fracture involving the dental arches.

The common operations, in rising order of trouble:

- Nasal bones: closed manipulation within two weeks of injury, ten minutes.
- Zygoma: the cheekbone is pushed in and rotated. It is elevated through a temporal (Gillies) or intraoral approach and, if unstable, plated at one to three points (frontozygomatic suture, infraorbital rim, zygomatic buttress).
- Orbital floor: a blowout fracture lets orbital fat and the inferior rectus drop into the maxillary sinus, giving double vision and a sunken eye. Through a lower lid or transconjunctival incision the contents are lifted back and the defect is bridged with a mesh or sheet.
- Mandible: fractures at the angle, body, parasymphysis and condyle, often two at once. Open reduction through the mouth (or the skin for the condyle and some angles) and plating along the lines of tension.
- Le Fort I, II and III and naso-orbito-ethmoid fractures: the midface separated from the skull at one of three levels, often impacted backwards. It is disimpacted, the bite is set, and the buttresses are plated, with coronal, lower lid and intraoral approaches. Panfacial injuries combine these with the mandible.

Most are done days after the injury, once swelling has fallen and other injuries have been dealt with. The pain logic is periosteum: exposure and plating of bone that was already broken. Fixation removes the fracture movement that was the main source of pain, and the nerves running through the fractures (inferior alveolar, infraorbital) are usually numb already. This is a mild-to-moderate pain operation. The card is about the airway, the eye and the injuries that came with the face.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment: the injury behind the fracture
**Why:** The force that breaks a midface also injures the brain, the cervical spine and the eye; the surgeon times the repair around those.
**Anaesthesia:** Read the trauma imaging yourself: head CT, cervical spine clearance, skull base. Ask whether an ophthalmologist has seen the eye, because a ruptured globe changes everything ([penetrating eye injury](penetrating-eye-injury.html)). Then the airway. Measure mouth opening and ask why it is limited. Pain and spasm relax with anaesthesia. A depressed zygomatic arch pressing on the coronoid process, or a displaced condyle, is a mechanical block that does not. A bilateral anterior mandibular fracture frees the tongue's attachment, and the patient holds the airway open by sitting forward; supine and anaesthetised, the tongue falls back. Look for loose or missing teeth and account for the missing ones on a chest radiograph. Alcohol, drugs and a stomach full of blood are common on the day of injury and less so at delayed repair.

### 2. Choosing the route for the tube
**Why:** The surgeon needs the mouth closed to set the bite for arch fractures, the nose free for nasal and naso-orbito-ethmoid repair, and both for panfacial injuries.
**Anaesthesia:** Decide with the surgeon before the patient arrives.

```
Fracture                          Usual route
--------------------------------  ---------------------------------
Zygoma, orbit, nasal bones        Oral (south-facing or reinforced)
Mandible, Le Fort I               Nasal
Le Fort II/III or NOE needing     Submental, or nasal then switch,
  the bite set AND nasal repair     or tracheostomy
Panfacial with head or chest      Tracheostomy
  injury, long ventilation
```

A nasal tube in the presence of a skull base fracture is the old prohibition, founded on case reports of intracranial passage. In the one case-control study of blind nasal intubation in the field, no tube went intracranially and complications of skull base fracture were no more frequent (24% against 25%). Many units accept a nasal tube railroaded over a flexible bronchoscope under vision when the fracture does not cross the cribriform area; a comminuted anterior skull base with a cerebrospinal fluid leak remains a reason to avoid the nose. That is a decision for the surgeon and you together, case by case.

### 3. Induction and intubation
**Why:** The repair needs a secure tube that will not be dislodged by moving bone fragments.
**Anaesthesia:** For isolated fractures with good mouth opening, a standard intravenous induction and a videolaryngoscope. Laryngoscopy is often easier than expected with a mobile mandibular fracture, and facemask ventilation harder: an unstable jaw gives a poor seal and no jaw thrust. Mechanical trismus, gross swelling, or a patient who can only breathe sitting up calls for awake tracheal intubation; the Difficult Airway Society guideline lists reduced mouth opening and head and neck pathology among its indications, and the technique is described under [dental and neck abscess](dental-and-neck-abscess.html) and in [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html). Keep the neck neutral unless the spine is cleared. On the day of injury treat the stomach as full of blood.

### 4. Submental intubation
**Why:** It gives the surgeon a closed mouth and a free nose without a tracheostomy, for the patient who will not need prolonged ventilation.
**Anaesthesia:** After oral intubation with a reinforced tube whose connector comes off easily (test it beforehand), the surgeon makes a 2 cm incision beside the midline under the chin and passes a clip bluntly through the floor of the mouth, hugging the inner surface of the mandible. Pre-oxygenate, then the pilot balloon is drawn out through the tunnel first, the tube is disconnected, its connector removed, the tube end pulled through, and the connector and circuit replaced. Disconnection is under a minute in practised hands. Check depth again by auscultation and capnography, because the tube commonly advances or withdraws by a few centimetres during the pull-through, and have the surgeon suture it to the skin. A systematic review of 2,229 patients found a mean time of 10 minutes and complications in 7%, mostly superficial infection and scarring, with 15 cases of damage to the tube or pilot balloon. At the end the sequence is reversed to an oral tube before waking, or the patient is extubated through the submental wound if staying wired.

### 5. Set-up: eyes, pack, steroids
**Why:** The surgeon operates around both orbits and needs to assess the eyes and the pupil during the case.
**Anaesthesia:** Do not tape the eyes shut without asking: the surgeon may want to place corneal shields or a temporary lid suture and to check the pupils and forced ductions. Lubricate and let them protect. A throat pack, if used, follows the consensus protocol with a visible label and a spoken removal. Dexamethasone 8 mg IV for swelling and nausea, antibiotics, head-up tilt. Arms tucked, lines extended.

### 6. Arch bars and intermaxillary fixation
**Why:** Wires or screws on the upper and lower teeth are joined to hold the patient's own bite while the fractures are plated.
**Anaesthesia:** The mouth is now shut. Anything oral (temperature probe, orogastric tube, pack) is out or accounted for before the last wire. Neuromuscular block helps the surgeon reduce a displaced mandibular fracture against masseter and pterygoid pull; give it if asked, and monitor it. The wire ends are sharp: more glove punctures happen at this step than anywhere else in the specialty.

### 7. Mandibular fracture: exposure and plating
**Why:** Through an intraoral incision the fracture is exposed subperiosteally, reduced with the bite as the guide and fixed with miniplates; condylar and some angle fractures are approached through the skin near the facial nerve.
**Anaesthesia:** Steady, moderately stimulating, little blood. For skin approaches near the marginal mandibular or main facial nerve the surgeon may use a nerve stimulator: know before you top up the relaxant. Once the plates are on, most surgeons release the fixation and check that the jaw closes into the bite on its own.

### 8. Zygoma: elevation and fixation
**Why:** An elevator passed under the temporalis fascia or through the mouth lifts the cheekbone outwards and forwards; unstable fractures are plated.
**Anaesthesia:** A closed elevation is short and intensely stimulating for a few seconds: deepen beforehand. Pressure on the globe and traction around the orbit can trigger the oculocardiac reflex. Afterwards the cheek must not be pressed on, including by a facemask in recovery: tell the recovery staff which side.

### 9. Orbital floor repair
**Why:** The surgeon retracts the globe upwards, frees the periorbita and inferior rectus from the fracture, and slides an implant across the defect. A forced duction test before and after confirms that the muscle moves freely.
**Anaesthesia:** The oculocardiac reflex is expected here: afferent in the trigeminal nerve from traction on the muscle and orbital contents, efferent in the vagus. Bradycardia, junctional rhythm or a pause follows the pull within seconds. Have the surgeon release the traction, check depth and carbon dioxide, and give glycopyrronium 0.2-0.4 mg or atropine 0.6 mg IV if it recurs; the reflex fatigues with repetition. The review of the reflex found it is augmented by rapid-acting opioids such as remifentanil and reduced by anticholinergics and deeper volatile anaesthesia. In children and young adults a trapdoor fracture can clamp the inferior rectus with almost no external bruising (the white-eyed blowout); the reflex is then active before surgery, with nausea, vomiting and bradycardia on upgaze, and repair is urgent because the muscle is ischaemic. After any orbital repair the emergence must be smooth and the patient must not vomit, cough hard or blow the nose, because pressure drives air and blood into the orbit.

### 10. Le Fort and panfacial reconstruction
**Why:** The impacted midface is gripped with disimpaction forceps and pulled forwards, the bite is wired, and the buttresses are rebuilt in a planned sequence, often through a coronal scalp flap for the frontal bar and nasal root.
**Anaesthesia:** A long operation with real blood loss. Disimpaction tears mucosa and vessels at the back of the maxilla and can bleed briskly; the coronal flap oozes from the scalp throughout. Arterial line, two large cannulae, tranexamic acid, warming, urinary catheter, and blood available. Disimpaction also produces trigeminocardiac bradycardia. If the brain is injured, cerebral perfusion pressure sets your blood pressure target and deliberate hypotension is not used. Expect a midface that is more swollen at the end than at the start; with a difficult initial airway, a tracheostomy or a period of postoperative ventilation is decided before the drapes come down, not at the wake-up.

### 11. End of surgery and emergence
**Why:** The surgeon decides whether the jaws stay wired (less common with rigid plates), are held with elastics, or are free.
**Anaesthesia:** Pack out, pharynx and nasopharynx suctioned under vision, stomach emptied through an orogastric tube before the mouth is closed. Awake extubation, sitting up, with antiemetics on board. If the jaws are fixed together: wire cutters taped to the bed, a diagram or a demonstration of which wires to cut, and a nurse who has seen it. Watch the eye on the operated side through recovery (step 9 and the pitfalls).

## Pain plan

The mechanism is periosteal exposure of a fracture that is now stable, so the pain is mild to moderate and often less than before the operation. The plan is paracetamol, an NSAID if the other injuries allow it, dexamethasone, the surgeon's long-acting local anaesthetic at each incision, and oral opioid for two to three days. Nerve blocks (inferior alveolar, mental, infraorbital) are simple, sit within the surgeon's field and are a reasonable request, though the nerves are often already numb from the injury. Analgesics must be liquid or dispersible while the bite is restricted. There is no PROSPECT guideline and no trial evidence specific to this surgery; the plan is consensus and author's judgement.

Expect worst pain of about 3 out of 10 on days 1 and 2 with swelling, settling over the week. Panfacial reconstruction with a coronal flap hurts more and for longer, mostly as scalp and headache, and the opioid plan is set by the other injuries. Rebound is not a feature.

Escalation triggers are the reason this section matters. Severe pain in or behind the eye after orbital or zygomatic surgery, with a tense proptosed globe, a fixed or sluggish pupil and falling vision, is retrobulbar haemorrhage with orbital compartment syndrome: the retina has about an hour or two, and the treatment is immediate lateral canthotomy and cantholysis by whoever is trained and present, not analgesia or a scan. Hourly eye observations (pain, acuity, pupil) are part of the handover for every orbital case. Increasing pain with a changed bite is failed fixation. Pain and swelling rising after day 3 is infection, more common in mandibular fractures open to the mouth.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Mechanical trismus. Zygomatic arch on coronoid, or a displaced condyle. It does not relax after induction. Plan awake.
- Bilateral mandibular fracture. The airway is held open by posture. Induce sitting up as far as possible, with the surgeon ready to pull the jaw or tongue forward, or intubate awake.
- Acute airway emergency on the day of injury. Bleeding, swelling and a backward-displaced maxilla obstructing the nasopharynx. Sitting forward, suction, and early front-of-neck access when intubation fails. That patient goes to tracheostomy, not to elective plating.
- Cervical spine. Not cleared means in-line stabilisation and a videolaryngoscope or bronchoscope.
- Skull base fracture and the nasal tube. Evidence of harm is anecdotal and the one comparative study found none, but a comminuted anterior base with a leak is still avoided.
- The lost tooth. Missing teeth are sought on the chest film before, not after.
- Throat pack. Label it, count it, say it.
- Retained wired jaws. Awake extubation, three antiemetics, cutters at the bed, and the knowledge that cutting the wires does not open a swollen airway.
- Retrobulbar haemorrhage. Pain, proptosis, pupil, vision. Minutes matter.
- Oculocardiac and trigeminocardiac reflexes. Orbital floor, zygoma elevation, midface disimpaction.
- Children. Greenstick and trapdoor fractures, tooth buds in the way of plates, and a preference for conservative treatment; see [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).
- Nasal manipulation. A ten-minute day case under a supraglottic airway or facemask with a brief bleed into the pharynx: lateral position and suction at the end.
- Invasive monitoring, blood and critical care. Not needed for isolated fractures. They enter with Le Fort II and III and panfacial repair, a coronal approach, brain injury, a tracheostomy or a decision to leave the patient intubated.

## Key sources

The evidence here is thin and mostly observational. Submental intubation rests on a systematic review of case series. The nasal tube and skull base question rests on case reports on one side and a single small case-control study on the other. The oculocardiac reflex material comes from a narrative review and a case report with literature review.
