## What the surgeon is doing and why

A colectomy removes a length of colon together with the wedge of mesentery that carries its blood supply and its lymph nodes, then either joins the two ends or brings one out as a stoma. For cancer, which is most of the elective work, the oncological operation is the mesentery, not the bowel: the named artery (ileocolic and right colic for a right hemicolectomy, left colic and sigmoid branches or the inferior mesenteric artery for a left-sided resection) is divided at its origin so that the draining nodes come out in one intact envelope. The colon itself is a retroperitoneal organ along the right and left sides, held down by a thin fused plane that the surgeon reopens to lift the bowel off the duodenum, the kidney, the gonadal vessels and the ureter. The flexures are the hard corners: the hepatic flexure sits against the duodenum and the gallbladder, the splenic flexure against the spleen and the tail of the pancreas, and freeing the splenic flexure is what turns a two-hour left colectomy into a three-and-a-half-hour one.

The surgeon has four aims, in order:

1. Devascularise and mobilise the segment without entering the wrong plane (duodenum and ureter are the structures that get injured).
2. Divide the bowel with margins and remove the specimen through the smallest wound that will take it.
3. Join two well-perfused, tension-free ends. The anastomosis is the whole risk of the operation: a leak happens in a few per cent of colonic and rather more of low pelvic joins, declares itself on day 3-7, and carries most of the mortality.
4. Leave a patient whose gut restarts quickly. Ileus is the commonest reason for a long stay, and almost everything you do (opioid dose, fluid volume, antiemetic, how early the patient eats) feeds into it.

Approach decides the pain. A laparoscopic or robotic resection uses four or five ports and one extraction wound of 5-8 cm, usually a Pfannenstiel or a short transverse or periumbilical midline incision; the anastomosis is made outside the abdomen through that wound or inside with staplers. An open resection uses a midline laparotomy or, in some hands for a right colectomy, a transverse incision, which PROSPECT prefers on pain grounds. Emergency resections for obstruction, perforation or toxic colitis are usually open, are often finished as a stoma rather than a join (Hartmann's procedure on the left, an end ileostomy after subtotal colectomy), and belong physiologically with the [emergency laparotomy](emergency-laparotomy.html) card.

The pain logic follows from the wound. After a laparoscopic resection the mechanisms are port sites, the extraction wound and the peritoneal and diaphragmatic stretch of two to three hours of gas: a moderate, short curve that peaks on day 1 and is largely done by day 3. After an open resection the mechanism is a long incision through the linea alba or the oblique muscles, which is under load with every cough, deep breath and transfer; pain on movement stays severe for three to four days and it is this dynamic pain, not pain at rest, that decides whether the patient breathes, coughs and walks. Visceral pain from the anastomosis and the stripped retroperitoneum contributes in the first 24 hours in both. That is why this card scores the laparoscopic operation at 3 and the open one at 4, and why the analgesic plan splits in two.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative optimisation and the pathway
**Why:** The outcome is set as much by the weeks before as by the day. Colorectal cancer patients arrive iron deficient, sometimes malnourished, sometimes just off chemotherapy. The surgeon's enhanced recovery pathway assumes a patient who is fed, carbohydrate loaded, not dehydrated by bowel preparation and expecting to walk on day 0.
**Anaesthesia:** Treat iron deficiency anaemia before elective surgery with intravenous iron if there are two or more weeks; a transfusion on the table is the more harmful substitute. Mechanical bowel preparation with oral antibiotics has returned in many units for left-sided resections and leaves the patient 1-2 L dry: replace it rather than discover it at induction. Clear fluids until two hours before, with a carbohydrate drink. Do not sedate with long-acting premedication. For the GLP-1 agonist patient with nausea or fullness, treat the stomach as full regardless of the fasting clock.

### 2. Induction, neuraxial dose or block, lines
**Why:** The surgeon needs a still, deeply relaxed abdomen, a decompressed stomach and bladder, and a patient who can be tilted to the limits of the table without sliding.
**Anaesthesia:** Tracheal tube, with a rapid sequence induction for any obstructive symptoms. If the plan is intrathecal morphine, place it awake before induction, with or without a small dose of bupivacaine, and tell the ward that the patient needs sedation and respiratory rate observations for 24 hours. If the plan is an epidural for an open resection, site it at T8-T10 awake and test it before induction. Otherwise the TAP blocks go in after induction or the surgeon infiltrates the ports and the extraction wound at the end; PROSPECT's rule that local anaesthetic is not given by two routes at once is a dose-accounting rule, so agree who is giving what. Orogastric tube to empty the stomach for the left upper quadrant port, removed at the end. Urinary catheter. Two cannulae before the arms are tucked, because you will not reach them again. An arterial line is for the patient (significant cardiac or respiratory disease, frailty) or the operation (open, emergency, expected blood loss), not for a routine laparoscopic right hemicolectomy.

### 3. Positioning and securing the patient
**Why:** Gravity is the laparoscopic surgeon's retractor. Small bowel has to fall out of the pelvis and away from the mesenteric root, so the table goes steeply head-down and rolled, then head-up for the flexures, several times.
**Anaesthesia:** Both arms tucked, legs in Lloyd-Davies supports for left-sided work so that a stapler can be passed from below, a non-slip mattress or bean bag, and shoulder supports only if they sit over the acromion and not the root of the neck, where they stretch the brachial plexus. Test the extreme positions before draping and watch the tube: the carina moves cephalad with head-down tilt and pneumoperitoneum, and a tube that was 2 cm above it becomes endobronchial. Calf pressure in the leg supports and the duration of head-down tilt are the risk factors for compartment syndrome of the well leg; ask for the legs to be levelled if the pelvic phase passes three to four hours.

### 4. Pneumoperitoneum and port placement
**Why:** Carbon dioxide at 12-15 mmHg lifts the abdominal wall off the viscera to make a working space. Entry is by open cut-down at the umbilicus or by optical trocar in the left upper quadrant.
**Anaesthesia:** The first insufflation is the moment for the vagal bradycardia or asystole from peritoneal stretch: ask for the gas to be released and it resolves, and an anticholinergic is rarely needed twice. Then the steady-state changes: compliance falls by a third or more, peak pressure rises, functional residual capacity falls further with head-down tilt, and end-tidal CO2 climbs for 20-30 minutes as the gas is absorbed. Raise minute ventilation, use PEEP of 5-8 cmH2O with recruitment after each position change, and accept mild hypercapnia. Venous return falls with the gas and rises with the tilt; the net effect in a euvolaemic patient is a higher afterload and little change in output, and in a dry patient it is hypotension. A sudden fall in end-tidal CO2 with hypotension during entry is gas embolism until proved otherwise; a slow unexplained rise with crepitus is subcutaneous emphysema tracking from a port.

### 5. Vascular pedicle ligation and mesenteric dissection
**Why:** The surgeon identifies the named vessels first (medial-to-lateral approach), divides them at their origin with clips, staples or an energy device, and then lifts the mesocolon off the retroperitoneum in the embryological plane so the nodal package stays intact.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step where the bleeding that converts a laparoscopic case happens: a slipped clip on the ileocolic or inferior mesenteric pedicle, or a torn branch of the gastrocolic trunk on the front of the pancreas during an extended right resection. It is venous, brisk and hard to see laparoscopically. Have the second cannula running and know where the blood is. Traction on the mesenteric root produces a brief fall in pressure and a flush in some patients; it is self-limiting. Keep the neuromuscular block deep: a cough here is a torn vein.

### 6. Colonic mobilisation and the flexures
**Why:** The colon is freed from its lateral attachments and from the omentum; the hepatic flexure is taken off the duodenum, or the splenic flexure off the spleen and pancreas, to give enough length for a tension-free join.
**Anaesthesia:** The table goes head-up and rolled the other way for a flexure, which unloads the diaphragm and drops the venous return at the same time. A capsular tear of the spleen from omental traction is the classic bleed of a left-sided resection and usually stops with pressure and a haemostatic agent. The ureter is identified on the left; if the surgeon cannot find it the case slows and occasionally opens.

### 7. Division of the bowel and specimen extraction
**Why:** The bowel is divided with linear staplers, and the specimen is delivered through the extraction wound inside a wound protector to keep tumour and bacteria off the fat.
**Anaesthesia:** The pneumoperitoneum comes down for an extracorporeal anastomosis, so ventilation becomes easy and the patient can be levelled. The extraction wound is the main somatic pain generator of a laparoscopic resection: this is the wound that the infiltration, the TAP block or the intrathecal morphine is aimed at. If the case has converted to open at any point before this, the analgesic plan changes with it (step 10).

### 8. Anastomosis and the perfusion check
**Why:** Ileocolic joins are usually side-to-side stapled; left-sided joins are end-to-end with a circular stapler passed through the anus, then tested under water with air or by endoscopy. Many surgeons give indocyanine green and look at the bowel ends under near-infrared light before joining them.
**Anaesthesia:** Indocyanine green is given as a bolus on request (commonly 5-10 mg IV, flushed); it reads as a transient false fall in the pulse oximeter. The surgeon is judging perfusion at this moment, so the mean arterial pressure should be at the patient's normal, without a high-dose vasoconstrictor infusion standing in for volume. The fluid evidence is the RELIEF trial: in 2,983 major abdominal operations a restrictive regimen (median 3.7 L to 24 hours) gave no better disability-free survival than a liberal one (6.1 L) and more acute kidney injury. The practical target is a balance 1-2 L positive at 24 hours, a low-dose vasopressor for anaesthetic vasodilatation, and boluses for a reason rather than a reflex.

### 9. Stoma, closure and emergence
**Why:** If the join is high risk, or has been omitted, a loop ileostomy or end colostomy is fashioned through the rectus muscle. The extraction wound and any port over 10 mm are closed in layers.
**Anaesthesia:** Keep the block deep until the fascia of the extraction wound is closed, then reverse fully with quantitative confirmation: residual weakness on top of opioid and a full abdomen is the mechanism of day 0 atelectasis. Give the second antiemetic. Surgeon infiltration or rectus sheath catheters go in now if that is the plan. Remove the orogastric tube. Extubate awake and sitting up. The pathway expects oral fluids within hours and a walk the same evening, which only works if the patient is not nauseated, not hypotensive and not oversedated.

### 10. Conversion to open, and the open resection
**Why:** Adhesions, bleeding, a bulky tumour or failure to progress lead to conversion in a minority of cases; some resections are planned open (a T4 tumour fixed to other organs, a multiply operated abdomen, an emergency).
**Anaesthesia:** A full midline incision changes the pain score to 4 and the plan with it. If there is an epidural, load it. If there is not, the choices at this point are intrathecal morphine already given (adequate for 18-24 hours), bilateral rectus sheath catheters or pre-peritoneal wound catheters placed by the surgeon at closure, or an intravenous lidocaine infusion, each of which PROSPECT accepts for open surgery when an epidural is not feasible. An open resection also brings the larger fluid shifts, the heat loss from exposed bowel, and the case for an arterial line.

### 11. The first 72 hours
**Why:** The surgeon wants a patient who eats, walks and passes flatus, and is watching for the leak.
**Anaesthesia:** Stop intravenous fluid when the patient is drinking, usually the morning of day 1. Regular paracetamol and a COX-2 inhibitor, opioid by mouth and only as rescue. Tachycardia, a new oxygen requirement, atrial fibrillation or a rising pain score on day 3-5 in a patient who had been improving is an anastomotic leak until imaging says otherwise; the response is a surgical review and a CT, not a bigger opioid dose or an epidural top-up.

## Pain plan

The plan follows the wound. For a laparoscopic or robotic colectomy the PROSPECT first-line recommendation is basic analgesia (paracetamol with an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor for colonic surgery) and wound infiltration; the panel could not reach consensus on intrathecal morphine or intravenous lidocaine. The author's practice is to add one of two things to that base. Intrathecal morphine, 100-200 micrograms, covers the visceral and extraction-wound pain of the first night with a single injection and no pump; in a 252-patient trial it improved quality of recovery and analgesia at 24 hours on top of a TAP block, with more itch. Bilateral TAP blocks are the alternative when a spinal is declined or contraindicated, and cover the wound but not the viscera. A thoracic epidural is the wrong tool here: in the one enhanced-recovery trial that tested it against PCA for laparoscopic resection, recovery was a day slower, complications were more frequent (54% against 33%) and nine in ten epidural patients needed vasopressors.

Intravenous lidocaine deserves a plain statement. It was adopted for laparoscopic colectomy largely on the promise of earlier gut function. ALLEGRO randomised 557 patients and found none: 57.3% against 59.0% with return of gut function at 72 hours and no difference in any secondary outcome. It keeps a place as an analgesic for open surgery without an epidural, where PROSPECT recommends it, and it should not run alongside local anaesthetic catheters.

For an open colectomy the mechanism is a loaded abdominal wall and the strongest evidence is still for a thoracic epidural run for 48-72 hours, with rectus sheath or pre-peritoneal catheters, bilateral TAP blocks or lidocaine as the stated alternatives. The epidural's cost is the same hypotension and immobility that hurt the laparoscopic patient, so the defensible default is to reserve it for the planned open operation in a patient with respiratory disease or opioid tolerance, and to use intrathecal morphine plus surgeon-placed catheters for the rest.

The NSAID question is real and unresolved. A meta-analysis of 17 mostly observational studies (26,098 patients) found twice the odds of anastomotic leak with NSAID use, with the signal weaker for selective agents. PROSPECT still recommends them for colonic (not rectal) surgery. A reasonable reading: a COX-2 inhibitor for 48-72 hours for a colonic anastomosis in a patient with normal renal function, none for a low rectal or otherwise fragile join, and surgeons vary on this more than on any other drug on the chart.

Escalation: uncontrolled pain on day 0-1 after laparoscopic surgery is usually shoulder-tip and diaphragmatic and responds to position and time; severe wound pain after conversion needs a catheter technique. Pain that returns after day 2 is a complication.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Right against left. A right hemicolectomy is shorter, needs less tilt and no lithotomy, and has a more reliable anastomosis. Left and sigmoid resections need the splenic flexure, the legs up, and a transanal stapler.
- Subtotal colectomy for acute severe colitis. A sick, steroid-treated, hypoalbuminaemic, often septic patient having a three-hour operation that ends in an ileostomy. Steroid cover, arterial line, and a critical care bed if the colon is toxic or perforated.
- The obstructed colon. A full stomach, third-space losses of several litres, and bowel that will not fit back: plan as an emergency laparotomy, with risk scoring and a low threshold for a stoma.
- Robotic resection. Longer docking and set-up, a fixed table position unless the system has integrated table motion, and restricted access to the patient: everything you need has to be in reach before the arms come in.
- Obesity. Head-down tilt and gas in a patient with a body mass index over 40 may be unventilatable at 15 mmHg; agree a lower pressure and a limit of tilt at the start, and expect conversion.
- Opioid tolerance and chronic pain. The laparoscopic plan fails in these patients more often than the operation predicts: intrathecal morphine plus ketamine, and an early plan for the patient's own long-acting opioid.
- Renal impairment. No NSAID, more caution with a dry fluid strategy, and remember that bowel preparation has already been through the kidneys.
- Urinary retention after intrathecal morphine. Leave the catheter until the morning of day 1.

## Key sources

This is one of the best-evidenced operations in the book. Two PROSPECT guidelines published in 2024 separate the laparoscopic from the open operation and reach different answers, which is the main point of this card. The ERAS Society guideline is consensus built on mixed evidence, and its individual elements have fared differently when tested alone: restrictive fluids were corrected by RELIEF, and lidocaine for gut function by ALLEGRO. The NSAID and leak signal comes almost entirely from observational data and has not been settled by a trial.
