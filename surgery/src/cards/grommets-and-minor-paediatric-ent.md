## What the surgeon is doing and why

The middle ear is an air-filled box that depends on the Eustachian tube to equalise pressure and drain mucus. In young children the tube is short, horizontal and easily blocked by adenoid tissue and viral oedema. Air is then absorbed from the middle ear faster than it is replaced, the pressure falls, and the space fills with a sticky effusion (glue ear) that damps the ossicles and gives the child a conductive hearing loss at the age when speech is being learned. A grommet is a small flanged tube placed through the tympanic membrane to do the Eustachian tube's job from the outside: it ventilates the middle ear to atmospheric pressure until the child's own tube matures. The same operation treats recurrent acute otitis media, where it gives infected fluid a way out.

The surgeon looks down an aural speculum with the operating microscope, clears wax, makes a radial incision of about 2 mm in the anterior-inferior quadrant of the drum (away from the ossicles and the round window), suctions the effusion, and seats the tube in the incision with its inner flange inside the middle ear. Then the head is turned and the other side done. There is no dissection, no bone and no closure. Tympanostomy tube insertion is the most common ambulatory operation in children.

The other operations grouped on this card share its anaesthetic shape: a few minutes of stimulation in or near the airway of a small child. They are examination and suction clearance of the ears, removal of a bead from an ear canal or a nostril, silver nitrate or bipolar cautery to Little's area for nosebleeds, division of a tongue-tie, and manipulation of a fractured nose in an older child.

The pain logic is brief. The drum has a rich sensory supply (auriculotemporal, vagal and glossopharyngeal branches) and the incision hurts for an hour or so, but nothing is under tension and the wound is tiny: the surface mechanism, score 1. What looks like severe pain in recovery is, more often than not, emergence delirium after a short sevoflurane anaesthetic, and the plan is built to prevent both with the same doses.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative assessment
**Why:** The surgeon is operating on the consequence of repeated upper respiratory infection, so the child with a perfectly clear nose seldom appears on this list.
**Anaesthesia:** The question on the day is not whether the child has a runny nose but whether the child is unwell. Clear rhinorrhoea in an otherwise bright, afebrile child with a clear chest is the baseline for this population and a reason to proceed, since blanket cancellation achieves little; fever, purulent secretions, a wet cough, wheeze or a parent who says the child is not right are reasons to defer two to four weeks. The respiratory history that predicts laryngospasm and bronchospasm can be taken in a minute: nocturnal dry cough, wheeze with exercise, recent wheezing, eczema, passive smoke exposure and a recent infection. Look specifically for the syndromic child: trisomy 21 (narrow ear canals that make the surgery slow, a large tongue, a small subglottis, cardiac disease, atlantoaxial instability), cleft palate and craniofacial anomalies. Give paracetamol 15 mg/kg and ibuprofen 10 mg/kg orally on the ward, because nothing given after induction of a five-minute anaesthetic is absorbed in time.

### 2. Induction
**Why:** The surgeon needs a still head under the microscope within a few minutes of the child entering the room.
**Anaesthesia:** Inhalational induction with sevoflurane, with a parent present where that is the unit's practice. Whether to place a cannula for bilateral grommets alone is a real choice. The case against is that the operation is over in the time it takes to site one and that many units have run cannula-free lists for years. The case for is that laryngospasm treated with intravenous propofol or suxamethonium is quicker and more reliable than the intramuscular alternative, and that propofol at the end reduces emergence delirium. The defensible default is a cannula in any child with a respiratory risk factor, a syndrome, an additional procedure, or age under one, and readiness for immediate intramuscular or intraosseous rescue when none is placed: suxamethonium 4 mg/kg IM and atropine 20 microgram/kg IM drawn up or within reach.

### 3. Airway and head position
**Why:** The surgeon turns the head fully to one side to line the ear canal up with the microscope, then to the other.
**Anaesthesia:** A face mask held by you, or a laryngeal mask, both work. The face mask avoids instrumenting an irritable airway and is quickest; it requires you to hold the airway with the head rotated and the surgeon's hands and microscope a few centimetres away, and it pollutes the theatre. A laryngeal mask frees your hands and gives a capnograph trace, at the cost of a second stimulus at removal. Head rotation can obstruct either. Keep the child spontaneously breathing and deep: 1.3 MAC or more before the surgeon starts.

### 4. Microscope, wax and suction
**Why:** The canal is cleaned so the drum can be seen. Suction in the ear is loud and stimulating, and a vagal branch (Arnold's nerve) supplies the posterior canal wall.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step at which a child who seemed settled coughs, breath-holds or moves. Canal stimulation can produce a cough reflex or a bradycardia through the auricular branch of the vagus. Do not let the surgeon start in a light plane; ask for a pause and deepen if the child reacts.

### 5. Myringotomy and tube, first ear
**Why:** The drum is incised in the quadrant furthest from the ossicles and the effusion is aspirated so the tube does not block at once.
**Anaesthesia:** The most stimulating second of the operation. With no cannula and no premedication, this is the time to give intranasal fentanyl 1-2 microgram/kg, divided between the nostrils, so that it peaks at emergence. A trial comparing it with intranasal dexmedetomidine 1 microgram/kg found no advantage to the latter. Nitrous oxide is harmless here; the tube vents whatever diffuses in.

### 6. Head turn and second ear
**Why:** The same steps on the other side. Narrow canals, as in trisomy 21, can turn five minutes into twenty.
**Anaesthesia:** The turn is another point at which the airway is lost or the laryngeal mask shifts. Do not start to lighten until the surgeon confirms the second tube is seated: a tube that will not sit is retried, and a moving head under a microscope damages the canal or the ossicles.

### 7. Added procedures
**Why:** Adenoidectomy is often added in children over four to reduce the need for repeat tubes; nasal cautery, foreign body removal or tongue-tie division may be the whole case.
**Anaesthesia:** Adding adenoidectomy changes the anaesthetic: a cannula, a secured airway (flexible laryngeal mask or tracheal tube), antiemetics and attention to blood in the pharynx, as in [adult and paediatric tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy](adult-and-paediatric-tonsillectomy-and-adenoidectomy.html). A nasal foreign body needs a plan for where it goes if it is pushed backwards: head-down, a laryngeal mask or tube in place before the surgeon instruments the nose, and never positive pressure by face mask before it is out. A button battery in the nose or ear is an emergency measured in hours because of liquefactive necrosis. Cautery and tongue-tie division bleed a little into the mouth: lateral position at the end.

### 8. Emergence
**Why:** The surgeon has finished before the anaesthetic has settled, and the list depends on turnover.
**Anaesthesia:** Remove a laryngeal mask deep or leave the child to wake on a face mask in the lateral position; avoid stimulating a half-awake child. If a cannula is in, propofol 1 mg/kg IV at the end reduces emergence delirium; an observational series in this operation found less agitation with propofol and ketorolac added at the end, without delaying discharge. Laryngospasm at this stage is treated in the usual sequence: jaw thrust and continuous positive pressure with 100% oxygen, propofol if there is a cannula, suxamethonium by whichever route is available if the saturation is falling.

### 9. Recovery and discharge
**Why:** The surgeon wants ear drops in, where used, and the child home.
**Anaesthesia:** Emergence delirium peaks in the first 15-20 minutes: an inconsolable, thrashing child who does not recognise the parent, as against a child in pain who can be comforted and localises to the ear. Sevoflurane, preschool age and a short anaesthetic are the risk factors and this list has all three. A quiet, dim recovery, the parent present early, and analgesia already working are the prevention; a small dose of fentanyl, propofol or an alpha-2 agonist treats it. Discharge is by criteria, usually within an hour or two. Blood-stained or clear discharge from the ear for a day or two is expected.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a small surface wound in a richly innervated membrane, and the pain it causes is real but short. The plan is therefore timing more than potency. Paracetamol 15 mg/kg and ibuprofen 10 mg/kg given orally 30-60 minutes before theatre are at effective levels at emergence; the same drugs given rectally or orally after a five-minute anaesthetic are not. An old double-blind comparison found ketorolac beat paracetamol for the first ten minutes only, with no difference by discharge, which is a fair measure of how brief the problem is.

The second layer exists to cover emergence, when pain and delirium are indistinguishable and feed each other. With a cannula: propofol 1 mg/kg IV at the end, with fentanyl 0.5-1 microgram/kg IV if the child is distressed. Without one: intranasal fentanyl 1-2 microgram/kg after induction. Intranasal dexmedetomidine is the alternative; the comparative trial found the two equivalent, and found that oral midazolam premedication added to either made recovery measures worse, which argues against routine sedative premedication on a grommet list. The author's practice is oral paracetamol and ibuprofen for every child and an intranasal or intravenous opioid for those not premedicated or with a history of difficult emergence.

At home most children need one or two further doses of paracetamol or ibuprofen and nothing after day 1. There is no rebound. Pain persisting or worsening after the first day, with discharge and fever, is infection and needs drops and review, not analgesia. Added procedures change the picture only modestly: adenoidectomy needs regular simple analgesia for three or four days; a reduced nasal fracture aches for two.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Laryngospasm. The principal risk of the list: a light plane, an irritable airway, a stimulating ear and frequently no cannula. The respiratory history identifies the children at risk in advance; for them place a cannula, consider an intravenous induction, and avoid airway instrumentation where a face mask will do.
- The syndromic child. Trisomy 21 brings a difficult mask airway, obstructive sleep apnoea, bradycardia on sevoflurane induction, a cervical spine to protect during head turns, and a slow operation through narrow canals. Cleft and craniofacial children may be difficult to intubate. These cases are not minor.
- The runny nose. Proceed with the bright afebrile child; defer the febrile, wheezy or lethargic one. If proceeding, the lower-risk recipe is an experienced anaesthetist, an intravenous induction with propofol where a cannula can be placed awake, a face mask in preference to a tube, and deep removal of any airway device.
- No intravenous access. Acceptable only with a healthy child, a quick surgeon and rescue drugs drawn up; add access as soon as the case departs from that description.
- Emergence delirium. Self-limiting within half an hour but distressing and occasionally injurious. Explain it to parents beforehand.
- Nasal and ear foreign bodies. Beads and seeds are elective; button batteries and paired magnets are urgent. A nasal object can be aspirated during induction: see [airway foreign body, epiglottitis and the obstructed airway](airway-foreign-body-epiglottitis-and-the.html) for the object that has already gone down.
- Fractured nose manipulation. A minute of intense stimulation with blood in the nasopharynx afterwards; a laryngeal mask, a propofol bolus before the manipulation, and lateral recovery.
- Adults. Grommets in adults are done under topical local anaesthetic to the drum in the clinic; an adult on the theatre list has a reason (anxiety, a narrow canal, a combined procedure) that is worth knowing.
- Emergency and high-risk variants. None arises from the surgery. Invasive monitoring, blood and critical care are never needed for the operation itself; the high-risk case is defined by the child's syndrome or cardiac disease and is planned as such.

## Key sources

Evidence specific to anaesthesia for grommets is thin and mostly small or observational: one double-blind analgesic comparison from 1997, one randomised trial of intranasal agents and one observational series on emergence agitation. The respiratory risk material comes from a large single-centre cohort and a narrative review. The cannula decision, the depth targets, the rescue drug doses and the criteria for deferring a child with a respiratory infection are consensus and author's judgement; no trial settles whether routine venous access for bilateral grommets changes outcome.
