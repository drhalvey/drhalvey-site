## What the surgeon is doing and why

The thoracic aorta is replaced for two reasons: it has grown wide enough that rupture or dissection is likely (aneurysm), or it has already split (dissection). In both the surgeon cuts out a length of diseased aorta and sews in a woven polyester tube. What makes one operation short and another a ten-hour undertaking is which segment is involved, because each segment has branches that someone has to keep perfused while the aorta they arise from is open.

```
 root        ascending      arch           descending / thoracoabdominal
 coronaries  (no branches)  head and arm   intercostals -> spinal cord
 + valve                    vessels        then gut and kidneys
 |           |              |              |
 cardioplegia  clamp        circulatory    distal perfusion, CSF drain,
 + buttons     and sew      arrest +       sequential clamping
                            brain perfusion
```

The ascending aorta has no branches, so it can be replaced between a clamp and the heart on ordinary bypass. The root carries the coronary ostia and the valve: the surgeon either replaces valve and root together with a valved conduit and re-implants the coronaries as buttons (Bentall), or keeps the patient's valve and re-suspends it inside the graft (valve-sparing replacement). The arch carries the vessels to the brain. It cannot be clamped without clamping them, so the surgeon stops the circulation altogether, opens the arch and works in a bloodless field while hypothermia and a trickle of selective perfusion protect the brain. The descending and thoracoabdominal aorta, approached from the left chest, carries the intercostal arteries that feed the spinal cord and then the visceral and renal arteries.

Acute type A dissection is the emergency version. Blood enters the media through an intimal tear in the ascending aorta and tracks in both directions, creating a false lumen. Backwards, it reaches the pericardium (tamponade), the aortic valve commissures (acute regurgitation) and the coronary ostia, usually the right (inferior infarction). Forwards, it can shear off or compress any branch: carotid, subclavian, mesenteric, renal, iliac. The surgeon's aim is limited and specific: resect the tear, replace the ascending aorta and as much arch as necessary so that flow is redirected into the true lumen, and re-suspend or replace the valve. The registry experience is that operating on these patients has become more common and that in-hospital mortality has fallen with it.

Pain after the sternotomy operations is that of any sternotomy and sits at a 4. These patients are usually ventilated overnight or longer, so the pain presents later. The thoracoabdominal incision is among the largest in surgery, dividing chest wall, costal margin and diaphragm, and is a 5 by the chest-wall mechanism with a real chronic pain tail (see [analgesia for the trunk](../a/a02-truncal-blocks-epidural.html)).

## Steps

### 1. Before induction in acute dissection
**Why:** Nothing can be repaired until the chest is open, and the outer wall of the false lumen is a few cell layers thick. The force that extends a dissection is the rate of rise of aortic pressure with each beat as much as the pressure itself.
**Anaesthesia:** Control rate before pressure: a short-acting beta-blocker to 60-80 per minute, then a vasodilator to a systolic pressure of 100-120 mmHg, with opioid for pain, as the guideline sets out. Then look for what has already happened. Compare the arms, feel the femorals, check the neurology, read the ECG for inferior ischaemia and the gas for lactate, because a malperfused limb, gut or brain changes the cannulation plan. An effusion on the transthoracic echo with a low pressure and a tachycardia is tamponade: do not beta-block it, do not drain it to normal (restoring pressure completes the rupture), and treat induction as described in [resternotomy, tamponade and mechanical circulatory support](resternotomy-tamponade-and-mechanical-circulatory-support.html), with the patient prepared and draped and the surgeon ready to cannulate before you give anything.

### 2. Lines and induction
**Why:** The surgeon's cannulation site determines which arterial line means what later.
**Anaesthesia:** Agree the sites. With right axillary cannulation, the right radial reads the perfusion pressure during antegrade cerebral perfusion and a left radial or femoral line reads the systemic circulation; in dissection, two sites in different territories also detect malperfusion when bypass starts. Place wide-bore access and a rapid infuser. Induce with a technique that avoids both the pressor response to laryngoscopy and a fall in a patient who may be hypovolaemic or regurgitant: generous opioid, a small hypnotic dose, vasopressor and vasodilator both in hand. Pass the echo probe only once the patient is deep.

### 3. The echo
**Why:** The surgeon needs the root dimensions, the valve mechanism and, in dissection, confirmation of the diagnosis and its complications.
**Anaesthesia:** Find the flap and the entry tear if visible, grade the aortic regurgitation and state its mechanism (dilated root, prolapsing flap, detached commissure), since that decides re-suspension against replacement. Look for pericardial blood, regional wall motion that indicates coronary involvement, and ventricular function. During cannulation, confirm on the descending aortic view that the guidewire lies in the true lumen.

### 4. Sternotomy and cannulation
**Why:** The arterial cannula must perfuse the true lumen and, ideally, allow brain perfusion during arrest. The right axillary artery, through a side graft, does both; femoral cannulation is quick; direct aortic cannulation over a wire is used in some units.
**Anaesthesia:** The saw is a hypertensive stimulus; deepen first. In a redo or a large aneurysm lying against the sternum the surgeon will cannulate peripherally and may start bypass, or cool, before opening. When bypass starts, watch every monitor at once for a minute: a sudden fall in one radial pressure, a one-sided drop in cerebral oximetry or a high line pressure means the false lumen is being pressurised and the true lumen compressed. Say it immediately; the surgeon will change the cannulation site.

### 5. Cooling
**Why:** Hypothermia lowers cerebral metabolic demand so that a period without flow is tolerated. Units choose between deep hypothermia near 18-20 degrees C and moderate hypothermia at 24-28 degrees C combined with selective cerebral perfusion.
**Anaesthesia:** Cooling must be long enough to be uniform, usually 20-30 minutes or more, because a cold nasopharynx over a warm brain core is the failure mode; bladder temperature lags and tells you about the body. The ventricle fibrillates as it cools and, if the aortic valve leaks, distends: the surgeon vents or clamps. Maintain neuromuscular block and anaesthetic depth during cooling, control glucose, and manage blood gases uncorrected for temperature. Ice around the head and drugs such as a barbiturate or steroid before arrest are local practice without good evidence; the EEG becoming isoelectric is a reasonable sign that cooling has reached the cortex.

### 6. Clamp, cardioplegia and the root
**Why:** While cooling continues the surgeon clamps the ascending aorta, arrests the heart and does the proximal work: valve re-suspension, valve-sparing root replacement, or a valved conduit with coronary button re-implantation.
**Anaesthesia:** With a regurgitant valve or an open root, cardioplegia is given directly into the ostia and retrograde. This is pump time in which nothing is asked of you except vigilance and preparation: send coagulation tests, confirm products are thawing, and plan the rewarming.

### 7. Circulatory arrest and cerebral perfusion
**Why:** The pump stops, the patient is partly exsanguinated into the reservoir, the clamp comes off and the surgeon looks inside the arch, resects it to healthy tissue and sews the distal anastomosis open, which is more secure than sewing against a clamp on dissected aorta.
**Anaesthesia:** Head down, ventilator off, infusions paused, time noted aloud. Selective antegrade perfusion runs cold blood up the right axillary cannula, or through cannulae placed directly in the head vessels, at a low flow and a right radial pressure that the perfusionist titrates. Your monitor is bilateral cerebral oximetry: with unilateral perfusion the left hemisphere relies on the circle of Willis, and a left-sided fall that does not recover is the signal for the surgeon to perfuse the left carotid as well. Retrograde perfusion through the superior cava is the alternative. The pooled comparisons favour either adjunct over arrest alone, more so as arrest lengthens, with no clear winner between them. Do not treat a short arrest as free: in the randomised comparison of three temperatures, about 40% of patients had a measurable cognitive deficit at four weeks after arrests of under 20 minutes, whatever the temperature.

### 8. Restart and rewarming
**Why:** Once the distal anastomosis is complete the graft is de-aired and clamped, flow is restored to the body through the graft or a side arm, and the remaining arch branches and proximal anastomosis are completed during rewarming.
**Anaesthesia:** Rewarm slowly, with a small gradient between arterial blood and the patient and no nasopharyngeal temperature above 37 degrees C: the brain has just been ischaemic and hyperthermia is the injury you can still prevent. Rewarming takes an hour or more and is when coagulation tests become interpretable, so run the viscoelastic test now and order against it. Expect vasodilatation, a falling haematocrit, rising potassium and glucose, and resume anaesthetic as the EEG returns.

### 9. Coming off bypass
**Why:** The heart has had a long ischaemic time and, after root surgery, has two re-implanted coronary arteries.
**Anaesthesia:** De-air as for any open left heart. New right ventricular failure or inferior ST change, or a new lateral wall abnormality, after a root replacement is a kinked or stretched coronary button until proved otherwise and the remedy is surgical. After a valve-sparing operation the echo must show a competent valve with a good depth of coaptation; more than mild regurgitation means revision. Pacing wires are needed. Ventricular function is often temporarily poor and an inotrope is usual.

### 10. Coagulopathy and haemostasis
**Why:** Long bypass, deep hypothermia and, in dissection, a false lumen that has been consuming clotting factors and platelets for hours produce the worst coagulopathy in cardiac surgery, in tissue that tears with each stitch.
**Anaesthesia:** Protamine at no more than the heparin dose, then treat aggressively against the viscoelastic results: fibrinogen first and to a high target, platelets, then prothrombin complex concentrate or plasma. A guided algorithm reduced transfusion and major bleeding in a large pragmatic trial. Keep the patient warm, the ionised calcium normal and the pH corrected. Hold the systolic pressure at 100-120 mmHg, because suture lines in dissected aorta bleed with pressure and a needle hole can become a tear. The surgeon may pack and leave the chest open if the heart is oedematous or the field will not dry; see [major haemorrhage, cell salvage and coagulation](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html).

### 11. Transfer and the first neurological examination
**Why:** The operation succeeds or fails on the brain, cord and viscera as much as on the graft.
**Anaesthesia:** Hand over the arrest time, the lowest temperature, the perfusion strategy, the oximetry trend, pressure limits and outstanding products. Use sedation that can be stopped for an early examination. A patient who does not wake, or wakes with a deficit, needs imaging promptly; delirium is common after arrest and does not by itself mean a stroke.

### 12. The descending and thoracoabdominal variant
**Why:** Through a left thoracotomy, extended across the costal margin and through the diaphragm for thoracoabdominal aneurysms, the surgeon clamps the aorta in stages and replaces it, re-attaching intercostal, visceral and renal arteries. Each clamp removes the blood supply to a length of spinal cord.
**Anaesthesia:** Right lateral position, left lung down with a double-lumen tube, right radial arterial line (the left subclavian may be clamped) with a femoral line for distal pressure. The surgeon perfuses the lower body during clamping with left heart bypass, from left atrium to femoral artery, which also unloads the heart from the proximal clamp. Cord perfusion pressure is mean arterial pressure minus cerebrospinal fluid pressure, and you control both: a lumbar drain placed beforehand and run to 10 mmHg or less, which in the randomised trial reduced paraplegia or paraparesis from 13.0% to 2.6%, and a mean arterial pressure of 80-100 mmHg throughout and for days afterwards. Mild hypothermia is permitted. Where motor evoked potentials are monitored the anaesthetic is total intravenous without neuromuscular block. Blood loss is large and rapid. Afterwards the legs are examined hourly: delayed weakness is treated at once by raising the pressure, draining more fluid within safe limits and correcting anaemia. The physiology of clamping and unclamping is covered with [open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair](open-abdominal-aortic-aneurysm-repair.html), and the endovascular alternative with [endovascular aneurysm repair](endovascular-aneurysm-repair.html).

## Pain plan

For sternotomy the strategy is the standard one and the block is the plan as a whole, with two differences from routine cardiac surgery. These patients stay ventilated longer, so early analgesia is by infusion and the transition to oral analgesia comes later; and coagulopathy and kidney injury are both more likely, so the NSAID that PROSPECT recommends for sternotomy is usually omitted for the first days. Paracetamol, opioid by infusion then patient-controlled, a parasternal block or surgeon infiltration at closure if the chest is being closed dry, and dexmedetomidine while ventilated are the defensible default. Do not place needles around a chest that is being packed or left open.

The thoracoabdominal wound deserves regional analgesia and makes it difficult. A thoracic epidural gives the best pain relief for a thoracotomy, but the patient is heparinised, frequently coagulopathic, already has a lumbar drain, and must have reliable hourly motor examination of the legs, which a dense epidural obscures. The author's judgement is that a surgeon-placed paravertebral or extrapleural catheter at closure, with dilute local anaesthetic, is the better balance, and units with long experience of pre-operative epidural placement run it with opioid alone or very dilute local anaesthetic for the same reason. Expect severe movement-evoked and cough-evoked pain for a week and persistent intercostal pain in a proportion.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Tamponade with dissection: the highest-risk induction on this card. Surgeon scrubbed, patient draped, peripheral cannulation or sternotomy within seconds of induction.
- Malperfusion: a cold leg, abdominal pain with a rising lactate, or a neurological deficit changes priorities; some centres restore visceral flow endovascularly before the central repair. A dense established stroke or coma is a reason for the team to reconsider operating at all.
- Chronic anticoagulation or dual antiplatelet therapy given for a presumed coronary syndrome before the diagnosis was made: reverse what can be reversed and have platelets in the room.
- Frozen elephant trunk and long descending coverage: a stent graft in the descending aorta deployed through the open arch covers intercostal arteries, so spinal cord protection (pressure targets, sometimes a drain) applies to an operation done through a sternotomy.
- Left recurrent laryngeal and phrenic nerves run through the arch field: hoarseness, aspiration risk and a raised left hemidiaphragm complicate extubation.
- Connective tissue disease (Marfan, Loeys-Dietz): young patients, valve-sparing surgery, fragile tissue, and a lifetime of further aortic operations, often as redo sternotomy.
- Elective root and ascending surgery without arrest is suitable for a fast-track pathway; arch surgery and dissection are not.

## Key sources

The management of dissection rests on registry data and guideline consensus; randomisation is not feasible. Cerebral protection has network meta-analysis of mostly observational series and one randomised temperature trial, which is sobering about short arrests. Cerebrospinal fluid drainage has a single positive randomised trial in open repair. The analgesic recommendations for the thoracoabdominal incision are judgement.
