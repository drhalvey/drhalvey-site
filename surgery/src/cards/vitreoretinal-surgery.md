## What the surgeon is doing and why

The retina is a thin sheet of nerve tissue lining the back of the eye, held against the wall by the pressure of the vitreous gel in front of it and by a pump in the pigment layer behind. Vitreoretinal surgery deals with the ways that arrangement fails. The gel shrinks with age and pulls a tear in the retina, fluid tracks through the tear and lifts the retina off (rhegmatogenous detachment). The gel pulls on the macula and opens a hole, or a membrane grows across the macula and wrinkles it. In diabetes, new vessels grow into the gel, bleed and form scar sheets that drag the retina off.

There are two operations, often combined.

Vitrectomy works from inside. Three small trocars (23 to 27 gauge) are placed through the pars plana, 3.5-4 mm behind the limbus where there is no retina to damage: one for an infusion that keeps the eye pressurised, one for a light pipe, one for the cutter. The gel is cut away and aspirated, which removes the traction. Membranes are peeled from the retinal surface with forceps under high magnification. The retina is flattened by replacing the fluid with air, tears are sealed with laser or a freezing probe, and the eye is left filled with something that holds the retina in place while the seal matures: air, a slowly absorbed gas (sulfur hexafluoride, SF6, or perfluoropropane, C3F8) or silicone oil.

Scleral buckling works from outside. The conjunctiva is opened around the cornea, the four rectus muscles are slung with sutures so the eye can be rotated, the tear is frozen through the sclera, and a silicone band or sponge is sutured to the sclera to indent the wall of the eye against the tear.

The surgeon's requirements are a completely still eye during work on the retinal surface, a dark theatre, and a tamponade bubble of known size. The pain logic follows the approach. A sutureless small-gauge vitrectomy is minimally painful. A buckle, with muscles pulled, sclera frozen and a band left under the muscles, is the painful version and adds nausea. Either way the mechanism is conjunctiva, muscle and sclera in the territory of the ciliary nerves, which an orbital block covers completely. The card scores 2 with a block in place. Its lasting message is the gas.

## Steps

### 1. Urgency and the patient
**Why:** A detachment that has not yet reached the macula (macula-on) threatens central vision by the hour and is repaired within about a day; once the macula is off, a delay of some days costs little. Macular holes, membranes and diabetic vitrectomies are elective.
**Anaesthesia:** Urgent rarely means unfasted: an evening or next-morning list is almost always acceptable to the surgeon, and a block removes the question. The patients fall into two groups. Detachments occur in myopes of any adult age, mostly well, with long eyes that matter for needle blocks. Diabetic vitrectomy patients have every other complication of diabetes: nephropathy and often dialysis, coronary disease, autonomic neuropathy and gastroparesis. For them check potassium, glucose and the dialysis schedule, and favour a block over general anaesthesia. Ask at every assessment whether the patient already has gas in either eye from previous surgery.

### 2. Local or general anaesthesia
**Why:** The surgeon can work under either; what they cannot work with is movement.
**Anaesthesia:** A sub-Tenon or peribulbar block is sufficient for most vitrectomies up to about 90 minutes in a patient who can lie flat and still. General anaesthesia is chosen for long or repeat operations, buckles in younger patients, anxiety, inability to lie still, and the surgeon's preference for delicate macular work. Combining the two is the author's default when general anaesthesia is used: the block provides the analgesia, suppresses the oculocardiac reflex and reduces the opioid and therefore the nausea. The comparative evidence is thin; in a small prospective cohort of ambulatory vitrectomies, patients with a peribulbar block had no postoperative pain, less intraoperative hypotension and earlier discharge than those with balanced general anaesthesia.

### 3. The block
**Why:** The whole operation lies within the ciliary nerve territory, and akinesia helps the surgeon.
**Anaesthesia:** A sub-Tenon block of 4-5 mL with a long-acting agent (ropivacaine 0.75% or levobupivacaine 0.5%, alone or mixed with lidocaine 2%). In a prospective series of 6,000 sub-Tenon blocks for intraocular surgery there were no serious block-related complications. Two things make it harder here than for cataract. The eye is often long, which is the argument for a blunt cannula over a needle. And repeat surgery or a previous buckle scars Tenon's capsule to the sclera, so the cannula will not pass; a peribulbar block placed with care, or general anaesthesia, is then the route. In a long case the surgeon can top up through the open conjunctiva. See [cataract surgery](cataract-surgery.html) for block complications and anticoagulants, which are handled the same way.

### 4. General anaesthesia: conduct
**Why:** The surgeon is operating on a structure a fraction of a millimetre thick with instruments inside the eye.
**Anaesthesia:** Supraglottic airway for most; a tracheal tube for the long case, the obese patient, reflux and gastroparesis. Secure everything before the lights go off, because the head is draped and the theatre is dark: circuit taped, cannula visible on an extension, alarms audible. A cough or movement with the cutter or forceps near the retina can tear it, so either run propofol and remifentanil at a depth that guarantees immobility or give relaxant with quantitative monitoring, and do not let it wear off unnoticed. No nitrous oxide (step 8). Normocapnia and a little head-up tilt keep choroidal blood volume down.

### 5. Ports and core vitrectomy
**Why:** Trocars are placed, the infusion is opened and the central gel is removed.
**Anaesthesia:** From this point the surgeon's machine sets the intraocular pressure through the infusion, typically 20-30 mmHg, and raises it deliberately to stop bleeding. That changes what blood pressure means for the eye: ocular perfusion pressure is mean arterial pressure minus intraocular pressure, so hypotension under anaesthesia with a raised infusion pressure can stop retinal and optic nerve flow. Keep the mean arterial pressure near the patient's normal, especially in diabetics and anyone with glaucoma. The step is not stimulating under a block.

### 6. Membrane peeling, laser and cryotherapy
**Why:** Epiretinal and internal limiting membranes are lifted from the macula with fine forceps; tears are surrounded with laser burns from inside or frozen from outside while the surgeon indents the sclera to bring the peripheral retina into view.
**Anaesthesia:** Peeling is the moment of absolute stillness; say so if you are about to do anything that might disturb the patient, and under local anaesthesia warn the patient not to speak without squeezing a hand first. Scleral indentation and cryotherapy are the painful stimuli of a vitrectomy and break through a light general anaesthetic or a patchy block as movement, hypertension or bradycardia. Extensive laser is uncomfortable under local anaesthesia and may need a top-up.

### 7. Scleral buckle
**Why:** The conjunctiva is opened for 360 degrees, each rectus muscle is hooked and slung, and the band is passed under the muscles and sutured to the sclera; subretinal fluid may be drained through a needle puncture.
**Anaesthesia:** The oculocardiac reflex belongs to this step: traction on a rectus muscle produces sinus bradycardia, junctional rhythm or a pause within seconds. Have the surgeon release the traction, confirm depth and carbon dioxide, and give glycopyrronium 0.2-0.4 mg or atropine 0.6 mg IV if it recurs. The reflex is less frequent with an orbital block, anticholinergic and deeper inhaled anaesthesia, and more frequent with rapid-acting opioids such as remifentanil. A buckle tightened around the globe raises intraocular pressure; the surgeon checks that the central retinal artery is still pulsing. Buckle surgery is where the pain and nausea of this card concentrate.

### 8. Fluid-air exchange and gas
**Why:** The eye is filled with air to flatten the retina, then the air is exchanged for a gas mixture at a concentration that will not expand (about 20% SF6 or 14% C3F8), or for oil.
**Anaesthesia:** The physics is the reason for the rule. Nitrous oxide is far more soluble in blood than nitrogen or the tamponade gases, so it enters a gas-filled space faster than the other gas can leave. A bubble in a closed eye cannot enlarge the eye, so the pressure rises instead, and when it exceeds retinal arterial pressure the retina infarcts. If nitrous oxide is in use when the surgeon sizes the bubble, the opposite happens afterwards: it diffuses out and the bubble shrinks, leaving the tamponade under-filled. Stopping it 15-20 minutes before the exchange avoids both, but the dependable practice is not to use it at all in this theatre. Silicone oil is not a gas and has none of these effects.

### 9. Closure
**Why:** Small-gauge ports seal themselves or take one absorbable suture; the conjunctiva is closed after a buckle; antibiotic and steroid are injected under the conjunctiva.
**Anaesthesia:** If no block was given, have the surgeon inject long-acting local anaesthetic into the sub-Tenon space now: it takes ten seconds through the open conjunctiva and covers the first evening. Paracetamol and an NSAID intravenously unless contraindicated. Smooth emergence without coughing or retching, because the eye is full of gas at a set pressure and any surge is transmitted to the retinal circulation. A wristband or card stating the gas, the date and the warning about nitrous oxide goes on before the patient leaves theatre.

### 10. Posturing, discharge and later anaesthetics
**Why:** A gas bubble floats; the patient is positioned so that it presses on the tear or the macular hole (face-down, or on one side) for days, as the surgeon directs.
**Anaesthesia:** Posturing starts in recovery, so the patient needs to be awake, comfortable and not vomiting. Air lasts about a week, SF6 about two weeks and C3F8 about eight weeks, with wide variation. Until the surgeon has confirmed that the bubble has gone the patient must not fly or travel to altitude and must not be given nitrous oxide for any procedure, including dental sedation and labour analgesia. The published cases of blindness followed anaesthetics for unrelated operations 10 to 37 days after vitreoretinal surgery, and a review of case reports puts the period of risk at three months or more after gas injection. The question "have you had eye surgery with a gas bubble" belongs in every preoperative assessment, not only on eye lists.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is conjunctiva, muscle and sclera, all supplied through the ciliary nerves, so one orbital block covers the operation and the first 6 to 12 hours. The defensible default is a sub-Tenon block with a long-acting agent for every patient, as the sole anaesthetic or as the analgesic component of a general anaesthetic, with paracetamol and an NSAID for two to three days. This is author's judgement supported by small comparative studies; there is no procedure-specific guideline.

After a small-gauge vitrectomy expect a worst pain of 2-3 out of 10 on the first evening and next day, described as scratchy and aching, with almost nothing by day 3. A buckle or extensive cryotherapy adds a deep ache and pain on eye movement for several days, and justifies a few doses of oral opioid. Rebound when the block wears off is mild because the underlying stimulus is modest. Neck and back pain from face-down posturing is common and is helped by the same simple analgesics and by proper support.

Escalation triggers. Severe eye pain with nausea or vomiting in the first 24 hours is a raised intraocular pressure from the gas bubble or from inflammation until measured, and the treatment is pressure-lowering (acetazolamide, drops, or release of gas by the surgeon), not opioid and antiemetic. Pain with falling vision and a red eye from day 2 is endophthalmitis. Sudden severe pain during surgery under local anaesthesia with a hardening eye is suprachoroidal haemorrhage, rare in vitreoretinal surgery but not absent.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Nitrous oxide at a later anaesthetic. The commonest route to harm. Wristband, card, and a direct question at every preoperative visit.
- Altitude. Flying and mountain roads expand the bubble by the same physics. The surgeon gives the clearance.
- Previous gas at this operation. A patient returning for further surgery may still have gas in the eye; no nitrous oxide from the first breath.
- The diabetic vitrectomy. The longest cases in the sickest patients: prefer a block with light or no sedation, treat it as a renal and cardiac anaesthetic if general anaesthesia is needed, and expect bleeding in the eye that makes the surgeon raise the infusion pressure.
- Movement. Under local anaesthesia from a patient who falls asleep and wakes; under general anaesthesia from a relaxant allowed to lapse. Both tear retinas.
- Dark theatre. Disconnection and a displaced airway are found late. Set alarm volumes and limits before the lights go down.
- Silicone oil removal. A short second operation months later, usually under a block.
- Combined cataract and vitrectomy. Adds 15 minutes at the start; nothing changes.
- Retinopathy of prematurity and children. General anaesthesia in ex-premature infants with the apnoea risk that carries; see [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).
- Posterior segment trauma and intraocular foreign body. The vitrectomy follows primary closure of the globe; see [penetrating eye injury](penetrating-eye-injury.html).
- Emergency and high-risk variant. The macula-on detachment out of hours in an unfasted patient is done under a block. Invasive monitoring is for the patient's cardiac or renal disease, not the operation; blood and critical care are not needed.

## Key sources

The nitrous oxide rule rests on physics and on case reports of blindness, which are sufficient: no trial will be done. The durations of the gases and the three-month figure are approximate and vary between patients. Evidence comparing block with general anaesthesia for vitrectomy is limited to small cohorts. The oculocardiac reflex material is from a narrative review. The stop time for nitrous oxide before gas exchange, ocular perfusion pressure reasoning, block volumes and the pain trajectory are consensus and author's judgement.
