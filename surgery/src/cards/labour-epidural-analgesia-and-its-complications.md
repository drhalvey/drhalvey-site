## What the surgeon is doing and why

There is no surgeon on this card. The operator is the uterus, supervised by a midwife and an obstetrician, and the anaesthetist is the proceduralist. The card is built the same way as the others because the same logic applies: know what the tissue is doing and the analgesic plan follows.

Labour has two stages that hurt for different reasons through different nerves. In the first stage the uterus contracts against a closed cervix and drags it open from 0 to 10 cm. The pain is visceral: stretch and ischaemia of the lower segment and cervix, carried by afferents that travel with the sympathetic chain and enter the cord at T10 to L1. It is felt as a deep, poorly localised cramp across the lower abdomen and back, in waves, and it is worse when the fetal occiput lies posterior and presses on the sacrum. In the second stage the presenting part descends and stretches the vagina, pelvic floor and perineum. That pain is somatic, sharp and well localised, and travels in the pudendal nerve to S2-4. An analgesic technique therefore has to cover T10 to S4, which is a long span, and has to do it for many hours without removing the motor power and the sensation of pressure that the woman needs in order to push.

That requirement defines the modern epidural: a large volume of very dilute local anaesthetic with a lipid-soluble opioid, spread widely, blocking small sensory fibres and sparing motor fibres. The COMET trial is the reason dilute solutions became standard: women given low-dose regimens had a normal vaginal delivery rate of about 43% against 35% with traditional 0.25% bupivacaine top-ups, entirely through fewer instrumental births. The Cochrane review of epidural against opioid or no analgesia found better pain relief and satisfaction, no effect on caesarean rate or long-term backache, and an increase in instrumental delivery that disappears when only trials after 2005 are analysed.

The obstetric team wants three things from you: a comfortable woman who can still push, a cardiotocograph that is not disturbed by the block, and a catheter that will convert to surgical anaesthesia in ten minutes if the labour ends in theatre. The third is the one that is forgotten at 3 am, and it is the reason a marginal epidural should be replaced rather than nursed along. Background physiology is in [obstetric essentials](../a/a09-obstetric-essentials.html).

## Steps

### 1. The request, the assessment and consent
**Why:** The midwife calls when the woman asks; the obstetrician may recommend an epidural early for pre-eclampsia, twins, breech, a high BMI, a trial of labour after caesarean or cardiac disease, because each raises the chance of operative delivery or makes general anaesthesia riskier.
**Anaesthesia:** Maternal request is a sufficient indication at any cervical dilatation. Check the things that stop you: anticoagulant timing, a platelet count where there is a reason to have one (pre-eclampsia, known thrombocytopenia), systemic sepsis, local infection, raised intracranial pressure, uncorrected hypovolaemia. The interdisciplinary consensus statement on thrombocytopenia puts the threshold at which haematoma risk is likely to be very low at 70 x 10^9/L in gestational thrombocytopenia, immune thrombocytopenia and hypertensive disorders, provided the count is stable and there is no other bleeding risk; below that it is an individual decision weighed against the risk of general anaesthesia. Consent between contractions is valid consent: say the common things (hypotension, itching, a patchy block needing adjustment or replacement in about 1 in 10, headache in about 1 in 100) and the rare serious ones (nerve damage, infection, haematoma), and document it. Assess and record the airway now.

### 2. Insertion
**Why:** The target is the epidural space at L3-4 or L2-3, below the conus and central enough to spread both ways.
**Anaesthesia:** Sitting gives a better midline in obese women; lateral is kinder to a distressed woman and to a compromised fetus. Loss of resistance to saline, catheter left 4-5 cm in the space: less and it falls out with movement, more and it finds a root sleeve or a vein and gives a unilateral block. Pause for contractions with the needle still. Ultrasound of the spine before insertion helps identify the midline and depth in a woman with a high BMI or scoliosis. Full aseptic precautions; chlorhexidine in alcohol is applied, allowed to dry, and kept off the tray because it is neurotoxic if it reaches the needle.

### 3. Initiation: epidural, combined spinal-epidural or dural puncture epidural
**Why:** The woman wants the next contraction not to hurt; the obstetric team wants no fetal bradycardia.
**Anaesthesia:** Plain epidural: 10-15 mL of the low-dose mixture in two fractions through the catheter after aspiration. The first fraction is the test dose, because a dilute solution given slowly declares an intrathecal catheter (rapid dense block, warm heavy legs within five minutes) or an intravascular one (no effect at all) without the risks of a concentrated test. Onset is 15-20 minutes. Combined spinal-epidural: bupivacaine 2-2.5 mg with fentanyl 15 micrograms intrathecally gives analgesia in under five minutes and reliable sacral cover, which suits a multipara at 8 cm, at the cost of an untested catheter and a recognised incidence of fetal bradycardia from uterine hypertonus in the first 15 minutes; glyceryl trinitrate 400 micrograms sublingually or terbutaline 250 micrograms SC is the treatment. Dural puncture epidural: the dura is punctured with a 25 G or 27 G pencil-point needle through the Tuohy but nothing is injected; the hole lets epidural drug cross. In the pooled trials more women were comfortable at 10 minutes (relative risk 1.43) with no increase in adverse events. It also confirms the midline. The author's reading is that it is a reasonable default for a difficult or late epidural and unnecessary for a straightforward early one.

### 4. Maintenance
**Why:** Labour lasts hours, and the block has to stay wide and light throughout.
**Anaesthesia:** Programmed intermittent boluses (for example 8-10 mL every 45-60 minutes) with patient-controlled boluses on top (5-8 mL, lockout 10-15 minutes) have replaced continuous infusion. A bolus delivered at pressure spreads through more of the epidural space than the same volume trickled in, and the Cochrane review of automated boluses against basal infusion confirms the result: less breakthrough pain, less local anaesthetic, probably better satisfaction, and no difference in caesarean or instrumental delivery. The patient-controlled bolus is the safety valve and the measure of adequacy: a woman pressing the button every lockout interval has a problem that the pump will not fix.

### 5. The first thirty minutes and ongoing observations
**Why:** Sympathetic block lowers maternal pressure, and the uterine circulation cannot compensate.
**Anaesthesia:** Blood pressure every five minutes for 20 minutes after the loading dose and after every clinician bolus, with the woman off her back. Treat a systolic fall of more than 20% or any fall accompanied by a change in the fetal heart with position, fluid and phenylephrine 50-100 micrograms or ephedrine 6 mg IV. Thereafter hourly sensory level to cold, hourly motor score, and pain score. The upper level should sit around T8-T10. A level above T6, or legs that will not lift with a low-dose solution, means the catheter is intrathecal or subdural until shown otherwise.

### 6. Breakthrough pain and the failing epidural
**Why:** The uterus changes what it asks of the block as labour advances: more intense contractions, then sacral pain, then pressure.
**Anaesthesia:** Assess before topping up. Bilateral but low or faded: give volume (10 mL of the dilute mixture, or 5-8 mL of bupivacaine 0.125% with fentanyl 50 micrograms once). Unilateral or a missed segment: withdraw the catheter 1 cm, top up with the painful side down. Sacral sparing late in labour: a sitting top-up, or re-site with a spinal component. No block at all after 20 mL: the catheter is not in the epidural space. The rule that protects you is two strikes: if two clinician interventions have not produced a symmetrical block, replace the catheter. In the meta-analysis of failed conversion at caesarean, the number of clinician top-ups in labour was the modifiable predictor. Three things mimic a failing epidural and are missed: a full bladder, an occipito-posterior position (back pain and rectal pressure through a good block), and uterine rupture in a woman with a previous caesarean, which presents as constant pain breaking through a previously effective epidural together with an abnormal fetal trace.

### 7. Second stage
**Why:** With full dilatation the obstetric plan is descent and then pushing; many units allow an hour or more of passive descent with an epidural before active pushing.
**Anaesthesia:** Do not turn the epidural off for pushing. It restores the pain without improving the outcome that matters, and a low-dose block does not remove the urge. What helps is sacral cover without dense motor block: keep the boluses running and add a sitting top-up for perineal pain. Temperature rises in a proportion of women with epidurals during long labours, by a non-infective inflammatory mechanism that is not fully understood; it cannot be distinguished at the bedside from chorioamnionitis and is treated as possible infection by the obstetric team.

### 8. When labour ends in theatre
**Why:** About one labour in four or five that starts with an epidural ends with an instrumental or caesarean delivery, depending on the unit.
**Anaesthesia:** The catheter is now the anaesthetic. The decision and doses are on the [emergency caesarean](emergency-caesarean-section-by-category.html) and [instrumental delivery](instrumental-delivery-manual-removal-of-placenta.html) cards. The point for this card is that the assessment happens before the emergency: at each visit, ask whether this catheter would be trusted for surgery.

### 9. Accidental dural puncture
**Why:** A 16 G or 18 G Tuohy needle through the dura leaves a hole large enough for cerebrospinal fluid to leak faster than it is made.
**Anaesthesia:** The incidence is of the order of 1 in 100, higher with less experience and at night, and more than half of these women develop a headache. Recognise it (fluid in the needle hub, or a catheter that aspirates clear fluid) and choose between two acceptable options. Thread the catheter intrathecally: immediate reliable analgesia with 1-2 mL boluses of the dilute mixture and no second attempt, but a catheter that can kill if a 10 mL epidural bolus goes down it, so it is labelled on the filter, the pump and the notes, and every dose is given by an anaesthetist. Or re-site at another interspace: the epidural then behaves like a dural puncture epidural, with a higher and faster block than expected for a given dose, so top-ups are fractionated. Tell the woman, the midwife and the obstetrician what has happened, do not restrict pushing on that account, and arrange daily review.

### 10. Post-dural puncture headache and the blood patch
**Why:** Low cerebrospinal fluid pressure lets the brain sag when upright, pulling on pain-sensitive dura and bridging veins, and the cerebral veins dilate to compensate.
**Anaesthesia:** The headache is postural (worse within minutes of sitting, eased lying flat), fronto-occipital, usually begins within three days, and often brings neck stiffness, tinnitus, muffled hearing or photophobia. The multisociety consensus guideline gives 47 recommendations, many of low certainty, and their shape is: regular simple analgesia and a short opioid course if needed; caffeine may be offered in the first 24 hours within a daily limit that is lower in breastfeeding women; enforced bed rest and fluid loading are not treatments; and an epidural blood patch is offered when the headache is severe enough to stop the mother caring for herself and her baby or is not settling. The patch is 15-20 mL of autologous blood injected at or below the level of puncture, stopped earlier if back pressure or pain develops. It relieves the headache in most women, though a proportion recur and need a second, and it is somewhat less durable when performed in the first 48 hours. A headache that loses its postural character, or comes with focal signs, seizures, drowsiness or fever, needs imaging for subdural haematoma, cerebral venous thrombosis or meningitis before anyone calls it a recurrence. Cranial nerve palsies (the sixth most often) and chronic headache are recognised sequelae, which is why untreated puncture headaches are followed up after discharge.

### 11. Removing the catheter and neurological follow-up
**Why:** Obstetric nerve injuries from labour itself are far commoner than injuries from the block, but only the block causes a compressive lesion that surgery within hours can reverse.
**Anaesthesia:** Remove the catheter when delivery and any repair are complete and haemostasis is secure, check the tip is intact, and time the thromboprophylaxis from removal. The joint guideline on neurological monitoring asks that every woman is able to straight-leg raise by 4 hours after the last dose of local anaesthetic, and that inability to do so, or any new weakness, back pain or sphincter disturbance, triggers anaesthetic review and, if not explained, urgent magnetic resonance imaging. The national audit of neuraxial complications found permanent injury in 2.0 to 4.2 per 100,000 blocks across all specialties, and two-thirds of initially disabling injuries recovered. The far commoner postpartum findings are compressive palsies from the fetal head or from positioning: a lumbosacral trunk lesion with foot drop, femoral neuropathy after prolonged hip flexion, lateral cutaneous nerve of thigh numbness, and common peroneal palsy from lithotomy poles. They are unilateral, follow a nerve rather than a level, and recover over weeks, but each deserves an examination and a documented diagnosis.

## Pain plan

The epidural is the pain plan, and the catheter decision is routine by definition. The mechanism (visceral T10-L1 then somatic S2-4, over hours, in a patient who must keep motor power) is exactly what a dilute local anaesthetic and opioid mixture delivered in boluses is designed for. The evidence is strong for low concentration (COMET) and for programmed boluses (Cochrane), moderate for dural puncture epidural, and the choice between ropivacaine and bupivacaine at these concentrations is custom.

When neuraxial block is contraindicated or declined, remifentanil patient-controlled analgesia is the most effective systemic option: in the RESPITE trial 19% of women on remifentanil went on to an epidural against 41% on intramuscular pethidine. A typical regimen is a 30-40 microgram bolus with a 2-minute lockout and no background. It requires continuous one-to-one midwifery presence, oxygen saturation monitoring and a dedicated cannula, because respiratory depression and desaturation are common and apnoea has been reported. Nitrous oxide 50% and intramuscular morphine or pethidine remain available and are weak. A pudendal block by the obstetrician covers an instrumental delivery when there is no epidural and no time.

After delivery the block wears off over 2-3 hours and what remains is perineal pain and afterpains: regular paracetamol and an NSAID, ice packs, and a few doses of oral opioid after an episiotomy or a significant tear. There is no rebound in the sense used elsewhere in this book. Escalation triggers afterwards are specific: perineal pain out of proportion to the visible injury within hours of delivery is a vulval or paravaginal haematoma; severe back pain with fever or new leg symptoms days later is an epidural abscess until imaging says otherwise.

## Pitfalls and variants

- High-risk variants. Severe pre-eclampsia (platelet trend, early epidural, see the [pre-eclampsia card](pre-eclampsia-eclampsia-and-the-parturient.html)); cardiac disease (slow titration, arterial line for stenotic lesions and pulmonary hypertension); BMI above 40 (early catheter, low threshold to re-site); trial of labour after caesarean (rupture breaks through the block). These are the women in whom an early, proven catheter matters most.
- Total spinal and high block. Unrecognised intrathecal or subdural placement followed by an epidural-sized bolus: rapid ascending block, hypotension, bradycardia, apnoea. Left lateral or tilt, ventilate, intubate, vasopressor; the fetus tolerates this if the mother is oxygenated and perfused. Subdural block is the slower, patchy, unexpectedly high version with relative motor sparing and Horner's syndrome.
- Local anaesthetic toxicity. Rare with dilute maintenance, plausible with a surgical top-up through a catheter that has migrated into a vein. Lipid emulsion 20% is kept on every labour ward.
- Fetal bradycardia after initiation. More often uterine hypertonus after intrathecal opioid than hypotension; palpate the uterus, stop oxytocin, give a tocolytic.
- Pruritus, urinary retention and shivering are common and minor. Retention matters because an overfull bladder obstructs descent and causes pain through the block.
- Anticoagulation. Insertion and removal are timed to the last dose: 12 hours after a prophylactic dose of low molecular weight heparin and 24 hours after a therapeutic dose. A woman on therapeutic anticoagulation needs a plan made antenatally, not at 4 cm.
- Platelet count between 50 and 70 x 10^9/L. Outside the consensus comfort zone; a decision for an experienced operator weighing aetiology, trend and the airway.
- The broken or knotted catheter. Do not pull against resistance; flex the spine, try again later, image if retained. A small retained fragment in an asymptomatic woman is usually left.
- When the case leaves the labour ward. A woman with a high block, toxicity or a suspected haematoma needs theatre-level monitoring and a senior anaesthetist immediately; the last needs imaging within hours.

## Key sources

The core practice is well supported: Cochrane reviews for epidural against other analgesia and for programmed boluses, and a large randomised trial for low-dose solutions. Dural puncture epidural rests on small trials with onset as the outcome. Post-dural puncture headache management is consensus built on weak evidence, as its authors state, and the statements here about blood patch timing and durability reflect that guideline and standard teaching rather than a single trial. The incidence figures quoted to women for accidental dural puncture and inadequate block are conventional consent figures, not drawn from a source in the list. The explanation of epidural-associated fever is incomplete in the literature.
