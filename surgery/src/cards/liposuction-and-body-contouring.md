## What the surgeon is doing and why

Liposuction removes subcutaneous fat through small stab incisions with a blunt hollow cannula attached to suction. It reshapes; it does not treat obesity. The technique that made it safe was wetting the fat first. In the "super-wet" method the surgeon infiltrates about 1 mL of solution for each millilitre of fat to be removed, and in the "tumescent" method 2-3 mL, until the tissue is firm. The solution is saline or Hartmann's with adrenaline at about 1 mg/L and lidocaine at 0.5-1 g/L. The adrenaline constricts the vessels so that the aspirate is nearly bloodless and the lidocaine is absorbed slowly; the fluid stiffens the fat so the cannula breaks it up evenly; the lidocaine gives analgesia for most of a day. Power-assisted, ultrasound-assisted and laser-assisted devices are variations on how the fat is loosened.

The same fat may be washed and injected elsewhere (breast, face, buttock) as a graft.

Excisional body contouring is a different scale of surgery for a different patient: someone who has lost 40-80 kg after bariatric surgery and is left with redundant skin. The surgeon cuts it out. A belt lipectomy or lower body lift removes a circumferential band of skin and fat from the trunk and closes the edges under tension, lifting the thighs and buttocks; a brachioplasty removes an ellipse along the inner arm; a thigh lift does the same in the groin and medial thigh. These are long operations with long wounds, turns on the table and real blood loss.

```
 in:   infiltrate (L)  ->  about 70% stays and is absorbed
       IV fluid (L)
 out:  aspirate (L) = fat + some infiltrate + about 1% blood
       urine
 hidden: lidocaine mg/kg, adrenaline mg, heat
```

The pain logic is skin and soft tissue. Liposuction leaves a wide zone of bruised, tunnelled fat that is numb on the day and feels beaten on days 1-3, with stiffness for a couple of weeks. Excision adds wound tension around a moving trunk or limb. Nothing deep is opened, so no single nerve block matches the field. The tumescent infiltrate is the block, and the anaesthetic problems of this card are pharmacological and physiological, not analgesic: a very large local anaesthetic dose with a late peak, several milligrams of adrenaline, litres of fluid in an unusual compartment, heat loss, and thromboembolism.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment, marking and the volume plan
**Why:** The surgeon marks the areas with the patient standing and estimates how much will be infiltrated and aspirated. That estimate decides the setting and the stay.
**Anaesthesia:** Agree four numbers before the patient is asleep: weight, total lidocaine in milligrams per kilogram, total adrenaline, and expected aspirate. Estimated ceilings for tumescent lidocaine are 45 mg/kg when liposuction follows and 28 mg/kg when it does not; many practices stay at or below 35 mg/kg. Ask about drugs that inhibit lidocaine metabolism (macrolides, azole antifungals, some antidepressants) and about liver disease. An aspirate above about 5 L is large-volume liposuction, with more fluid shift and a case for overnight monitoring. The post-bariatric patient needs a haemoglobin, iron studies, albumin and a look at what the bariatric operation has done to absorption (see [bariatric surgery](bariatric-surgery-sleeve-gastrectomy-and-gastric.html)). Oestrogens, previous thrombosis and long operating time set the thromboprophylaxis plan.

### 2. Induction, position and warming
**Why:** Several areas on both sides of the body mean a circumferential preparation, often standing, and one or more turns.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia is usual for anything beyond a small volume; some practitioners work under tumescent anaesthesia with sedation, and deep sedation in the prone position without a secured airway is where reported deaths have come from. Intubate if the patient will be prone. The patient is exposed and wet with preparation solution from neck to knee: pre-warm, warm the room, use an underbody blanket and warm the infiltrate to body temperature. Calf compressors go on before induction.

### 3. Infiltration
**Why:** The surgeon pumps the wetting solution into the deep and superficial fat through the access incisions until the area is turgid, then waits 10-20 minutes for vasoconstriction.
**Anaesthesia:** Record each bag, its lidocaine and adrenaline content and the time. Under general anaesthesia the lidocaine contributes little that you need intraoperatively, and some teams omit or halve it and keep the adrenaline, which removes most of the toxicity risk at the cost of the first day's analgesia; surgeons vary. Adrenaline absorption is slow but the totals are large: expect a higher heart rate and pressure than the depth of anaesthesia explains, and be cautious in patients on non-selective beta-blockers. Do not add a bupivacaine or ropivacaine block without subtracting it from the plan, because toxicity is additive.

### 4. Aspiration
**Why:** The cannula is passed in a fan pattern through the wet fat under suction; the surgeon watches the colour of the aspirate and feels the contour with the other hand.
**Anaesthesia:** Stimulation is modest where lidocaine was used and noticeable where it was not. The aspirate canister separates into yellow fat above and pink infiltrate below; the pinker the fat layer, the more blood is being lost. A sudden problem during aspiration is rare and serious: the cannula can pass through the abdominal wall, particularly through an unrecognised hernia or an old scar, and the perforation of bowel declares itself a day or two later as pain out of proportion and sepsis.

### 5. Fluid accounting
**Why:** Fat removal leaves a raw space that behaves like a burn, drawing fluid in, while most of the infiltrate is still in the tissues waiting to be absorbed.
**Anaesthesia:** Treat the infiltrate as fluid given. In a retrospective series using the super-wet technique, patients were given maintenance fluid only, with replacement added once aspirate exceeded 5 L, for a ratio of total fluid in to aspirate out of 1.8 in small-volume and 1.2 in large-volume cases, and had brisk urine output and no pulmonary oedema. The practical rule is restraint: maintenance crystalloid, vasopressor for anaesthetic vasodilation, and a urinary catheter and an hourly tally for large-volume cases. Over-resuscitation is the commoner error; the patient with a 6 L aspirate who is also under-filled exists but is recognised by tachycardia and poor urine output, not by formula.

### 6. Turning
**Why:** Flanks, back and buttocks are reached prone or lateral; the surgeon re-preps and continues.
**Anaesthesia:** A turn in the middle of a case with wet skin, a cold patient and a single cannula is where tubes, lines and corneas are lost. Disconnect, turn as a team, re-check the tube, eyes, breasts, genitals and pressure points, and resume warming at once. Venous return can fall on turning prone after several litres of vasodilating anaesthetic and little intravenous fluid.

### 7. Fat grafting
**Why:** Harvested fat is washed and injected in small aliquots into the subcutaneous plane of the recipient site, where it survives as a graft.
**Anaesthesia:** Injection into the breast or face is uneventful. Gluteal fat grafting is the exception and the highest-mortality operation in aesthetic surgery: fat injected into or under gluteus maximus can enter a torn gluteal vein and reach the lungs as a massive embolus, presenting on the table as sudden hypoxia, a fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide and collapse. Surgical safety guidance restricts injection to the subcutaneous plane. An abrupt cardiorespiratory event during buttock injection is fat embolism and is managed as a cardiac arrest from pulmonary embolism.

### 8. Excisional contouring
**Why:** For a body lift the surgeon excises the marked band of skin and fat, undermines as little as possible, and closes the superficial fascia and skin under tension, working prone and then supine or lateral then lateral. Brachioplasty and thigh lift follow the same pattern along a limb.
**Anaesthesia:** A different operation from liposuction: 4-7 hours, two or three positions, wounds over a metre in total, blood loss of 500-1,000 mL or more in patients who often start anaemic, and steady heat loss. Have a group and screen, two cannulae and, in the author's practice, an arterial line for circumferential surgery. Check the haemoglobin during the case. Closure under tension means the patient is positioned flexed afterwards, as after [abdominoplasty](breast-reduction-augmentation-and-abdominoplasty.html), which is the anterior half of the same operation.

### 9. Closure, garments and emergence
**Why:** Access incisions are left open or loosely closed to drain; compression garments are applied to limit oedema and bruising.
**Anaesthesia:** Expect the bed to be wet with pink fluid for hours: it is infiltrate and it is not haemorrhage. Garments are put on before the patient wakes. Aim for a smooth emergence with antiemetics given; these patients have had a long anaesthetic, are frequently cold, and shiver.

### 10. Recovery and the first night
**Why:** The surgeon wants the patient walking the same evening and wearing the garment continuously.
**Anaesthesia:** In the largest survey of deaths after liposuction, many occurred during the first night at home, and pulmonary embolism was the leading cause. The first night is also when lidocaine peaks. Discharge criteria for a day case are therefore strict: normal temperature, urine passed, walking, an adult staying overnight who has been told the signs of toxicity (tingling around the mouth, tinnitus, agitation, twitching, drowsiness) and of embolism. Large-volume cases and anyone with a long operation stay in a monitored bed.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is diffuse soft-tissue trauma, so the plan is the infiltrate followed by systemic analgesia. Where lidocaine has been used in the wetting solution it provides analgesia for 12-24 hours. The patient feels little on the evening of surgery and then a rising, bruised, burning soreness on day 1 that peaks on days 1-2 and is provoked by moving, sitting on treated areas and putting the garment on. That late rise is this operation's version of rebound and the reason the oral regimen starts in recovery, not on demand.

The defensible default is paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor regularly for a week, dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction, a small intraoperative opioid dose, and a limited supply of oral opioid for the first three nights. There is no PROSPECT guideline and no block that covers a liposuction field. For the excisional operations, a transversus abdominis plane block or long-acting local anaesthetic into the wound edges helps the anterior trunk closure, but only if the lidocaine total leaves room for it: the author's practice is to ask for a lidocaine-free or low-lidocaine infiltrate under general anaesthesia when a long-acting block is planned, on the reasoning that two local anaesthetics with different peak times are harder to keep safe than one. That is judgement and not trial evidence.

After a circumferential body lift, expect worst pain of 4-5 on days 1-2 with movement and a slower fall, driven by wound tension; patient-controlled opioid for the first night is reasonable in hospital.

Escalation triggers: neurological or cardiac symptoms in the first 24 hours are local anaesthetic toxicity and are treated with lipid emulsion and support; abdominal pain that worsens after day 1, with fever or tachycardia, is a visceral perforation until excluded; breathlessness, confusion and a petechial rash at 24-72 hours suggest fat embolism syndrome; breathlessness or collapse at any time in the first weeks is pulmonary embolism; dizziness on standing with a tachycardia after a large-volume case is hypovolaemia or concealed bleeding.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. The emergency variant most specific to this card, and delayed. Know where the lipid emulsion is in a free-standing facility.
- Volume. Small-volume liposuction is a minor day case. Above about 5 L of aspirate the physiology changes (fluid shift, hypothermia, blood loss into tissue) and the advisory documents recommend a monitored overnight stay and discourage combination with other major procedures.
- Mortality. A census of plastic surgeons in the 1990s found 1 death in 5,224 procedures, with thromboembolism, perforation, anaesthesia and sedation, fat embolism and haemorrhage as causes. Technique has changed since, but the list of causes has not.
- Thromboprophylaxis. Mechanical measures and early walking for all. Chemoprophylaxis is debated in aesthetic surgery: retrospective abdominoplasty data show more bleeding without a clear reduction in thrombosis, yet circumferential and combined operations are the highest-risk cases. Follow a policy agreed with the surgeon.
- Fluid overload. Pulmonary oedema after liposuction is nearly always the infiltrate plus generous intravenous fluid in a patient whose adrenaline-induced vasoconstriction is wearing off.
- Hypothermia. Predictable and preventable; it also worsens bleeding and delays discharge.
- Sedation-only practice. If you are asked to supervise deep sedation for prone liposuction, the safer offer is a general anaesthetic with an airway.
- Lipoedema surgery. Staged large-volume liposuction of the legs in patients with a chronic painful fat disorder; several litres per stage, with the large-volume precautions.
- After massive weight loss. Anaemia, micronutrient deficiency, poor wound healing, sleep apnoea that may have improved or not, and altered drug absorption. Staging the contouring into operations of under six hours is common surgical policy.
- Ultrasound and laser devices. Thermal burns to skin are a surgical complication; they do not change the anaesthetic.
- Invasive monitoring, blood and critical care are for the circumferential excisional operations and for complications. Routine liposuction needs none of them.

## Key sources

The numbers that drive this card are a volunteer pharmacokinetic study (lidocaine), a 1990s mortality census (deaths) and an 89-patient retrospective series (fluid): useful, old or small, and none of them randomised. The ASPS advisory is consensus. Statements about gluteal fat grafting, the proportion of infiltrate retained, the blood content of aspirate and the timing of peak lidocaine levels are widely taught and are not drawn from the listed abstracts. The choice between lidocaine in the infiltrate and a long-acting block is the author's judgement.
