## What the surgeon is doing and why

The pancreas lies across the back of the upper abdomen, wrapped around the vessels that supply the entire gut. Its head sits in the curve of the duodenum, shares the duodenum's blood supply, and is pierced by the bile duct; the superior mesenteric and portal veins run in a groove behind its neck; the splenic vessels run along its body and tail. So the organ cannot be removed in pieces that respect only itself. A tumour in the head takes the duodenum and bile duct with it. A tumour in the tail usually takes the spleen.

Pancreaticoduodenectomy (the Whipple operation) is done for cancers of the pancreatic head, ampulla, distal bile duct and duodenum, and for some premalignant cysts. It has two halves.

```
 RESECTION (3-4 h)                    RECONSTRUCTION (2-3 h)
 gallbladder, distal bile duct        one jejunal limb, three joins:
 duodenum (+/- antrum)                1 pancreas   -> jejunum (the one that leaks)
 head of pancreas, divided at neck    2 bile duct  -> jejunum
   over the portal vein               3 stomach or duodenum -> jejunum
 first loop of jejunum
```

The dangerous moment of the resection is dividing the pancreatic neck and peeling the head off the portal and superior mesenteric veins, where small fragile tributaries enter directly; a tear there bleeds fast from a vein that cannot simply be tied. If tumour involves the vein, a segment is resected and rejoined with the portal flow clamped. The dangerous feature of the reconstruction is the pancreatic anastomosis: a soft gland with a small duct, secreting enzymes that digest suture lines. A pancreatic fistula is the root of most of the serious complications, including the late arterial bleed in the trap line and much of the delayed gastric emptying.

Distal pancreatectomy removes the body and tail to the left of the mesenteric vessels, usually with the spleen because the splenic vessels are embedded in the gland; spleen-preserving versions exist for benign disease. There is no anastomosis, only a stapled or sutured pancreatic stump, which leaks in a substantial minority but with less consequence. It is now commonly laparoscopic or robotic, which makes it a different anaesthetic and a different recovery from the Whipple. Total pancreatectomy combines both and leaves the patient with no insulin and no glucagon.

The patient is typically in their sixties or seventies with weight loss, new diabetes, sometimes jaundice (with its vitamin K-dependent coagulopathy, renal vulnerability and, if stented, a colonised biliary tree), and increasingly several months of neoadjuvant chemotherapy behind them. Routine preoperative biliary stenting of the jaundiced patient increases complications, so many now come to theatre jaundiced by design.

The pain logic: open pancreatic surgery is open upper abdominal surgery at full length and duration, and scores 5. The wall mechanism dominates: a long midline or rooftop incision, hours of fixed retraction on the costal margins, pain on every cough for a week or more. Under it is an extensive retroperitoneal dissection around the coeliac and superior mesenteric plexuses, which gives a deep epigastric and back pain that many of these patients already had from the tumour, and several arrive on opioids. Minimally invasive distal pancreatectomy has only ports, a small extraction wound and a left upper quadrant dissection, and its course is moderate and short.

## Steps

### 1. Preassessment
**Why:** The surgeon has judged the tumour resectable or borderline and may be operating after chemotherapy.
**Anaesthesia:** Nutrition and weight loss (the ERAS guideline gives high-level support to preoperative nutrition when loss exceeds 15%), diabetes control, bilirubin and INR, renal function, functional capacity and frailty. Give vitamin K if jaundiced. Record preoperative opioid use, because it changes the analgesic plan more than any choice of block. For distal pancreatectomy with planned splenectomy, vaccination against encapsulated organisms should have been given two weeks before.

### 2. Regional technique and induction
**Why:** Nothing surgical yet.
**Anaesthesia:** Thoracic epidural at T7-T9 awake, or intrathecal morphine, according to the plan below. Rapid sequence induction if the duodenum is obstructed. Tracheal tube, arterial line, central line for Whipple, warming from the start (hypothermia prevention is one of the five best-evidenced items in the guideline and this is a long open abdomen). Antibiotic chosen with the biliary stent in mind.

### 3. Exploration and assessment of resectability
**Why:** The surgeon looks for liver and peritoneal metastases and tests whether the tumour will come off the mesenteric vessels; unresectable disease found now ends the operation with a bypass or nothing.
**Anaesthesia:** A fifth-hour analgesic plan is wasted on a 40-minute laparoscopy. Keep the long-acting choices reversible until the surgeon commits, and ask.

### 4. Kocher manoeuvre and mobilisation
**Why:** The duodenum and pancreatic head are lifted off the vena cava and aorta; the hepatic flexure is taken down; the lesser sac is opened.
**Anaesthesia:** Lifting the head compresses the vena cava and the arterial pressure dips; mesenteric traction produces flushing and vasodilatation early in the case. Begin as you mean to continue with fluid: a restrained maintenance rate, boluses only against a stroke volume response.

### 5. Hilar dissection, cholecystectomy and bile duct division
**Why:** The hepatic artery nodes are cleared, the gastroduodenal artery is test-clamped and divided, the gallbladder is removed and the bile duct is cut.
**Anaesthesia:** Infected bile from a stented duct spills into the field: bacteraemia with hypotension and a temperature rise 20-30 minutes later is common and is the reason for broader antibiotic cover. The surgeon checks for a hepatic artery pulse with the gastroduodenal artery clamped; a patient with coeliac stenosis may depend on that vessel, which changes the operation.

### 6. Division of stomach or duodenum and of jejunum
**Why:** The gut is divided proximally and distally so the specimen is attached only by the pancreatic neck and its venous tributaries.
**Anaesthesia:** Withdraw the nasogastric tube clear of the stapler when asked. Nothing else happens, which makes it a good time for a gas, a glucose and an antibiotic re-dose.

### 7. Pancreatic neck division and dissection off the portal vein
**Why:** The gland is divided over the portal vein and the head is separated from the superior mesenteric vein and artery, dividing the uncinate tributaries one by one.
**Anaesthesia:** The highest-risk step for sudden haemorrhage. Have blood checked and in the room, the rapid infuser primed and a second pair of hands identified. If a vein resection is planned or forced, the portal and superior mesenteric veins are clamped for 15-30 minutes: the gut congests, venous return falls modestly, and unclamping returns cold acidotic blood. The surgeon may ask for heparin 2,500-5,000 units IV before the clamp; agree whether it is to be reversed, and remember the epidural.

### 8. Pancreatic anastomosis
**Why:** The pancreatic stump is joined to jejunum (or occasionally stomach), duct to mucosa, sometimes over a stent.
**Anaesthesia:** Forty-five minutes of fine suturing during which the surgeon wants a still field, a perfused bowel and no oedema. Hold mean arterial pressure near baseline with noradrenaline, not with litres of crystalloid: in the randomised comparison of epidural and intravenous analgesia, the epidural group gained more weight by day 4 and received more vasopressors, which is the fluid cost of treating epidural hypotension with volume. Some surgeons ask for a somatostatin analogue around this point; it is a surgical choice, given subcutaneously or by infusion.

### 9. Biliary and gastric or duodenal anastomoses
**Why:** The bile duct is joined to the same jejunal limb downstream, then the stomach or duodenum further down still.
**Anaesthesia:** The nasogastric tube, if one is to stay, is positioned under the surgeon's direction. Glucose commonly rises through the reconstruction; run insulin by infusion against hourly measurements. After total pancreatectomy start a low fixed-rate insulin infusion with glucose and expect very high sensitivity to both insulin and its absence.

### 10. Distal pancreatectomy (the other operation)
**Why:** The lesser sac is opened, the splenic artery and vein are controlled and divided, the pancreas is stapled across at the neck or body, and the tail and spleen are mobilised out of the left upper quadrant.
**Anaesthesia:** Usually laparoscopic or robotic: head up, pneumoperitoneum for two to four hours, limited access once the robot is docked. The splenic vein and its junction with the portal vein are the bleeding risk, and conversion for haemorrhage is abrupt, so two reliable cannulae and blood available are not optional. Mobilising the spleen can breach the left diaphragm and admit carbon dioxide to the pleura. For neuroendocrine tumours, handle the functional ones on their merits: glucose every 15 minutes around insulinoma excision.

### 11. Drains, closure and catheters
**Why:** Drains are placed beside the pancreatic and biliary joins; the fascia is closed.
**Anaesthesia:** If wound or fascial plane catheters are the plan they go in here, by the surgeon in the preperitoneal plane along the length of the wound, or as rectus sheath or subcostal TAP catheters. Keep the patient relaxed until the fascia is closed. Check temperature, gas and glucose before waking.

### 12. Emergence and the first two weeks
**Why:** The surgeon wants an awake, mobilising patient and will measure drain amylase on day 1 and 3 to predict a fistula.
**Anaesthesia:** Extubate in theatre. The complications have a calendar. Days 1-3: epidural hypotension, fluid balance, glucose. Days 3-10: pancreatic fistula (amylase-rich drain fluid, fever, tachycardia, ileus) and delayed gastric emptying, which means nausea, a nasogastric tube again and no reliable oral route for analgesics. Days 5-14: post-pancreatectomy haemorrhage from a pseudoaneurysm of the gastroduodenal stump or hepatic artery, heralded by a small sentinel bleed. That patient needs angiographic embolisation or stenting in preference to a relaparotomy through a hostile abdomen, and comes to you shocked, in the radiology suite, at night.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a long upper abdominal wound loaded for a week or more, over a deep visceral dissection, often in a patient with pre-existing cancer pain. A continuous technique is indicated for every open resection. This is one of the few operations in the book where the choice of technique has been tested against the outcome surgeons care about.

The PAKMAN randomised trial compared thoracic epidural with intravenous patient-controlled opioid after pancreatoduodenectomy and found gastrointestinal complications, including fistula and delayed gastric emptying, to be comparable. The epidural's costs were measurable: more weight gain by day 4, more vasopressor use, and failure in 18.5%. The authors concluded that the choice should not rest on gastrointestinal complications and that the epidural had several shortcomings. The 2019 ERAS guideline, for its part, lists wound catheters as an alternative to epidural analgesia among its five items with the highest level of evidence. There is no PROSPECT guideline for pancreatic surgery; the open liver resection guideline, for a similar incision, recommends epidural or bilateral subcostal TAP blocks on a base of paracetamol and an NSAID.

Taken together these support two defensible strategies and no automatic winner. An epidural at T7-T9 gives the best dynamic pain relief while it works, and is the author's preference for the patient with significant lung disease, provided it is run dilute, the hypotension is treated with noradrenaline and not fluid, and there is a charted rescue for the one in five that fail. The alternative is intrathecal morphine 150-300 micrograms before induction with surgeon-placed preperitoneal wound catheters or bilateral rectus sheath or subcostal TAP catheters (ropivacaine 0.2%) and a PCA, which avoids the haemodynamic cost and the motor block and is the author's default when the fluid plan is strict or the ward's epidural experience is thin. That preference is judgement, not a trial result.

Around either: regular IV paracetamol (delayed gastric emptying makes the oral route unreliable for days); NSAIDs are avoided by most pancreatic surgeons in the first days because of the kidneys, the anastomoses and bleeding concerns; low-dose ketamine infusion at 0.1-0.2 mg/kg/h for patients who were taking opioids before surgery, with their baseline opioid continued in equivalent parenteral form. For minimally invasive distal pancreatectomy, intrathecal morphine or single-shot subcostal TAP blocks, port infiltration, paracetamol and oral or PCA opioid for 48 hours are enough.

Rebound is high: a failed epidural or a dislodged catheter on day 1 exposes the worst wound in abdominal surgery, so the rescue is written before it is needed. Escalation triggers: pain that increases from day 3 with fever, tachycardia or ileus is a fistula or collection; new back or epigastric pain with any bleeding from day 5 is the sentinel event and is not treated with analgesia.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Post-pancreatectomy haemorrhage. The high-risk variant of this card. Early bleeds (first 24 hours) are technical and return to theatre. Late bleeds are erosive, arterial and intermittent: mobilise blood, call interventional radiology, secure the airway before the patient is exsanguinating in a scanner, and avoid normalising the pressure before control.
- The jaundiced patient. Coagulopathy corrected with vitamin K, kidneys vulnerable to hypovolaemia and to NSAIDs, endotoxaemia, and bradycardia with high bilirubin. Stented patients have colonised bile and more infective complications.
- Portal or superior mesenteric vein resection. Heparin, clamping, gut congestion and a longer, bloodier operation; discuss epidural timing with the surgeon beforehand.
- Total pancreatectomy. No endogenous insulin or glucagon: glucose swings in both directions with small insulin doses, hypoglycaemia is unbuffered, and an endocrinology plan must exist before the patient leaves theatre.
- Splenectomy with distal pancreatectomy. Thrombocytosis afterwards, lifelong infection risk, vaccinations two weeks before or two weeks after, and antibiotic prophylaxis.
- Functional neuroendocrine tumours. Insulinoma (hypoglycaemia before, rebound hyperglycaemia within minutes of excision), gastrinoma (high-dose proton pump inhibitor), and rarely a carcinoid-type syndrome with liver metastases needing octreotide to hand.
- Chronic pancreatitis. Opioid-tolerant, malnourished, often alcohol-related: resections and drainage procedures in these patients carry the hardest analgesic problem in the specialty and warrant a pain service plan in advance.
- Delayed gastric emptying. Common, benign and miserable. It converts every oral analgesic plan to intravenous for a week.
- Robotic Whipple. Seven to ten hours head up with fixed access; the pain course afterwards is shorter than open, but the anaesthetic is longer and the consequences of an unreachable bleeding portal vein are the same.

## Key sources

The ERAS guideline for pancreatoduodenectomy is recent, graded and candid that only five items carry the highest level of evidence. PAKMAN is a proper randomised trial with a clear message about what the epidural does not buy. The preoperative biliary drainage trial is included because it explains why jaundiced patients come to theatre undrained. The statements on fluid balance and fistula reflect an association in observational studies and the reasoning in the guideline; no fluid volume threshold was verified during drafting, so none is given. The timing and management of late haemorrhage are standard hepatopancreatobiliary teaching and are unsourced. The choice between epidural and intrathecal morphine with wound catheters is author's judgement informed by the two sources above.
