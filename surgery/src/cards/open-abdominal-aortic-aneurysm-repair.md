## What the surgeon is doing and why

An abdominal aortic aneurysm is a wall that has lost its elastin and is dilating under pulse pressure; wall tension rises with diameter, so the larger it is the faster it grows and the more likely it is to rupture. Nothing reverses this. The operation excludes the weak segment from arterial pressure by sewing a fabric tube inside it, from normal aorta above to normal aorta or iliac arteries below. The aneurysm is not removed. The sac is opened, the graft is laid within it, and the sac is closed over the graft to separate it from the duodenum.

Open repair has become the minority operation. Endovascular repair has lower early mortality (in EVAR trial 1 the hazard of death in the first six months was about 40% lower) but that advantage is lost within a few years and reverses after eight, with more late ruptures and reinterventions. Open repair is therefore chosen for the younger, fitter patient with long life expectancy, for anatomy that will not take a stent graft (a short, angulated or conical neck, or narrow access vessels), and for a failed or infected endograft. The patient on your list is either the fittest of the aneurysm population or the one with the worst anatomy, and the second group includes clamps above the renal arteries.

To sew the top anastomosis the surgeon must stop flow in the aorta. Where the clamp sits decides the physiology. Below the renal arteries, about a third of the cardiac output's distribution is cut off, afterload rises modestly, and the kidneys, gut and liver remain perfused. Above the renals, the kidneys are ischaemic for the duration. Above the coeliac axis, the gut and liver are as well, the afterload rise is severe, and release returns the washout of the entire splanchnic bed. Everything below the clamp is perfused only by collaterals for 30-60 minutes; it vasodilates, accumulates lactate and potassium, and is waiting to take a large fraction of the circulating volume back when the clamp is released.

Access is the other half of the insult. The transperitoneal route is a long midline incision with the small bowel lifted out of the abdomen to the right, the duodenum mobilised off the aorta and the left renal vein exposed at the neck. A fixed retractor holds the wound open for hours. Evisceration causes evaporative heat and fluid loss, mesenteric traction causes a prostaglandin-mediated flush and hypotension in the first 20 minutes, and bowel handling causes an ileus of several days. The left retroperitoneal route, through a flank incision with the peritoneal sac swept forward, avoids the evisceration and gives better access to the suprarenal aorta, at the price of a muscle-cutting incision and a lateral position.

The pain logic follows. The dominant mechanism is the abdominal wall: a full-length incision through the linea alba, or through three muscle layers in the flank, stretched by a retractor and then re-provoked by every cough and every attempt to sit up for a week. A visceral and retroperitoneal component adds a deep ache for the first two days. This is an operation in which the consequence of pain is respiratory: a patient who cannot cough after an upper abdominal incision, usually a smoker with obstructive lung disease, fails by atelectasis and pneumonia. The analgesic plan is therefore judged on movement-evoked pain and on cough, which is the case for an epidural.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative assessment and the plan for the clamp
**Why:** The surgeon's imaging tells you where the clamp will go, whether the iliacs need a bifurcated graft, and whether the left renal vein, an inflammatory rind or a horseshoe kidney will complicate the neck.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask for the clamp level before the day. An infrarenal tube graft in a patient with good functional capacity is a predictable case. A juxtarenal or suprarenal clamp changes the renal risk, the blood loss and the size of the release event. The population carries coronary disease, obstructive lung disease and renal impairment in combination; cardiopulmonary exercise testing or an equivalent objective measure of capacity is widely used to sort those who should have endovascular repair or no repair. Continue the statin, the antiplatelet (aspirin) and the beta-blocker; stop ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers on the day.

### 2. Epidural, lines and induction
**Why:** The surgeon needs a relaxed abdomen for three hours and a patient who can be extubated and can cough at the end.
**Anaesthesia:** Site a thoracic epidural at T8-T10 awake, test it, and establish it with a small bolus only: a dense block before the clamp is released makes the release harder to manage, so many anaesthetists load the epidural with opioid early and local anaesthetic after reperfusion is stable. The heparin dose at clamping comes at least 1 hour after an atraumatic epidural insertion, which in practice it always does. Arterial line before induction. The induction target is haemodynamic stability, because a hypertensive surge on laryngoscopy is a rise in wall tension in the aneurysm and hypotension is coronary ischaemia. Central access, two large-bore lines with a fluid warmer, urinary catheter, nasogastric tube, temperature probe. Upper-body forced-air warming starts before the skin is prepared, since heat lost in the first hour through an open abdomen is not recovered until the next day.

### 3. Laparotomy, evisceration and exposure of the neck
**Why:** The small bowel is displaced to the right and wrapped, the posterior peritoneum is opened over the aorta, the duodenum is reflected, and the neck of the aneurysm is dissected below the left renal vein. The iliac arteries are controlled next.
**Anaesthesia:** Mesenteric traction syndrome appears within minutes of bowel handling: facial flushing, tachycardia and a fall in pressure from prostacyclin release, lasting 20-30 min. It responds to fluid and a vasopressor and is blunted in patients who have taken an NSAID. Fluid requirement from here is real (evaporation, sequestration) but modest; the old practice of several litres of crystalloid before the clamp produces gut oedema and a wound that is hard to close. The left renal vein and its lumbar and gonadal tributaries are the source of the worst venous bleeding in this operation, and an inferior vena cava or iliac vein injury during dissection is the scenario in which the blood loss becomes several litres without warning. Start cell salvage collection at incision.

### 4. Heparin and aortic clamp
**Why:** Heparin prevents thrombosis in the static distal vessels. The iliac clamps usually go on first to prevent distal embolisation of sac thrombus, then the aortic clamp at the neck.
**Anaesthesia:** Give unfractionated heparin 70-100 units/kg IV and announce it; an activated clotting time is useful for long or suprarenal cases. At an infrarenal clamp expect a rise in systemic vascular resistance and arterial pressure of 10-20%, little change in heart rate, and a fall in cardiac output. A healthy ventricle tolerates this. A ventricle with coronary disease or poor function may show ST change, a rising filling pressure or new wall motion abnormality; treat with glyceryl trinitrate infusion or deepening of volatile, not with large doses of beta-blocker. Turn off any lower-body warming, because ischaemic tissue that is heated is injured. With a suprarenal clamp, note the time, since renal ischaemia beyond 30 min carries a rising risk of acute kidney injury, and with a supracoeliac clamp expect severe proximal hypertension requiring active vasodilatation.

### 5. Sac opening and control of back-bleeding
**Why:** The sac is opened longitudinally, laminated thrombus is scooped out, and the lumbar arteries and the inferior mesenteric artery, which now bleed backwards into the open sac, are oversewn from within.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the largest predictable blood loss of the case: 300-800 mL over a few minutes, at a time when the clamped circulation above is well filled and the pressure gives no warning. Watch the suction and the swabs, not the monitor. Cell salvage returns most of it. Use this period to prepare for release: bring the filling up, correct ionised calcium, check a blood gas, haemoglobin and potassium, and warm the fluids.

### 6. Proximal anastomosis
**Why:** The graft is sewn end to end to the aortic neck with a continuous monofilament suture, usually taking 15-25 min. The surgeon then moves the clamp onto the graft to test the suture line under pressure.
**Anaesthesia:** The test of the proximal suture line is a brief partial release with a surge of bleeding through needle holes. For a suprarenal clamp this moment, when the clamp is moved below the renal arteries, is the end of renal ischaemia and a small reperfusion event in its own right. Urine output during and after the clamp does not predict renal outcome; maintaining pressure and volume is the only renal protection with a physiological rationale, and mannitol, furosemide and dopamine have not been shown to help.

### 7. Distal anastomosis and preparation for release
**Why:** A tube graft is sewn to the aortic bifurcation; a bifurcated graft is sewn to each common iliac or tunnelled to the femoral arteries. Before completing the last suture line the surgeon flushes the graft forwards and backwards to clear thrombus and air.
**Anaesthesia:** Agree the release with the surgeon in words: which limb first, how slowly, and that they will reapply if asked. Before the clamp comes off, lighten the volatile, stop vasodilators, have the vasopressor infusion connected, give volume to the upper end of the filling range, and have calcium chloride 10% 5-10 mL ready. If the epidural has been run with local anaesthetic, turn it down or off until after release.

### 8. Clamp release and reperfusion
**Why:** Flow is restored to one leg, then the other, and the surgeon checks the anastomoses, the colour of the sigmoid colon and the femoral pulses.
**Anaesthesia:** On release, systemic vascular resistance falls abruptly as blood enters a maximally dilated, empty vascular bed; venous return falls as that bed fills; and the washout of lactate, potassium, carbon dioxide and vasoactive mediators reaches the heart within a minute. Expect a fall in pressure of 20-40%, a rise in end-tidal carbon dioxide and a transient rise in pulmonary artery pressure. Treat with volume and vasopressor boluses (metaraminol, phenylephrine or noradrenaline), increase minute ventilation, and if the fall is severe ask for the clamp to be partly reapplied; that is always available and always works. Sequential release of the limbs halves each event. After a suprarenal or supracoeliac clamp the same event is several times larger, with significant acidosis and hyperkalaemia, and coagulopathy from hepatic and gut ischaemia follows over the next hour.

### 9. Haemostasis, sac closure and abdominal closure
**Why:** The aneurysm sac is closed over the graft and the posterior peritoneum over the sac, so that the duodenum never lies on fabric. The bowel is returned and the long incision is closed with a mass technique.
**Anaesthesia:** Reverse heparin with protamine only if the surgeon asks, and slowly. Reinfuse the salvaged blood. Check the feet: a cold, mottled foot or an absent pulse at this point is embolism or a clamp injury and is dealt with before the patient leaves theatre, usually by embolectomy through the groin (see [embolectomy and fasciotomy for acute limb ischaemia](embolectomy-and-fasciotomy-for-acute-limb.html)). Now load the epidural: with reperfusion stable, establish the block to T6 with local anaesthetic so that it is working before emergence. Full neuromuscular reversal matters for closure and for the first cough.

### 10. Emergence and transfer
**Why:** The surgeon wants a patient who is warm, not bleeding and awake enough to move both legs.
**Anaesthesia:** Extubate in theatre if the patient is warm (above 36 degrees), haemodynamically stable on little or no vasopressor, has a normalising lactate and a working epidural. Leave intubated for a short period if the clamp was suprarenal with a large transfusion, if the temperature is below 35.5 degrees, or if the acidosis is not clearing. Leg movement before the epidural is loaded, or early in recovery with a low-concentration infusion, gives the baseline against which an epidural haematoma or spinal cord ischaemia will later be judged.

### 11. The first 48 hours
**Why:** The graft is secure; the complications are of the organs that were underperfused and of the patient's own heart and lungs.
**Anaesthesia:** Myocardial infarction peaks in the first three days and is usually silent under an epidural: troponin surveillance is reasonable. Acute kidney injury declares itself by day 2. Colonic ischaemia (the inferior mesenteric artery has been sacrificed) presents as bloody diarrhoea, rising lactate or unexplained acidosis on day 1-3. Epidural hypotension on the ward is treated with a vasopressor in a monitored bed before the infusion is turned off, because losing the epidural on day 1 exposes a full-length laparotomy with nothing else in place.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is abdominal wall, the wound crosses T6 to T12, and the outcome that matters is the ability to cough. A thoracic epidural addresses all three, and it is the technique with randomised evidence specific to this operation. In the Cochrane review of 15 trials and 1,498 patients, adding an epidural to general anaesthesia for open aortic surgery reduced pain scores on movement (high-quality evidence), reduced myocardial infarction (risk ratio 0.54), respiratory failure (risk ratio 0.69, number needed to treat 8) and gastrointestinal bleeding, and shortened ventilation and intensive care stay. It did not change 30-day mortality. The trials predate modern multimodal analgesia and catheter-based abdominal wall blocks, so the size of the benefit against a contemporary comparator is not known, but no alternative has comparable data in this operation.

The defensible default is therefore a T8-T10 epidural with a low-concentration local anaesthetic and opioid infusion (for example ropivacaine 0.2% with fentanyl 2 micrograms/mL) for 3-4 days, regular paracetamol, and a step-down plan written before the epidural comes out. NSAIDs are usually withheld for the first days, as an author's judgement: clamp-related renal injury, heparin and an older arteriopath population make the risk concrete. Thromboprophylaxis timing follows the neuraxial rules for catheter removal.

When an epidural is contraindicated (dual antiplatelet therapy that cannot be stopped, anticoagulation, spinal pathology, refusal) or fails, the author's alternative is surgically or ultrasound-placed bilateral rectus sheath catheters with intermittent boluses of ropivacaine 0.2% 20 mL per side every 6 h (80 mg per bolus, 320 mg in 24 h), combined with either intrathecal morphine 150-300 micrograms before induction or PCA opioid. That is an adult dose and not a fixed one: the 24 h total is checked in mg/kg against the local maximum, with the surgeon's infiltration and any other local anaesthetic counted in it, and the volume is reduced for the small or frail patient and when the clamp has left a kidney or liver injury, because this population clears amide local anaesthetic slowly. Rectus sheath catheters cover the midline somatic component only; the visceral ache of days 0-2 needs the opioid. Low-dose ketamine and intravenous lidocaine infusions are reasonable adjuncts under PCA. For a retroperitoneal flank incision the epidural still covers the wound, sited at the same level, since the flank incision lies within the dermatomes already blocked for the midline; a unilateral erector spinae or quadratus lumborum catheter is the fallback.

Rebound here is the day the epidural stops, planned or not. Worst pain is reported on days 1-2 with movement and falls from day 3-4 as mobilisation becomes routine, but coughing and sitting up still provoke the wound at a week, which is why the trajectory ends at 3 and not lower. Give a long-acting oral opioid or regular immediate-release doses before the infusion is stopped, not after. Escalation triggers: a unilateral or patchy block on day 0 is resited or replaced with rectus sheath catheters that day, not nursed along; pain out of proportion with abdominal distension, acidosis or bloody stool is gut ischaemia; new leg weakness with or without back pain is an epidural haematoma or cord ischaemia and needs the infusion stopped and imaging within the hour.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Juxtarenal and suprarenal aneurysms. The clamp goes above one or both renal arteries. Renal ischaemic time, cold renal perfusion by some surgeons, larger blood loss and a larger release event. Arterial blood gases every 30 min and a low threshold for post-operative ventilation.
- Inflammatory aneurysm. A thick fibrotic rind binds the duodenum, left renal vein and ureters to the sac. Dissection is slow and bloody; ureteric stents may be placed first.
- Retroperitoneal approach. Lateral tilt, flank incision, no evisceration, less ileus and fluid shift, better suprarenal access, poor access to the right iliac. The pain is muscle-cutting flank pain with a risk of a chronic flank bulge and neuralgia.
- Aortobifemoral graft for occlusive disease. The same operation on a non-aneurysmal aorta with chronic collaterals: the clamp and release are haemodynamically milder, and two groin wounds are added.
- Explantation of an infected or failed endograft. A suprarenal clamp, a difficult neck, heavy blood loss and a septic patient. Treat as the highest risk tier.
- Severe ventricular dysfunction or aortic stenosis. The clamp is an acute afterload test. Transoesophageal echocardiography or cardiac output monitoring guides volume and vasodilator better than pressure alone.
- Epidural and anticoagulation. Intra-operative heparin after atraumatic insertion is accepted practice; a bloody tap changes the conversation and the options are to delay the case or to proceed with close neurological observation, a decision shared with the surgeon. Clopidogrel or a direct oral anticoagulant not stopped in time removes the epidural from the plan.
- Spinal cord ischaemia. Rare after infrarenal repair, more likely with a high clamp, prolonged hypotension or loss of pelvic inflow. It must be distinguished from epidural block and epidural haematoma, which is the reason for a low-concentration infusion and regular motor checks.
- The emergency variant. Symptomatic non-ruptured aneurysms are repaired urgently with the same plan and less preparation. Rupture is a different operation with a different sequence and has its own card ([ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm](ruptured-abdominal-aortic-aneurysm.html)).
- The endovascular alternative is on its own card ([endovascular aneurysm repair](endovascular-aneurysm-repair.html)).

## Key sources

The analgesic recommendation is one of the better-supported in vascular anaesthesia: a Cochrane review of 15 randomised trials specific to open aortic surgery, with high-quality evidence for pain on movement and moderate-quality evidence for cardiac and respiratory outcomes, and no mortality effect. Its weakness is age, since the comparator was systemic opioid alone. There is no PROSPECT guideline for aortic surgery. Evidence for rectus sheath catheters in this operation is observational, and their place on this card is the author's judgement. Clamp physiology is textbook, with Gelman's review as the standard reference. The statements on renal protection reflect the absence of positive trials for any drug.
