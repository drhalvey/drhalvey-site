## What the surgeon is doing and why

A flexible bronchoscope is a steerable camera 4 to 6 mm across with a 2 to 3 mm working channel, passed through the nose, mouth or an airway device into the trachea and on to the segmental bronchi. Through it the bronchoscopist can look, wash a segment with saline and suck it back (bronchoalveolar lavage), brush, and take forceps biopsies of the airway wall or, blind or under fluoroscopy, of the lung beyond it. That was the whole of bronchoscopy for decades, and it was done awake with lidocaine and a little midazolam.

Interventional pulmonology has since taken over work that used to belong to the thoracic surgeon, and each new procedure makes a different demand.

- Endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS). A scope with a curved ultrasound array at its tip shows the lymph nodes outside the tracheal and bronchial wall, and a needle is passed through the wall into them under real-time vision. It stages lung cancer and diagnoses sarcoidosis and lymphoma without a [mediastinoscopy](mediastinoscopy-and-anterior-mediastinal-mass.html). The scope is wider and stiffer, the procedure takes 30 to 60 minutes with repeated needle passes at several stations, and a cough during a pass moves the node off the needle and puts the pulmonary artery near it.
- Peripheral navigation. A thin catheter or robotic scope is steered to a nodule in the outer third of the lung along a path planned from the CT, confirmed with a radial ultrasound probe, fluoroscopy or cone-beam CT, and biopsied. The plan was made on a CT taken awake at full inspiration. Under anaesthesia the dependent lung collapses, the nodule moves, and collapsed lung looks like tumour on ultrasound. The bronchoscopist therefore needs the lung held open and still: this is the one bronchoscopic procedure where your ventilator settings decide the diagnostic yield.
- Cryobiopsy. A probe is frozen onto lung parenchyma and pulled out with the scope, tearing away a piece large enough to diagnose interstitial lung disease. It leaves a bleeding hole with the scope out of the airway.
- Endobronchial valves. One-way valves are placed in every segment of the most emphysematous lobe so that it collapses and lets the better lung work. The patients have severe airflow obstruction and hyperinflation, and the intended lobar collapse causes a pneumothorax in about a quarter of them.

Rigid bronchoscopy, stents and tumour debulking have their own card: [rigid bronchoscopy, stents and tracheal resection](rigid-bronchoscopy-stents-and-tracheal-resection.html).

What unites them is that the airway is both the operative field and the route of ventilation, and that the dominant reflex to suppress is cough. The pain logic is short: mucosal irritation only, a day of cough and sore throat, and nothing that needs more than paracetamol. New chest pain afterwards means the pleura has been breached.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment and choosing the technique
**Why:** The bronchoscopist needs access, a quiet airway and, for some procedures, a motionless inflated lung; the technique is matched to which of those dominates.
**Anaesthesia:** Read the CT yourself: tracheal compression, a mediastinal mass, a central tumour that will bleed, and bullae all change the plan. For EBUS the evidence allows a choice. In the one randomised comparison, diagnostic yield was the same under general anaesthesia (70.7%) and moderate sedation (68.9%), with completion in all of the general anaesthesia group against 93.3% of the sedation group and more minor complications with sedation. So sedation is acceptable when staff and patient suit it, and general anaesthesia is the more reliable way to finish a multi-station staging. Navigation, cryobiopsy and valves are general anaesthetics. Severe COPD, pulmonary hypertension, superior vena cava obstruction and oxygen dependence are common on these lists and are reasons for a consultant, not reasons to cancel; antiplatelets and anticoagulants are stopped as for any biopsy of a vascular organ.

### 2. Topical anaesthesia of the airway
**Why:** Lidocaine to the nose or pharynx, the cords and the carina is what stops the cough; the bronchoscopist sprays as they go through the working channel.
**Anaesthesia:** Topicalisation matters under sedation and still helps under general anaesthesia, where it reduces the depth needed and the coughing at emergence. Lidocaine is absorbed quickly from the bronchial mucosa, and the dose is delivered by two people who are not counting together: nebulised or sprayed by you beforehand and instilled in aliquots by the bronchoscopist. Keep a running total aloud and agree the ceiling before starting. Toxicity presents as agitation or a seizure in a sedated patient and is easily misread as inadequate sedation. A topicalised larynx is an unprotected one for an hour or two afterwards: nil by mouth until then.

### 3. Sedation with a natural airway
**Why:** For diagnostic bronchoscopy, lavage and many EBUS lists the scope goes through the nose or a bite block with the patient breathing around it.
**Anaesthesia:** The scope partly obstructs the glottis and suction removes inspired oxygen, so hypoxaemia is the expected adverse event. Conscious sedation with midazolam and fentanyl or alfentanil keeps the patient cooperative; remimazolam is an alternative with faster recovery, a median six minutes to full alertness against 12 with midazolam in the registration trial. Propofol with a low-dose remifentanil infusion gives better cough suppression and a narrower margin before apnoea. High-flow nasal oxygen through the nostril the scope is not using raises the floor: in a small Australian trial of EBUS under sedation the lowest saturation was 97.5% against 92% with standard oxygen, though the difference in desaturation events (4 of 30 against 10 of 30) did not reach significance and the authors caution that it does not prevent every episode. Capnography from a nasal or bite-block sampler is the early warning. See the [remote site chapter](../a/a12-remote-site-sedation.html) for the room and staffing.

### 4. General anaesthesia and the airway device
**Why:** The bronchoscopist wants the largest, shortest conduit to the trachea that leaves the anatomy they need to see.
**Anaesthesia:** A supraglottic airway gives a wide lumen, allows inspection of the cords and subglottis, and lets the EBUS scope reach high paratracheal stations that a tracheal tube covers; it suits EBUS and valves. A tracheal tube of 8.0-8.5 mm gives a secure seal for PEEP, protects the other lung from blood and allows a bronchial blocker alongside the scope; it suits navigation, cryobiopsy and any high bleeding risk. A swivel connector with a diaphragm lets ventilation continue around the scope. Total intravenous anaesthesia with propofol and remifentanil is the default, because the circuit leaks at the port and suction strips volatile from the lung. Induction in the patient with a central airway tumour or mediastinal mass follows the [shared airway chapter](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html).

### 5. Scope in: ventilating around an obstruction
**Why:** The bronchoscope is passed through the device and the airway is inspected to the subsegmental level on both sides before any sampling.
**Anaesthesia:** A 6 mm EBUS scope inside an 8 mm tube leaves a crescent to ventilate through. Peak pressure rises, expiratory flow is limited and gas traps, most of all in the emphysematous patient who is here for valves. Use a slow rate with a long expiratory time, accept a higher end-tidal carbon dioxide, and disconnect briefly if the pressure falls with a slowing heart rate, which is dynamic hyperinflation. Suction through the scope removes tidal volume and PEEP; ask for it in short bursts. End-tidal readings are unreliable with the leak, so the chest, the saturation and the pressure-volume loop are the monitors.

### 6. EBUS needle aspiration of mediastinal nodes
**Why:** The balloon tip is pressed against the airway wall to couple the ultrasound, the node is identified with Doppler on to mark vessels, and a 19-22 gauge needle is passed through the wall repeatedly, often with a cytologist in the room judging adequacy before the next station.
**Anaesthesia:** Each needle pass is a cough stimulus at the carina and main bronchi, the most sensitive part of the tree. Under general anaesthesia a remifentanil infusion or a small dose of neuromuscular blocker abolishes it; under sedation it is topical lidocaine and opioid. The case length is set by the cytology, so plan for 45 minutes and do not use a long-acting relaxant without a reversal plan. Bleeding from a node is usually a few millilitres. Pneumomediastinum and mediastinitis are rare and late.

### 7. Peripheral navigation and biopsy
**Why:** The catheter or robotic scope is driven to the nodule along the planned path, position is confirmed by radial ultrasound and fluoroscopy or cone-beam CT, and needle, forceps and brush samples are taken through the airway wall or lung.
**Anaesthesia:** Atelectasis is the enemy and it is yours to prevent. In patients surveyed about half an hour after induction, 89% had atelectasis in at least one dependent segment, worse with higher BMI and with time. A randomised trial then showed that a ventilation strategy (tracheal tube, a recruitment manoeuvre after intubation, the lowest FiO2 that maintains saturation, and PEEP held throughout) reduced atelectasis on CT from 84.2% to 28.9% with no added complications, against a supraglottic airway on 100% oxygen with no PEEP. So: pre-oxygenate briefly, intubate, recruit, drop the FiO2, hold PEEP, avoid disconnections, and use neuromuscular block so that the lung is where the map says it is. Cone-beam CT spins need an apnoeic breath-hold at a set inflation pressure, which you deliver on request. A robotic arm docked at the mouth fixes the tube and the head; emergency access needs it undocked, so know how.

### 8. Cryobiopsy
**Why:** The cryoprobe is advanced under fluoroscopy to within about a centimetre of the pleura, frozen for a few seconds, and withdrawn with the scope as one unit because the frozen sample will not pass back through the channel. The airway is therefore unattended by the scope for the seconds when the biopsy site starts to bleed.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step with a planned bleeding drill. An expert working group published a statement specifically because of concern about bleeding and pneumothorax and the variation in how the procedure was being done. Standard practice in most units is a tracheal tube or rigid scope, with a bronchial blocker or balloon placed in the target lobar bronchus beforehand and inflated as the probe is pulled, so the blood stays in one lobe. Paralysis prevents the cough that drags the frozen probe through pleura. Have iced saline, adrenaline diluted for topical use and tranexamic acid drawn up, and the bleeding side identified so the patient can be turned that side down. A chest radiograph or ultrasound follows, because pneumothorax is common enough to look for every time.

### 9. Endobronchial valves
**Why:** Collateral ventilation is measured with a balloon catheter in the target lobe (a lobe with collateral flow will not collapse and is not treated), then valves are deployed in each segmental bronchus of that lobe.
**Anaesthesia:** The measurement of collateral ventilation needs quiet spontaneous or gentle controlled breathing for several minutes with a balloon occluding a lobe, which is why some bronchoscopists prefer deep sedation or a supraglottic airway with spontaneous ventilation; agree this beforehand. These are the worst lungs on any list: long expiratory times, permissive hypercapnia, no nitrous oxide, minimal opioid at the end, and extubation awake and sitting up. Pneumothorax was the commonest serious adverse event in the pivotal trial, in 26.6% of valve patients within 45 days, with deaths in that window; most occur in the first few days as the treated lobe collapses and the adjacent lobe is pulled across. The patient stays in hospital for that reason, and sudden deterioration in recovery is a tension pneumothorax until excluded.

### 10. Emergence and recovery
**Why:** The bronchoscopist finishes with a look for bleeding and suctions both sides clear.
**Anaesthesia:** Coughing at emergence restarts bleeding from biopsy sites, so stop the remifentanil late, consider lidocaine 1 mg/kg IV, and remove a tracheal tube smoothly or exchange it for a supraglottic airway under depth. Reverse neuromuscular block fully and confirm it; residual block in a patient with an FEV1 of 0.8 L is a reintubation. Sit the patient up. Bronchospasm after lavage or in the asthmatic responds to salbutamol. Fever on the evening of a lavage is common and self-limiting. Nil by mouth until the cough reflex is back. Any chest pain, breathlessness or a new oxygen requirement gets a radiograph before discharge.

## Pain plan

There is no pain to plan for beyond a sore throat and a cough, and paracetamol covers both. Opioid after the procedure is avoided in this population because it adds hypoventilation to poor lungs. The block entry on this card is airway topicalisation, and its purpose is cough suppression during the procedure and smoother emergence, with the dose shared between two operators and counted.

Intra-procedural antinociception is a short-acting opioid by infusion, matched to a stimulus that is intense and stops abruptly when the scope comes out. That mismatch, a deeply anaesthetised patient with no stimulus and an irritable airway, is why emergence needs planning.

Escalation is diagnostic. Pleuritic pain is a pneumothorax. Central chest pain with fever a day or two after EBUS is mediastinitis and is rare. Neither is treated as post-procedure pain.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Sedation or general anaesthesia for EBUS. Units run both. The randomised evidence says yield is the same; general anaesthesia completes more procedures and has fewer minor complications. Local capacity usually decides.
- Hypoxaemia during the procedure. Scope out, 100% oxygen, re-recruit. A saturation that will not recover is a mucus plug, a bleed into the good lung, a pneumothorax or bronchospasm, in that order of likelihood.
- Massive bleeding. More than a few tens of millilitres that does not stop: the bronchoscopist wedges the scope in the segment and holds suction, the bleeding side goes down, you give 100% oxygen and call for a blocker or a double-lumen tube and a thoracic surgeon. The principles of isolating a lung are in the [one-lung ventilation chapter](../a/a03-one-lung-ventilation.html).
- Laser, argon plasma and electrocautery through the flexible scope. Airway fire risk: FiO2 below 0.4 before energy is applied, and say the number aloud.
- The patient with superior vena cava obstruction. Upper limb cannulae deliver drugs slowly and airway oedema is likely; a lower limb line and a head-up position.
- Bronchoalveolar lavage in the hypoxic patient. Each 50 mL aliquot left in a segment costs oxygenation for hours; in the patient already on high-flow oxygen the lavage may be what leads to intubation, and that should be planned before it starts.
- Bronchial thermoplasty and other asthma procedures. Three sessions, an irritable airway each time, prednisolone cover from the respiratory team and a low threshold for overnight stay.
- Children and airway foreign bodies are a different operation with a different scope: see [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).

## Key sources

The comparison of sedation and general anaesthesia for EBUS rests on one randomised trial of 149 patients. The atelectasis work is a prospective cohort followed by a multicentre randomised trial and is the best evidence on this card that anaesthetic technique changes a procedural outcome. High-flow nasal oxygen for sedated bronchoscopy has one small trial with a favourable trend. Cryobiopsy practice is expert consensus. The lidocaine ceiling is not given as a figure here: published limits vary, and the working rule is a ceiling agreed beforehand and a running total kept aloud.
