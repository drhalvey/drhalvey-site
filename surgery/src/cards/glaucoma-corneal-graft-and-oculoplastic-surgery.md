## What the surgeon is doing and why

This card gathers the eye operations that are neither cataract, retina nor squint. They share a patient population and a set of anaesthetic principles, and they differ in the one thing each surgeon needs.

Glaucoma surgery lowers the pressure in the eye by giving the aqueous fluid another way out. In a trabeculectomy the surgeon lifts the conjunctiva, cuts a partial-thickness trapdoor in the sclera at the limbus, removes a small block of tissue beneath it to open the anterior chamber, and sutures the trapdoor down loosely so that fluid seeps out under the conjunctiva to form a bleb. An antimetabolite (mitomycin C) is applied to stop the bleb scarring shut. A drainage tube does the same through a silicone tube to a plate sutured to the sclera between the muscles. Minimally invasive stents are placed at the end of a cataract operation. Cyclodiode laser reduces the production of fluid by burning the ciliary body through the sclera. In infants with congenital glaucoma the drainage angle itself is opened (goniotomy, trabeculotomy), and the child returns for repeated examinations under anaesthesia.

Corneal grafting replaces diseased cornea. A penetrating keratoplasty removes a full-thickness disc with a trephine and sutures a donor disc in its place with sixteen or more fine sutures; for the minutes between the two the eye is open to the room. Lamellar operations replace only the diseased layer through a closed eye: the front layers (DALK) or the endothelium (DSAEK, DMEK), the latter held up against the cornea by a bubble of air or gas.

Oculoplastic surgery covers the lids, the tear drainage system and the orbit: ptosis repair, blepharoplasty, entropion and ectropion, lid tumour excision and reconstruction; dacryocystorhinostomy, which opens a new channel from the lacrimal sac into the nose through the bone of the lacrimal fossa, externally or endoscopically; and enucleation, evisceration, orbital decompression for thyroid eye disease and tumour surgery.

The pain logic is small wounds in sensitive surfaces. Most of this is score 1 to 2 and is abolished by the local anaesthetic the operation is done under. Three procedures hurt more than their size suggests: cyclodiode laser, dacryocystorhinostomy and enucleation. The plan for each is a surgeon-placed block and simple analgesics.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment: the glaucoma patient and their drops
**Why:** The surgeon has usually exhausted medical treatment, so the patient arrives on the maximum of it.
**Anaesthesia:** Topical timolol is absorbed through the nasal mucosa without first-pass metabolism and behaves like an intravenous beta-blocker in a frail patient: bradycardia, heart block, bronchospasm. Oral acetazolamide gives a hypokalaemic, hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis, worse with a thiazide; check potassium if it has been taken for more than a few days. Intravenous mannitol or acetazolamide given before surgery for a very high pressure is a volume load followed by a diuresis, so think of the heart and the bladder. Pilocarpine and the other drops matter little. An acutely raised pressure causes pain, vomiting and vagal symptoms on its own account. Anticoagulants follow the principles on the [cataract surgery](cataract-surgery.html) card, with more caution for trabeculectomy and lid surgery, where the surgeon may ask for a lower INR or a pause.

### 2. Trabeculectomy and drainage tubes
**Why:** The surgeon is making a controlled leak, working at the limbus under the upper lid with the eye rotated down by a traction suture.
**Anaesthesia:** Local anaesthesia is the norm. In advanced glaucoma the optic nerve has little reserve, and a review of anaesthesia for trabeculectomy argues that anaesthetic placed posteriorly (retrobulbar, peribulbar, posterior sub-Tenon) could injure it or compress its blood supply and extinguish what vision is left, while acknowledging that good evidence is lacking. The practical consequences: prefer subconjunctival or anterior sub-Tenon anaesthesia in small volume, avoid adrenaline near the nerve, and do not apply external pressure devices to the eye. Sedation is minimal for the reasons given on the cataract card; a cohort of over 19,000 eye operations associated intravenous sedatives with more adverse medical events. When the anterior chamber is opened the pressure falls from high to nothing in a second, the moment of risk for suprachoroidal haemorrhage in an eye with fragile choroidal vessels: no coughing, and a controlled blood pressure. A tube implant needs the eye rotated further and a larger dissection, and is more often done with a full sub-Tenon block or general anaesthesia.

### 3. Cyclodiode laser
**Why:** A probe at the limbus delivers 15 to 25 burns through the sclera to the ciliary body.
**Anaesthesia:** Brief and painful, each burn felt as a deep ache with a pop. It needs a real block (sub-Tenon or peribulbar) or a short general anaesthetic; topical anaesthesia with sedation is not adequate. Pain afterwards is inflammatory for one to two days, and these are often blind or nearly blind painful eyes in patients already on analgesics.

### 4. Children: examination under anaesthesia and angle surgery
**Why:** The surgeon needs a pressure reading that reflects the awake eye, then corneal diameter, optic disc and angle examination, and may proceed to surgery in the same anaesthetic.
**Anaesthesia:** Anaesthetic agents change the number being measured. In a randomised trial, intraocular pressure fell by 12% within two minutes of sevoflurane induction and by 17-19% from four minutes, while pressure after ketamine stayed nearer the awake value. Laryngoscopy and coughing raise it. So the sequence is agreed in advance: induce, and let the surgeon measure straight away, before the airway is instrumented and before the agent has had time to act, or use ketamine where the service prefers it. Record what was given and when beside the reading, so that serial examinations are comparable. Then place a supraglottic airway and continue. These children have many anaesthetics; see [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).

### 5. Penetrating keratoplasty: the open eye
**Why:** A trephine cuts out the central cornea, leaving a 7-8 mm hole; the donor button is laid in and sutured. Until the first four sutures are in, nothing holds the contents of the eye except the absence of any pressure behind them.
**Anaesthesia:** Anything that raises pressure behind the lens can expel it: a cough or strain, external pressure from a speculum or a squeezing lid, congestion of the choroid from a high venous pressure or carbon dioxide, or a suprachoroidal haemorrhage, which is why this operation carries the worst outcome of that complication. Two routes are in use. In the United Kingdom 86.6% of over 6,000 penetrating grafts were done under general anaesthesia, mainly from this concern; a Canadian department reported 2,143 under local anaesthesia with one expulsive haemorrhage. Under general anaesthesia: tracheal tube or a well-seated supraglottic airway, controlled ventilation to normocapnia, head up 10-15 degrees, deep anaesthesia and neuromuscular block with a quantitative monitor showing no twitches from before trephination until the graft is secure, and normotension. Under local anaesthesia: a dense block with akinesia of the globe and lids, time for it to soften the eye, and no sedation that might end with a startled patient. Tell the surgeon before the trephine if anything is unsettled; ask to be told when the eye is closed.

### 6. Lamellar and endothelial grafts
**Why:** Only the diseased layer is replaced through small incisions; an endothelial graft is unrolled inside the eye and pressed against the back of the cornea by an air or SF6 bubble.
**Anaesthesia:** A closed-eye operation, done under sub-Tenon or peribulbar block like a long cataract. Two specifics. No nitrous oxide if general anaesthesia is used, because the bubble will expand and raise the pressure, then shrink when the nitrous oxide leaves. And the patient must lie supine, face up, for the first hours so the bubble floats against the graft: plan a recovery in that position and warn that nausea must be reported early. A deep anterior lamellar graft can convert to a penetrating graft if the deep layer perforates, at which point step 5 applies.

### 7. Eyelid surgery
**Why:** Skin, muscle and tarsal plate are incised, shortened or repositioned; in ptosis repair the surgeon adjusts the lid height with the patient opening and closing the eyes on request.
**Anaesthesia:** Local infiltration with adrenaline by the surgeon, with light sedation for the injection and none afterwards if cooperation is needed. Ptosis repair under sedation deep enough to stop eye opening defeats the purpose. Open oxygen under drapes beside bipolar or monopolar diathermy on the lid is a recognised cause of facial burns: use room air, or the lowest oxygen concentration that maintains saturation delivered by a method that does not pool it, and tell the surgeon when it is on. Long reconstructions and bilateral lower lid surgery are reasonable under general anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway.

### 8. Dacryocystorhinostomy
**Why:** The surgeon removes the thin bone between the lacrimal sac and the nose with a rongeur or drill, opens the sac and the nasal mucosa into each other and leaves silicone tubes through the new channel. The approach is through a skin incision beside the nose or endoscopically through the nostril.
**Anaesthesia:** This is a nasal operation and is run like one: it bleeds from mucosa into the nasopharynx, and the field is helped by topical vasoconstrictor (prepared by the surgeon or by you), head-up tilt, a modest blood pressure and a smooth, still anaesthetic with propofol and remifentanil. The airway must be protected from blood: a tracheal tube, or a reinforced supraglottic airway with a good seal in practised hands, with a throat pack only under the usual protocol. Before waking, suction the pharynx and nasopharynx under vision. Extubate awake and sitting up. Some units do the external operation under infratrochlear and infraorbital blocks with sedation; the blood still runs backwards, so the sedation must leave airway reflexes intact. See [septoplasty, turbinate surgery and functional endoscopic sinus surgery](septoplasty-turbinate-surgery-and-functional-endoscopic.html).

### 9. Enucleation, evisceration and orbital surgery
**Why:** Enucleation removes the globe by detaching each extraocular muscle and cutting the optic nerve with scissors or a snare, then places an implant and reattaches the muscles. Evisceration empties the scleral shell. Orbital decompression removes bone from the orbital walls to make room in thyroid eye disease.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia. The oculocardiac reflex is at its strongest here: traction on all four recti in turn and then the clamp and cut across the optic nerve, which can produce a profound bradycardia or a pause. Give glycopyrronium 0.2-0.4 mg IV beforehand or have atropine in hand, and ask the surgeon to announce the nerve cut. A retrobulbar injection of long-acting local anaesthetic by the surgeon, before the muscle work or at the end, blunts the reflex and covers the first night. Pain and nausea afterwards are greater than for any other operation on this card. Orbital decompression and tumour surgery add bleeding, a longer case and a need for hourly checks of vision and pupil afterwards, because a postoperative orbital haematoma compresses the optic nerve.

### 10. Emergence and aftercare
**Why:** Every operation here leaves a fresh wound that a pressure surge can open or bleed into.
**Anaesthesia:** No coughing on the airway device: remove a supraglottic airway deep or run remifentanil to an awake extubation. No retching: antiemetic prophylaxis by default. Eye shield on before the patient can rub. Hand over the specifics: posture after an endothelial graft, eye observations after orbital surgery, the gas wristband if SF6 was used.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is skin and soft tissue on a small scale, so local anaesthesia placed by the surgeon or by you is the analgesic plan as well as the anaesthetic for most of these operations, and paracetamol with or without an NSAID covers what follows. There is no procedure-specific guideline, and the plan is consensus and author's judgement.

By operation: trabeculectomy, tubes and lamellar grafts need paracetamol alone. Penetrating grafts give a foreign-body sensation from the sutures for days, helped by lubricants more than analgesics. Lid surgery is sore and bruised for two to three days; cold compresses and paracetamol, with NSAIDs at the surgeon's discretion because of bruising. Dacryocystorhinostomy gives a blocked, aching nose and a headache for two to three days: paracetamol, an NSAID if the surgeon allows, and a few doses of oral opioid. Cyclodiode and enucleation are the two that need a long-acting block at the time and oral opioid available for one to three days. Rebound when a block recedes is seldom troublesome.

Expect a worst pain of about 2 out of 10 across the group on the first evening and next day, and 4-5 after enucleation without a block.

Escalation triggers are all surgical. After any intraocular operation, severe pain with nausea is a high pressure, and pain with a red eye and falling vision from day 2 is infection. After trabeculectomy a painful, very soft or very hard eye needs the surgeon the same day. After lid or orbital surgery, a tense swollen lid with proptosis, pain and falling vision is a retrobulbar haematoma: the sutures are opened and a canthotomy done at the bedside.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Systemic effects of eye drops. Timolol bradycardia and bronchospasm, and phenylephrine 10% drops causing hypertension in the frail and in infants, are the two that reach the anaesthetist.
- Posterior blocks in advanced glaucoma. Small volumes placed anteriorly; the nerve cannot spare any perfusion.
- Cough during the open-sky period. Preventable only beforehand: depth, monitored block, and a patient not woken until the eye is closed.
- Suprachoroidal haemorrhage. About 1 in 800 intraocular operations overall; the sudden decompression of a high-pressure eye and the open-sky graft are the settings in which it is most feared. Sudden pain and a hard eye; control pressure and straining while the surgeon closes.
- Sedation for lid surgery. Disinhibition, sneezing on injection (reported with propofol and periocular injection), and a head that moves under a scalpel. A brief deep bolus for the injections and little afterwards works better than a steady infusion.
- Airway fire. Oxygen, alcohol-based skin preparation and diathermy on the face.
- Blood in the pharynx after dacryocystorhinostomy. Laryngospasm at extubation, and vomiting later.
- Thyroid eye disease. Check thyroid function, protect a proptosed eye that will not close, and expect a difficult orbital dissection with reflex bradycardia.
- Children. Congenital glaucoma, retinoblastoma (enucleation and repeated examinations) and lid surgery are all under general anaesthesia; the ex-premature infant carries an apnoea risk.
- Emergency and high-risk variant. Acute angle closure is treated medically and by laser, seldom in theatre. The urgent operations are a perforated cornea needing a tectonic graft, managed as an open globe (see [penetrating eye injury](penetrating-eye-injury.html)), and evacuation of an orbital haematoma. Invasive monitoring is for the patient's own disease; blood is relevant only to major orbital surgery; critical care is not needed.
