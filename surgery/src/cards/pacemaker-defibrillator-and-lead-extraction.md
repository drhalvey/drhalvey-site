## What the surgeon is doing and why

A cardiac implantable electronic device is a generator in a pocket connected to the heart by one or more leads. The operator, usually a cardiologist, has to get a lead from a vein in the upper chest to a stable position on the endocardium, prove that it senses and captures, and bury the generator under the skin. For a pacemaker that is a ventricular lead and usually an atrial one. A defibrillator lead is thicker, carries one or two shock coils, and sits in the right ventricle. Resynchronisation adds a third lead, steered through the coronary sinus into a lateral cardiac vein so that it paces the left ventricle from outside, which is the fiddly, time-consuming part. Two newer designs avoid transvenous leads: a leadless pacemaker, a capsule delivered up the femoral vein through a large sheath and fixed in the right ventricle, and a subcutaneous defibrillator, whose generator sits on the left lateral chest wall and whose lead is tunnelled under the skin to lie beside the sternum.

Implantation asks little of the patient: a small incision, a superficial pocket, and veins that are punctured, not cut. The patients are the difficulty. They are having a device because they have complete heart block, a poor ventricle or a history of ventricular arrhythmia, and the anaesthetic problems are those of sedating that patient flat on a narrow table under drapes (see [remote site anaesthesia and procedural sedation](../a/a12-remote-site-sedation.html)).

Extraction is a different operation that happens to use the same pocket. Within a year or so of implantation a lead is encased in fibrous tissue wherever it touches vein or heart, and old leads calcify into the wall of the innominate vein, the superior vena cava, the right atrium and the tricuspid valve. A lead that must come out (infection of the pocket or the bloodstream is the usual reason; failure or redundancy the others) cannot be pulled. The operator locks a stylet down its lumen for traction and advances a sheath over it, with a laser tip or a rotating mechanical cutter, to core the lead out of its scar centimetre by centimetre. The sheath is cutting next to thin venous wall under traction. In the European registry, procedure-related major complications occurred in 1.7% and death in 0.5%, mostly tears needing pericardial or pleural drainage or surgical repair, and both were less frequent in higher-volume centres.

The pain is minor: skin and a fascial pocket, a 1 on the book's scale, rising to a 2 when the dissection is under muscle or along the lateral chest wall.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment: the device, the rhythm and the anticoagulant
**Why:** What is being implanted or removed, and what the heart does without it, determine the risk more than the incision.
**Anaesthesia:** Establish the indication and the underlying rhythm: complete heart block with a slow or absent escape needs reliable backup pacing before anything is disconnected. For a patient who already has a device, the perioperative consensus framework applies: device type and manufacturer, dependence, the magnet response, and whether tachycardia therapies are on. Read the echo; a resynchronisation or defibrillator candidate has a poor ventricle by definition and will not tolerate lying flat with heavy sedation. Warfarin is continued, not bridged: in the randomised trial heparin bridging gave a pocket haematoma rate of 16.0%, about five times that of continued warfarin.

### 2. Sedation and local anaesthesia
**Why:** The operator infiltrates the skin, the pocket and the track to the vein. Most implants need nothing more.
**Anaesthesia:** Many units run these with nurse-administered sedation; you are involved for the frail, the breathless, the confused, the child, the patient who cannot lie flat, and longer resynchronisation cases. Keep it light: small doses of midazolam and fentanyl or a low-dose propofol or dexmedetomidine infusion, capnography under the drape, the face visible. The stimulating moments are the infiltration and the blunt dissection of the pocket. Count the local anaesthetic dose in a small elderly patient, since the operator will top up repeatedly. Pads are on and connected.

### 3. Venous access
**Why:** The cephalic vein is exposed by cut-down in the deltopectoral groove, or the axillary or subclavian vein is punctured under ultrasound or fluoroscopy, and peel-away sheaths are passed over wires.
**Anaesthesia:** Pneumothorax and haemothorax are the puncture complications and may not show until recovery. An open sheath in a central vein in a spontaneously breathing, snoring patient entrains air: avoid airway obstruction and deep sighs at this point, and a head-down tilt helps if the patient tolerates it. Hypovolaemic patients have collapsed veins; a fluid bolus is a reasonable request.

### 4. Lead positioning and testing
**Why:** Each lead is steered into position, fixed (an extendable screw or passive tines), and tested for sensing, capture threshold and impedance. High-output pacing checks that the lead does not stimulate the diaphragm.
**Anaesthesia:** A lead tip in the ventricle causes ectopy and short runs of ventricular tachycardia. In complete heart block, catheter trauma to the remaining bundle can produce asystole: this is what the pads and the temporary wire are for, and transcutaneous pacing in a sedated patient hurts, so be ready to deepen. Perforation by a lead tip is uncommon and presents as pericardial pain in the awake patient, a rising threshold, or hypotension with an effusion; elderly women on steroids or anticoagulants are the usual victims.

### 5. The coronary sinus lead
**Why:** For resynchronisation the operator cannulates the coronary sinus, performs an occlusive venogram and advances a lead into a lateral branch. Anatomy is variable and this can take hours or fail.
**Anaesthesia:** A long case in the sickest group: orthopnoea, restlessness and a full bladder end more of these procedures than any complication. Dissection of the coronary sinus is usually benign but can cause effusion. Contrast load matters in cardiorenal patients. If sedation is escalating in a patient with a poor ventricle and a rising carbon dioxide, a controlled general anaesthetic with an arterial line is safer than deep sedation by increments.

### 6. Pocket and closure
**Why:** The generator is connected and placed in a pocket on the pectoral fascia, or under pectoralis major in thin patients and for some defibrillators, and the wound closed in layers.
**Anaesthesia:** Submuscular dissection is the painful step and benefits from a pectoral block placed beforehand or generous infiltration by the operator. For a generator change in a dependent patient, the moment the old generator is disconnected is a moment of no pacing: the operator connects the new one at once or uses a temporary system, and you watch the arterial trace or pulse oximeter waveform, not the ECG, which shows pacing spikes whether or not they capture.

### 7. Defibrillation testing and the subcutaneous defibrillator
**Why:** The subcutaneous system needs three incisions or two, a lateral pocket between serratus anterior and latissimus dorsi, and a tunnelled parasternal lead. Its function is commonly tested by inducing ventricular fibrillation and letting the device detect and shock. Routine testing of transvenous defibrillators has largely been abandoned.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the device implant that hurts, intra-operatively and for a few days. Options are general anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway, or a serratus anterior plane block combined with a parasternal block and sedation; a retrospective comparison associated the regional approach with less vasopressor, less postoperative opioid and more same-day discharge. For testing, the patient needs to be unaware for a shock of full output across the chest: a bolus of propofol in a fasted patient, with external pads as rescue if the device fails. Fibrillation in a poor ventricle is not always followed by a prompt recovery of pressure; have a vasopressor drawn up and limit the number of inductions.

### 8. The leadless pacemaker
**Why:** A large sheath in the femoral vein carries the capsule to the right ventricular septum, where it is fixed and released after a tug test.
**Anaesthesia:** Sedation and groin infiltration suffice. The delivery system is stiff and wide, and perforation with tamponade is the complication that defines the procedure in frail patients. Know where the pericardiocentesis kit is and have a plan for the groin, which is closed with a figure-of-eight stitch and needs the patient flat afterwards.

### 9. Extraction: setting up for the catastrophe
**Why:** The risk rises with lead age, defibrillator coils, the number of leads, female sex and low body mass. The operator plans tools and, for higher-risk cases, pre-positions a wire from the femoral vein to the internal jugular so that a compliant occlusion balloon can be run up into the superior cava in seconds.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with a tracheal tube in a theatre or hybrid room. Before the first sheath is advanced: arterial line; large-bore venous access in a femoral vein, because lines in the arm or neck drain into the vessel that is about to tear; a rapid infuser primed; crossmatched blood in the room, checked; transoesophageal echo in place with a baseline look at the pericardium, the tricuspid valve and any vegetations; pads on; the chest and groins prepared and draped for sternotomy; and a surgeon, saw and perfusionist immediately available, which the extraction consensus statement treats as a requirement. Say the plan aloud at the time-out: who calls it, who inflates the balloon, who opens the chest.

### 10. Extraction: freeing the lead
**Why:** With traction on the locking stylet, the powered sheath is advanced over the lead through each binding site: the subclavian and innominate veins, the cava, the atrium, the tricuspid valve and the ventricular tip, where counter-traction against the sheath pulls the tip free.
**Anaesthesia:** Traction on the lead pulls the right ventricle inward and the tricuspid valve open, so pressure dips and ectopy with each pull are expected and recover when traction is released. That is the difficulty: benign and catastrophic hypotension look the same for the first few seconds. Keep the echo on the right heart and pericardium during traction, watch the arterial trace continuously, and tell the operator when pressure falls so they can release. Infected leads shed vegetations to the lungs; large ones seen on echo beforehand may be an argument for open removal.

### 11. Extraction: when the pressure does not recover
**Why:** A tear below the pericardial reflection (low cava, atrium, ventricle) bleeds into the pericardium and causes tamponade. A tear of the cava above the reflection, or of the innominate vein, bleeds into the right pleura and mediastinum with an empty heart and no effusion.
**Anaesthesia:** Call it at once; two thirds of major complications in the registry occurred during the procedure, not afterwards. Echo makes the distinction in seconds: effusion, or an empty underfilled heart. For a caval tear the operator inflates the occlusion balloon, which in a series of 35 confirmed tears was followed by survival in all nine patients in whom it was properly deployed against half of those without it. You give blood through the femoral line, start a vasopressor, give 100% oxygen and get the surgeon's hands in: sternotomy for a caval or atrial tear, subxiphoid or percutaneous drainage first for tamponade from a ventricular perforation. Do not reverse position or remove drapes. This is the major haemorrhage drill compressed into five minutes (see [major haemorrhage, cell salvage and coagulation](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html)); cell salvage is acceptable for uninfected blood only.

### 12. After extraction: pacing, sepsis and re-implantation
**Why:** An infected system is removed completely and not replaced on the same side at the same sitting. The patient still has the rhythm problem that led to the device.
**Anaesthesia:** A dependent patient leaves with a temporary pacing system, often an active-fixation lead through the jugular vein to an external generator, and its settings and threshold belong in your handover. Bacteraemic patients vasodilate after manipulation of an infected pocket; treat as sepsis. Look at the tricuspid valve again on echo before waking, and keep the arterial line until the patient is stable in recovery, since a small tear can tamponade late.

## Pain plan

The "block" is the operator's local infiltration, and for a prepectoral pacemaker or defibrillator nothing else is needed: paracetamol regularly for a few days and an occasional weak opioid. NSAIDs are best avoided in a group that is largely anticoagulated, elderly and has heart failure. The shoulder is kept below 90 degrees for some weeks to protect the leads, and stiffness from that is a more common complaint at follow-up than wound pain.

Submuscular pockets and subcutaneous defibrillators are a step up, because muscle planes have been separated: movement-evoked pain for three to five days. A pectoral block for the former and a serratus anterior plane block with a parasternal block for the latter are logical because they cover exactly the planes dissected; the supporting evidence is retrospective, so this is the author's judgement and not a guideline position. Single shot only, placed before the incision so it also reduces the sedation needed.

After extraction the wound is the old pocket, debrided and often left to heal with a drain or by secondary intention if infected. Pain is modest. New severe chest or shoulder-tip pain after any of these procedures is a pneumothorax, a haemothorax or a pericardial effusion until shown otherwise.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Complete heart block presenting as an emergency: transcutaneous pacing with capture confirmed on a pulse waveform, isoprenaline or adrenaline infusion as a bridge, and a temporary wire. Sedation is minimal until the rhythm is secure.
- Ventricular tachycardia storm coming for a defibrillator or lead revision: continue antiarrhythmic infusions, avoid catecholamine surges, pads on from arrival.
- Heart failure patient who cannot lie flat: a wedge and patience first; if that fails, a planned general anaesthetic with an arterial line is safer than repeated sedative boluses.
- Children and adults with congenital heart disease: general anaesthesia, epicardial systems through a thoracotomy or sternotomy in the small or the anatomically excluded, and shunts that make venous air a systemic problem.
- Extraction in theatre by surgeons against extraction in the laboratory by cardiologists: both are practised. A surgical series of 104 cases under general anaesthesia with echo reported no caval tears and no procedural deaths; the constant is immediate surgical capability, not the room.
- Renal failure, endocarditis and diabetes mark the extraction patient most likely to die in hospital from the underlying illness, not the procedure: in the laser series all-cause mortality was 4.3% with endocarditis.
- Electromagnetic interference from diathermy during a generator change can inhibit pacing in a dependent patient; ask for bipolar or short bursts and keep watching the pulse waveform.

## Key sources

Extraction risk is described by large prospective and retrospective registries and a society consensus statement; there are no randomised trials of anaesthetic technique or environment. The rescue balloon evidence is a small database series. Anticoagulation through implantation is supported by a randomised trial. Regional anaesthesia for subcutaneous defibrillators rests on retrospective cohorts.
