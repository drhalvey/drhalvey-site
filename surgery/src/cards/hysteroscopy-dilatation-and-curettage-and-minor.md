## What the surgeon is doing and why

Every operation on this card goes through the vagina to reach the cervix or the uterine cavity, lasts minutes, and is done on a woman who expects to go home within two or three hours. What separates them is what is in the uterus and what the surgeon does once inside.

The non-pregnant uterus is a thick-walled muscular organ with a slit for a cavity and a cervical canal a few millimetres wide. To look inside (diagnostic hysteroscopy) the surgeon grasps the cervix with a tenaculum, dilates the canal with graduated rods to 5-6 mm, and distends the cavity with fluid under pressure so that the walls separate and can be seen. Abnormal bleeding is then investigated by sampling the endometrium (curettage) and treated by removing what is found: a polyp twisted or cut off its stalk, a submucous fibroid shaved out of the wall with a resecting loop or a mechanical tissue-removal device, a septum divided, or the whole endometrium resected or destroyed (ablation by radiofrequency mesh, thermal balloon or rollerball) so that it cannot regenerate and bleed. The bigger the resection, the wider the dilatation (9-10 mm for a resectoscope), the longer the cavity is held open under pressure, and the more raw myometrium with open venous sinuses is exposed to the irrigating fluid. That is the gynaecological cousin of the [transurethral resection](transurethral-resection-of-the-prostate-and.html), with the same absorption syndrome.

The pregnant uterus is a different organ: soft, vascular, thin-walled and easy to perforate. Surgical management of miscarriage, termination, and evacuation of retained products or a molar pregnancy all use the same sequence of dilatation, a suction cannula sized to the gestation, and a check that the cavity is empty. Bleeding stops only when the emptied uterus contracts on its own spiral arteries, so anything that relaxes the myometrium works against the surgeon.

The third family is on the outside: excision of the cervical transformation zone with a diathermy loop (LLETZ) or a knife cone for dysplasia, marsupialisation of a Bartholin abscess, vulval biopsies, removal or insertion of an intrauterine device, and examination under anaesthesia. These are skin and mucosa operations on vascular tissue, usually with local anaesthetic and a vasoconstrictor injected by the surgeon.

The pain logic is simple and often ignored. Dilating the cervix and scraping, resecting or emptying the uterus makes the myometrium contract and release prostaglandin, which is felt as a cramp across the lower abdomen and sacrum. It is visceral, it peaks in the first hour or two, and it is the same mechanism as dysmenorrhoea, which is why an NSAID is the specific treatment and opioid is a poor one. Ablation and fibroid resection leave a burnt or raw cavity that cramps harder and into the next day. The 179-procedure cohort is the reminder that minor operations given almost nothing score badly on day 1; these patients go home with whatever was given before they woke.

## Steps

### 1. Reading the booking: which operation is this
**Why:** "D&C" on a list covers a diagnostic look in a well woman, a resection of a 4 cm fibroid, and an evacuation in a woman who has been bleeding at home for two days. The surgeon knows which; the list does not say.
**Anaesthesia:** Sort the patient into one of four groups before she arrives in the anaesthetic room. Non-pregnant and short: the default plan below. Non-pregnant and resecting: a fluid-balance case that needs a named person recording the deficit. Pregnant and stable: uterotonics drawn up, propofol maintenance, a gestation in weeks written on the chart. Pregnant and bleeding or septic: an emergency that needs resuscitation, blood and a secured airway. Ask for the gestation, the haemoglobin, the last oral intake and whether there has been vomiting. A pregnancy test in any woman of reproductive age having a non-pregnancy procedure is a unit rule, because a hysteroscope passed through an unrecognised pregnancy is an avoidable harm.

### 2. Induction and airway
**Why:** The surgeon needs five to fifteen minutes of a still pelvis and a relaxed perineum, then a patient awake enough to walk.
**Anaesthesia:** Propofol with a short-acting opioid (fentanyl 50-100 micrograms IV or alfentanil 500-1,000 micrograms IV) and a supraglottic device with spontaneous ventilation suits nearly everyone. The first trimester does not by itself mandate intubation: gastric emptying is normal until labour and reflux symptoms, not a positive test, are the trigger. The author's threshold for a tracheal tube is symptomatic reflux, a uterus above the umbilicus or roughly 18-20 weeks, shock, recent food with an emergency indication, or morbid obesity in lithotomy. Give paracetamol 1 g and the NSAID (parecoxib 40 mg IV or ketorolac 15-30 mg IV) at induction so they are working when she wakes; withhold the NSAID in a bleeding evacuation until the uterus is empty and contracted.

### 3. Lithotomy
**Why:** Access to the perineum with the cervix in the line of the instruments; the buttocks are brought to the edge of the table and the legs lifted together.
**Anaesthesia:** Raising both legs returns several hundred millilitres to the central circulation and lowering them at the end takes it away, which unmasks hypovolaemia in a woman who has bled. Functional residual capacity falls, more so in the obese and with head-down tilt. The common peroneal nerve at the fibular head, the femoral nerve with extreme hip flexion and the fingers at the table break are the injuries; the case is short, but the stirrups are positioned in seconds by whoever is nearest, so look.

### 4. Tenaculum and cervical dilatation
**Why:** The cervix is grasped and pulled to straighten the canal, then forced open with rods; this is the most stimulating moment of the operation and it comes within a minute of the patient going off to sleep.
**Anaesthesia:** Cervical stretch is a vagal stimulus through the pelvic splanchnic nerves: sinus bradycardia is common and a few seconds of asystole is well recognised. It stops when the surgeon stops; glycopyrrolate 200 micrograms IV or atropine 600 micrograms IV if it does not, and a pre-emptive dose in the patient with a resting rate in the 40s. The same stimulus under light anaesthesia produces breath-holding, a cough or laryngospasm on a supraglottic airway, which is why the opioid goes in before the propofol and a further 30-50 mg of propofol is given as the tenaculum goes on. Tissue lodged in the cervical os of a miscarrying woman produces the same reflex awake ("cervical shock": bradycardia and hypotension out of proportion to the blood loss) and resolves when it is removed with sponge forceps. A paracervical block reduces the pain of dilatation in awake women in the Cochrane review but does nothing measurable afterwards, so under general anaesthesia it adds little.

### 5. Distension and inspection
**Why:** Fluid is run into the cavity under pressure (gravity, a pressure bag or a pump set to 70-100 mmHg) to separate the walls; the surgeon inspects the cavity and the tubal ostia.
**Anaesthesia:** Know which fluid is hanging. Saline is used with bipolar loops and mechanical devices and can only cause volume overload. Glycine 1.5% and sorbitol are used with monopolar loops, are hypotonic and electrolyte-free, and cause dilutional hyponatraemia as well. Fluid enters the circulation through opened veins whenever intrauterine pressure exceeds venous pressure, and passes down the tubes into the peritoneum, so a pressure set well above the mean arterial pressure buys a clearer view at the cost of faster absorption. Air in the inflow tubing or gas generated by the electrode can enter the same open veins: with the uterus above the heart in a head-down patient, a sudden fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide with desaturation or hypotension during hysteroscopy is gas embolism. Stop the procedure, level the table, flood the cavity and support the circulation.

### 6. Resection or ablation and the fluid deficit
**Why:** Fibroids, polyps, septa or the endometrium are cut away in strips down to myometrium, or the endometrium is destroyed with a thermal or radiofrequency device. Resection opens venous sinuses; second-generation ablation devices do not use free-flowing fluid at all.
**Anaesthesia:** The deficit (volume in minus volume recovered from the outflow, the drape pouch and the floor) is the monitor, and somebody has to be told to call it every ten minutes. The British and European guideline sets the stopping points: 1,000 mL of hypotonic fluid or 2,500 mL of saline in a healthy woman, and 750 mL and 1,500 mL in the elderly or those with cardiac or renal disease. Published cases of the absorption syndrome have had deficits from 2,400 mL to nearly 12 L, with hyponatraemia, pulmonary oedema and unplanned intensive care, and they share a missing count. Under general anaesthesia the early signs (restlessness, nausea, visual disturbance) are lost; rising airway pressure, falling saturation, hypertension then hypotension, and a puffy face are late. Premenopausal women tolerate acute hyponatraemia worse than men, so a deficit at the limit with a hypotonic fluid earns a sodium measurement before she leaves theatre. Treat symptomatic hyponatraemia promptly with hypertonic saline 3% 150 mL IV over 20 minutes, repeated to effect, and furosemide 20-40 mg IV for overload: the rapid-correction case report makes the point that acute hyponatraemia is the situation where delay, not correction, causes the harm. A spinal for a planned long monopolar resection keeps the cerebral monitor awake, which is the same argument as for the prostate.

### 7. Suction evacuation of the pregnant uterus
**Why:** A cannula matched to the gestation is passed to the fundus and the contents aspirated; the surgeon feels the uterus contract down on the cannula as it empties and finishes with a gentle check curettage.
**Anaesthesia:** The uterus has to contract to stop bleeding, and sevoflurane above about 1 MAC relaxes it. Maintain with propofol, or keep the volatile low, and give oxytocin 5 units IV slowly as the cannula goes in or when the surgeon asks; a fast bolus drops the pressure and raises the heart rate. For continued bleeding from poor tone: ergometrine 250 micrograms IM (not in hypertension), misoprostol 400-800 micrograms rectally or sublingually, carboprost 250 micrograms IM (not in asthma), TXA 1 g IV, and bimanual compression by the surgeon. Blood loss rises with gestation, so beyond about 12 weeks have a second cannula and a valid group and screen. Molar pregnancy is the high-risk variant: a uterus large for dates, brisk haemorrhage, hyperemesis with electrolyte disturbance, pre-eclampsia before 20 weeks, thyrotoxicosis from human chorionic gonadotrophin, and acute breathlessness at evacuation from trophoblastic embolisation or fluid overload. That patient has an arterial line considered, blood in the room and a beta blocker available. Septic miscarriage is a source-control operation in a vasodilated patient: antibiotics before induction, noradrenaline ready, and the expectation that she gets worse for an hour after the uterus is instrumented.

### 8. Cervical excision and vulval procedures
**Why:** The transformation zone is excised with a diathermy loop or a scalpel cone and the bed coagulated; a Bartholin abscess is incised and its edges sutured open; lesions are biopsied.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon injects the cervix with local anaesthetic and a vasoconstrictor (lidocaine with adrenaline, prilocaine with felypressin from dental cartridges, or dilute vasopressin). The cervix is vascular and uptake is fast: expect tachycardia and hypertension within a minute or two of adrenaline, and bradycardia with hypertension and pallor after vasopressin analogues. Know the dose before it goes in and count it against the local anaesthetic maximum. A knife cone bleeds more than a loop and occasionally needs sutures and a pack. Diathermy smoke from a loop excision in a patient breathing spontaneously on a supraglottic airway is the surgical team's problem, not the airway's, but an alcohol-based preparation pooled in the vagina is a fire risk that has burned patients. A Bartholin abscess is tender beyond what its size suggests, and infiltrated local works poorly in pus; give the opioid and expect a brisk response to incision.

### 9. Perforation
**Why:** A dilator, hysteroscope or cannula passes through the fundus or a lateral wall. The surgeon notices an instrument that goes further than the uterine length, a cavity that will not distend, or omentum in the view.
**Anaesthesia:** You notice a fluid deficit that jumps, a distension pressure that cannot be maintained, or unexpected hypotension. A midline fundal perforation with a blunt instrument in a non-pregnant uterus is usually observed. A lateral perforation (uterine vessels and broad ligament haematoma), a perforation with an activated electrode or a suction cannula (bowel or bladder injury), or any perforation in a pregnant uterus needs a laparoscopy now: convert to a tracheal tube and relaxant, and the case becomes the one on the [laparoscopic gynaecology](laparoscopic-gynaecology.html) card. Abdominal pain out of proportion in recovery, shoulder-tip pain, or a rising heart rate after a "routine" curettage is a missed perforation until the surgeon has reviewed.

### 10. Emergence, recovery and discharge
**Why:** The cavity is empty or treated, bleeding is checked with the legs down, and the patient is expected home in two hours.
**Anaesthesia:** Cramping peaks in the first hour. If the NSAID went in at induction most women need nothing more than a small dose of fentanyl 25 microgram IV in recovery; after ablation or a large resection the cramps are stronger for 4-6 hours and a second agent (oral oxycodone 5 mg, or a heat pack, which works) is worth charting. Nausea delays discharge more often than pain. Bleeding heavier than a period, pain that is not cramping in character, or pain that is increasing after the first hour is escalated to the surgeon before discharge, not after. Send her home with paracetamol and an NSAID to take regularly for 24-48 hours.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is prostaglandin-mediated uterine cramping with a small contribution from the cervix, so the block for this card is the systemic strategy: an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor and paracetamol given before the end of the procedure, a short-acting opioid for the dilatation, and dexamethasone for nausea. No nerve block improves on that under general anaesthesia. The Cochrane review of paracervical block (26 studies, 2,790 women) found that it reduced the pain of dilatation against placebo and abdominal pain during the intervention, but not afterwards, and that no technique gave reliable control in awake women, who reported mean scores of 7 to 9 out of 10 during uterine instrumentation. The practical reading is that a paracervical block is a reasonable adjunct to sedation and no substitute for general or neuraxial anaesthesia in a woman who wants to feel nothing.

The plan scales with what was done to the cavity. Diagnostic hysteroscopy, curettage, polypectomy and device insertion: paracetamol and an NSAID are the whole plan. Endometrial ablation and resection of fibroids: add a longer-acting opioid dose in recovery and warn about 4-6 hours of strong cramps; this is the author's judgement from the mechanism, since the trials are of awake office procedures. Suction evacuation: the NSAID is given once bleeding is controlled; cramping is what a contracting uterus should produce and settles within hours. Cervical excision: the surgeon's infiltration covers it. Vulval surgery and Bartholin abscess: infiltration where the tissue is not infected, and regular simple analgesia for several days because sitting re-provokes it.

Spinal anaesthesia (hyperbaric bupivacaine 0.5% 1.5-2 mL, or a short-acting agent such as prilocaine 2% where available, to a T10 level) is an option for the long resection where an awake patient is wanted, and for the patient in whom general anaesthesia is unattractive. It delays discharge and is not the day-case default.

Rebound is not a feature. Escalation triggers are surgical: pain that increases after the first hour, constant rather than cramping pain, shoulder-tip pain, a rigid abdomen or a rising heart rate mean perforation, haematometra or retained tissue.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The emergency hidden in the elective list. Incomplete miscarriage with ongoing bleeding, retained placental tissue days after delivery, and septic abortion are booked under the same three letters as a diagnostic curettage. The first two can lose a litre before anyone counts; the third is septic shock with a surgical source. Resuscitate, cross-match, secure the airway and have uterotonics and noradrenaline ready.
- Retained products after delivery. A postpartum uterus is large, soft, infected and easy to perforate, and the patient is within the window in which she is treated as a full stomach. This overlaps with the [manual removal](instrumental-delivery-manual-removal-of-placenta.html) card.
- Fluid absorption. The deficit limits apply whether or not the patient looks well. With saline the presentation is pulmonary oedema; with glycine it is hyponatraemia, hyperammonaemia and transient visual loss as well. Bipolar instruments have removed the hyponatraemia, not the overload.
- Gas embolism. Purge the inflow tubing, avoid steep head-down, and keep the last dilator in the cervix until the hysteroscope is ready so that the open cervix is not left entraining room air.
- Vasoconstrictor injected into the cervix. Hypertension, tachyarrhythmia or profound bradycardia within two minutes of the injection is the drug; deepen, wait, and treat only what persists.
- The patient with significant cardiac disease. Lithotomy autotransfusion, vagal bradycardia, a fluid load of unknown size and an oxytocin bolus are four separate insults in fifteen minutes. Slow everything down, use saline and a low distension pressure, give oxytocin as an infusion or not at all, and consider an arterial line for stenotic valve lesions and pulmonary hypertension.
- Obesity in lithotomy. Spontaneous ventilation on a supraglottic device in a head-down, legs-up patient with a body mass index above 40 is a poor compromise for anything longer than a few minutes; a tracheal tube and controlled ventilation is the defensible choice.
- Office hysteroscopy coming to theatre. The patient who could not tolerate it awake is selected for cervical stenosis, anxiety and pain sensitivity: expect a harder dilatation, a higher perforation rate and more analgesic need.
- Rhesus status and contraception are the surgeon's, but anti-D given in recovery is often left to whoever is there.
- Cervical cerclage is a different operation with a different patient and is on the [cerclage](cervical-cerclage-and-non-obstetric-surgery.html) card.

## Key sources

The fluid limits come from the joint British and European guideline, which is consensus built on case series and physiological studies, not on outcome trials; the case reports cited show what happens beyond the limits. The paracervical block evidence is a Cochrane review of awake procedures and says more about the limits of local techniques than about what to do under general anaesthesia. The uterotonic sequence, the effect of volatile agents on uterine tone, the vagal response to dilatation and the features of molar pregnancy are standard teaching and are unsourced. The thresholds for tracheal intubation in early pregnancy and the scaling of analgesia to the extent of resection are the author's judgement.
