## What the surgeon is doing and why

The thyroid is two lobes joined by an isthmus, wrapped around the front of the trachea at the second to fourth rings, with four structures on each side that the whole operation is designed to avoid: the recurrent laryngeal nerve in the groove between trachea and oesophagus, the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve beside the upper pole vessels, and two parathyroid glands sharing a blood supply with the thyroid capsule. A lobe is removed for an indeterminate or malignant nodule, for a toxic adenoma or for a goitre that is compressing the trachea; the whole gland for cancer, Graves disease or a multinodular goitre. A central neck dissection adds the nodes between the carotids for cancer.

The surgeon makes a collar incision in a skin crease, raises flaps under platysma, separates the strap muscles in the midline and retracts them. Each lobe is then mobilised in a fixed order: middle vein, upper pole vessels taken close to the gland to spare the superior laryngeal nerve, lobe rolled medially, recurrent nerve found and followed, parathyroids peeled off on their vascular pedicles, and the gland freed from the trachea at the ligament of Berry, where the nerve is closest and most at risk. Most surgeons now use intraoperative nerve monitoring: a tracheal tube with surface electrodes resting between the vocal cords records the electromyogram when the surgeon stimulates the vagus or recurrent nerve with a probe. A lost signal on the first side of a planned total thyroidectomy usually stops the operation, because bilateral cord palsy is an airway catastrophe.

Parathyroidectomy uses the same incision or a smaller lateral one. In primary hyperparathyroidism a single adenoma, localised beforehand by sestamibi or four-dimensional CT, is removed through a 2-3 cm incision and cure is confirmed by a fall in parathyroid hormone in blood you draw at set times after excision. If localisation fails, or in renal (secondary and tertiary) hyperparathyroidism, all four glands are found and three and a half are removed or all four with a forearm autograft.

The pain logic is simple. The wound is skin and platysma; the straps are retracted and not cut; nothing is repaired under tension. That is a score of 2, and the day 1 complaints are as likely to be throat and occiput as incision. The reason this card is long is not pain. It is that the operation sits on the airway, and its two serious complications, haematoma and bilateral cord palsy, present to the anaesthetist.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative review: hormones, airway and calcium
**Why:** The surgeon wants a euthyroid patient and a known airway before starting; operating on uncontrolled thyrotoxicosis risks a thyroid storm.
**Anaesthesia:** Thyroid function should be normal for elective surgery, with Graves patients on a thionamide, a beta-blocker and often iodine for the last ten days. A resting tachycardia, tremor or new atrial fibrillation on the day is a reason to stop. For a goitre, read the CT yourself: tracheal diameter at its narrowest, deviation, and how far below the manubrium the gland goes. Stridor, orthopnoea or a lumen under about 5 mm calls for a plan made jointly with the surgeon; most compressed tracheas accept a small tube that then stents the narrowing. Preoperative laryngoscopy by the surgeon documents cord function. In hyperparathyroidism note the calcium and renal function; in medullary carcinoma a phaeochromocytoma must have been excluded (see [splenectomy and adrenalectomy including phaeochromocytoma](splenectomy-and-adrenalectomy-including-phaeochromocytoma.html)).

### 2. Induction and the nerve-monitoring tube
**Why:** The electrodes must sit between the cords and stay there through neck extension, and the muscles they record from must not be paralysed.
**Anaesthesia:** Place the tube with a videolaryngoscope so that the surgeon or assistant can see the electrode band at the cords, then re-check after the neck is extended, because extension draws the tube up by a centimetre or more. Keep lubricant and local anaesthetic gel off the electrodes. For relaxation there are three workable routes: rocuronium 0.3 mg/kg and time; rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg with sugammadex 2 mg/kg if the first vagal signal is poor (in a 290-patient randomised comparison this gave no intraoperative movement, against 16 patients moving with the low dose, and a positive signal in 99.3% after reversal); or remifentanil and propofol with no relaxant. Maintain with propofol TIVA and remifentanil: it holds a still patient without paralysis, suits the nausea risk and gives a quiet emergence.

### 3. Positioning
**Why:** Extension lifts the gland out of the thoracic inlet and flattens the neck for the incision; head-up tilt empties the veins.
**Anaesthesia:** Extend only as far as the awake range allows, with the occiput supported in a ring and not hanging, since over-extension is the source of the posterior neck pain and headache on day 1 and a real hazard in cervical spondylosis or rheumatoid disease. Arms are tucked, so the cannula needs an extension and the pressure cuff a clear run. Tape and pad the eyes with particular care in Graves ophthalmopathy, where the lids may not close. Head-up tilt and a large open vein make air embolism possible though rare. Place the cervical plexus blocks now if you are doing them.

### 4. Incision, flaps and strap muscles
**Why:** The surgeon raises subplatysmal flaps to the thyroid notch above and the sternal notch below and opens the midline raphe between the straps.
**Anaesthesia:** Brief stimulation at incision, then little. The surgeon often infiltrates adrenaline-containing local anaesthetic; a transient tachycardia a minute later is that.

### 5. Upper pole and middle vein
**Why:** The superior thyroid vessels are ligated individually on the capsule to protect the external branch of the superior laryngeal nerve, which tenses the cord and gives a singer her high notes.
**Anaesthesia:** Traction on the upper pole pulls on the carotid sheath: bradycardia from carotid sinus stimulation settles when the surgeon lets go and seldom needs atropine. In a thyrotoxic gland, handling can release hormone; a tachycardia and temperature rise from this point is a storm until explained and is treated with a beta-blocker, cooling and steroid.

### 6. Recurrent laryngeal nerve and parathyroids
**Why:** The lobe is rotated medially, the nerve identified and stimulated, and the parathyroid glands dissected off the capsule with their end-artery supply intact. A gland that looks dusky is diced and reimplanted in sternomastoid.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon stimulates the vagus first for a baseline, then the recurrent nerve. If there is no signal, the stepwise check is yours as much as theirs: tube depth and rotation, residual neuromuscular block on a train-of-four, the stimulator circuit and earth lead, and only then the nerve. Vagal stimulation at the currents used can slow the heart for a beat or two. Do not give a relaxant top-up during this phase for a cough; deepen the remifentanil.

### 7. Berry's ligament and removal of the lobe
**Why:** The last attachment to the trachea is divided with the nerve in view, and the lobe is delivered. For a total thyroidectomy steps 5 to 7 are repeated on the other side, provided the first nerve still conducts.
**Anaesthesia:** A lost signal on the first side with a planned total thyroidectomy leads most surgeons to stop and stage the second side. This is the surgeon's decision but it changes your extubation: a patient with one palsied nerve has a weak voice and a poor cough; a patient with two has cords resting in the midline and obstructs at extubation.

### 8. Parathyroid hormone sampling (parathyroidectomy)
**Why:** The half-life of parathyroid hormone is a few minutes, so a fall of more than half from baseline within ten minutes or so of removing the adenoma tells the surgeon the remaining glands are normal and the operation can end.
**Anaesthesia:** You are the phlebotomist: a baseline sample after induction and timed samples after excision (commonly at 5 and 10 minutes), from a dedicated cannula or by venepuncture in the arm without the infusion, labelled with the time. Propofol contamination of the sample and haemolysis both spoil the assay, so do not draw through the TIVA line. Each result takes 15-20 minutes; the wound stays open while you wait.

### 9. Haemostasis and closure
**Why:** The bed is dried meticulously because the space is closed and small; many surgeons ask for a Valsalva manoeuvre and a head-down tilt to show up venous bleeding before the straps are approximated. Drains are optional and do not prevent haematoma.
**Anaesthesia:** Level the table or tip it head down and hold 30-40 cmH2O for 10-15 seconds on request, watching the blood pressure. Bring the pressure back to the awake level now, not in recovery. Give the NSAID unless the surgeon objects, paracetamol, and a small dose of long-acting opioid. Confirm full recovery of neuromuscular function if any relaxant was used.

### 10. Extubation
**Why:** The surgeon wants no coughing; you want to know the cords move.
**Anaesthesia:** A remifentanil-based wake-up with the patient sitting up gives the least coughing. Exchange to a supraglottic airway under deep anaesthesia is an alternative and allows a fibreoptic look at the cords as the patient lightens, which is worth doing when the monitoring signal was lost or the dissection was difficult. Stridor immediately after extubation is bilateral cord palsy, laryngospasm or, after a long-standing large goitre, tracheomalacia; all three are managed by reintubation first and diagnosis afterwards.

### 11. Recovery and the first night
**Why:** The surgical team are watching for bleeding, voice change and calcium.
**Anaesthesia:** The consensus guideline from the Difficult Airway Society and the endocrine and ENT surgical associations sets out what the ward needs: scheduled wound and airway observations, a post-thyroid surgery emergency box at the bedside, staff who know the signs (neck swelling, pain or pressure, difficulty swallowing, agitation, a change in voice, and breathing difficulty late), and a sequence of oxygenation and evaluation, then haematoma evacuation, then tracheal intubation. The bedside evacuation follows the guideline's SCOOP aid: skin exposure, cut sutures, open skin, open muscles (superficial and deep layers), pack the wound. Hand over that sequence in recovery every time.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is skin and soft tissue in the territory of the superficial cervical plexus (C2-C4), so the regional option is a bilateral superficial cervical plexus block and the systemic plan is light. A meta-analysis of 14 trials found the block cut the need for rescue analgesia (odds ratio 0.30), lowered early pain scores and shortened stay by about six hours; the 2023 update with 31 trials confirmed lower opioid consumption and pain scores to 24 hours and, unlike the first analysis, a reduction in nausea. There is no PROSPECT guideline for this operation.

The defensible default is paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction, and either a bilateral superficial cervical plexus block (about 10 mL of ropivacaine 0.375-0.5% per side, injected superficially along the posterior border of sternomastoid) or thorough surgeon infiltration of the wound. The author's practice is to block for total thyroidectomy and day cases, where avoiding opioid matters most for nausea, and to accept infiltration alone for a focused parathyroidectomy. Keep the injection superficial: a deep or large-volume block risks phrenic and recurrent laryngeal nerve spread on both sides and a hoarse voice that the surgeon will attribute to the operation. Intraoperatively remifentanil does the work; give a small dose of longer-acting opioid before it stops, because remifentanil leaves nothing behind.

Expect worst pain of 2-3 out of 10 on the first evening and day 1, with swallowing, and very little by day 3. The sore throat responds to paracetamol and time; the neck and occipital ache responds to the NSAID, warmth and movement. Rebound is not a feature. Escalation triggers are almost all surgical: increasing neck pain, tightness or difficulty swallowing in the first 24 hours is a haematoma until examined; perioral tingling, cramps or carpopedal spasm on day 1-2 is hypocalcaemia and needs a calcium level and replacement, not analgesia.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Haematoma. Uncommon, mostly within the first day, and lethal when missed. Laryngeal oedema from venous obstruction makes intubation harder with every minute, so the order is oxygen, open the wound, then secure the airway. If you are called to intubate a patient whose wound is still closed, open it.
- Bilateral recurrent nerve injury. Immediate stridor at extubation. Reintubate; the surgeon decides between a period of intubation with steroids and a tracheostomy.
- Retrosternal goitre. Almost all are delivered through the neck. A gland reaching the aortic arch, a posterior mediastinal component or recurrent or malignant disease may need a sternal split: for that case have an arterial line, large-bore access below the diaphragm if the great veins are compressed, cross-matched blood and a cardiothoracic surgeon. Superior vena cava obstruction changes induction and positioning.
- Tracheomalacia. Feared more than seen. A cuff-leak test and extubation over an exchange catheter are reasonable after a very large long-standing goitre.
- Thyroid storm. The emergency variant on the hormonal side: fever, tachyarrhythmia and agitation during or in the hours after surgery on an inadequately prepared Graves patient. Beta-blockade, a thionamide, steroid, cooling and critical care.
- Hypothyroid patients. Mild treated hypothyroidism needs nothing; severe untreated disease means sensitivity to every depressant drug and a delay if the surgery is elective.
- Renal parathyroidectomy. A dialysis patient with a fistula arm to protect, potassium to know on the day, bone disease, and a fall in calcium after surgery (hungry bone syndrome) that can be profound and needs a monitored bed and an infusion plan. Dialysis the day before.
- Hypercalcaemic crisis. Calcium above about 3.5 mmol/L with dehydration, confusion and arrhythmia: rehydrate and lower the calcium before an urgent parathyroidectomy.
- Lateral neck dissection. Adds hours, the accessory nerve (another reason to avoid relaxant), the thoracic duct on the left and a larger wound; still not a painful operation.
- Day-case surgery. Possible for selected hemithyroidectomies and focused parathyroidectomies when the conditions in the haematoma guideline are met: low-risk patient, accompanied overnight, close to the hospital and counselled on the warning signs.
- Invasive monitoring, blood and critical care are not required for routine surgery. They enter with the sternal split, the thyrotoxic patient and the take-back for haematoma with a compromised airway.

## Key sources

The haematoma advice is a consensus guideline built on a systematic review with few high-quality studies; its SCOOP aid is quoted here from the guideline. The cervical plexus block evidence is two meta-analyses of small heterogeneous trials. The relaxant strategy rests on one randomised trial plus common practice. Parathyroid hormone sampling times, extension limits, the Valsalva pressure and the airway thresholds for goitre are consensus and author's judgement.
