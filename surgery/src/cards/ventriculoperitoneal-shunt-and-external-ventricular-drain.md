## What the surgeon is doing and why

Cerebrospinal fluid is made in the ventricles at about 20 mL an hour, flows through the aqueduct and fourth ventricle into the subarachnoid space, and is reabsorbed into the venous sinuses. Hydrocephalus is a mismatch between production and drainage. It is obstructive when a tumour, a clot or a narrowed aqueduct blocks the path inside the brain, and communicating when blood, infection or age impair reabsorption beyond it. The ventricles enlarge, and in a closed adult skull the pressure rises; in the acute form the patient becomes drowsy, vomits and stops breathing. The background physiology is in [neuroanaesthesia essentials](../a/a08-neuroanaesthesia-essentials.html).

There are two answers, one temporary and one permanent, and this card covers both because the same patient often has them in sequence.

An external ventricular drain is a catheter passed through a frontal burr hole into the lateral ventricle, tunnelled a few centimetres under the scalp and connected to a closed collection system at the bedside. It drains fluid against a set height and lets intracranial pressure be measured. It is the emergency treatment for acute hydrocephalus after subarachnoid or intraventricular haemorrhage, with posterior fossa masses and after head injury, and it stays for days. You will place it with the surgeon as an emergency, and you will meet it far more often already in place, in a patient coming for [aneurysm treatment](aneurysm-clipping-and-endovascular-coiling.html), a [craniotomy](posterior-fossa-surgery.html) or a scan.

A ventriculoperitoneal shunt is the permanent version, entirely under the skin. A ventricular catheter enters through a frontal or parieto-occipital burr hole and connects to a valve, fixed or programmable, sitting behind the ear. A distal catheter is passed down a subcutaneous tunnel over the mastoid, neck, clavicle and chest wall to the abdomen, where it is placed in the peritoneal cavity through a small open incision or under laparoscopic vision. The valve opens at a set pressure and the peritoneum absorbs the fluid. Where the abdomen is unusable the distal end goes to the right atrium or the pleura. An endoscopic third ventriculostomy avoids hardware altogether for some obstructive cases by fenestrating the floor of the third ventricle.

Shunts fail. The catheter blocks, disconnects or becomes infected, and a large share of shunt surgery is revision. Infection is the surgeon's preoccupation because an infected shunt has to come out: it affects up to 15% of shunts, and in the BASICS trial antibiotic-impregnated catheters reduced revision for infection to 2% from about 6%. The theatre rituals around a shunt, which include being first on the list, a closed door and hardware that nobody touches, come from that.

The pain logic is tunnelling and an abdominal incision. The scalp wound is small. The tunnel is made by pushing a long blunt rod through the subcutaneous plane, which tears small nerves along the neck and chest and is the most stimulating minute of the operation; afterwards it is a bruised line. The abdominal wound is through the rectus sheath. Together they make a mild to moderate pain of two to three days that is easy to under-treat because the operation is thought of as minor.

## Steps

### 1. Which patient is this
**Why:** The same operation is done for an elderly person with normal pressure hydrocephalus, an adult after subarachnoid haemorrhage, a child with a blocked shunt, and a patient with a tumour.
**Anaesthesia:** Sort them by pressure. Normal pressure hydrocephalus (gait disturbance, incontinence, cognitive decline) is elective, the pressure is normal, and the concerns are those of a frail older person: comorbidity, falls, delirium. Acute hydrocephalus and the blocked shunt are raised intracranial pressure emergencies: headache, vomiting, drowsiness, sometimes bradycardia and hypertension, sunsetting eyes in a child. For those, no sedative premedication, a full stomach assumed, and theatre without delay. Parents and regular shunt patients know what a blocked shunt looks like and are usually right. Check sodium and hydration after days of vomiting.

### 2. Induction with raised pressure
**Why:** The surgeon cannot relieve the pressure until the burr hole is made or the old valve is exposed.
**Anaesthesia:** Rapid sequence induction with a dose that prevents the laryngoscopy response without dropping perfusion pressure: propofol titrated, an opioid, rocuronium 1-1.2 mg/kg, a vasopressor to hand. Ventilate at once to a low-normal carbon dioxide and keep the head up. A patient who is bradycardic and hypertensive before induction is compensating; do not treat the pressure. If the patient deteriorates before the surgeon is ready, the bridge is hyperventilation and mannitol 0.5-1 g/kg IV, and in a shunted patient the surgeon can tap the reservoir with a butterfly needle through the scalp.

### 3. Position and preparation for a shunt
**Why:** The tunneller is rigid, so the entry point behind the ear, the neck, the chest and the abdominal incision must lie along one line with nothing in the way.
**Anaesthesia:** Shoulder roll, head turned fully away and slightly extended. That position kinks the opposite jugular vein and can move the tube, so check both after the turn and keep the head up until the surgeon needs it flat. ECG electrodes, lines and the warming blanket are kept off the track from mastoid to umbilicus. Skin preparation is extensive and takes time, and the exposed area cools the patient: warm from the start, particularly children. Antibiotic in before incision.

### 4. Burr hole and ventricular catheter
**Why:** The catheter is passed freehand, with navigation or under ultrasound to the frontal horn. Fluid under pressure confirms placement.
**Anaesthesia:** When the ventricle is tapped in a patient with raised pressure, intracranial pressure falls within seconds and blood pressure and heart rate often follow as the sympathetic drive goes; have the vasopressor ready and reduce the hyperventilation. The surgeon lets fluid out slowly, because sudden collapse of large ventricles tears bridging veins and causes a subdural haematoma, and in a patient with an unsecured aneurysm a fall in intracranial pressure raises the pressure across the aneurysm wall. A pass that produces blood is a tract haemorrhage and is watched with a post-operative scan.

### 5. Tunnelling
**Why:** The rod is pushed subcutaneously from the scalp to the abdomen, or in two stages with a relay incision at the neck, and the distal catheter is pulled through.
**Anaesthesia:** One to two minutes of intense stimulus arriving after a quiet period. Anticipate it with a remifentanil bolus of 0.5-1 microgram/kg or fentanyl 1-2 micrograms/kg IV and adequate depth, and tell the surgeon when you are ready. The tunneller passes over the clavicle and the pleural apex and beside the external jugular vein: a sudden change in airway pressure or saturation is a pneumothorax until excluded, and bleeding along the track shows as a swelling in the neck.

### 6. Distal catheter: peritoneum, atrium or pleura
**Why:** The peritoneum is opened through a small paraumbilical or subcostal incision, or the catheter is introduced under laparoscopic vision, which helps in the obese and after previous abdominal surgery.
**Anaesthesia:** Neuromuscular block helps the abdominal part and is the only step that needs it. Pneumoperitoneum raises intracranial pressure through raised venous and intrathoracic pressure, as described in [laparoscopy and the pneumoperitoneum](../a/a01-laparoscopy-pneumoperitoneum.html): ask for the lowest workable insufflation pressure and a short insufflation, keep the head up, and keep carbon dioxide normal. For a ventriculoatrial shunt the catheter is passed down the internal jugular vein to the cavoatrial junction with ECG, fluoroscopic or echocardiographic confirmation, and an open vein in a head-up patient entrains air: head-down for the venotomy. A pleural catheter leaves a small pneumothorax and later an effusion.

### 7. Closure and emergence
**Why:** The surgeon checks spontaneous flow from the distal end before it is placed, sets a programmable valve, and closes every wound in layers over the hardware.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask for all wounds to be infiltrated. Wake the patient promptly and avoid coughing. A patient who was drowsy from hydrocephalus should be better within hours: brighter, less headache, vomiting stopped. Failure to improve, or deterioration after initial improvement, is a malpositioned or blocked catheter or a haemorrhage and needs a scan. Posture after surgery follows the valve: some surgeons mobilise at once, others nurse flat for a day to prevent over-drainage.

### 8. Emergency external ventricular drain
**Why:** A deteriorating patient with large ventricles needs fluid out now. The burr hole is at a standard point about 11 cm behind the nasion and 3 cm from the midline, usually on the right.
**Anaesthesia:** Most are done under general anaesthesia in a patient who needs the airway secured anyway; a cooperative adult can have it under scalp infiltration. Coagulation matters more than for almost any other small procedure because the catheter passes through brain: reverse anticoagulants and correct platelets first where time allows, as set out for [chronic subdural haematoma](burr-hole-drainage-of-chronic-subdural.html). The patient returns to intensive care ventilated or awake according to the underlying disease, with the drain levelled and its output recorded hourly.

### 9. Anaesthetising a patient who already has a drain
**Why:** The drain is protecting the patient from hydrocephalus and reporting intracranial pressure, and both functions are easily lost during a transfer.
**Anaesthesia:** Before moving, find out the prescribed height, the hourly output and whether the drain is being left open or clamped with intermittent drainage. Clamp it for every transfer and every change of table height or position, because an open drain siphons when the bag drops below the head and over-drains when the patient sits up or coughs. On arrival, mount the system on a pole, zero the transducer at the tragus, set the height, open it and confirm an oscillating waveform. Do not leave it clamped unobserved in a patient who depends on it: watch the pressure and reopen if it climbs. Never inject into it or aspirate from it. An output well above 20 mL an hour means the level is too low. Over-drainage collapses the ventricles and can cause a subdural haematoma or, with an unsecured aneurysm, a rebleed.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is skin and subcutaneous tissue along a long tunnel, plus a small abdominal wall incision, so the strategy is infiltration at every wound with regular simple analgesia, and that is usually sufficient. Ask the surgeon to infiltrate the scalp, the relay incision and the abdominal wound with ropivacaine or bupivacaine within the weight limit, which in a small child is reached quickly. A unilateral rectus sheath or transversus abdominis plane block is a reasonable addition for an open abdominal incision in an adult or older child; it has no specific trial support in shunt surgery and is author's judgement. The tunnel itself cannot be blocked.

Paracetamol regularly. An NSAID is acceptable after an uncomplicated shunt from the first evening in most units, since the intracranial part is a single catheter pass; it is withheld after a bloody pass, in a patient with a recent haemorrhage, and with a fresh ventricular drain. Opioid need is a few doses of oral oxycodone or equivalent on the day of surgery and the next, and less in the elderly patient with normal pressure hydrocephalus, in whom delirium is the more likely harm. Rebound is not an issue.

Escalation is where the plan matters. Headache after shunting is expected to improve. Headache that is worse on standing and relieved by lying down is over-drainage and is treated by the surgeon adjusting the valve. Headache with drowsiness and vomiting is under-drainage or blockage. Abdominal pain with distension or fever is a catheter complication or infection. Opioid-induced nausea and drowsiness imitate shunt failure and generate scans, which is a further reason for restraint.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Shunt revision. The length is unpredictable: the surgeon tests each component and replaces what has failed. The ventricular catheter may be stuck to choroid plexus and removing it can bleed into the ventricle.
- Children. The majority of shunt surgery over a lifetime. Latex allergy is common in the spina bifida population after repeated operations; many units treat them as latex-sensitive by default. Dosing, fluids and temperature are in [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).
- Slit ventricles and the over-drained patient. Small stiff ventricles with intermittent blockage and severe symptoms on a scan that looks reassuring.
- Shunted patients having other surgery. Laparoscopy with a ventriculoperitoneal shunt in place is generally safe because the valve prevents reflux; discuss with the neurosurgical team, limit insufflation pressure and watch for failure to wake. Programmable valves are reset after magnetic resonance imaging.
- Neuraxial anaesthesia with a shunt. Possible in a functioning, uninfected system; a spinal in a patient with a blocked shunt and raised pressure is contraindicated.
- Endoscopic third ventriculostomy. Irrigation through the endoscope can raise intracranial pressure abruptly and produces bradycardia or asystole; tell the surgeon and it resolves when the fluid is let out. Injury to the basilar artery beneath the third ventricle floor is the rare catastrophe.
- Lumbar drains. The same rules of height, clamping and over-drainage apply, with the zero at the level the surgeon prescribes.
- Infection. Presents weeks later with fever, wound erythema, abdominal pain or malfunction; the whole system is removed and a ventricular drain placed.

## Key sources

Infection prevention has a large randomised trial supporting antibiotic-impregnated catheters. The handling of ventricular drains is governed by two society consensus documents that both acknowledge thin evidence; the rules given here are the widely taught ones and are consensus. The cerebrospinal fluid production rate is textbook physiology. There is no PROSPECT guideline or procedure-specific analgesic trial for shunt surgery: the pain score, the infiltration strategy and the abdominal wall block are author's judgement.
