## What the surgeon is doing and why

The operator here is a cardiac electrophysiologist, and the operation is done with catheters passed up the femoral veins. An arrhythmia needs either a circuit (a loop of tissue around which an impulse can chase its tail) or a focus (a patch that fires on its own). Ablation finds the critical piece of tissue with recording catheters and a three-dimensional map, then destroys a few millimetres of it so that the circuit is cut or the focus is fenced off.

What has to be destroyed depends on the rhythm. In typical atrial flutter the circuit runs round the right atrium through a narrow isthmus between the tricuspid valve and the inferior vena cava; a line of lesions across it is curative and the whole procedure stays in the right heart. In nodal re-entry the target is the slow pathway beside the atrioventricular node, millimetres from the fast pathway whose loss means a pacemaker. In atrial fibrillation the triggers come mostly from the muscle sleeves of the pulmonary veins, so the operator crosses the atrial septum and encircles each vein with lesions to isolate it electrically. In ventricular tachycardia the circuit threads through surviving muscle within an old infarct scar, sometimes on the outside surface of the heart.

Three energy sources are in use and they differ in what they injure nearby. Radiofrequency heats tissue through a catheter tip pressed against the wall, point by point; it needs steady contact, takes longest, and can heat the oesophagus behind the left atrium. A cryoballoon freezes a ring around each vein in a single application and can freeze the right phrenic nerve. Pulsed field ablation delivers microsecond high-voltage pulses that kill myocardium with relative sparing of oesophagus and nerve; it was non-inferior to thermal ablation in the randomised comparison, is much faster, and makes the patient's chest muscles and diaphragm contract with each delivery.

Cardioversion is the simple end of the same list: a synchronised shock across the chest depolarises the whole heart at once and lets the sinus node resume. It takes seconds and needs a patient who is unaware for those seconds.

There is essentially no wound, so pain is a 1. The anaesthetic contribution is immobility, a quiet chest, preserved inducibility of the arrhythmia when that matters, and readiness for the one common serious complication, which is tamponade. The setting is a remote site (see [remote site anaesthesia and procedural sedation](../a/a12-remote-site-sedation.html)).

## Steps

### 1. What this procedure needs from the anaesthetic
**Why:** The operator's needs differ by rhythm and conflict with each other: a perfectly still left atrium for an hour, or an arrhythmia that can still be provoked, or simply five minutes of oblivion.
**Anaesthesia:** Decide by procedure. Atrial fibrillation ablation with radiofrequency: general anaesthesia is the defensible default, because in the one randomised trial it gave more durable vein isolation and better freedom from arrhythmia than conscious sedation (88% against 69%). Pulsed field ablation: general anaesthesia or deep sedation by unit; the pooled observational data show no difference in success or major complications, and the muscle contraction and cough make light sedation unsatisfactory. Supraventricular tachycardia and flutter: minimal sedation. Ventricular tachycardia: individualised, with the ventricle setting the terms. Cardioversion: a brief general anaesthetic.

### 2. Set-up in the laboratory
**Why:** Mapping systems need reference patches on the chest and back, and the imaging equipment surrounds the patient.
**Anaesthesia:** External defibrillator pads on and connected before anything else, placed with the operator so they do not overlie mapping patches. Long extension lines, a circuit that reaches, and a view of the patient's face. For thermal left atrial ablation, place an oesophageal temperature probe after induction, level with the left atrium under fluoroscopy; a small randomised study found no thermal injury on endoscopy with a multisensor probe and injury in about a quarter of patients with a single sensor or none. Warm the patient: a cold room, exposed groins and litres of room-temperature irrigation fluid add up over three hours.

### 3. Induction and maintenance
**Why:** Nothing surgical; the patient is usually well apart from the arrhythmia, often obese with sleep apnoea, and may be in atrial fibrillation with a fast rate.
**Anaesthesia:** A standard induction with a tracheal tube or supraglottic airway. Maintenance by volatile or propofol; both are used, and there is no good evidence that either impairs ablation of atrial fibrillation. If phrenic nerve pacing is planned, use a single intubating dose of relaxant and let it recover, and tell the operator the train-of-four count. Fluid is the quiet hazard: do not add your own litre to the catheter irrigation.

### 4. Venous access and catheter placement
**Why:** Two to four sheaths go into the femoral veins under ultrasound. Catheters are placed in the coronary sinus and right heart, and an intracardiac echo probe may sit in the right atrium.
**Anaesthesia:** Catheter contact provokes ectopy and brief runs of tachycardia. Atrial fibrillation may start or stop on its own account; the operator may ask for a cardioversion at any point, so the pads are live. Vagal bradycardia with groin puncture occurs in sedated patients.

### 5. Transseptal puncture and heparin
**Why:** The left atrium is reached by puncturing the thin fossa ovalis with a long needle from the right atrium under echo and fluoroscopic guidance, then passing sheaths across. Heparin is given as the left side is entered, because catheters there form thrombus that goes to the brain.
**Anaesthesia:** The needle passes within a centimetre or two of the aortic root and the posterior atrial wall. A still patient and, at the operator's request, a breath-hold make it safer. Heparin is given as a bolus and infusion to an activated clotting time above 300 s, which you may be asked to measure every 15-30 minutes. Sheaths in the left atrium are a route for air: flush lines carefully, and under sedation avoid obstructed inspiratory efforts that generate negative intrathoracic pressure. Sudden ST elevation in the inferior leads with hypotension shortly after the puncture is coronary air and usually passes with support.

### 6. Mapping and the quiet chest
**Why:** The mapping system builds a shell of the atrium from thousands of catheter positions. Breathing moves the heart several millimetres, which blurs the map and makes the catheter slide during a lesion.
**Anaesthesia:** Give the operator a stable chest: low tidal volumes with a higher rate, or high-frequency jet ventilation with total intravenous anaesthesia, or short apnoeas during lesions. Jet ventilation was adopted for point-by-point radiofrequency; with pulsed field energy a cohort of 512 patients showed no difference in outcome with or without it, so its hypercapnia and barotrauma risk are not worth taking there. A sedated patient who snores and obstructs produces the worst possible map; that is the argument for a controlled airway.

### 7. Lesion delivery
**Why:** Each vein is isolated by a ring of lesions, and further lines may be added on the posterior wall or elsewhere.
**Anaesthesia:** What you watch depends on the energy. Radiofrequency: the oesophageal temperature, announcing any rise so the operator stops or moves, and the blood pressure, because a steam pop can perforate the atrium. Cryoballoon: the operator paces the right phrenic nerve from the superior cava during right-sided freezes and feels or records the diaphragm; you must have no neuromuscular block working, and you say so if the twitches weaken. Pulsed field: each delivery causes a jolt of skeletal muscle contraction and often a cough or a vagal pause; a short-acting relaxant or deeper anaesthesia for the applications, and atropine or glycopyrrolate in hand. Lesions near the ganglionated plexi cause abrupt bradycardia and hypotension with any energy source.

### 8. Testing
**Why:** The operator proves each vein is isolated and may try to provoke residual triggers with isoprenaline, adenosine or burst pacing.
**Anaesthesia:** Isoprenaline produces tachycardia and vasodilatation; expect the pressure to fall and support it with a vasoconstrictor so the test can continue. Adenosine causes seconds of asystole by design. If atrial fibrillation persists at the end you will be asked to keep the patient asleep for a synchronised shock.

### 9. When the pressure falls
**Why:** A catheter, needle or overheated lesion has perforated the atrium or appendage of a fully heparinised patient, and blood is accumulating in a pericardium that cannot stretch quickly.
**Anaesthesia:** Effusion needing drainage complicated 0.9% of 21,141 ablations in a national registry, more often with radiofrequency and in low-volume centres. The signs under drapes are a falling pressure with a rising heart rate, a narrowing pulse pressure, and loss of the normal excursion of the left heart border on fluoroscopy. Say it early; echo confirms in seconds. Treatment is percutaneous pericardial drainage by the operator, protamine, reversal of the oral anticoagulant, and blood returned from the drain or transfused. Your part is to maintain preload and heart rate, avoid large falls in afterload, keep spontaneous ventilation if the patient is breathing, or use small tidal volumes without PEEP if ventilated, and call for a cardiac surgeon if the drain keeps running. The physiology is set out in [resternotomy, tamponade and mechanical circulatory support](resternotomy-tamponade-and-mechanical-circulatory-support.html).

### 10. Sheath removal and emergence
**Why:** Venous sheaths are removed with a figure-of-eight suture or manual pressure, with or without protamine.
**Anaesthesia:** A smooth emergence matters for the groins: coughing, retching and leg movement cause haematomas in an anticoagulated patient, so give antiemetics, extubate without bucking where the airway allows, and keep the legs straight for the hours the operator specifies. Check for a hoarse voice or weak cough (phrenic palsy), basic neurology (stroke), and the saturation on air (fluid overload). The patient should be told that chest pain on swallowing with fever in the following weeks needs urgent review, since atrio-oesophageal fistula is rare, late and lethal.

### 11. Supraventricular tachycardia and ventricular tachycardia variants
**Why:** For nodal re-entry, accessory pathways and focal tachycardias the operator must be able to start the arrhythmia to find it and fail to start it afterwards to prove cure. Ventricular tachycardia ablation is long, in a patient with a scarred ventricle and usually a defibrillator, and may need access to the pericardial space by a subxiphoid needle.
**Anaesthesia:** For supraventricular work, anaesthetic drugs and deep sedation can make the arrhythmia hard to induce, so the default is local anaesthetic with small doses of midazolam and fentanyl or none, and children and those who cannot tolerate that have a general anaesthetic with the operator's understanding that isoprenaline may be needed. Lesions near the node carry a small risk of complete block. For ventricular tachycardia: arterial line, defibrillator therapies switched off and external pads on, vasopressor infusion from the start, and a plan agreed beforehand for how long induced tachycardia will be tolerated before it is terminated; mapping in sinus rhythm is preferred in poor ventricles, and mechanical circulatory support is arranged in advance for the worst. Hours of irrigation in a failing ventricle make a urinary catheter and diuretic routine. Epicardial puncture can lacerate the right ventricle or a coronary vessel and leaves pericarditic pain.

### 12. Cardioversion
**Why:** A synchronised shock of 150-200 J biphasic through anteroposterior or anterolateral pads resets atrial fibrillation or flutter. Where the duration of the arrhythmia or the adequacy of anticoagulation is uncertain, a transoesophageal echo first excludes thrombus in the atrial appendage.
**Anaesthesia:** A fasted patient, full monitoring, oxygen by mask, and a hypnotic titrated to loss of response: propofol in reduced dose given slowly is the usual choice, etomidate where the ventricle is poor; the Cochrane review found no agent better than another. No opioid is needed. Circulation time is slow in atrial fibrillation with a poor ventricle, so wait for the drug to work before giving more. Confirm that the defibrillator is synchronising on the R wave before every shock. Support the jaw, expect a brief apnoea, and expect a pause or bradycardia after the shock, particularly in patients on rate-controlling drugs, with atropine and external pacing through the same pads available. With a pacemaker or defibrillator in place the pads go at least 8 cm from the generator and the device is checked afterwards. If an echo is done first, topical pharyngeal anaesthesia and the same hypnotic cover both. The unstable patient in an emergency department or ward bed is the same procedure with less preparation: a smaller dose, a rapid sequence induction only if the stomach is full and time allows, and no delay for the shock.

## Pain plan

The "block" on this card is the absence of one. Groin punctures are infiltrated by the operator and need paracetamol at most. After left atrial ablation a third or so of patients describe a central or left-sided chest ache, worse on inspiration and lying flat, for two to four days: this is pericardial irritation from the lesions, it responds to paracetamol and sometimes a short course of an anti-inflammatory agent prescribed by the cardiologist with regard to the anticoagulant, and it should be distinguished from the tamponade that presents late with breathlessness and a falling pressure. Opioids are not needed and make the nausea and urinary retention of a long case worse.

Epicardial ventricular tachycardia ablation is the exception: the pericardium has been instrumented and often ablated from within, and pericarditic pain is expected for several days. The operator may leave a drain and instil a steroid; systemic paracetamol and a short opioid course are reasonable. After cardioversion, chest wall soreness or erythema under the pads is treated with simple analgesia and an emollient.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Obesity and sleep apnoea are the typical atrial fibrillation phenotype: a secured airway beats sedation in a patient who will obstruct for three hours with heparin on board.
- Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction: the irrigation fluid, the isoprenaline and the loss of atrial contraction all tell; an arterial line and early diuretic.
- A left atrial appendage thrombus on the pre-procedure echo cancels the case.
- Phrenic nerve palsy shows as a raised right hemidiaphragm and reduced saturation in recovery; most recover over weeks to months.
- Stroke or transient ischaemic attack from catheter thrombus or air presents on emergence: delayed waking after an ablation gets imaging, not more time.
- Pulmonary vein stenosis and atrio-oesophageal fistula present weeks later and reach an anaesthetist only at the next procedure.
- Paediatric and congenital heart disease ablation: general anaesthesia, awareness of shunts, and operators who need inducibility preserved.
- Cardioversion in pregnancy is safe for the fetus at standard energies; left lateral tilt and an obstetric plan after 20 weeks.
- Digoxin toxicity and uncorrected hypokalaemia make post-shock ventricular arrhythmia more likely: check the potassium.

## Key sources

General anaesthesia for radiofrequency atrial fibrillation ablation is supported by one single-centre randomised trial and by consensus; for pulsed field ablation the comparison with sedation is observational. Tamponade rates come from large registries. Oesophageal temperature monitoring has small trials only. For cardioversion the Cochrane review found the trials too heterogeneous to pool, and practice rests on familiarity with propofol.
