## What the surgeon is doing and why

Small bowel obstruction is a mechanical block to a tube that secretes 7-8 L a day into its own lumen. Above the block the bowel fills with fluid and swallowed air, distends, stops absorbing and starts secreting more; its wall becomes oedematous; fluid refluxes into the stomach and is vomited or sits there. The patient loses litres of isotonic, potassium-rich fluid into a space that does not show on any chart. If the wall tension or a twisted mesentery cuts off the blood supply, the bowel strangulates, infarcts and perforates, and the illness changes from dehydration to sepsis.

The cause in most adults who have had previous abdominal surgery is adhesions: fibrous bands between loops or from bowel to the abdominal wall. The next most common are hernias (groin, incisional, internal) and malignancy. The cause decides the operation. A single band across one loop can be divided in minutes, sometimes laparoscopically. A matted abdomen after multiple laparotomies means hours of dissecting loops apart millimetre by millimetre. A hernia needs reduction and repair. Malignant obstruction may need a bypass or a stoma and nothing more.

Most adhesive obstruction is not operated on at first. Guidelines support a trial of nasogastric decompression, intravenous fluid and a water-soluble contrast study, and in the UK national audit half of 2,341 patients never needed surgery. The ones who reach you fall into two groups, and they are different anaesthetics:

- Immediate surgery (about 30% in the audit) for peritonitis, a closed loop or signs of ischaemia on CT, or a strangulated hernia. Sick, in pain, often with a raised lactate, operated on within hours and incompletely resuscitated.
- Delayed surgery (about 20%) after conservative management has failed over two to five days. Less acutely ill, but starved for most of a week, depleted of potassium and magnesium, with kidneys that have had several days of hypovolaemia. This group had the higher major complication rate in the audit (23.6% against 19.0%).

In theatre the surgeon enters the abdomen without injuring bowel stuck to the old scar, finds the transition point between dilated and collapsed bowel, releases it, decides whether the affected segment is viable, resects it if not, and then has to get distended, oedematous bowel back into an abdomen that no longer has room for it.

Pain logic. The dominant mechanism is the abdominal wall: a midline laparotomy through scar, usually longer than the surgeon first intended. Visceral pain from handled and still-distended bowel adds colicky discomfort for the two to five days of ileus. With a block or catheters covering the wall, the plan holds pain on movement at about 5 for the first two days. A laparoscopic band division, when it succeeds, is a much smaller event. Mortality in the audit was 6.6% whether or not the patient was operated on, which is a reminder that this is an illness of frail people, and the pain plan has to work in a patient with renal impairment, ileus and often delirium.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment and resuscitation before theatre
**Why:** The surgeon's decision to operate is made on pain, peritonism, CT findings and failure to progress; the timing is negotiable in some patients and not in others.
**Anaesthesia:** Establish which group this is. For suspected ischaemia, resuscitate on the way and do not delay: fluid, antibiotics, analgesia, arterial line, blood gas. For failed conservative management there are usually a few hours to correct potassium, magnesium and volume, and they should be used. Estimate the deficit from the history (days of vomiting, nasogastric output, urine output, creatinine against baseline) and expect it to be 3-5 L or more. Give balanced crystalloid with potassium; the vomiting patient has a hypochloraemic alkalosis, the patient with ischaemic bowel a lactic acidosis. Calculate a formal risk score, document it, and book the critical care bed if predicted mortality is 5% or more. This is an [emergency laparotomy](emergency-laparotomy.html) and belongs on that pathway, including frailty assessment and a consultant-delivered anaesthetic.

### 2. Emptying the stomach and securing the airway
**Why:** The surgeon wants a decompressed stomach and bowel; you want the same thing for a different reason.
**Anaesthesia:** A nasogastric tube goes in before induction if there is not one already, and is aspirated and left on free drainage or suction immediately beforehand. It reduces the volume; it does not make the stomach empty, because bowel content keeps refluxing and thick or particulate fluid does not come up a fine-bore tube. In a national cohort of obstructed patients managed with a nasogastric tube, 7.3% developed aspiration pneumonia and their 30-day mortality was 31% against 10%. So: head-up 20-30 degrees, thorough preoxygenation, suction on and under the pillow, rapid sequence induction with a dose reduced for hypovolaemia (or ketamine), a vasopressor drawn up and connected, and a tracheal tube. Whether to apply cricoid pressure and whether to leave the nasogastric tube in during induction are matters of local practice; leaving it in, open to air or suction, is reasonable. Avoid mask ventilation unless oxygenation demands it.

### 3. Maintenance set-up
**Why:** The case may be 45 minutes or four hours and the surgeon often cannot say which.
**Anaesthesia:** Arterial line if not already placed, second large cannula, urinary catheter, temperature probe, warming. Avoid nitrous oxide, which expands the gas-filled loops. Deep neuromuscular block with quantitative monitoring. Place rectus sheath or TAP blocks now if the patient is stable, or agree that the surgeon will place catheters at closure. A lung-protective strategy matters more than usual: these patients have aspirated or will have basal atelectasis from distension.

### 4. Entry: laparoscopy or laparotomy
**Why:** Laparoscopy suits a first obstruction with a single band on CT and modest distension. Otherwise the surgeon opens through or beside the old midline scar, starting in virgin territory above or below it, because bowel is often adherent to the back of the scar.
**Anaesthesia:** Insufflating an abdomen full of dilated loops gives little working space and a high risk of bowel injury at port insertion, and conversion is common, so prepare for a laparotomy whatever the booking says. On opening, the release of abdominal pressure in a hypovolaemic patient can drop the arterial pressure; bowel eviscerates and heat loss begins.

### 5. Adhesiolysis and finding the transition point
**Why:** Loops are separated by sharp dissection until the bowel can be followed from the ligament of Treitz to the caecum and the point of obstruction is released. The surgeon tries to do as much as is needed and no more, because each division creates new raw surface.
**Anaesthesia:** Slow, with steady insensible and third-space loss from exposed oedematous bowel. Inadvertent enterotomies happen in a hostile abdomen and each one adds contamination, time and a suture line. Traction on the mesentery causes vagal bradycardia and transient hypotension. Guide fluid by stroke volume or pulse pressure variation and lactate trend; run a low-dose vasopressor for the vasodilatation of anaesthesia and sepsis and do not treat it with unlimited crystalloid, since bowel oedema is already the problem at closure.

### 6. Viability and resection
**Why:** Once released, a strangulated segment is wrapped in warm packs and watched for colour, peristalsis and mesenteric pulsation, sometimes with indocyanine green. Dead bowel is resected and joined, or the ends are brought out or left stapled for a second look in 24-48 hours if the patient is unstable.
**Anaesthesia:** Releasing a strangulated loop returns potassium, lactate and inflammatory mediators to the circulation: expect a fall in pressure and a rise in vasopressor requirement within minutes. The surgeon's choice between an anastomosis and a stoma or a damage-control laparotomy depends partly on what you report: tell them the noradrenaline dose, the lactate and the trend. An anastomosis in a patient on a rising vasopressor dose is a leak in waiting.

### 7. Decompression, return of the bowel and closure
**Why:** The surgeon milks luminal content back to the stomach to be aspirated through the nasogastric tube, so that the bowel will fit and the suture lines are not under tension, then closes the fascia.
**Anaesthesia:** Check the tube is in the stomach and suction it actively during milking; a litre or more arrives in a minute, and this is the second aspiration risk if the cuff is not sealing. Closing over oedematous bowel raises intra-abdominal pressure: watch peak airway pressure and tidal volume as the fascia comes together, and tell the surgeon if plateau pressure rises steeply, because abdominal compartment syndrome on the first night presents as oliguria and difficult ventilation. If it will not close without tension, a temporary closure is the safer course. Rectus sheath catheters are placed under vision before the fascia is closed.

### 8. Emergence
**Why:** The operation is over but the obstruction physiologically is not; the bowel will not work for days.
**Anaesthesia:** Extubate only if the patient is warm, has a clearing lactate, a low or absent vasopressor requirement and good gas exchange; otherwise ventilate in intensive care. For extubation: nasogastric tube aspirated and left on free drainage, full reversal confirmed, head-up, awake with airway reflexes. Do not remove the nasogastric tube.

### 9. The first days
**Why:** The surgeon waits for the ileus to resolve (falling nasogastric output, flatus), watches the wound, and starts parenteral nutrition if the gut has been out of use for a week.
**Anaesthesia:** Continued fluid and electrolyte replacement including nasogastric losses, daily potassium, magnesium and phosphate, and awareness of refeeding in the patient starved before surgery. Pulmonary complications are the commonest serious morbidity: analgesia that permits coughing and sitting out is prevention, not comfort. New pain, tachycardia or rising lactate on day 3-5 is a leak or a missed enterotomy.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a midline laparotomy plus sore bowel, in a patient whose kidneys, gut and often clotting are impaired. That narrows the options before the evidence does. NSAIDs are out for most of these patients in the first days because of acute kidney injury and a fresh anastomosis. An epidural has the best analgesic evidence for a midline wound, but sepsis, coagulopathy, hypovolaemia and the need for vasopressors make it the wrong choice in the unstable patient; it remains reasonable in a resuscitated, non-septic patient having planned surgery after failed conservative management, particularly with lung disease.

The defensible default for the rest is bilateral rectus sheath catheters, placed with ultrasound after induction or by the surgeon at closure, with intermittent boluses or infusion of dilute long-acting local anaesthetic for 48-72 hours, regular intravenous paracetamol, and an opioid PCA. This is the author's judgement, extrapolated from PROSPECT's acceptance of TAP blocks and pre-peritoneal infusion for open colorectal surgery when an epidural is not feasible; there is no procedure-specific guideline for emergency laparotomy analgesia. Rectus sheath catheters cover the midline somatic wound and nothing visceral, so opioid is still needed, at a lower dose. A low-dose ketamine infusion helps the opioid-tolerant and the patient with ileus in whom every milligram of opioid counts. If no local anaesthetic catheter is used, an intravenous lidocaine infusion is an alternative, never both together.

Opioid has a specific cost here: it prolongs the ileus that is keeping the patient in hospital. Oral analgesia is not available until the gut works, so the intravenous and regional components carry the first three to five days.

For a laparoscopic band division: port-site infiltration or TAP blocks, paracetamol, and a small amount of opioid for a day.

Escalation is mostly diagnostic. Pain that was controlled and is no longer, with distension, is ileus or re-obstruction; with tachycardia and fever it is a leak; with a tense abdomen, oliguria and rising airway pressure it is compartment syndrome.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The elderly, frail patient. The typical patient, not the exception. Smaller induction doses, invasive pressure monitoring from the start, delirium prevention, and an early conversation about goals if the bowel turns out to be extensively infarcted.
- Strangulated groin or incisional hernia as the cause. A smaller incision if the bowel is viable, a laparotomy if it is not; the airway and fluid issues are identical. See the [inguinal hernia](inguinal-and-femoral-hernia-repair-open.html) card for the emergency variant.
- Malignant obstruction. Carcinomatosis, ascites, cachexia and opioid tolerance; the operation may be a bypass or a venting procedure, and goals of care are set beforehand.
- The virgin abdomen. Obstruction without previous surgery suggests a tumour, an internal hernia, a volvulus or Crohn's disease, and the operation is less predictable.
- Early postoperative obstruction. Within weeks of another laparotomy, adhesions are vascular and dense; surgeons avoid operating and when they must, it bleeds.
- Damage control. An unstable patient with infarcted bowel leaves with the ends stapled and a temporary closure, and comes back in 24-48 hours: plan sedation, ventilation and the second anaesthetic.
- Residual neuromuscular block and aspiration at extubation. The same full-stomach rules apply at the end as at the start.
- Recurrent obstruction. Patients who have been through this before often know what worked; ask them.

## Key sources

The epidemiology is solid: the UK national audit gives the proportions operated on and the outcomes, and the Bologna guideline sets out the surgical decision. The anaesthetic recommendations are extrapolation and consensus. There is no trial of induction technique in bowel obstruction, and the nasogastric tube advice rests on physiology, observational aspiration data and long-standing practice. The analgesic plan borrows from elective open colorectal surgery and is weakest exactly where these patients differ from that population: sepsis, kidney injury and ileus.
