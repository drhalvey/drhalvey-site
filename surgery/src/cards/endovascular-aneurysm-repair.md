## What the surgeon is doing and why

An aneurysm ruptures because its wall is exposed to arterial pressure. Open repair replaces the wall. Endovascular repair leaves the wall where it is and takes the pressure off it: a fabric tube supported by a metal stent is delivered through the femoral arteries, opened inside the aorta, and held by radial force and small hooks against healthy aorta above the aneurysm (the neck) and healthy iliac artery below. Blood now runs through the graft, the sac around it thromboses, and with luck shrinks. There is no laparotomy, no aortic clamp, no lower-body ischaemia and no reperfusion, and that is the source of the early survival advantage: in EVAR trial 1 the hazard of death in the first six months was about 40% lower than with open repair. The price is durability. The seal can fail, the sac can be pressurised again (endoleak), and after eight years open repair had the lower mortality. Endovascular repair therefore suits the older or less fit patient with suitable anatomy, which is to say most of the aneurysm population and most of the patients you will meet.

The operator has four tasks. First, get a device of 14-24 French calibre from the groin to the aorta, through iliac arteries that may be narrow, calcified and tortuous. This is where the acute disasters come from. Second, place the top of the graft immediately below the lowest renal artery, to within a few millimetres, using angiography; cover a renal artery and the kidney is lost, land too low and the seal is short. Third, for a standard bifurcated device, cannulate the short contralateral limb from the other groin and extend it into the other iliac. Fourth, balloon the seal zones, check with a completion angiogram that the sac no longer fills, and close the arteries.

The three operations on this card differ in where the graft goes and what it covers.

```
 TEVAR            descending thoracic aorta
                  covers intercostals; may cover left subclavian
                  risks: cord ischaemia, stroke, arm ischaemia
 FEVAR / BEVAR    visceral segment
                  fenestrations or branches to renal, SMA, coeliac
                  risks: long case, contrast, cord, visceral ischaemia
 EVAR             infrarenal aorta to iliacs
                  risks: access vessel injury, endoleak, renal coverage
```

A thoracic endograft covers intercostal arteries that feed the spinal cord, and often the left subclavian artery, which feeds the vertebral and the cord's upper collateral supply. The deployment needs a few seconds of very low cardiac output so that the graft is not blown downstream as it opens. Fenestrated and branched grafts extend the seal up through the visceral segment, with a hole or side arm for each renal and mesenteric artery, each of which has to be cannulated and stented: these are long, contrast-heavy, radiation-heavy cases with large sheaths in both legs for hours, and they combine the cord risk of thoracic repair with leg and visceral ischaemia.

The pain logic is the shortest in the book. Two groin punctures or two 5 cm groin incisions are the only wounds. There is no muscle, no bone and no peritoneum. Wound pain is minimal with percutaneous access and mild with cut-downs. The discomfort patients report is lying flat for hours on a hard table, back and buttock ache, and post-implantation syndrome, a sterile inflammatory response to the graft and fresh sac thrombus that produces fever, malaise and raised inflammatory markers in about a quarter of patients for a few days. The anaesthetic is therefore chosen for the procedure's demands (duration, stillness, apnoea, haemodynamic control, the capacity to manage a catastrophe) and never for analgesia.

## Steps

### 1. Planning: which device, which access, which anaesthetic
**Why:** The operator plans from the CT angiogram: neck length and angulation, iliac calibre, and whether access is percutaneous or by cut-down.
**Anaesthesia:** Three questions set your plan. How long: under 2 h suits local anaesthesia and sedation, over 3 h does not. How difficult is the access: small, calcified iliacs are the warning for rupture and for an iliac conduit, which is a retroperitoneal incision under general anaesthesia. Does the graft cover thoracic aorta or a long segment: if so cord protection is part of your case. For standard infrarenal repair the pooled observational data favour loco-regional techniques, with lower perioperative mortality (odds ratio 0.70) and morbidity and a shorter stay across 15,472 patients, but there is no randomised trial and fitter anatomy and calmer patients are selected for local anaesthesia. The honest reading is that local anaesthesia with light sedation is at least as safe and is a reasonable default for a straightforward case in a patient who can lie flat and still; general anaesthesia is not wrong and is better for the patient who cannot.

### 2. Lines, position and radiation set-up
**Why:** The C-arm needs an unobstructed arc from chest to groins, and the operator stands at the patient's right hip.
**Anaesthesia:** Arterial line for all but the most routine cases; right radial if the left arm is to be used for access or the left subclavian may be covered. Two large cannulae with extensions, because the arms are tucked and you will be 2 m from the patient behind a lead screen. Urinary catheter for complex repair. Pressure areas matter in a long case on a thin radiolucent mattress in a patient with vascular disease: heels, sacrum, occiput. Warm from the start; hybrid theatres are cold and the patient is exposed from nipples to knees.

### 3. Femoral access
**Why:** Percutaneous access places two suture-mediated closure devices before the large sheath goes in (the pre-close technique). A cut-down exposes the common femoral artery through a short groin incision. Randomised comparison shows no difference in access complications; percutaneous access is quicker and has less seroma.
**Anaesthesia:** The operator infiltrates each groin with lidocaine 1% 10-20 mL; keep count toward the total dose. The sheath insertion is the stimulating part: a brief pushing pain in the groin and iliac fossa. Remifentanil 0.02-0.05 micrograms/kg/min, or small boluses of fentanyl with propofol by target-controlled infusion at a low target, cover it. Keep sedation light enough that the patient can hold a breath on command. Give heparin 70-100 units/kg IV when the sheaths are in and check an activated clotting time if the case will be long.

### 4. Device delivery through the iliac arteries
**Why:** The main body is advanced over a stiff wire from the groin to the renal level. Stiff wires straighten tortuous iliacs; a large sheath in a small calcified artery can split or avulse it.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the first rupture risk. The sign is often delayed, because the sheath itself tamponades the tear until it is withdrawn at the end. Sudden loin or groin pain in an awake patient, or unexplained hypotension, is an iliac rupture until an angiogram excludes it. The response is the operator's (an occlusion balloon, a covered stent) and yours runs in parallel: call for help, blood and the rapid infuser, and convert to general anaesthesia once the bleeding is controlled by a balloon, not before, because induction in an exsanguinating patient removes the tone that is keeping them alive.

### 5. Angiography and deployment
**Why:** With the device at the level of the renal arteries, a breath-hold angiogram marks the lowest renal, and the graft is unsheathed just below it.
**Anaesthesia:** The operator needs an immobile patient and motionless diaphragm for 10-20 s at a time: breath-hold on request under sedation, apnoea under general anaesthesia. Infrarenal deployment needs no pressure manipulation. Thoracic deployment does: the windsock effect of full cardiac output can push the opening graft distally, so systolic pressure is briefly lowered to 70-80 mmHg, by a bolus of short-acting vasodilator or deepening of anaesthesia, by rapid ventricular pacing at 160-200 beats/min through a temporary wire, or by adenosine, according to the unit. Restore pressure immediately afterwards and then raise it, because from this moment the cord is on collaterals.

### 6. Contralateral limb, extensions and visceral branches
**Why:** In standard repair the contralateral gate is cannulated from the other groin and the second iliac limb deployed. In fenestrated and branched repair each visceral artery is cannulated through its fenestration or branch and bridged with a covered stent, which is most of the operating time.
**Anaesthesia:** For complex repair this is hours of stillness, contrast and heparin. Repeat the activated clotting time every 30-60 min and top up heparin. Both legs have large sheaths obstructing iliac flow: leg ischaemia time is counted in hours, and lactate, potassium and creatine kinase rise accordingly, with a reperfusion event when the sheaths come out. Monitor arterial gases hourly. Keep the mean pressure up and the haemoglobin above 100 g/L if the thoracic or visceral aorta is covered. Ask for the contrast volume at intervals; the request itself moderates use.

### 7. Balloon moulding and completion angiogram
**Why:** A compliant balloon is inflated in the proximal and distal seal zones and at the graft overlaps to press fabric against wall, then an angiogram looks for endoleak.
**Anaesthesia:** Balloon inflation in the aorta is a brief total occlusion: a short rise in proximal pressure, then a fall on deflation. In the thoracic aorta the afterload rise is large, so the same pressure-lowering manoeuvre as deployment is used. Aortic neck rupture during ballooning is rare and is managed by re-inflating the balloon as an occluder while a cuff or conversion is prepared.

### 8. Sheath removal and closure
**Why:** The closure-device sutures are tied down as the sheath is withdrawn, or the arteriotomy is repaired directly and the groin closed.
**Anaesthesia:** Unmasking of an iliac injury happens now. Stay attentive until both sheaths are out and the pressure has been stable for several minutes. Protamine is given at the operator's request to help closure-device haemostasis. Check foot perfusion with the operator before the drapes come down: an ischaemic leg from a dissection flap, embolus or occluded limb is fixed now. After long complex repair, the sheath removal is also the reperfusion of both legs, with a fall in pressure and a rise in potassium and carbon dioxide.

### 9. Emergence and the first leg examination
**Why:** The operator wants no coughing against fresh closure devices and, for thoracic and complex repair, an early neurological baseline.
**Anaesthesia:** For standard repair there is little to do: stop the sedation, lie flat for 2-4 h with the groins observed. For thoracic and extensive repair wake the patient promptly and examine leg power in theatre or on arrival in recovery, then hourly. Avoid long-acting sedatives and dense neuraxial local anaesthetic that would obscure the examination.

### 10. The first 48 hours
**Why:** The graft is in; what remains is the cord, the kidneys, the groins and the inflammatory response.
**Anaesthesia:** Spinal cord ischaemia after thoracic and extensive repair is as often delayed as immediate, typically provoked by a hypotensive episode on day 1-3. The rescue bundle is the same everywhere: raise mean arterial pressure to 90-100 mmHg with a vasopressor, correct haemoglobin, give oxygen, and drain cerebrospinal fluid to 10 mmHg if a drain is in place or can be placed. Post-implantation syndrome (fever, leucocytosis, raised C-reactive protein, no source) occurs in about 25% after infrarenal repair, more with polyester grafts, and is treated with paracetamol and, where renal function allows, a short NSAID course. Contrast-associated kidney injury shows on day 2-3.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is two small groin wounds, so the analgesic strategy is local infiltration by the operator at the start and regular paracetamol afterwards. That is sufficient for percutaneous repair, and nothing on this card should be read as a reason to add more. For bilateral cut-downs, top up the wounds with ropivacaine 0.375% 10 mL per side at closure, or add ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric blocks if the case is under general anaesthesia; a few doses of oral opioid on the first night are the upper limit of normal. No catheter technique has a role.

A spinal anaesthetic is a reasonable choice for standard infrarenal repair with cut-downs in a patient who would rather not be aware, provided the timing with heparin follows the usual neuraxial rules and the case is reliably under 2-3 h. It is avoided in thoracic and extensive repair for a specific reason: motor block makes cord ischaemia undetectable in the hours when it is most treatable. That is an author's judgement shared by most units, not a trial finding.

NSAIDs are withheld on the day of surgery because of the contrast load and are reasonable from day 1-2 in a patient with preserved renal function, where they also treat the fever and malaise of post-implantation syndrome.

Escalation triggers are diagnostic, not analgesic. Back, loin or abdominal pain after repair is a retroperitoneal bleed from an access vessel until imaging excludes it. A painful, cold leg is limb occlusion or embolism. Buttock claudication after internal iliac coverage is expected and chronic. Pain requiring repeated opioid after a percutaneous repair has a cause that is not the wound.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Spinal drains. Prophylactic cerebrospinal fluid drainage for thoracic and thoracoabdominal endovascular repair is supported by physiology and by open-surgery experience, but pooled observational data in 4,814 endovascular patients show the same cord ischaemia rate, about 5%, with and without a drain. The drain is not benign: in fenestrated and branched repair, drain-related mortality was 1.4%, major complications 6.1%, with intracranial haemorrhage in 2.8% and spinal haematoma in 1.4%, and 11% of drains did not function. Many units have moved from routine to selective prophylactic drains (long coverage, previous aortic surgery, occluded internal iliac or left subclavian arteries) or to rescue drainage only. If one is placed, it goes in awake or at induction at least 1 h before heparin, drains to 10-12 mmHg at no more than 10-15 mL/h, and is removed only with normal coagulation.
- Left subclavian coverage. Planned coverage is preceded by carotid-subclavian bypass or transposition in many patients, to protect the vertebral artery, a left internal mammary coronary graft and the cord. Unplanned coverage means a left arm pressure that reads low: use the right arm.
- Stroke in thoracic repair. Wires and devices cross the arch. Stroke is an embolic complication of the procedure, and it is found by the same early wake-up that looks for cord ischaemia.
- Conversion to general anaesthesia. Decide the trigger in advance: restlessness with the device half deployed is the worst time. A patient who is uncomfortable lying flat at 20 min will not manage 120.
- Iliac conduit. When the iliacs will not accept the sheath, a graft is sewn to the common iliac through a retroperitoneal incision. This is a general anaesthetic with a muscle-cutting flank wound and pain on the scale of an open hernia repair, managed with a transversus abdominis plane or quadratus lumborum block.
- Renal impairment. The contrast volume and the proximity of the graft to the renal arteries both matter. Hydration and avoidance of hypotension are the measures with support; carbon dioxide angiography and intravascular ultrasound are operator options.
- Endoleak reinterventions. Later procedures (limb extensions, embolisation of lumbar or inferior mesenteric feeders, translumbar sac puncture in the prone position) are shorter, done under local anaesthesia or sedation, and occasionally prone.
- The emergency variant. Endovascular repair of a ruptured aneurysm is done under local anaesthesia wherever possible, with an aortic occlusion balloon as the equivalent of the clamp; see [ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm](ruptured-abdominal-aortic-aneurysm.html). Open conversion of an elective case that has ruptured on the table follows the same card. The open elective operation is on [open abdominal aortic aneurysm repair](open-abdominal-aortic-aneurysm-repair.html).

## Key sources

The choice between open and endovascular repair rests on large randomised trials with 15-year follow-up. The choice of anaesthetic does not: it rests on pooled registries and cohorts that consistently favour loco-regional techniques and are consistently confounded by selection. The spinal drain evidence is retrospective and of very low quality, and its direction over the last few years has been toward selective use as the complication data accumulated. There is no procedure-specific pain literature of substance, because the operation produces little pain; the pain plan is author's judgement built on the anatomy of the wounds.
