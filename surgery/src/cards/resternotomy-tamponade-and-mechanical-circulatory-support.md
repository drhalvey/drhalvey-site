## What the surgeon is doing and why

This card gathers three situations in which the chest is opened again or the heart is given a machine to help it. They share patients, physiology and risk.

The first is going back after cardiac surgery. Blood is collecting faster than the drains remove it, or it has clotted around the heart and is compressing it. The surgeon reopens the sternotomy, removes the wires, evacuates clot, washes out, and looks for a source: a side branch of a graft, a sternal wire hole, an anastomosis, a cannulation site. In a substantial proportion nothing discrete is found and the cause was coagulopathy. Tranexamic acid halved the rate of return to theatre in the largest trial, from 2.8% to 1.4%, and patients who do go back have about five times the odds of operative death, which reflects both the bleeding and what it does to the kidneys, lungs and brain.

The second is the planned redo sternotomy, for a failed valve, new coronary disease or the next stage of aortic surgery. The first operation left the pericardium open, so the right ventricle, the aorta and any patent grafts are now stuck to the back of the sternum by scar. The surgeon's problem is to divide the bone without dividing what lies behind it, and then to dissect the heart out of adhesions far enough to cannulate and operate.

The third is mechanical support for a heart that cannot maintain a circulation, after surgery or in cardiogenic shock. The devices form a ladder. An intra-aortic balloon pump augments diastolic pressure and reduces afterload but adds little flow. A microaxial pump sits across the aortic valve and moves blood from the left ventricle to the aorta. Veno-arterial extracorporeal support drains venous blood, oxygenates it and returns it to the arterial tree, replacing heart and lungs. A durable left ventricular assist device is implanted into the apex of the ventricle with an outflow graft to the aorta and a driveline through the skin, as a bridge to transplant or as permanent therapy.

```
 support        what it does              what it costs
 balloon pump   coronary flow up,         needs a working ventricle
                afterload down            and a competent aortic valve
 microaxial     unloads LV, 2.5-5 L/min   haemolysis, limb, bleeding
 VA ECMO        full flow, heart + lung   loads the LV, limb, bleeding
 durable LVAD   long-term LV output       depends entirely on the RV
```

Pain is that of a sternotomy, a 4, with nothing distinctive: these patients are typically sedated and ventilated through the first days, and analgesia is part of intensive care sedation. The base operation is described in [coronary artery bypass grafting](coronary-artery-bypass-grafting-on-and.html) and the pump in [cardiopulmonary bypass and cardiac essentials](../a/a07-cardiopulmonary-bypass.html).

## Steps

### 1. Recognising bleeding and tamponade after cardiac surgery
**Why:** The surgeon decides to reopen on drain losses, haemodynamics and echo. Thresholds vary between surgeons; a common pattern is brisk loss in the first hour or sustained loss over several.
**Anaesthesia:** Tamponade after cardiac surgery is not the textbook circumferential effusion. The pericardium is open and clot is localised, often behind the left atrium or against the right atrium, where transthoracic echo cannot see it. Suspect it on the trend: drains that were running and have stopped, a rising central venous pressure, falling pressure and urine output, a climbing vasopressor dose and lactate. A transoesophageal echo makes the diagnosis; a normal transthoracic study does not exclude it. While theatre is prepared, correct what is correctable: temperature, calcium, protamine for residual heparin, and products directed by viscoelastic testing.

### 2. Arrest after cardiac surgery
**Why:** The common causes in the first days are tamponade, bleeding, graft failure and arrhythmia through a chest that can be reopened in a minute. The consensus protocol is built on that.
**Anaesthesia:** The society statement starts from the premise that external compressions are ineffective when the cause is tamponade or hypovolaemia, and sets a target of resternotomy within 5 minutes. The cardiac surgical variations to standard life support follow from it: defibrillate ventricular fibrillation with up to three stacked shocks, and pace asystole through the epicardial wires, before starting compressions if this can be done within a minute; withhold the full 1 mg dose of adrenaline, because a patient who regains output on it will tear suture lines with the rebound pressure, and give small increments instead; and get the reopening set and a gowned operator to the bedside in parallel. Your roles are the airway, ventilation at 100% oxygen with a check for tension pneumothorax and tube position, stopping sedative and vasodilator infusions, and running the drugs.

### 3. Induction with tamponade
**Why:** Nothing surgical yet, and that is the point: the surgeon must be ready before you start.
**Anaesthesia:** In tamponade the pericardial pressure has risen to meet the filling pressures, stroke volume is small and fixed, and output is kept up by heart rate, by vasoconstriction, and by the fall in intrathoracic pressure on each spontaneous inspiration that draws blood into the right heart. Anaesthesia threatens all three. If the patient is not already intubated: prepare and drape awake, surgeon scrubbed, vasopressor and adrenaline connected, volume running. Give a reduced dose of ketamine or etomidate, keep the patient breathing spontaneously as long as is practical, and when you must ventilate use small tidal volumes at a low inflation pressure with no PEEP. For a large effusion outside the post-surgical setting, the safest sequence is drainage under local anaesthetic first, by needle or subxiphoid window, with general anaesthesia afterwards if still needed. The patient who is already ventilated in intensive care has lost the spontaneous breathing component and is living on sympathetic tone: do not deepen sedation for transfer.

### 4. Opening and release
**Why:** Removing the wires and spreading the sternum decompresses the heart at once, usually before any clot is removed.
**Anaesthesia:** Expect the pressure to rise sharply within seconds. A patient on a high dose of adrenaline and noradrenaline for a compressed heart is now a patient with a free heart on a large dose of both: turn the infusions down as the retractor opens and be ready with a vasodilator, because a systolic surge onto fresh suture lines turns a clot problem into a bleeding problem. If the pressure does not recover on release, the diagnosis was incomplete: look for a graft problem, right ventricular failure or continuing hypovolaemia on echo.

### 5. Finding the bleeding
**Why:** The surgeon washes out with warm saline and inspects systematically. A discrete source is repaired; diffuse ooze from raw surfaces is coagulopathy and is not fixed with stitches. If the heart is swollen or the field will not dry, the chest is packed and left open under a membrane for closure a day or two later.
**Anaesthesia:** Run this as major haemorrhage with a cardiac flavour (see [major haemorrhage, cell salvage and coagulation](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html)): warm the patient, keep ionised calcium and pH normal, use cell salvage, and give fibrinogen, platelets and factor replacement against viscoelastic results, an approach that reduced transfusion and major bleeding in a large trial. Send a heparinase-paired sample to settle whether more protamine is needed. An open chest is nursed sedated and ventilated; the absence of sternal splinting alters ventilation little but any cough is dangerous.

### 6. Planning a redo sternotomy
**Why:** A computed tomogram shows what lies behind the sternum and how close: the right ventricle, the aorta or an aneurysm of it, the innominate vein, and above all a patent internal mammary graft crossing the midline, injury to which causes immediate ischaemia of the anterior wall.
**Anaesthesia:** Know the imaging and the surgeon's plan for each scenario. External pads are placed before draping, since fibrillation during dissection cannot be treated with internal paddles on a heart that is still encased. Wide-bore access, a rapid infuser, six units of blood checked and in the room, cell salvage set up from skin incision. Place the arterial line away from any site the surgeon may cannulate. Series that used routine imaging and protective strategies report re-entry injury in about 1.5% of cases, against 5-10% historically.

### 7. Peripheral cannulation and re-entry
**Why:** Where the scan shows a structure against the bone, the surgeon exposes or cannulates the femoral or axillary vessels first, so that bypass can be started in seconds, or starts it before the saw to empty the heart and let it fall back. The sternum is then divided with an oscillating saw through the anterior table and scissors or the saw under direct vision through the posterior table, with the old wires left in place as a guard until the last moment.
**Anaesthesia:** Heparin may be given early; know whether it has been. During the saw, hold ventilation with the lungs deflated and watch the surgeon and the arterial trace, not the monitor behind you. A torn right ventricle or aorta presents as blood welling through the sternotomy: the response is to announce it, give volume through your lines, go on bypass peripherally, and cool if the surgeon needs circulatory arrest to repair an aortic injury. In a series of 336 redo cases with peripheral bypass established before re-entry, injury occurred in 1.5% and all were repaired without haemodynamic collapse.

### 8. Adhesiolysis
**Why:** The heart is dissected out of scar with diathermy and scissors far enough to cannulate centrally, clamp the aorta and reach the target. Old vein grafts are handled as little as possible because they contain friable atheroma that embolises into the coronary bed.
**Anaesthesia:** This takes an hour or more and bleeds continuously from raw surfaces. Diathermy on the ventricle causes ectopy and fibrillation, treated through the external pads. Ischaemia from a manipulated graft shows as new ST change or wall motion abnormality before bypass; tell the surgeon, who may go on early. The bypass run and the coagulopathy after it will both be longer than in a first-time operation, so order products early.

### 9. The intra-aortic balloon pump
**Why:** A balloon in the descending aorta inflates at aortic valve closure, raising diastolic pressure and coronary flow, and deflates just before systole, leaving a low-pressure space for the ventricle to eject into. It is inserted through the femoral artery before, during or after surgery.
**Anaesthesia:** Confirm the tip on echo just below the left subclavian artery. Timing is set against the arterial trace: inflation at the dicrotic notch, deflation just before the upstroke; early inflation or late deflation makes the ventricle eject against a full balloon. Read the pressure correctly, since the highest peak on the trace is now augmented diastole. Atrial fibrillation, tachycardia and diathermy interference degrade triggering; switch to pressure triggering in theatre. It is contraindicated with more than mild aortic regurgitation and in dissection. The evidence is unflattering: no mortality benefit in infarct-related shock in the randomised trial. Its remaining roles are ischaemia with a mechanical complication awaiting surgery, and modest support when coming off bypass.

### 10. Microaxial pumps and extracorporeal support
**Why:** When the ventricle cannot sustain a circulation despite inotropes and a balloon, the choice is a pump that takes over the left ventricle's work or a circuit that takes over heart and lungs. After cardiac surgery the circuit can be connected to the cannulae already in the chest or to the femoral vessels.
**Anaesthesia:** A microaxial pump needs echo guidance to place its inlet in the ventricular cavity clear of the mitral apparatus; its alarms for suction mean hypovolaemia, malposition or right ventricular failure. In infarct-related shock it reduced deaths at 180 days, with more device-related adverse events and more renal replacement therapy. Peripheral veno-arterial support returns blood up the aorta against the ventricle's ejection, which has three consequences you must look for. The left ventricle may distend and stop opening the aortic valve, with pulmonary oedema and stasis: watch the arterial pulsatility and the echo, and tell the surgeon, who will add a vent or a microaxial pump. If the heart recovers before the lungs, poorly oxygenated blood from the left ventricle supplies the coronaries and the brain while the circuit supplies the lower body: measure saturation and gases from the right arm. And the cannulated leg needs a distal perfusion cannula. Volatile agents do not reach a patient whose lungs are bypassed; use intravenous anaesthesia and expect drug sequestration in the circuit. Outcomes are sobering: in a multicentre series of post-cardiotomy support after coronary surgery more than half the patients died in hospital.

### 11. Implanting a left ventricular assist device: the echo and the pump
**Why:** Through a sternotomy on bypass, usually with the heart beating, the surgeon cores the left ventricular apex, fixes the inflow cannula of the pump pointing at the mitral valve, sews the outflow graft to the ascending aorta and tunnels the driveline out through the abdominal wall.
**Anaesthesia:** These are patients with end-stage heart failure, often on inotropes, with a defibrillator (therapies off, pads on), and frequently a previous sternotomy. Induce as for the worst ventricle you will meet. The pre-bypass echo answers specific questions that change the operation: a patent foramen ovale must be closed, because unloading the left atrium will otherwise shunt desaturated blood right to left; more than mild aortic regurgitation must be dealt with, because the pump would recirculate its own output through the leak; apical thrombus must be removed; and right ventricular function and tricuspid regurgitation predict how separation from bypass will go. After implantation, confirm the inflow cannula is aligned with the mitral valve and not the septum, and de-air thoroughly before the pump starts, since it will entrain any residual air.

### 12. Coming off bypass on an assist device
**Why:** The pump can only eject what the right ventricle delivers to it through the lungs.
**Anaesthesia:** The right ventricle is the whole problem. Before separation: sinus rhythm or atrioventricular pacing, an inotrope running, pulmonary vascular resistance minimised (oxygenation, normocapnia or mild hypocapnia, no acidosis, inhaled nitric oxide or prostacyclin set up, lungs gently recruited), and systemic pressure sufficient to perfuse the right coronary. Then the pump speed is raised in steps while you watch the septum: it should sit in the midline. A septum bowing to the left with a small left ventricle means the pump is outrunning the right heart or the patient is empty; reduce the speed, give volume, or support the right ventricle further. A septum bowing to the right with a full left ventricle means too little pump or too high an afterload. Continuous-flow devices are sensitive to afterload, so keep the mean pressure moderate, commonly 65-80 mmHg, and remember that the arterial line shows little pulsatility and the cuff and oximeter may not read. Refractory right ventricular failure is treated with a temporary right-sided device, not escalating adrenaline. Bleeding is expected: a long pump run, redo dissection and liver congestion together.

## Pain plan

There is no block on this card in the emergency, and the heading describes the analgesic strategy as a whole. A patient returning to theatre for bleeding or tamponade is sedated with an opioid infusion before, during and after; the plan is to continue it, add intravenous paracetamol, avoid NSAIDs entirely in the presence of bleeding and kidney injury, and use dexmedetomidine to reduce opioid and benzodiazepine exposure as the patient is woken. Needles around a coagulopathic or open chest are not justified by the modest benefit shown for parasternal blocks.

For the elective redo sternotomy and for assist device implantation the sternotomy strategy applies once the chest is closed and dry: paracetamol, opioid by infusion then patient-controlled then oral, and a parasternal block or surgeon infiltration at closure if haemostasis is secure and no early anticoagulation is planned. Device patients start heparin within a day or two, which is the reason the author would not place a catheter. Expect a slower trajectory than a first sternotomy, because the ventilated period hides the first days and deconditioning lengthens the rest; the driveline exit site is a persistent source of discomfort and needs to be immobilised.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Late tamponade, days to weeks after surgery, in a patient now on warfarin: breathless, tired, with a rising creatinine, and an effusion that is often posterior. Treat the induction exactly as in the early case.
- Tamponade from other causes reaches you in the catheter laboratory, after [ablation](electrophysiology-ablation-and-cardioversion.html) or [lead extraction](pacemaker-defibrillator-and-lead-extraction.html), and with [aortic dissection](surgery-of-the-thoracic-aorta-and.html), where draining to a normal pressure is itself dangerous.
- Re-exploration in the intensive care bed saves the transfer and is right for arrest or extremis; lighting, suction, diathermy and blood supply are all worse than in theatre, so move the stable patient.
- Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia in a patient who needs bypass or a device changes the anticoagulant to bivalirudin, with no reversal agent and a different monitoring test.
- Non-cardiac surgery in a patient with a durable assist device: device team present, arterial line, preload maintained, afterload not raised, anticoagulation plan agreed, diathermy interference anticipated. Chest compressions are a late resort because of the risk of dislodging the cannula; check the controller and power first.
- Withdrawal of support is a frequent end point for post-cardiotomy extracorporeal support; the consensus document addresses the ethics, and the family conversation starts when the device goes in.
- Sternal wound infection and dehiscence bring patients back weeks later for debridement and flap closure: a septic, deconditioned patient with an unstable chest wall.

## Key sources

Resuscitation after cardiac surgery rests on a society consensus built from small studies and simulation. Re-entry injury rates come from single-centre series. The balloon pump and the microaxial pump each have a randomised trial in infarct-related shock, with opposite results, and neither trial was done in post-cardiotomy patients. Tamponade induction technique and right ventricular management on an assist device are physiology and expert review, not trial evidence.
