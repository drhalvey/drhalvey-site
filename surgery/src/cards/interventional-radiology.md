## What the surgeon is doing and why

The interventional radiologist reaches a target through a needle hole, guided by fluoroscopy, ultrasound or CT, and then does one of four things to it: blocks a vessel, opens or creates a channel, destroys tissue in place, or leaves a tube. The skin wound is trivial. The physiological event at the target is not, and it is the target, not the access, that sets the anaesthetic.

- Blocking a vessel (embolisation). A catheter is steered from the femoral or radial artery into the branch feeding the problem, and coils, particles, glue or gel foam are released. For haemorrhage (pelvic fracture, splenic and hepatic injury, gastrointestinal bleeding, postpartum haemorrhage) the aim is to stop arterial inflow without an operation. For tumours and fibroids the aim is infarction: uterine artery embolisation starves fibroids, and chemoembolisation delivers cytotoxic-loaded particles into the hepatic artery branch supplying a tumour. Infarcting tissue hurts, and that is the pain on this card.
- Creating a channel (TIPS). A transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt joins a hepatic vein to a portal vein branch through the liver with a covered stent, dropping portal pressure to treat variceal bleeding or refractory ascites. It converts portal hypertension into a sudden increase in venous return to a cirrhotic heart.
- Destroying tissue in place (ablation). A probe is placed in a tumour of liver, kidney, lung or bone under CT or ultrasound and heats it (radiofrequency, microwave) or freezes it (cryoablation). The probe must sit within millimetres of the plan, so the organ must not move with breathing while it is placed.
- Leaving a tube. Percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage and nephrostomy decompress an obstructed, often infected, system. Tunnelled central lines and ports give durable venous access.

Arterial revascularisation, aortic stent grafts and neurointervention share the suite and have their own cards: [peripheral angioplasty and stenting](peripheral-angioplasty-and-stenting.html), [endovascular aneurysm repair](endovascular-aneurysm-repair.html), [endovascular thrombectomy for stroke](endovascular-thrombectomy-for-stroke.html) and [aneurysm clipping and endovascular coiling](aneurysm-clipping-and-endovascular-coiling.html).

The work is called minimally invasive, and the phrase misleads. These patients were sent to radiology because they were too sick, too coagulopathic or too frail for the open operation. And the room is hostile to anaesthesia: a narrow table that rises and slides but does not tilt, a C-arm that needs the space around the head, dim light, radiation, an anaesthetic machine that is old or borrowed, and the nearest colleague and the blood fridge several minutes away ([remote site chapter](../a/a12-remote-site-sedation.html)).

The pain logic: skin puncture is covered by local anaesthetic. Embolisation of a solid organ produces ischaemic visceral pain that starts as the vessel occludes, peaks over the first 12 to 24 hours and comes with nausea and fever (the post-embolisation syndrome). Ablation near the liver capsule or diaphragm gives capsular and shoulder-tip pain for a day or two. Transhepatic tracks cross intercostal spaces and hurt with breathing. Diagnostic angiography, lines and most drains hurt little.

## Steps

### 1. Triage of the request
**Why:** The radiologist books by procedure; the same word, embolisation, covers a day-case fibroid treatment and a patient exsanguinating from a pelvic fracture.
**Anaesthesia:** Sort every request on three axes before agreeing a technique. The patient: bleeding, septic, encephalopathic, unable to lie flat, a child. The procedure: its length, how painful the target is, how still it must be. The room: what is there and how far away help is. Local anaesthesia with nurse-led sedation is right for much of the list. Anaesthetic involvement is for patients who cannot keep still or lie flat, the long and painful procedures, the unstable, and children ([the child](../a/a13-the-child.html)). Check the coagulation results and platelet count the radiologist is working to, the anticoagulant plan ([the vascular patient](../a/a10-vascular-patient.html)), the eGFR and any contrast reaction.

### 2. Setting up the suite
**Why:** The radiologist needs the C-arm to travel from head to groin and rotate round the table, and a sterile field over the access site and the whole of that side.
**Anaesthesia:** Place the machine, pumps and yourself where the C-arm will not hit them, usually at the patient's left shoulder or the foot, with circuit, lines and monitoring cables long enough for the table to slide a metre. Secure everything to the table, not to the floor. Position the arms as the imaging needs, pad them, and keep access to a cannula and the arterial line. The table does not tilt, so regurgitation and hypotension cannot be managed by position. Warm the patient: suites are cold and the cases long. Confirm suction, a defibrillator, and how blood reaches this room.

### 3. Access, angiography, contrast and radiation
**Why:** Arterial access is by common femoral or radial puncture with a sheath left in; runs of contrast are then filmed with subtraction, which removes everything that has not moved between the mask image and the contrast image.
**Anaesthesia:** Subtraction is why the radiologist asks for stillness: under sedation the patient holds their breath on instruction, under general anaesthesia you hold ventilation for each run. Contrast is the drug of the procedure. Its nephrotoxicity has been overstated by uncontrolled studies; the consensus position is intravenous saline prophylaxis for acute kidney injury or an eGFR below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 in patients not on dialysis, and no reason to withhold a needed study above that. Anaphylaxis to contrast is treated as any other, and a previous reaction is discussed before the case. Radiation scatter comes off the patient towards the side opposite the image intensifier, which is where you tend to stand: measured facial dose to the anaesthetist in neurointerventional cases was more than three times the radiologist's and rose with each trip to the patient. Lead glasses, a thyroid shield, a mobile screen and two steps back during runs are the measures.

### 4. Embolisation for haemorrhage
**Why:** Selective occlusion of the bleeding artery stops the haemorrhage without opening a tamponading haematoma, which is why it is preferred for pelvic, retroperitoneal, solid organ and postpartum bleeding when the patient can be kept alive long enough to get there.
**Anaesthesia:** This is a major haemorrhage managed on an angiography table ([major haemorrhage chapter](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html)). Activate the transfusion protocol to the suite, not to theatre. Large-bore access and an arterial line before the drapes, or transduce the radiologist's sheath. Most of these patients need a tracheal tube: they are agitated, hypovolaemic, in pain and cannot keep still, and a rapid sequence induction on the angiography table with theatre-grade assistance is safer than sedation that fails halfway. Resuscitate with blood products, calcium and warmth to a pressure that perfuses without washing out clot until the vessel is occluded. Agree a time limit with the radiologist and surgeon: a patient who is losing ground after a set interval goes to theatre. Obstetric bleeding has its own card: [major obstetric haemorrhage](major-obstetric-haemorrhage-and-placenta-accreta.html).

### 5. Embolisation to infarct: uterus and liver tumour
**Why:** Particles are delivered until flow in the uterine arteries, or the tumour-feeding hepatic branch, stops. The intended result is ischaemic necrosis.
**Anaesthesia:** The procedure itself needs only local anaesthesia and light sedation; the pain begins as flow stops and builds over hours. After uterine artery embolisation, clinically significant post-embolisation syndrome (pain above 5 of 10 with heavy opioid use, fever or repeated antiemetics) occurred in 47.8% of one Australian cohort, with nulliparity the strongest predictor. Start before the vessel is occluded: paracetamol and an NSAID, dexamethasone 8 mg IV, an antiemetic, and opioid by patient-controlled analgesia set up to leave the suite with the patient. The systematic review of analgesic strategies found the superior hypogastric plexus block, placed by the radiologist under fluoroscopy through the anterior abdominal wall, the most consistent single measure, with intra-arterial lidocaine a short-lived alternative. A spinal with intrathecal morphine or an epidural are used in some units and are author's-judgement options for the patient with a previous bad experience. For chemoembolisation, prophylactic dexamethasone reduced abdominal pain, fever and nausea in pooled trials.

### 6. Transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt
**Why:** From the right internal jugular vein a catheter is wedged in a hepatic vein, a long curved needle is passed through liver parenchyma into a portal vein branch, the track is balloon-dilated and a covered stent deployed, and the portosystemic gradient is measured before and after.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with a tracheal tube is the defensible default: the patients are encephalopathic or bleeding with a stomach full of blood, the needle passes and balloon dilatation are painful, and the case averages well over an hour. A matched comparison found less radiation, contrast and fluoroscopy time under general anaesthesia. The patient is a cirrhotic: coagulopathy, low systemic resistance, ascites, altered drug handling ([liver transplantation](liver-transplantation.html) covers the physiology). Three events to anticipate. Wires in the right heart cause ectopy and runs of tachycardia. A needle pass through the liver capsule or into the hepatic artery causes intraperitoneal bleeding that shows only as unexplained hypotension, with nothing visible in the field. And when the shunt opens, portal blood enters the right atrium: in 150 cases portal pressure fell from 30 to 14 mmHg and right atrial pressure rose from 8 to 12 mmHg. A cirrhotic heart with diastolic dysfunction can go into pulmonary oedema within minutes, so run the patient dry until the stent opens. Emergency TIPS for uncontrolled variceal bleeding is all of this in a patient with a balloon tamponade tube and a transfusion running.

### 7. Tumour ablation
**Why:** One or more probes are placed under CT or ultrasound and energy is applied for several minutes per position; treatment margins of a few millimetres decide recurrence, and the lung base, diaphragm, bowel and bile ducts sit just outside the target.
**Anaesthesia:** Practice ranges from local anaesthesia to general anaesthesia and depends on the site. Thermal ablation near the liver capsule or diaphragm is painful during energy delivery, and the radiologist needs the organ in the same place for every scan and probe adjustment. General anaesthesia gives controlled apnoea at a fixed lung volume on request; high-frequency jet ventilation gives a near-static organ throughout and is reported to reduce probe misplacement and radiation, at the cost of equipment, intravenous anaesthesia, and no reliable end-tidal carbon dioxide. Sedation is workable for small peripheral renal or bone lesions. CT guidance means you leave the room for each scan and watch through glass: monitors turned to the window, infusions visible, alarms audible. Radiofrequency current returns through large dispersive pads that can burn the thighs and can interfere with pacemakers. Lung and hepatic dome ablations cause pneumothorax; cryoablation of a large volume can drop the platelet count and temperature; a lesion near the adrenal can produce a hypertensive crisis when heated.

### 8. Percutaneous biliary drainage
**Why:** A needle is passed through the lower right intercostal spaces or the epigastrium, through liver, into a dilated bile duct; a wire is negotiated across the obstruction and a drain or stent left across it.
**Anaesthesia:** Often under-estimated. The track crosses intercostal muscle, parietal peritoneum and liver capsule, and dilating a stricture is very painful, so local anaesthesia with nurse sedation fails often enough that anaesthetic help is requested for the repeat. Deep sedation or general anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway is reasonable; an intercostal or paravertebral block on the right covers the track and the first night. The bile is frequently infected, and injecting contrast into an obstructed system forces bacteria into hepatic veins: rigors and hypotension minutes after the cholangiogram is the septic shower. Antibiotics are given before the puncture, fluid and a vasopressor are ready, and the patient with established cholangitis has an arterial line and a higher-care bed arranged. Jaundiced patients have vitamin K-dependent coagulopathy and vulnerable kidneys.

### 9. Nephrostomy and drainage of collections
**Why:** An obstructed, infected kidney is decompressed by a pigtail drain placed through the flank into a calyx under ultrasound and fluoroscopy; abscesses elsewhere are drained by the same technique under CT or ultrasound.
**Anaesthesia:** Most are done prone or prone-oblique under local anaesthesia, and the anaesthetist is asked for the patient in septic shock, the one who cannot lie prone, and the child. The priority order is resuscitation, antibiotics, then drainage as the source control; do not delay the drain to optimise. Sedating a septic patient prone on a narrow table is a poor choice: either local alone with you managing the circulation, or a tracheal tube. Expect the pressure to fall after decompression as the collecting system empties its contents into the circulation. The elective counterpart through the same track is [percutaneous nephrolithotomy](percutaneous-nephrolithotomy.html).

### 10. Lines and ports
**Why:** A tunnelled cuffed catheter or a subcutaneous port is placed into the internal jugular vein under ultrasound, tunnelled to the chest wall, with the tip positioned at the cavoatrial junction under fluoroscopy.
**Anaesthesia:** Local anaesthesia and light sedation for adults. The patients are those having chemotherapy, dialysis or parenteral nutrition, with poor veins and sometimes a mediastinal mass or superior vena cava obstruction that makes lying flat under sedation dangerous. Air embolism occurs at the moment the dilator is removed from a peel-away sheath in a spontaneously breathing patient; head-down tilt is not available on most tables, so the radiologist pinches the sheath and the patient holds their breath. Wire ectopy is common and brief. Children have these under general anaesthesia.

### 11. Closure, transfer and recovery
**Why:** The arterial sheath is removed with a closure device or manual pressure, and the patient must then lie flat with the leg straight for two to six hours to protect the puncture.
**Anaesthesia:** Emergence should be smooth: coughing and straining against a fresh femoral puncture produce a groin or retroperitoneal haematoma, and the second presents as hypotension and flank pain an hour later. Extubate deep or use remifentanil to the end. Radiology recovery areas vary; a patient who has had a general anaesthetic goes to a staffed post-anaesthesia unit, which may mean a monitored transfer through corridors. Hand over the contrast volume, heparin given, sheath site, the limb to watch, and for embolisation the analgesia already running.

## Pain plan

The plan depends on the target, and the default assumption that a needle procedure is painless is the commonest error. Three tiers.

Minimal (angiography, lines, most drains, small ablations away from capsule): the radiologist's local infiltration and paracetamol.

Moderate (liver and renal ablation, biliary drainage, chemoembolisation): paracetamol, an NSAID if kidneys and platelets allow, dexamethasone, and oral opioid for one to two days. A right-sided intercostal or paravertebral block is a reasonable addition for transhepatic tracks and subcapsular liver ablation; that is author's judgement from the anatomy and not trial evidence.

Severe, time-limited (uterine artery embolisation, large-volume tumour embolisation): the mechanism is ischaemia, peaking in the first day. Pre-emptive paracetamol, NSAID, dexamethasone and antiemetic, opioid patient-controlled analgesia overnight, and where the radiologist offers it a superior hypogastric plexus block, which has the most consistent evidence. Pain falls steeply on day 2 and the patient goes home on simple analgesia.

Escalation: pain that rises after day 2 with fever is infection of infarcted tissue or non-target embolisation, not post-embolisation syndrome. Pleuritic pain after ablation is a pneumothorax or pleural effusion. Flank or groin pain with hypotension is a retroperitoneal bleed from the puncture.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The procedure that outgrows its sedation. Converting to general anaesthesia mid-case on an angiography table with a sheath in the artery is the hardest version of the task. If the case is long, painful or needs stillness, start with the airway secured.
- Radial access. Leaves the groin alone, the patient sits up sooner, and the arm you wanted for the arterial line belongs to the radiologist.
- Carbon dioxide angiography. Used instead of iodinated contrast in renal impairment and allergy, below the diaphragm only; gas trapping in the right heart causes transient hypotension.
- Contrast reaction history. Use of a different agent and a plan agreed with the radiologist; steroid premedication is practised widely and does not replace readiness to treat anaphylaxis.
- Pregnancy testing and shielding. The abdomen and pelvis are in the beam for most of this card.
- Non-target embolisation. Particles reaching the gut, gallbladder, spinal cord or skin cause ischaemic pain in the wrong place; pain that does not match the target is reported to the radiologist at once.
- Encephalopathy after TIPS. Develops over days; sedatives given for the procedure are blamed for it and last longer than expected in a cirrhotic.
- Tumour lysis and carcinoid crisis. Embolising or ablating a large neuroendocrine deposit can release mediators; octreotide is drawn up for known carcinoid.
- Children. Almost everything is a general anaesthetic, radiation dose matters more, and contrast and fluid volumes are counted per kilogram.
