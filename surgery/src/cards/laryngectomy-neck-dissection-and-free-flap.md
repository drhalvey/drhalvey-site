## What the surgeon is doing and why

Squamous cancer of the larynx, pharynx and mouth spreads locally into the structures of speech and swallowing and regionally to the lymph nodes of the neck. Surgery with curative intent therefore has three parts, done at one sitting by two or three teams: remove the primary with a margin, remove the nodal basins at risk, and rebuild what is left so that the patient can swallow, breathe and, if possible, speak.

A total laryngectomy removes the larynx from the hyoid to the upper tracheal rings. The pharynx is opened and then closed again as a tube in front of the spine; the trachea is transected and brought out to the skin as a permanent end stoma. From that moment the airway and the food passage are separate for life: the mouth and nose no longer connect to the lungs. A puncture between trachea and oesophagus for a speaking valve is often made at the same time. Pharyngolaryngectomy takes the hypopharynx as well and needs a flap to restore the conduit.

A neck dissection removes the fat and nodes between the fascial layers of the lateral neck, level by level, peeling them off the carotid sheath, the internal jugular vein, the accessory nerve and the floor of the posterior triangle. A selective dissection keeps all three of those structures; older radical operations sacrificed them. The surgeon works for an hour or two per side along the carotid bulb, the vagus, the phrenic nerve on scalenus anterior, the hypoglossal nerve and, low on the left, the thoracic duct.

For the mouth and oropharynx, resection of part of the tongue, floor of mouth or mandible leaves a hole that cannot be closed directly. A free flap fills it: skin and fascia from the forearm on the radial artery, skin and muscle from the lateral thigh on a perforator of the descending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery, or fibula with a skin paddle to replace mandible. The flap is detached with its artery and vein, carried to the neck and joined to a branch of the external carotid and a tributary of the internal jugular under the microscope. From then on the transferred tissue has no nerve supply, no lymphatics and a single arterial inflow, and its survival depends on flow through two vessels a couple of millimetres across.

The pain logic surprises people. A neck dissection divides the sensory branches of the cervical plexus, so much of the wound is numb and nothing is repaired under tension; the neck feels tight and heavy more than painful. The mucosal suture line hurts on swallowing but the patient is not swallowing. The donor site, especially a thigh skin graft donor area or a fibula osteotomy, is commonly the worst wound. Overall this is a score of 3 on a multimodal regimen: moderate, lasting days, with shoulder pain from accessory nerve handling as the long-term complaint.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-assessment
**Why:** The surgeon is offering a ten-hour operation to a patient whose cancer shares its causes, tobacco and alcohol, with coronary, lung and liver disease.
**Anaesthesia:** Look for the things that change the plan. Functional capacity and cardiorespiratory disease. Alcohol intake, with a withdrawal plan started before surgery, since delirium on day 2 pulls out tracheostomy tubes and tears anastomoses. Nutrition: dysphagia and weight loss are usual; the enhanced recovery consensus recommends correcting what can be corrected, carbohydrate loading and avoiding prolonged fasting. Anaemia. Previous radiotherapy, which stiffens the neck, fixes the larynx, makes mask ventilation and front-of-neck access harder, causes hypothyroidism (check thyroid function) and can leave carotid baroreceptor dysfunction. For a radial forearm flap, the surgeon has chosen an arm and tested its ulnar collateral supply: no cannula or arterial line goes in that limb from now on.

### 2. The airway plan
**Why:** The tumour sits in or beside the airway and the surgeon has looked at it with a nasendoscope within days.
**Anaesthesia:** Review that examination and the CT together. The questions are whether the glottis can be seen, whether a tube will pass it, whether the tumour is friable and will bleed, and whether mask ventilation and a front-of-neck rescue are feasible. The answers sort patients into three groups. Most laryngectomy patients with a fixed cord but an adequate lumen are intubated asleep with a videolaryngoscope and a small tube. Bulky supraglottic or tongue base tumours, trismus and irradiated necks get an awake tracheal intubation, which the Difficult Airway Society guideline describes as high in success and underused. A stridulous patient or a tumour that fills the glottis gets a tracheostomy under local anaesthesia first: see [tracheostomy](tracheostomy.html). For oral cavity resections a nasal tube keeps the mouth clear until the tracheostomy. The framework is in [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html).

### 3. Lines and set-up for a long day
**Why:** Two teams will work at once on the neck and a limb for most of a day.
**Anaesthesia:** Arterial line in a non-donor limb. Venous access away from the neck: two large peripheral cannulae with extensions, or a femoral or subclavian central line when vasopressor infusion or poor veins demand one. Urinary catheter, oesophageal or bladder temperature, forced-air and fluid warming from the start, and a nasogastric tube placed by you or under vision by the surgeon. Pad everything: heels, sacrum, elbows, occiput. Protect the eyes with waterproof dressings. Secure the tube without circumferential tape. Give antibiotics and dexamethasone. Maintenance is by preference: propofol TIVA or volatile, with remifentanil to provide stillness without relaxant.

### 4. Tracheostomy or tracheal transection
**Why:** For oral resections a tracheostomy is made early to move the airway out of the field. In laryngectomy the trachea is divided later, as the larynx comes out, and its end becomes the stoma.
**Anaesthesia:** The tracheostomy sequence, with oxygen reduction and cuff protection before the trachea is opened, is as on the tracheostomy card. In laryngectomy the surgeon tells you when the trachea is about to be cut: withdraw the oral tube as a sterile reinforced or preformed tube is placed into the distal trachea and passed over the drapes to you. Confirm capnography and bilateral ventilation each time, because the carina is close and the tube is short. The tube is changed again at the end for the definitive stoma tube or left out altogether. Whether a tracheostomy is needed after oral reconstruction is itself a judgement: scoring systems weigh the site of the defect, bilateral neck dissection, flap bulk and previous radiotherapy.

### 5. Neck dissection
**Why:** Nodal tissue is cleared off the great vessels and nerves from the mandible to the clavicle.
**Anaesthesia:** Four things belong to you. Carotid sinus traction produces sudden bradycardia and hypotension; it stops when the surgeon stops, and lidocaine infiltrated around the bulb by the surgeon prevents recurrence. The accessory, marginal mandibular and hypoglossal nerves are identified by stimulation, so no relaxant after the intubating dose. A hole in the internal jugular vein with the head up entrains air: flood the field, level the table. And venous drainage of the head: with one internal jugular tied the other copes, but bilateral ligation, or a tight closure, raises intracranial pressure and swells the face and airway. Keep 15-20 degrees head up and the head near neutral. A milky fluid low in the left neck is a chyle leak, which the surgeon may ask you to help show with a sustained inflation.

### 6. Resection of the primary
**Why:** The larynx is freed from the pharynx and removed, or the oral tumour is excised with tongue, floor of mouth or a segment of mandible.
**Anaesthesia:** Blood loss is steady, not sudden, and hard to see in swabs and under drapes; send a blood gas with haemoglobin every hour or two. Aim for a haemoglobin that carries oxygen without the viscosity that slows flow in a flap: most units transfuse below about 70-80 g/L and do not chase higher. Keep the patient warm. Core temperature drifts over hours with a large exposed field and two teams.

### 7. Flap harvest
**Why:** A second team raises the flap on its pedicle while the resection proceeds, and leaves it perfusing in place until the neck vessels are ready.
**Anaesthesia:** A forearm or leg tourniquet is inflated for an hour or more: note the time and expect the usual rise in pressure and the fall on release. The donor limb is not available for monitoring. The moment the pedicle is divided starts the flap's ischaemia time, which the surgeons will want kept short; have the patient at their best haemodynamically by then, not catching up afterwards.

### 8. Microvascular anastomosis and reperfusion
**Why:** Artery and vein are sutured under the microscope, the clamps come off and the flap pinks up.
**Anaesthesia:** Flow through a denervated flap is passive: it follows perfusion pressure and falls with vasoconstriction in the feeding artery. The targets are a normal blood pressure for that patient (a mean within about 20% of baseline), normothermia, a full but not overloaded circulation, good analgesia and a normal carbon dioxide. Fluid is the lever that is easy to overuse: oedema in a flap with no lymphatics does harm, and a meta-analysis of three randomised trials found fewer flaps at risk and fewer reoperations with goal-directed than with conventional fluid therapy. When pressure is low and stroke volume is not fluid-responsive, use a vasopressor. The old prohibition is not supported: in a meta-analysis of 8,427 flaps vasopressor use was associated with a lower risk of flap failure (relative risk 0.70). An infusion of noradrenaline or metaraminol, started early and run steadily, is better than boluses. The surgeon may ask for heparin before the clamps are applied.

### 9. Inset and closure
**Why:** The flap is sutured into the defect, the pharynx closed in layers, suction drains placed and the neck flaps closed over the pedicle.
**Anaesthesia:** Any movement now pulls on the anastomosis. Keep depth and remifentanil steady through closure. Start the transition analgesia: paracetamol 1 g IV, a long-acting opioid titrated in, and the NSAID if the surgeon agrees. Check that nothing encircles the neck: the tracheostomy tube is sutured to the skin, not tied with tapes over the pedicle, and oxygen tubing and gown ties are kept clear.

### 10. Emergence
**Why:** The surgeon needs the first hours to be quiet: no coughing, straining, hypertension or retching.
**Anaesthesia:** The enhanced recovery approach is to wake the patient at the end where the day has gone well, breathing through the tracheostomy, since sedation overnight brings hypotension, vasopressor and delirium of its own. Wake on a low-rate remifentanil infusion or with dexmedetomidine, sitting 30 degrees up, with the head neutral or turned gently towards the side of the pedicle as the surgeon directs. Delay waking only for a specific reason: hypothermia, large transfusion, an unstable patient or a tenuous flap. Dexmedetomidine during these operations has been associated with less delirium in the first five days.

### 11. The first 72 hours
**Why:** Most flap failures are venous and occur early; a compromised flap can be saved only by a prompt return to theatre.
**Anaesthesia:** Flap checks are hourly: colour, capillary refill, temperature, Doppler signal. A pale, cool flap with no bleeding on pinprick is arterial; a blue, swollen, briskly dark-bleeding flap is venous. Either means theatre within the hour, for a patient who was anaesthetised for ten hours yesterday, with a fresh tracheostomy that makes the airway easy and a neck that may be full of haematoma. Maintain the same haemodynamic targets. For a laryngectomy patient the bedhead sign must say that the patient is a neck breather with no upper airway: oxygen goes to the stoma, and nobody attempts oral intubation.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a large skin and soft tissue dissection in a partly denervated field plus a donor site, so the plan is systemic and multimodal for the neck with optional regional cover for the donor limb. The enhanced recovery consensus recommends opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia; a before-and-after cohort study of such a protocol reported about a ninth of the postoperative opioid use of the previous regimen (median 10.0 against 89.6 morphine equivalents), with better pain scores. NSAIDs are the contested element: surgeons vary, weighing haematoma against the flap's interest in an opioid-sparing, well-perfused patient, and a COX-2 inhibitor is the usual compromise.

The defensible default is paracetamol regularly by intravenous then enteral route, a COX-2 inhibitor from day 0 or day 1 as the surgeon allows, a patient-controlled opioid for 24-48 hours converting to enteral doses down the feeding tube, and a block or infiltration for the donor site: fascia iliaca or lateral femoral cutaneous nerve block for the thigh, infiltration and a non-adherent dressing for a skin graft donor area, a single-shot popliteal block for a fibula if the surgeons are content to assess the foot later. Low-dose ketamine or dexmedetomidine helps the opioid-tolerant and the heavy drinker. There is no PROSPECT guideline for this surgery, and the block choices are author's judgement.

Expect worst pain of 3-4 out of 10 on days 1 and 2, mostly from the donor site and from tube suctioning, falling by the end of the first week. Inadequate analgesia matters beyond comfort because pain causes vasoconstriction. Escalating neck pain with swelling is a haematoma compressing the pedicle. A stiff, aching shoulder weeks later is accessory nerve dysfunction and needs physiotherapy.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Flap take-back. The emergency variant: return to theatre for a failing flap or a neck haematoma, usually within three days. The airway is the tracheostomy; the urgency is real; the same flap physiology applies.
- Neck haematoma without a tracheostomy. After laryngectomy the stoma protects the airway. After a neck dissection alone it does not, and the mechanism and management are those of the thyroid haematoma: open the wound, then secure the airway. See [thyroidectomy and parathyroidectomy](thyroidectomy-and-parathyroidectomy.html).
- Salvage surgery after chemoradiotherapy. Harder airway, more bleeding, poor healing and a high rate of pharyngocutaneous fistula; a pectoralis major pedicled flap is often added, which brings a chest wall wound.
- Carotid blowout. Late, in irradiated or fistulated necks: a herald bleed, then catastrophe. Pressure, large-bore access, the massive transfusion protocol and interventional radiology or theatre: [major haemorrhage, cell salvage and coagulation](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html).
- Alcohol withdrawal and delirium. Common on days 2-4 and dangerous to tubes and flaps. Prophylaxis from admission; avoid long benzodiazepine infusions otherwise.
- Bilateral neck dissection. Facial and laryngeal oedema and raised intracranial pressure if both internal jugular veins are compromised; a strong argument for a covering tracheostomy.
- Laryngectomy without a flap. Shorter, with the same airway sequence and stoma issues, and a pain score nearer 2.
- Hypothyroidism and hypocalcaemia. After laryngectomy with thyroid lobectomy or total thyroidectomy, check calcium from the first day.
- Free flaps at other sites share this physiology: see [free flap breast reconstruction (DIEP)](free-flap-breast-reconstruction.html).

## Key sources

The perioperative package is an Enhanced Recovery After Surgery Society consensus whose authors state that much of the evidence is borrowed from other surgery. The vasopressor meta-analysis is of observational studies and is open to confounding by indication, though its direction is the opposite of the traditional teaching. Goal-directed fluid evidence in this population is three small trials. The analgesic data are a before-and-after cohort. Blood pressure, haemoglobin and temperature targets, the choice of central access, the indications for awake intubation and for delayed emergence, and the donor-site block choices are consensus and author's judgement.
