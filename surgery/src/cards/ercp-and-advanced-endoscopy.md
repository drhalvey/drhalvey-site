## What the surgeon is doing and why

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography reaches the bile duct and pancreatic duct from inside the duodenum. The endoscopist passes a side-viewing duodenoscope to the second part of the duodenum, faces the papilla of Vater, and threads a guidewire and catheter up through it into the duct the indication calls for. Contrast under fluoroscopy outlines the duct. Then the therapeutic step: a sphincterotomy (a diathermy cut through the roof of the papilla) to open the outlet, a balloon or basket to sweep out stones, a plastic or metal stent across a stricture, brushings or cholangioscopy for a diagnosis. It replaced a laparotomy and a duct exploration, and its indications are now almost all therapeutic: stones, cholangitis, malignant obstruction, bile leak (the surgical alternative for stones is on the [cholecystectomy card](laparoscopic-cholecystectomy-and-bile-duct-exploration.html)).

Three features of the procedure set the anaesthetic. The scope is larger and stiffer than a gastroscope and sits in the pharynx for 30 to 90 minutes, not five. The endoscopist needs the papilla still, because cannulation is a millimetre task and every failed pass at the pancreatic orifice raises the pancreatitis risk; a patient who retches or bucks moves the duodenum off the scope. And the patient is prone or semi-prone on a fluoroscopy table, because that position lays the duodenoscope in its short, stable configuration and puts the ducts in profile for the image intensifier. Prone, head turned, in a darkened room with a C-arm over the chest, is the worst position in which to rescue an airway.

The patients are also sicker than a routine scope list. Jaundice, cholangitis, pancreatic cancer, cirrhosis and recent pancreatitis are the indications. Many are elderly, some are septic, and biliary or duodenal obstruction means the stomach may not be empty.

The other procedures on this card share the room and the reasoning. Endoscopic ultrasound uses an echoendoscope with a rigid tip for staging, needle biopsy and drainage of pancreatic collections into the stomach. Enteroscopy is a long, uncomfortable push through the small bowel. Endoscopic submucosal dissection removes early cancers in one piece over one to three hours. Peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) treats achalasia by tunnelling in the oesophageal submucosa and dividing the circular muscle from inside; the wall is deliberately breached, so gas escapes into the mediastinum and peritoneum by design.

The pain logic: nothing is incised that has somatic innervation, so the procedure itself leaves bloating and a sore throat. The pain that matters is a complication. Post-ERCP pancreatitis is the commonest, driven by papillary oedema and pancreatic duct instrumentation, and it declares itself as epigastric pain within hours. The plan below is therefore about preventing and recognising that pain, not treating a predictable curve.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment: who gets a tube
**Why:** The endoscopist's requirement is 30 to 90 minutes of a motionless duodenum; how that is delivered is your decision, and it is made on the patient, the procedure and the room.
**Anaesthesia:** Deep propofol sedation with a natural airway works for most fit patients: in 799 advanced procedures under deep sedation, hypoxaemia occurred in 12.8% and airway manoeuvres were needed in 14.4%, but nobody required bag-mask ventilation or a tube. The risk is concentrated. In a randomised trial restricted to high-risk patients (obesity, sleep apnoea by STOP-BANG, ascites, chronic lung disease, ASA above 3), sedation-related adverse events occurred in 51.5% under sedation against 9.9% with a tracheal tube, one in ten of the sedation group had to be converted mid-procedure, and the tube cost no time in the room or in recovery. In registry data across 61,735 cases the trade is plain: general anaesthesia gives less hypoxaemia and more hypotension. The defensible rule is a tracheal tube for any of the trial's risk factors, for a possibly full stomach (gastric outlet or duodenal obstruction, a recent meal, GLP-1 receptor agonist without the clear-fluid day), for sepsis, and for the planned long case. That rule is evidence for the high-risk group and author's judgement beyond it.

### 2. The room and the radiation
**Why:** ERCP needs fluoroscopy, so it happens in a radiology or endoscopy fluoroscopy room, with the C-arm, the scope stack and two or three assistants around the patient's head and chest.
**Anaesthesia:** You are at the foot or the side, behind lead, with long lines and a long circuit. Bring what the room lacks: an anaesthetic machine or at least a means of ventilating with oxygen, capnography, suction of your own, a second trolley beside the table to turn the patient onto if the airway is lost. Agree aloud before starting who will help turn the patient supine. Wear lead and a thyroid collar and stand back during screening. The general rules for a remote site are in [the remote site chapter](../a/a12-remote-site-sedation.html).

### 3. Induction and positioning
**Why:** The duodenoscope sits best with the patient prone or semi-prone; some endoscopists accept left lateral or supine, with a longer scope position and a less familiar image.
**Anaesthesia:** For general anaesthesia, intubate supine on the trolley and turn onto the table: reinforced or well-secured tube, eyes taped and padded, chest and pelvis supported so the abdomen hangs free, neck not rotated beyond comfort, arms in the swimmer's position without stretch on the brachial plexus. For sedation, the patient positions themselves awake, which protects nerves and neck, and oxygen goes on before the first dose. High-flow nasal oxygen is the right default for prone sedation: in a randomised trial of prone ERCP, hypoxaemia occurred in none of the high-flow group against 19% with ordinary cannulae, and only the cannula group needed the procedure interrupted for airway rescue. Capnography sampling is unreliable under high flow, so watch the abdomen and chest move.

### 4. Passing the duodenoscope
**Why:** The side-viewing scope is passed semi-blind through the pharynx and oesophagus, then through the pylorus, and shortened along the lesser curve until it faces the papilla.
**Anaesthesia:** Pharyngeal intubation is the peak stimulus and wants a bolus on top of the infusion; traversing the pylorus and shortening the scope distends the stomach and can provoke retching in the under-sedated patient. Under sedation this is where laryngospasm and regurgitation occur. Ask for gastric fluid to be suctioned as the scope passes through the stomach. With a tube in, check the cuff and the tube position again after the scope is down, because the scope drags on it.

### 5. Cannulation
**Why:** A wire is steered into the bile duct through an orifice a few millimetres across. Repeated attempts and inadvertent pancreatic duct cannulation are what cause pancreatitis, so the endoscopist wants a still target and may give hyoscine butylbromide or glucagon to stop duodenal peristalsis.
**Anaesthesia:** Depth must be even. A propofol infusion (target-controlled or manual) with small opioid increments or a low-dose remifentanil infusion does this better than boluses. Hyoscine butylbromide 20 mg IV produces a tachycardia that matters in the patient with coronary disease or atrial fibrillation; glucagon raises glucose. This phase can be long and unstimulating, which is when sedated patients drift into apnoea.

### 6. Cholangiogram and sphincterotomy
**Why:** Contrast confirms the anatomy and the pathology. The sphincterotome then cuts the papilla with blended current to open the duct for stone extraction or stent passage.
**Anaesthesia:** Contrast injected into an infected, obstructed duct under pressure pushes bacteria into the bloodstream: the patient with cholangitis can become hypotensive and rigor within minutes, and the treatment is drainage, antibiotics already given, fluid and a vasopressor. Diathermy matters for implanted cardiac devices. Bleeding from the sphincterotomy is usually trivial; in the anticoagulated or coagulopathic patient it is not, and it can present hours later as melaena.

### 7. Stone extraction, stenting and cholangioscopy
**Why:** Balloons and baskets sweep the duct; large stones are crushed mechanically or fragmented by laser or electrohydraulic lithotripsy under direct vision through a cholangioscope passed down the duodenoscope; stents are placed across strictures or leaks.
**Anaesthesia:** This is when gas embolism happens. With precordial Doppler on 843 ERCPs, venous gas embolism was detected in 2.4%, half with haemodynamic change, all during therapeutic steps and most often during stent exchange and cholangioscopy, where gas or irrigant is delivered under pressure into ducts lying against portal and hepatic veins. A sudden fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide with hypotension or desaturation during these steps is embolism until proven otherwise: tell the endoscopist to stop insufflating and decompress, give 100% oxygen, support the circulation, and turn supine for resuscitation if needed. Carbon dioxide insufflation is the mitigation, because it dissolves; confirm it is in use before the case, not after the event. Paradoxical embolism through a patent foramen ovale gives a patient who does not wake.

### 8. Advanced variants: ultrasound drainage, dissection and POEM
**Why:** Endoscopic ultrasound-guided drainage and necrosectomy empty an infected pancreatic collection into the stomach. Submucosal dissection is slow, meticulous knife work in a gas-distended stomach or colon. POEM tunnels in the oesophageal wall and cuts muscle, so carbon dioxide tracks into the mediastinum, pleura and peritoneum as an expected consequence.
**Anaesthesia:** Necrosectomy floods the stomach with pus and debris and needs a tracheal tube. Submucosal dissection is a long general anaesthetic or a carefully run deep sedation in selected patients. POEM is always a general anaesthetic with a tracheal tube and neuromuscular block, supine, and the achalasia patient is a full oesophagus whatever the fasting time: clear fluids for a day or more beforehand, rapid sequence induction head-up, and many endoscopists clear the oesophagus under sedation first. During the tunnel expect end-tidal carbon dioxide and airway pressure to climb together. Increase minute ventilation; palpate the abdomen; a tense capnoperitoneum that impairs ventilation or venous return is vented with a needle in the upper abdomen, and a pneumothorax with haemodynamic effect is drained. Subcutaneous emphysema of the neck is common and resolves; check for a cuff leak before extubation if it is marked.

### 9. Withdrawal, emergence and turning
**Why:** The endoscopist decompresses the stomach on the way out; a rectal dose of indometacin 100 mg is given at the end of the case to reduce pancreatitis.
**Anaesthesia:** Rectal indometacin is the endoscopist's prophylaxis and should not be forgotten or doubled: in high-risk patients it reduced pancreatitis from 16.9% to 9.2%. It is an NSAID dose, so do not add another and withhold it in significant renal impairment. Turn supine before extubation unless you are confident of the airway prone. The sedated patient turns themselves when awake. Give the antiemetic if opioid was used.

### 10. Recovery and the four-hour question
**Why:** The endoscopist wants to know by the end of the afternoon who has pancreatitis, a perforation or a bleed, because each has a different pathway and all three are easier to manage early.
**Anaesthesia:** Simple analgesia covers the expected course. Epigastric pain radiating to the back with vomiting is pancreatitis until the lipase says otherwise: admit, fluid resuscitation with a balanced crystalloid, opioid analgesia, no discharge. Severe pain with a rigid abdomen, or neck and chest pain with emphysema, is perforation and needs imaging. Rigors and hypotension after drainage of an infected system is bacteraemia and may need critical care. Day-case discharge is for the patient who is pain-free and eating at four hours.

## Pain plan

No block has a role. The mechanism is transient visceral distension with no somatic injury, so the expected course needs paracetamol and nothing else, and the analgesic strategy is really the anaesthetic strategy: enough depth for a still papilla, by infusion, with opioid kept low so the patient breathes during sedation and is not nauseated afterwards.

The one drug with trial evidence against post-procedure pain is rectal indometacin, and it works by preventing the complication, not by analgesia. Aggressive peri-procedural hydration with a balanced crystalloid is widely practised for the same purpose; it is stated here as common practice and not sourced, and it is not suitable for the patient with heart failure.

Escalation: pain scored above 4 at any time after the first hour is investigated, not treated and discharged. Opioid for established pancreatitis is appropriate and should not be withheld on the old belief about sphincter spasm.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Sedation or general anaesthesia by default. Units vary from almost all sedation to almost all intubated. Both are defensible for the low-risk patient; for the high-risk patient the randomised evidence favours the tube, and the registry signal is that the cost is hypotension.
- Cholangitis. A septic patient having a source-control procedure: arterial line, vasopressor running, tracheal tube, antibiotics before the scope, and a critical care bed asked for beforehand.
- The altered stomach. Previous gastrectomy or gastric bypass means a long enteroscope route or a laparoscopic-assisted approach in theatre, and a long anaesthetic.
- Left lateral or supine ERCP. Some endoscopists accept it; it makes airway rescue easier and the cannulation harder. Useful for the patient with a tracheostomy, a large abdomen, late pregnancy or an unstable neck.
- Pregnancy. Left lateral tilt, minimal fluoroscopy with shielding, and a tube after the first trimester.
- Gas where it should not be. Embolism, capnoperitoneum and pneumothorax all present as a change in end-tidal carbon dioxide and airway pressure before they present as hypotension. The capnograph is the monitor for the complication as well as for the airway.
- Contrast allergy. Contrast in the duct is absorbed in small amounts; reactions are rare, and a history of anaphylaxis to iodinated contrast is still worth a conversation with the endoscopist.
- The frail patient with malignant obstruction. The stent is palliative and the anaesthetic should be proportionate: sometimes the right answer is light sedation, left lateral and a short procedure.
- Simple endoscopy next door. The routine scope list has its own card: [gastroscopy and colonoscopy](gastroscopy-and-colonoscopy.html).

## Key sources

The airway decision has one good single-centre randomised trial in high-risk patients and a very large registry study, and they agree. The oxygen evidence for prone sedation is one small randomised trial. The gas embolism incidence comes from a single prospective Doppler cohort and is higher than most clinicians assume. Rectal indometacin is supported by a large placebo-controlled trial. Anaesthesia for POEM rests on case series and one review; the management of capnoperitoneum described here is that review's and standard practice, not trial evidence.
