## What the surgeon is doing and why

Coronary disease that matters surgically is a fixed narrowing in the first few centimetres of an epicardial artery, with a usable vessel beyond it. The operation does not touch the narrowing. It brings a new source of blood to the artery downstream, so that the muscle beyond the stenosis is fed whatever the plaque does next. Everything else in the case is a means to that end: getting to the heart, finding something to use as a pipe, and holding the target still and bloodless for the ten minutes it takes to sew a 1.5 mm anastomosis.

The pipes are chosen for how long they last. The left internal mammary artery stays attached to the subclavian at its origin and is swung down onto the left anterior descending artery; it is the graft that changes survival and it is used in almost every case. The rest are free grafts with one end on the aorta or on the mammary: radial artery from the non-dominant forearm, long saphenous vein from the leg (open or endoscopic), or the right internal mammary. Using both mammary arteries did not improve 10-year survival in the one large trial and devascularises both halves of the sternum, so surgeons are selective.

There are two ways to make the target still. On pump, the heart is drained, the aorta is clamped and cardioplegia stops the heart in diastole, so the surgeon sews on a flaccid, empty, bloodless organ and the pump perfuses everything else (see [cardiopulmonary bypass and cardiac essentials](../a/a07-cardiopulmonary-bypass.html)). Off pump, the heart keeps beating and keeps supplying the body while a suction stabiliser holds a few square centimetres of it still; the price is that the heart has to be lifted and rotated to reach the back wall, and you have to keep a circulation going through a heart that is folded over. The largest trial found no difference in death, stroke, infarction or dialysis at 30 days or five years; off pump bought less transfusion, less re-exploration and less kidney injury and paid with slightly more early repeat revascularisation. The choice is the surgeon's, with a calcified aorta the strongest patient-level reason to stay off pump and off the aorta altogether.

The pain logic is the sternotomy. The saw cut itself is rigidly wired and hurts less than its reputation; the pain comes from spreading the two halves, which levers every rib at its posterior joint and stretches the intercostal nerves, and from the mammary harvest, which strips the pleura and fascia off the back of the left costal cartilages with diathermy. Add drains through the upper abdominal wall and a leg or forearm wound. The result is a moderate, diffuse, movement-evoked and cough-evoked pain that peaks on days 1 and 2 as sedation and intra-operative opioid wear off and the patient is asked to breathe deeply and sit out. It sits at a 4 on the book's scale because coughing on a divided sternum is unavoidable for days, not because the peak is extreme.

## Steps

### 1. Lines, induction and the pre-bypass window
**Why:** Nothing surgical has happened yet, but this is where the coronary patient is most exposed. Supply is fixed by the stenoses; demand is set by heart rate, wall tension and contractility, all of which you are about to disturb.
**Anaesthesia:** Arterial line awake, on the side that is not having its radial artery taken. Induce slowly with whatever you can titrate; the drug matters less than the haemodynamics, and a large trial of volatile against intravenous maintenance found no difference in mortality or infarction. The targets are a heart rate of 50-70 per minute, a diastolic pressure that perfuses the coronaries (vasopressor running before induction, not after the fall), and no surge at laryngoscopy. Left main stenosis and severe ventricular impairment are the patients in whom a perfusionist and a scrubbed surgeon in the room at induction is reasonable. The baseline echo records ventricular function, regional wall motion to compare against later, the mitral valve, and aortic atheroma that changes where the surgeon will cannulate and clamp.

### 2. Sternotomy
**Why:** A midline split of the sternum gives access to the whole anterior mediastinum, both mammary arteries and the aorta, and heals as a bone held by wires.
**Anaesthesia:** The saw is the largest stimulus before bypass; deepen and give opioid beforehand because hypertension and tachycardia here are ischaemia in a patient with no pump yet. Disconnect or pause ventilation as the saw passes so the pleura and lungs fall away from the blade. A previous sternotomy changes this step completely (see [resternotomy, tamponade and mechanical circulatory support](resternotomy-tamponade-and-mechanical-circulatory-support.html)).

### 3. Conduit harvest
**Why:** The left hemi-sternum is lifted on a mammary retractor so the surgeon can dissect the artery off the chest wall from the first rib to its bifurcation, while an assistant harvests vein or radial artery. This is 30-45 minutes in which the heart is untouched.
**Anaesthesia:** The table goes up and rolls to the left, and the retractor pulls the left clavicle and first rib up: this is where brachial plexus stretch happens, so keep the arms neutral and the retractor no higher than the surgeon needs. Smaller tidal volumes keep the left lung out of the field. Stimulation is low, so pressure sags; this is the quiet period to send baseline blood gas and activated clotting time, start tranexamic acid, and place a block if you are doing one before heparin. The surgeon will ask for heparin before dividing the distal mammary, because a clamped pedicle clots.

### 4. Heparin and the activated clotting time
**Why:** Blood in contact with a bypass circuit clots within minutes without full anticoagulation, and the result is fatal. Even off pump, a coronary snared for an anastomosis needs anticoagulated blood either side of it.
**Anaesthesia:** Heparin 300-400 units/kg IV into a central lumen with blood aspirated before and after, announced aloud. Check the activated clotting time after three minutes; the conventional threshold to start bypass is above 480 s, and the society guideline is candid that the number rests on old and limited data. A poor response is usually antithrombin depletion from pre-operative heparin: more heparin first, then antithrombin concentrate or plasma. Off-pump surgeons vary between a full dose and half of it with a lower target. Nobody cannulates on an unmeasured clotting time; say the number aloud.

### 5. Cannulation
**Why:** A purse-string in the distal ascending aorta takes the arterial cannula; a two-stage cannula through the right atrial appendage drains both cavae. The aorta goes first, so that the pump can transfuse if the atrium tears.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon wants a systolic pressure of 90-100 mmHg for the aortic stab, because cannulating a tense aorta risks dissection, and wants it back up again afterwards. Atrial handling causes ectopy, atrial fibrillation and a drop in filling; a cold patient in a new atrial fibrillation with critical coronaries is cardioverted directly by the surgeon or put on bypass, not managed with drugs. Watch the arterial line as the perfusionist tests the aortic cannula: a high line pressure with a swinging trace is a dissection or a cannula against the wall.

### 6. Going on bypass
**Why:** Venous blood drains by gravity to the reservoir, is oxygenated and pumped back to the aorta. Once full flow is reached the heart is empty and the lungs are redundant.
**Anaesthesia:** Run through the same items every time: clotting time adequate, anaesthesia continuing by a route that still works (volatile through the oxygenator or a propofol infusion, because the vaporiser on your machine is now feeding nothing), neuromuscular block, ventilator off once the perfusionist calls full flow, infusions and fluids stopped, pulmonary artery catheter withdrawn a few centimetres, urine bag emptied. Look at the face: a suffused head with a high central venous pressure is a venous cannula obstructing the superior cava, and a unilaterally blanched face or a fall in one-sided cerebral oximetry is an arterial cannula directing flow down or away from an arch vessel. Mean pressure on pump is the perfusionist's flow against the resistance you control; 50-80 mmHg is the usual range, higher in the hypertensive and those with carotid disease.

### 7. Cross-clamp, cardioplegia and the distal anastomoses
**Why:** With the aorta clamped proximal to the cannula, cardioplegia given into the root runs down the coronaries and arrests the heart in diastole. Retrograde delivery through a coronary sinus catheter reaches muscle beyond tight stenoses that antegrade flow cannot. The surgeon then opens each target and sews the distal ends, usually right, circumflex, then mammary to anterior descending last.
**Anaesthesia:** The electrocardiogram should go flat within a minute or two and stay flat; returning activity means the heart is rewarming or washing out and the surgeon will want another dose. Echo confirms the coronary sinus catheter and shows a ventricle distending if the aortic valve leaks, which the surgeon must vent. Your work is the rest of the patient: depth, glucose, potassium from repeated cardioplegia, haematocrit and urine output.

### 8. Proximal anastomoses, rewarming and de-airing
**Why:** Free grafts are sewn to punch holes in the ascending aorta, either under the same cross-clamp or with the clamp off and a side-biting clamp on a beating, perfused heart. The perfusionist rewarms. Because the left heart was never opened, the only air is in the root and the grafts, and it is vented through the cardioplegia cannula and a needle in each vein graft.
**Anaesthesia:** Each clamp application on the aorta is an embolic event, which is the logic behind the single-clamp technique and the no-touch off-pump operation, where avoiding the aorta altogether was associated with less stroke. Rewarm without overshooting: nasopharyngeal temperature tracks the brain, and arterial blood above 37 degrees C is the avoidable injury. When the clamp comes off the heart may fibrillate; internal shocks of 10-20 J are the surgeon's, magnesium and lidocaine are yours. Air travels to the highest point, which in a supine patient is the right coronary ostium: inferior ST elevation and a sluggish right ventricle minutes after clamp release are air until proved otherwise, and the treatment is perfusion pressure and time on the pump.

### 9. Coming off bypass
**Why:** The heart has to take over a circulation it has not supported for an hour, through new grafts, after a period of ischaemia.
**Anaesthesia:** Before the perfusionist begins to fill the heart: temperature at target, rhythm organised or paced at 80-90 per minute through epicardial wires you have tested, lungs recruited and ventilating with the mammary pedicle in view so the left lung does not stretch it, potassium between 4.5 and 5.5 mmol/L, haemoglobin and gases acceptable, vasopressor and inotrope attached and labelled. Then separate on the echo and the arterial trace together. A new regional wall motion abnormality in a grafted territory with ST change is a graft problem (kinked, twisted, too short, air, or a technically poor anastomosis) and is the surgeon's to fix now, not something to push through with adrenaline. A globally poor ventricle gets rest on the pump, an inotrope, and then an intra-aortic balloon pump if it still cannot manage.

### 10. Protamine and decannulation
**Why:** Heparin has to be reversed for the patient to stop bleeding, but protamine given while the pump suckers are running will clot the circuit, so the timing is shared: venous cannula out, test dose, "protamine on, suckers off", aortic cannula out as the pressure allows.
**Anaesthesia:** Give protamine slowly over 5-10 minutes into a peripheral or central line while watching pulmonary and systemic pressure. Three reactions are worth separating because the treatment differs. Fast injection causes vasodilatation and responds to slowing down and a vasopressor. True anaphylaxis is rare and treated as any other. The catastrophic one is pulmonary vasoconstriction with a distending right ventricle and systemic collapse within minutes: stop the protamine, support the right ventricle, tell the surgeon, and be ready to re-heparinise and return to bypass. Dose to the heparin given, not more: the review evidence is that ratios above 1:1 are associated with more bleeding, because unbound protamine is itself an anticoagulant. Recheck the clotting time against baseline.

### 11. Haemostasis and sternal closure
**Why:** The surgeon packs, inspects every anastomosis and the back of the sternum, and waits. Bleeding now is either a hole or a coagulopathy, and the answer decides whether the chest can be closed. Drains and pacing wires are then brought out and the sternum is closed with six to eight steel wires.
**Anaesthesia:** Run a viscoelastic test after protamine and treat what it shows: fibrinogen concentrate or cryoprecipitate for a low fibrinogen trace, platelets for a weak clot with normal fibrinogen (and for the patient still on a P2Y12 inhibitor), a small further protamine dose only if the heparinase channel shows residual heparin. An algorithm built this way reduced red cell and platelet transfusion and major bleeding across 12 hospitals. Red cells follow a restrictive threshold, haemoglobin below 75 g/L, which was non-inferior to a liberal one in over 5,000 patients. Tranexamic acid halved re-exploration for bleeding at the cost of a small, dose-related rise in seizures (see [major haemorrhage, cell salvage and coagulation](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html)). Closing the sternum takes volume away from an oedematous heart: a fall in pressure with a rise in filling pressure as the wires tighten is tamponade physiology or a kinked graft, and the surgeon needs to know before the skin is closed. Place the parasternal block now if it was not done earlier, and transfer with the pacing box visible, drains unclamped and the last clotting results in the handover.

### 12. The off-pump variant: lifting the beating heart
**Why:** Deep pericardial sutures and an apical suction device lift the apex out of the chest and rotate it to bring the circumflex and posterior descending targets up; a suction or compression stabiliser holds the target; the artery is snared or shunted while the anastomosis is sewn.
**Anaesthesia:** Verticalising the heart folds the atria, compresses the thin right ventricle against the pericardium and distorts the mitral annulus, so output falls most for the lateral and posterior grafts. The response is positional and pre-emptive: head down and rolled to the right to fill the right heart, a vasopressor to hold coronary perfusion pressure, volume, and atrial pacing if the rate falls. Each snared artery is a few minutes of regional ischaemia; the surgeon will usually graft the anterior descending first so the largest territory is revascularised before the heart is lifted. Keep the patient warm, because there is no heat exchanger to recover a cold, irritable heart. Agree a threshold with the surgeon and perfusionist for conversion, and have the pump primed or within reach: an elective conversion is uneventful, an emergency one during cardiac arrest is not.

## Pain plan

The "block" on this card is the analgesic strategy as a whole. The patient arrives in intensive care sedated with intra-operative opioid on board, so day 0 pain is modest and what matters is that analgesia does not delay extubation. The pain then climbs on days 1 and 2 with deep breathing, coughing and sitting out.

PROSPECT's recommendations for sternotomy are paracetamol and an NSAID started intra-operatively and continued, parasternal block or wound infiltration, magnesium or dexmedetomidine as intra-operative adjuncts where basic analgesics cannot be given, and opioid for rescue only. Two caveats apply to coronary patients. NSAIDs are the element most often withheld, for kidney injury, bleeding and the labelled warnings against their use after coronary grafting; PROSPECT itself does not recommend COX-2 selective drugs. And the block evidence is weaker than the enthusiasm: the pooled trials of superficial parasternal block show lower early pain and earlier extubation but an opioid saving of about 8.5 mg morphine equivalent at 24 hours, below the threshold for clinical importance, with no effect on chronic pain.

The defensible default is therefore regular paracetamol, a parasternal block or surgeon infiltration at closure because it is safe, quick and helps the extubation window, short-acting opioid by nurse bolus or patient-controlled analgesia for 24-48 hours, then oral opioid stepping down by day 3-4, with an NSAID added from day 1-2 only when kidney function, drains and the surgeon allow. Gabapentinoids are not recommended. Erector spinae blocks placed before heparin have low-certainty evidence of a similar modest benefit. Thoracic epidural analgesia works and is avoided by most units because full heparinisation follows; that is a risk judgement, not an efficacy one.

Rebound is not a feature because no dense block is relied on. Escalate when pain stops the patient coughing: that is the route to sputum retention, atelectasis and reintubation. A new, sharp, positional or clicking pain after the first week is sternal instability, and pleuritic pain with a rub is pericarditis. A proportion of patients have persistent anterior chest wall pain months later, often a burning strip beside the sternum on the mammary side; say so beforehand.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Left main stem and critical proximal three-vessel disease: the patient in whom induction is the highest-risk moment of the day. Vasopressor running, slow induction, surgeon scrubbed.
- Poor left ventricle or acute infarction: an intra-aortic balloon pump placed before induction, a pulmonary artery catheter, and an early conversation about how the ventricle will be supported off bypass. This is the patient who needs every line and a bed that can run mechanical support.
- Emergency grafting from the catheter laboratory: loaded with aspirin and a P2Y12 inhibitor, often heparinised, sometimes with a balloon pump or in cardiogenic shock. Platelets available, viscoelastic testing early, and no delay for lines that can be placed after the chest is open.
- Calcified aorta found on echo or by the surgeon's fingers: the plan changes to a different cannulation site, a single clamp, or off pump with no aortic manipulation.
- Vasoplegia after bypass: a warm, dilated patient with good ventricular function on echo and a low pressure, commoner after pre-operative ACE inhibitors and long pump runs. Noradrenaline, then vasopressin; the echo is what separates it from a failing ventricle.
- Fast-track: low-dose opioid anaesthesia with a time-directed extubation protocol shortens ventilation and intensive care stay without more complications in low and moderate risk patients. It fails for a predictable list: bleeding, a cold patient, a struggling ventricle, and residual neuromuscular block.

## Key sources

Surgical strategy and blood management rest on large randomised trials. Anticoagulation targets and protamine dosing rest on consensus, and the guideline says so. The analgesic evidence is many small heterogeneous trials, and those behind the block recommendation mostly lacked baseline paracetamol and NSAID.
