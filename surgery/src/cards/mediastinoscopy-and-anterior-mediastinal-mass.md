## What the surgeon is doing and why

Two different problems share this card because they share a compartment and are often on the same list. In the first, the surgeon is staging a lung cancer in an otherwise unremarkable airway, and the risk is the scope. In the second, the surgeon is trying to get tissue from a mass, and the risk is the anaesthetic.

Cervical mediastinoscopy uses a natural plane. The pretracheal fascia runs from the neck into the chest in front of the trachea, and a finger pushed down behind the manubrium along that plane opens a tunnel to the carina. A rigid scope in the tunnel reaches the paratracheal (stations 2 and 4), pretracheal and subcarinal (station 7) nodes, which are the nodes that decide whether a lung cancer is resectable. The tunnel is small and its walls are important:

```
            scope in the pretracheal tunnel
                        |
   R                    v                    L
   innominate artery  [scope]  aortic arch
   crossing in front            left recurrent
   SVC, azygos arch   trachea   laryngeal nerve
   (station 4R)                 (station 4L)
   right pulmonary    carina    oesophagus
   artery below                 behind
```

The innominate artery crosses in front of the trachea exactly where the scope levers forward, so it is squeezed between scope and sternum. The azygos vein and the superior vena cava lie beside the right lower paratracheal nodes and look like a pigmented node through the scope. The left recurrent laryngeal nerve runs in the left tracheo-oesophageal groove. In 2,145 consecutive procedures the complication rate was 1.07%, mostly vocal cord dysfunction and bleeding, and five of the seven vascular injuries happened at station 4R.

Endobronchial and oesophageal ultrasound have taken most of the staging work (see [bronchoscopy, EBUS and interventional pulmonology](bronchoscopy-ebus-and-interventional-pulmonology.html)): in one randomised trial endosonography found more nodal disease than surgical staging, and in another, confirmatory mediastinoscopy after negative endosonography found metastases in only 8%. What reaches the mediastinoscopy list now is the harder residue: restaging after chemoradiotherapy, a negative needle in a suspicious patient, lymphoma needing architecture, and the repeat procedure through scar.

An anterior mediastinal mass (lymphoma, thymoma, germ cell tumour, retrosternal thyroid) sits in front of the trachea, the superior vena cava, the pulmonary artery and the heart. The surgeon needs a core of tissue, by mediastinoscopy, by anterior mediastinotomy through the second interspace, or by thoracoscopy. Awake and upright, the patient holds the airway open with inspiratory muscle tone, negative intrapleural pressure and a large lung volume. Supine, anaesthetised and paralysed, lung volume falls, the mass rests on whatever is beneath it, and compression of the airway beyond the tube tip, of the cava or of the pulmonary artery can make ventilation or circulation impossible.

The pain logic: a short incision in the neck, blunt dissection in a fascial plane and no chest wall retraction. The mechanism is skin and soft tissue, the score is 2, and the analgesic plan is the least interesting part of the case.

## Steps

### 1. Read the imaging before meeting the patient
**Why:** The surgeon chooses the route from the CT: which stations, which side, and whether a mass is in the way.
**Anaesthesia:** For staging, note a previous mediastinoscopy, radiotherapy, a retrosternal goitre, superior vena cava obstruction and a severe cervical spine or carotid problem that limits neck extension; each makes the tunnel more dangerous or the position impossible. For a mass, measure the tracheal and bronchial calibre at the narrowest point, and look for cava compression, pulmonary artery encasement and pericardial effusion. In 105 anaesthetics for adult mediastinal mass, complications were predicted by cardiorespiratory symptoms at presentation (OR 6.2), tracheal compression over 50% (OR 7.4 for postoperative respiratory events) and pericardial effusion (OR 19.8 for intraoperative events). Ask about the positions the patient avoids and the one they sleep in: that is the rescue position.

### 2. Decide whether a general anaesthetic is needed at all
**Why:** The surgeon wants tissue, not this particular operation.
**Anaesthesia:** For the symptomatic mass with a compressed airway or cava, the safest anaesthetic is none. A peripheral node, a CT-guided core, or an anterior mediastinotomy under local anaesthesia sitting up may give the diagnosis. Withholding steroid until the biopsy is taken is the usual request, because lymphoma can melt within a day or two; when the airway will not wait, treatment before tissue is a joint decision with the oncologist. Low-risk patients (no symptoms, no significant compression) can have a conventional anaesthetic.

### 3. Lines and monitors chosen for the anatomy
**Why:** The scope will compress the innominate artery in a proportion of patients, and a bleed will come from the cava, the azygos or an artery.
**Anaesthesia:** Put the pulse oximeter, or an arterial line if one is indicated, on the right hand, and the non-invasive cuff on the left arm. A flattened right-hand trace with a normal left-arm pressure is innominate compression; the same occlusion reduces right carotid and vertebral flow, which matters most in a patient with existing cerebrovascular disease. Site one large cannula. With superior vena cava obstruction, or when the surgeon expects a difficult dissection, site it in a leg: fluid given into an arm drains to the vein that is obstructed or torn.

### 4. Induction for staging mediastinoscopy
**Why:** The surgeon needs a motionless patient with a relaxed neck.
**Anaesthesia:** Standard intravenous induction, a reinforced tube taped to the left corner of the mouth, and full neuromuscular block maintained against a monitor until the scope is out. A cough or a breath with the forceps closed on tissue beside the azygos vein is how a biopsy becomes a tear. Short-acting agents throughout: the stimulus is intense during dissection and ends abruptly.

### 5. Induction for the high-risk mass
**Why:** The surgeon and a rigid bronchoscope should be in the room, scrubbed if the risk is high, before any drug is given.
**Anaesthesia:** The conventional plan is staged and reversible: sitting or semi-recumbent, awake fibreoptic assessment or intubation where the narrowing is tracheal, then an inhalational or titrated intravenous induction with spontaneous ventilation preserved, gentle assisted breaths to prove that positive pressure works, and neuromuscular block only after that proof and only if needed. A long reinforced tube is passed beyond the narrowing under bronchoscopic vision where it can be. The 2022 prospective study that scored airway patency by bronchoscope through each of those stages found no worsening with general anaesthesia and slight improvement with positive pressure and with paralysis, and its authors drew the careful conclusion: the dogma needs reassessing, but conventional induction is not thereby proven safe. The defensible position is to keep the staged approach for symptomatic patients and severe compression, and not to fear relaxant in the rest.

### 6. If ventilation or circulation fails after induction
**Why:** The mass has settled onto the airway distal to the tube, the cava or the pulmonary artery.
**Anaesthesia:** In order: change position to the one the patient chose when awake (lateral, sitting forward or prone), which lifts the mass off the structure; pass the tube, or a rigid bronchoscope, beyond the obstruction and ventilate through it; lighten and restore spontaneous breathing. If the problem is circulatory, with a full venous system and no output, it is cava or pulmonary artery compression: position change again, volume through a leg vein, and the surgeon lifts the mass through a sternotomy. Extracorporeal support only works if the femoral vessels were cannulated under local anaesthesia before induction; "on standby" in another room is not a rescue within the minutes available.

### 7. Positioning: neck extended, airway out of reach
**Why:** Extension draws the trachea up out of the chest and opens the suprasternal notch for the scope.
**Anaesthesia:** A bolster under the shoulders with the head supported, not hanging. The table is tilted head-up to reduce venous congestion, which lowers bleeding and raises the chance of venous air entrainment if a vein is opened while the patient breathes spontaneously; controlled ventilation removes that risk. The surgeon sits at the head: secure the tube and the circuit as for any shared-airway case.

### 8. Incision, pretracheal tunnel and scope insertion
**Why:** A transverse incision above the notch, strap muscles parted, pretracheal fascia opened, and a finger sweeps down to the carina, feeling the innominate artery pulse on its front. The scope follows the finger.
**Anaesthesia:** Finger and scope stimulate the trachea and the carina directly, and pressure on the trachea and the aortic arch produces reflex bradycardia, sometimes abrupt; ask for the scope to be eased back before reaching for atropine. Watch the right-hand trace as the scope is levered: when it flattens, say so, and the surgeon repositions. Peak airway pressure rises if the scope compresses the trachea against a soft tube.

### 9. Node dissection and biopsy
**Why:** Each station is identified, the node is cleaned with a sucker-dissector, needled if there is any doubt that it is a vessel, and sampled with cup forceps.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the part where stillness matters most. Minor bleeding is common and is packed for a few minutes. The left recurrent laryngeal nerve is at risk at the left paratracheal stations, mostly from diathermy and traction. A sudden leak around the cuff or bubbling in the field is a tracheal or bronchial tear. A sudden loss of tidal volume with rising airway pressure is a pneumothorax, right more often than left, from pleural entry.

### 10. Major haemorrhage
**Why:** The surgeon's first move is always the same: pack the tunnel tightly through the scope and wait. In 3,391 mediastinoscopies major haemorrhage occurred in 0.4% and packing gave initial control in 13 of 14.
**Anaesthesia:** Use the minutes that packing buys. Call for help and blood. Site or confirm large access in the legs, because a torn cava or azygos sends arm fluid into the mediastinum. Place an arterial line if there is none. Keep pressure modest while the pack holds. Agree the incision: sternotomy for the innominate artery and most venous injuries, right thoracotomy for the azygos or a right pulmonary artery tear, which means a turn and lung isolation with a bronchial blocker through the tube already in place, not a tube change in a bleeding patient. Transfusion in the published series ranged from none to 18 units.

### 11. Anterior mediastinotomy
**Why:** Aortopulmonary window and anterior mediastinal tissue cannot be reached from the neck. A 4-5 cm incision over the second or third interspace, usually on the left, enters the mediastinum beside the sternum, sometimes after excising a costal cartilage.
**Anaesthesia:** The internal mammary vessels lie in the field and the pleura is often opened, so expect a small pneumothorax; a sustained inflation as the surgeon closes over a catheter under water usually avoids a drain. The left phrenic and recurrent laryngeal nerves are nearby. This is the approach that can be done under local infiltration with the patient sitting up when the mass makes general anaesthesia unsafe.

### 12. Closure, emergence and recovery
**Why:** The fascia and skin are closed without a drain. The surgeon wants a quiet wake-up and a radiograph.
**Anaesthesia:** Reverse fully against a quantitative monitor and extubate head-up and awake. A patient with a mass is at greater risk now than at induction: in the adult series the life-threatening respiratory events were mostly early postoperative, so extubate these patients wide awake, sitting, and observe them in a monitored bed. In recovery: chest radiograph, voice (hoarseness is a recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy until shown otherwise, with aspiration risk if both sides were dissected), neck swelling, and a neurological check of the left arm and face if the innominate was compressed for long.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a small neck wound, so the plan is local infiltration by the surgeon, paracetamol 1 g IV, an NSAID where there is no contraindication, and a short-acting opioid during the dissection. Pain scores of 2-3 on day 1 fall to almost nothing by day 3. Sore throat from the tube, the extended neck and the scope is often the main complaint; dexamethasone helps it but is withheld when lymphoma is on the differential.

There is no PROSPECT guideline for this operation and no trial evidence worth citing; the plan is author's judgement and matches what is done for other clean neck incisions such as [thyroidectomy](thyroidectomy-and-parathyroidectomy.html). A bilateral superficial cervical plexus block is a reasonable alternative to infiltration and adds little for a 3 cm incision. For an anterior mediastinotomy, a parasternal plane block or infiltration by the surgeon covers the interspace.

No catheter, no rebound to plan for. Discharge on paracetamol and an NSAID with a few doses of oral opioid. Escalation triggers are all surgical: increasing pain with neck swelling is a haematoma and an airway question before it is an analgesic one; pleuritic chest pain and breathlessness is a pneumothorax; fever with chest pain and surgical emphysema some days later is mediastinitis from an unrecognised oesophageal or tracheal injury.

If the mass is then resected, the pain plan belongs to the incision used: see [thymectomy and myasthenia gravis](thymectomy-and-myasthenia-gravis.html) for the sternotomy and thoracoscopic approaches.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Repeat mediastinoscopy and the irradiated mediastinum. The plane is scarred to the vessels; bleeding and nerve injury are commoner. Lines as for a major case.
- Superior vena cava obstruction. Swollen face and airway, engorged neck veins, bleeding from every venous tributary in the tunnel, and delayed drug onset from the arm. Head-up, leg access, and expect airway oedema at extubation.
- Video mediastinoscopic lymphadenectomy. A longer, more complete dissection with a spreading scope: more recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy, same hazards.
- Thoracoscopic biopsy of a mass. Needs the lateral position and one-lung ventilation in the patient least able to tolerate either; see [one-lung ventilation and thoracic essentials](../a/a03-one-lung-ventilation.html). A mediastinotomy is usually the safer route.
- The child or adolescent with a mass. Smaller, softer airways, less cooperation with awake techniques, and a higher reported rate of serious complications than adults; the adult data on this card do not transfer.
- Pericardial effusion with a mass. Tamponade physiology on top of compression: drain it under local anaesthesia first.
- Myasthenia with a thymoma. The relaxant question changes; see the thymectomy card.
- The low-risk patient over-treated. Most adults with an incidental anterior mass and no symptoms have an ordinary anaesthetic. The staged plan is for the patient whose history and CT say they need it, and for the difficult airway behind it see [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html).

## Key sources

Complication rates for mediastinoscopy come from two large single-centre series and are reliable for experienced units. The risk factors for mediastinal mass come from one retrospective adult cohort of 105 anaesthetics; the management algorithm is expert review built on that cohort and on case reports, and the one prospective physiological study challenges the central assumption without overturning the practice. Innominate artery compression, its monitoring on the right hand and the lower limb cannula are consensus teaching; no incidence figure was verified and none is given. The statement that paediatric risk is higher is taken from the review literature without a number.
