## What the surgeon is doing and why

Both halves of this card are operations inside the airway you are trying to use. In rigid bronchoscopy the surgeon's instrument is the airway. In tracheal resection the airway is cut in two and for a period the only route to the lungs lies open in the wound.

A rigid bronchoscope is a steel tube 8-14 mm across with a bevelled tip, passed through the cords with the head extended. It does what a flexible scope cannot: it holds a collapsing airway open, cores through tumour with its own tip, takes large forceps, suction and laser fibre side by side, tamponades bleeding, and delivers silicone stents. The indications are central airway obstruction from tumour within the lumen, tumour compressing it from outside, or benign stricture after intubation or tracheostomy. The surgeon restores a lumen by some combination of mechanical debulking, thermal ablation (laser, argon plasma, electrocautery or cryotherapy), balloon or rigid dilatation, and a stent to hold it. The current guideline supports this multimodality approach on evidence it grades as very low certainty.

The patients are not well. In a 15-centre registry of 1,115 therapeutic bronchoscopies for malignant obstruction the complication rate was 3.9%, and 30-day mortality was 14.8%, which reflects the disease. Complications were commoner in urgent cases, in ASA 4 patients and under moderate sedation, which is the argument for a controlled general anaesthetic even in a frail patient.

Tracheal resection is the definitive treatment for a short benign stenosis or a localised tumour. The diseased rings are excised and the ends sutured together. The limit is tension: about half the adult trachea can be removed if the surgeon mobilises the pretracheal plane, releases the larynx or the hilum, and flexes the neck to bring the ends together. The blood supply enters laterally and is segmental, so circumferential dissection is kept to the segment being removed, and the recurrent laryngeal nerves run in the grooves on either side. In 901 resections anastomotic complications occurred in 9% and were the main cause of death; they were predicted by long resections, reoperation, diabetes and laryngeal involvement.

The pain logic: rigid bronchoscopy leaves mucosal soreness and a stiff neck, a 1. A cervical tracheal resection is a collar incision in the same plane as a thyroidectomy with no muscle cut, a 2. The analgesic aim is unusual: comfort with the least possible opioid, because the patient must wake promptly, breathe quietly through a fresh anastomosis and not cough.

## Steps

### 1. Read the obstruction
**Why:** The surgeon plans from the CT and often a flexible bronchoscopy: site, length, diameter, whether the wall is rigid or collapses, and what the airway beyond it looks like.
**Anaesthesia:** You need the same four facts. Where: a subglottic or high tracheal lesion can be bypassed from below by an emergency tracheostomy, a low tracheal or carinal lesion cannot. How tight: stridor at rest implies a lumen of about 5 mm or less, and the tube you can pass is set by that number. Fixed or dynamic: a fixed stenosis behaves the same asleep, while a tumour on a stalk or a malacic segment can close when tone is lost. And what makes it worse: the position the patient avoids. A patient who has had a stent, a tracheostomy or previous dilatation will know more about their airway than the referral letter does. See [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html).

### 2. Induction with a critical stenosis
**Why:** The surgeon stands ready with the rigid scope assembled and light source on, because the scope is the rescue.
**Anaesthesia:** No sedative premedication. Preoxygenate sitting up, with high-flow nasal oxygen if tolerated. Two schools exist and both are defensible. One keeps spontaneous ventilation through an inhalational or slow intravenous induction until the scope is through the narrowing, on the reasoning that muscle tone is holding the airway open. The other gives a full intravenous induction with relaxant and hands straight to the surgeon, on the reasoning that a fixed stenosis does not change with tone, that inhalational induction through a 5 mm orifice is slow and stormy, and that the scope passes most easily in a paralysed patient. The author's practice is the second for fixed benign strictures and the first, or an awake look, for mobile tumour and extrinsic compression. Either way nothing is given until the operator is gloved at the head.

### 3. Passing the rigid bronchoscope
**Why:** The scope is advanced under vision behind the epiglottis and through the cords with the bevel turned, then down to the lesion. The surgeon needs full neck extension and no movement.
**Anaesthesia:** Guard the upper teeth, confirm before induction that the neck extends and the cervical spine is stable, and tape the eyes. Laryngoscopy with a steel tube is as stimulating as anything in anaesthesia: remifentanil and adequate propofol first, not after the pressure rises. From this moment maintenance is intravenous. Topical lidocaine to the cords and trachea, within the dose limit, lowers the anaesthetic requirement and smooths emergence.

### 4. Ventilating through the scope
**Why:** The surgeon needs an open proximal end to pass instruments, which is incompatible with a sealed circuit.
**Anaesthesia:** Three workable methods. Intermittent positive pressure through the side arm with the eyepiece capped and the pharynx packed, interrupted each time an instrument goes down. Manual low-frequency jet through a side port, entraining room air, watching the chest rise and fall with each breath. High-frequency jet from an automated ventilator with pressure limiting. A review of the options found major complications uncommon and similar across them, so use what the unit knows. The rule for any jet is the trap: confirm that gas can leave before each breath goes in. Barotrauma during jet ventilation is a failure of exhalation, and in the largest laryngeal series every case of barotrauma occurred with a jet. Capnography is unreliable in an open system; look at the chest, the saturation and, for a long case, a blood gas. See [microlaryngoscopy, laser airway surgery and jet ventilation](microlaryngoscopy-laser-airway-surgery-and-jet.html).

### 5. Debulking, ablation and dilatation
**Why:** Tumour is cored out or shaved, the base coagulated, and a stricture is cut radially and dilated with a balloon or with scopes of increasing size.
**Anaesthesia:** Three hazards. Fire: before laser, argon plasma or diathermy, bring the FiO2 below 0.4 and say that it is done; the surgeon waits for you, not the reverse. Bleeding: there is nowhere for blood to go but the lungs, so the surgeon tamponades with the scope and suctions while you ventilate the other lung through the scope's side holes or accept apnoea; if it is brisk, turn the patient bleeding side down. Balloon inflation is total occlusion for 30-60 seconds, so preoxygenate for it. Argon plasma adds a gas embolism risk when the probe is against tissue.

### 6. Stent deployment
**Why:** A silicone stent is pushed out of a loader through the rigid scope and seated with forceps; a self-expanding metal stent is released over a wire. Y-shaped stents sit astride the carina. Stents hold open what would otherwise recoil or regrow.
**Anaesthesia:** Deployment needs a still airway and usually a short apnoea with the scope occupied. A stent that opens in the wrong place can obstruct completely until it is repositioned. Once a stent is in, the airway has a foreign body that migrates, plugs with secretions and grows granulation tissue; any later intubation in this patient must be done under vision with a small tube and a bronchoscope, because a blind tube can push a tracheal stent into a bronchus. In the registry, stent placement was associated with higher 30-day mortality, which marks the patients who need one.

### 7. Emergence after rigid bronchoscopy
**Why:** The surgeon withdraws the scope after a last look and suction.
**Anaesthesia:** The airway is now unprotected, oedematous and possibly bloody in a patient who is still paralysed or just reversed. Bridge with a supraglottic airway or, if oedema or bleeding is a concern, a tracheal tube placed before the scope is fully out; either lets a flexible scope check the result. Reverse rocuronium with sugammadex against a monitor. Dexamethasone 8 mg IV, sitting up, and nebulised adrenaline available in recovery. Carbon dioxide retention after a long jet case shows as a slow wake-up.

### 8. Tracheal resection: positioning, incision and mobilisation
**Why:** A low collar incision, sometimes with a partial sternal split for lower lesions, exposes the anterior trachea. The surgeon stays on the tracheal wall to protect the recurrent nerves and the lateral blood supply, and often starts with a rigid bronchoscopy to measure the lesion and dilate it.
**Anaesthesia:** After that bronchoscopy, intubate with a small reinforced tube, either left above the stenosis or passed through it if it was dilated enough. Arterial line; if the innominate artery will be retracted, a right-hand trace shows it. Intravenous maintenance from the start, because the circuit will be open later. Keep fluids modest: airway and anastomotic oedema are the enemy.

### 9. Opening the airway: cross-field ventilation
**Why:** The trachea is divided below the lesion first, so that the distal airway is secured before the diseased segment is removed.
**Anaesthesia:** Before the knife, hand over a sterile reinforced tube and sterile circuit tubing, drop the FiO2 if diathermy will be used on the airway, and pull the oral tube back above the lesion without removing it. The surgeon places the sterile tube in the distal trachea and passes the circuit over the drapes to you. Confirm carbon dioxide and both sides ventilating, since a tube pushed too far sits in one bronchus. The diseased segment is then excised. Alternatives to the cross-field tube are a jet catheter through the oral tube into the distal trachea, which gives the surgeon more room at the cost of the outflow problem and blood spray, and venovenous ECMO, which removes the tube from the field altogether; in a series of 41 resections ECMO did not change operating time or blood loss and was reserved for the difficult cases.

### 10. The anastomosis
**Why:** Sutures are placed around the circumference but not tied. The neck is then flexed to take tension off the join, and the sutures are tied from the back forwards.
**Anaesthesia:** While posterior sutures go in, the surgeon lifts the cross-field tube out and puts it back repeatedly; preoxygenate between each apnoea and call the saturation aloud. When the back wall is done the cross-field tube comes out for the last time and you advance the oral tube, guided by the surgeon's finger, across the gap into the distal trachea, with the cuff well below the suture line. On request, remove the shoulder roll and flex the head on pillows; hold it there from now until the patient leaves theatre. Ventilate gently.

### 11. Leak test, closure and the chin stitch
**Why:** The join is tested under saline, covered with strap muscle or thymus where it lies against the innominate artery, and the wound closed over a small drain. Many surgeons place a heavy suture from chin to presternal skin to stop the patient extending the neck for about a week.
**Anaesthesia:** For the leak test deflate the cuff or draw the tube back above the anastomosis and give a sustained 20-30 cmH2O. Stop the remifentanil late, not early. Reverse fully. Suction the pharynx under vision now, while the patient is deep.

### 12. Extubation and the first days
**Why:** Positive pressure and a cuff are both bad for a fresh anastomosis; the surgeon wants the tube out in theatre.
**Anaesthesia:** The aim is a patient who opens their eyes, breathes and obeys with the neck still flexed and has not coughed. A low-dose remifentanil infusion run to extubation, or exchange of the tube for a supraglottic airway under deep anaesthesia with a flexible bronchoscopic look at the cords and the suture line, are the two usual ways to get there. Before the tube comes out, have ready a flexible bronchoscope loaded with a small uncuffed or lightly cuffed tube: reintubation must be done with the neck flexed, under vision, with the tube tip placed beyond the anastomosis, and a direct laryngoscopy with neck extension can tear the join apart. Stridor in recovery is laryngeal oedema or a recurrent nerve problem; treat with head-up position, nebulised adrenaline and, if needed, a helium and oxygen mixture, and involve the surgeon at once. Over the following days the complications are anastomotic: granulation, separation, and, rarely and late, erosion into the innominate artery.

## Pain plan

The mechanism after rigid bronchoscopy is mucosal soreness and after cervical tracheal resection a clean neck wound. Neither is a severe pain problem. The plan is shaped by what analgesia must not do: sedate, depress the cough reflex unpredictably, or cause vomiting.

For rigid bronchoscopy: topical lidocaine to the airway, paracetamol 1 g IV, dexamethasone, and nothing longer-acting than fentanyl. Most patients need only paracetamol afterwards. Cough from a new stent is treated with humidification and saline nebulisers, not opioid.

For tracheal resection the author's practice is bilateral superficial cervical plexus blocks after induction or generous wound infiltration by the surgeon, paracetamol regularly, an NSAID from day 1 if the surgeon is content about bleeding and healing, and small doses of oral opioid on request. Remifentanil carries the intraoperative analgesia. There is no PROSPECT guideline and no trial for this operation; this is judgement by analogy with other collar incisions such as [thyroidectomy](thyroidectomy-and-parathyroidectomy.html). The discomfort that needs explaining in advance is the flexed neck: a week of looking at the floor produces occipital and trapezius ache that responds to positioning, heat and paracetamol.

No catheter and little rebound. Escalation triggers are not analgesic: new neck swelling, surgical emphysema, a change in voice or increasing stridor is an anastomotic or laryngeal problem; a small herald bleed from the wound or airway in the second or third week is the innominate artery until proved otherwise.

When the approach is a right thoracotomy for a low tracheal or carinal lesion, the pain is that of a thoracotomy and the plan is on the [lobectomy](video-assisted-and-open-lobectomy-and.html) card: a paravertebral catheter or epidural.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Complete obstruction on induction. Push the rigid scope or a small tube past the lesion; turn to the position the patient preferred awake; a lesion at or above the cricoid can be bypassed by a surgical airway, a low one cannot, and for those extracorporeal support must be decided on and cannulated before induction.
- Tracheo-oesophageal fistula. Positive pressure inflates the stomach and soils the lungs: keep spontaneous ventilation until a cuff or a stent is beyond the fistula, and decompress the stomach.
- The existing tracheostomy. Stenosis usually lies at or below the stoma; the stoma is the route for the first tube and is excised with the specimen.
- Laryngotracheal and cricotracheal resection. The anastomosis is at the larynx; oedema and recurrent nerve dysfunction are more frequent, and a small tracheostomy or T-tube below the join is sometimes left.
- Carinal resection. Right thoracotomy, cross-field ventilation of the left main bronchus alone for a long period, high-frequency jet or ECMO as alternatives, and the highest mortality of the group. Fluid restriction as for [pneumonectomy](pneumonectomy.html).
- Flexible bronchoscopic alternatives. Much debulking and metal stenting is now done through a tracheal tube or supraglottic airway by physicians; see [bronchoscopy, EBUS and interventional pulmonology](bronchoscopy-ebus-and-interventional-pulmonology.html).
- Foreign body removal. The same scope and the same ventilation choices in a different patient; see [airway foreign body, epiglottitis and the obstructed airway](airway-foreign-body-epiglottitis-and-the.html).
- Awareness. Relaxant, interrupted intravenous delivery and no end-tidal agent: use a processed EEG monitor and check the cannula every time the arms are moved.
- If the airway cannot be reconstructed, the outcome is a [tracheostomy](tracheostomy.html) or a T-tube, and the conversation about that belongs before the operation.

## Key sources

Evidence here is observational throughout. The registry figures for therapeutic bronchoscopy are multicentre and credible, and the guideline that builds on them grades its own certainty as very low. The choice between ventilation strategies rests on a narrative review that found no clear winner, and the warning about jet barotrauma on a laryngeal surgery series, applied to the trachea by analogy. Tracheal resection practice is dominated by one institution's 901-patient series and its anaesthetic review; cross-field technique, extubation strategy and the reintubation plan are expert consensus. The two schools of induction for critical stenosis have never been compared, and the preference stated is the author's. The 5 mm figure for stridor at rest and the half-trachea limit of resection are standard teaching, not verified during drafting.
