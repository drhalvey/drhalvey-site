## What the surgeon is doing and why

An open globe is a full-thickness wound in the wall of the eye. A sharp object (a knife, glass, a fragment of metal thrown from hammering or grinding) cuts the cornea or sclera, sometimes passing through and out the back, sometimes leaving a foreign body inside. A blunt blow (a fist, a ball, a fall in an older person) raises the pressure in the eye until the wall bursts at its weakest points, under the rectus muscle insertions or along an old surgical wound. Ruptures are larger and further back than lacerations, and do worse.

The eye normally holds its shape because its contents are at 10-20 mmHg inside a tough shell. With the shell breached, the inside is at atmospheric pressure and the eye is soft. Iris, lens, vitreous and retina stay inside only as long as nothing pushes them out, and anything that squeezes the globe from outside or swells the choroid from within can do that.

The first operation has one aim: close the eye. The surgeon removes the shield, examines under the microscope, opens the conjunctiva and follows the wound backwards until its end is found, lifting the rectus muscles if it passes under them. Prolapsed iris and uveal tissue are put back if viable or trimmed if not. The cornea is closed with 10-0 nylon and the sclera with slightly heavier sutures, working from the limbus backwards. Antibiotics are injected into the eye. A lens that is ruptured may be removed; an intraocular foreign body and damage to the retina are usually dealt with by a vitrectomy at a second operation days later, sometimes at the first. Repair is done within about 24 hours of injury, mainly to reduce the risk of infection inside the eye.

The pain logic is modest. The cornea is exquisitely sensitive, and a sutured corneal wound with a conjunctival opening hurts like a bad abrasion for a day or two; systemic simple analgesics and topical treatment cover it. The card scores 2. What makes this operation a standard examination question is the conflict at induction between a possibly full stomach and an eye that cannot tolerate a pressure surge, and the conflict is smaller than tradition suggests.

## Steps

### 1. Before theatre: what raises pressure on an open eye
**Why:** The eye team have placed a rigid shield and want nothing to touch or squeeze the globe until it is closed.
**Anaesthesia:** Think of the forces in three groups, because each has its own prevention.

```
External squeeze        lid closure, rubbing, crying, a facemask or
                        hand on the eye, extraocular muscle contraction
Venous congestion       coughing, straining, vomiting, breath-holding,
(choroid swells)        head-down posture, tight collar, hypercarbia
Arterial surge          laryngoscopy and intubation at a light plane,
                        pain, emergence
```

So from the first contact: antiemetic early (ondansetron 4 mg IV), analgesia so that the patient is not squeezing the eye shut, head up, no eye drops or pads that press, and a calm child. Read the CT for an intraocular or intracranial foreign body and facial fractures, and confirm that head and other injuries have been excluded. See [facial fracture fixation](facial-fracture-fixation.html) for the combined injury.

### 2. Timing and the stomach
**Why:** The surgeon is working to a window of about a day, not minutes.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask directly how long the surgeon will wait. For most injuries the answer allows six hours from the last meal, and the full-stomach dilemma disappears. Remember that injury, pain, opioids and alcohol slow gastric emptying, so a fasted trauma patient may still have a full stomach; the time from the last meal to the injury is as informative as the time since. Where the surgeon will not wait (gross contamination, a prolapsing eye, a child who cannot be kept calm), plan a rapid sequence induction. There is no place for a nasogastric tube or for emetic gastric emptying: both cause retching.

### 3. Local or general anaesthesia
**Why:** The surgeon needs a still eye for microsurgery of uncertain length and extent.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia is the default. An orbital injection adds volume behind an open globe and a needle block risks more, the wound's posterior extent is unknown at the start, and the patient is frightened and often young. Local anaesthesia has a place in selected adults: a meta-analysis of four retrospective series (1,690 patients) found no difference in visual outcome, with the local anaesthesia cases having smaller and more anterior wounds and shorter operations, which describes how they were chosen. For the frail patient with a small corneal laceration, topical anaesthesia with a careful low-volume sub-Tenon injection by the surgeon is a reasonable alternative to a high-risk general anaesthetic. For a rupture, a child, or an uncooperative patient it is not.

### 4. Induction
**Why:** The surgeon wants the patient asleep and intubated without a single cough, strain or press on the eye.
**Anaesthesia:** Pre-oxygenate with the mask held clear of the injured eye; high-flow nasal oxygen avoids the mask altogether. Head up. Give a full induction dose of propofol with an opioid to blunt the pressor response to laryngoscopy (alfentanil 15-20 micrograms/kg or fentanyl 2 micrograms/kg IV), then the relaxant.

The choice of relaxant is the traditional argument. Suxamethonium raises intraocular pressure by several mmHg for a few minutes, through sustained contraction of the extraocular muscles and choroidal vascular effects; in a randomised comparison during rapid sequence induction with propofol and fentanyl, pressure after suxamethonium 1.5 mg/kg averaged 21.6 mmHg against 13.3 mmHg after rocuronium 0.9 mg/kg, with equally good intubating conditions. In a second trial with alfentanil and propofol, pressure did not rise above baseline with either drug. A Cochrane review found suxamethonium gave better intubating conditions overall but no statistical difference against rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg. With sugammadex available to reverse it, rocuronium 1.0-1.2 mg/kg IV is therefore the first choice: the same conditions in 60 seconds without the pressure rise. Suxamethonium is not forbidden. Reviews of the teaching conclude that there are circumstances in which it is the safest relaxant, a predicted difficult airway with a full stomach where sugammadex is unavailable being the obvious one, and the pressure rise it causes is small beside that of a cough or a laryngoscopy attempted too early.

### 5. Laryngoscopy and intubation
**Why:** The tube protects the airway for a long microsurgical case with the head under drapes.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the largest pressure event of the anaesthetic if done badly. Wait for the relaxant: confirm no response to a nerve stimulator, and do not shorten the 60 seconds because the saturation is fine. Use a videolaryngoscope and take the best first look; a second attempt in a patient who is lightening is the scenario to avoid. Cricoid pressure, if used, is released if it worsens the view. Inflate the cuff, confirm capnography, and tie the tube without a tight band around the neck that would obstruct venous return from the head.

### 6. Maintenance
**Why:** The surgeon is suturing a soft, open eye under the microscope and any movement or venous congestion pushes tissue into the wound.
**Anaesthesia:** Maintain deep anaesthesia and full neuromuscular block with quantitative monitoring until the surgeon says the globe is closed. Ventilate to normocapnia or just below; avoid high airway pressures and positive end-expiratory pressure beyond what oxygenation needs. Volatile agents and propofol both lower intraocular pressure; either is suitable. Avoid nitrous oxide, since the surgeon may leave air or gas in the eye. Keep the blood pressure near normal: hypertension congests the choroid, and hypotension matters less to an eye with no pressure inside it but still matters to the patient. Ketamine is avoided in adults by convention because of blepharospasm and nystagmus.

### 7. Exploration and the oculocardiac reflex
**Why:** To find the end of a scleral wound the surgeon hooks and retracts rectus muscles, and may detach one temporarily.
**Anaesthesia:** Traction on a muscle triggers the oculocardiac reflex as it does in squint surgery: bradycardia or a pause within seconds. Have the surgeon release the traction, check depth and carbon dioxide, and give glycopyrronium 0.2-0.4 mg or atropine 0.6 mg IV (atropine 20 micrograms/kg in children) if it recurs. Details are on the [strabismus surgery](strabismus-surgery.html) card.

### 8. Closure of the globe
**Why:** Sutures restore a watertight shell; the anterior chamber is reformed with fluid or air, and intravitreal antibiotics are given.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask to be told when the eye is closed. From that moment the eye has a wall again and a normal pressure, and the constraints ease, though the repair is a row of fine sutures in bruised tissue. If lens removal, foreign body extraction or vitrectomy follows in the same sitting, the case becomes a [vitreoretinal surgery](vitreoretinal-surgery.html) case, with the gas precautions on that card. Have the surgeon place sub-Tenon or subconjunctival long-acting local anaesthetic now for postoperative analgesia. Give paracetamol and, if the surgeon accepts it, an NSAID.

### 9. Emergence
**Why:** The surgeon wants no coughing, retching or rubbing.
**Anaesthesia:** Second antiemetic given. Reverse fully (sugammadex 2-4 mg/kg IV by depth of block). If the patient was fasted, a deep extubation or exchange to a supraglottic airway is the smoothest. If the stomach was full at induction it may still be, so extubate awake, and reduce coughing with a low-dose remifentanil infusion run to extubation or lidocaine 1-1.5 mg/kg IV a few minutes before. Shield on before the patient can reach the eye. Sit up in recovery.

### 10. The child with an open eye
**Why:** A large share of these injuries are in children, who cannot be reasoned into keeping still and calm.
**Anaesthesia:** A screaming, struggling child generates more pressure on the eye than any drug. The priority is a calm induction by whatever route achieves it. With a cannula in place (topical anaesthetic cream applied on arrival, inserted with distraction), an intravenous induction with propofol, opioid and rocuronium is ideal. Without one, a gentle inhalational induction with sevoflurane in a child who accepts the mask is better than a fight for a vein, and sevoflurane lowers intraocular pressure within minutes; the aspiration risk is accepted and managed with a fasted wait where possible. Oral midazolam premedication is reasonable. Parents present, mask kept off the injured side. Doses and airway sizes are in [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a sutured corneal or scleral wound and a conjunctival opening: surface and soft-tissue pain, mild to moderate, lasting two or three days. The plan is paracetamol and an NSAID (if the surgeon and the patient's other injuries allow), a surgeon-placed sub-Tenon or subconjunctival injection of long-acting local anaesthetic once the globe is closed, topical cycloplegic and steroid drops prescribed by the surgeon, which relieve ciliary spasm, and a small amount of oral opioid for the first night. Antiemetics are part of the analgesic plan here, because vomiting is the event to prevent. There is no procedure-specific guideline, and this plan is consensus and author's judgement.

Expect a worst pain of 2-3 out of 10 on the evening of surgery and day 1, described as a foreign-body sensation and ache with light sensitivity, falling by day 3. Larger ruptures with muscle disinsertion, and combined lid or orbital repair, sit higher. Rebound is not a feature.

Escalation triggers. Increasing pain with a red eye, lid swelling and falling vision, from the second day onwards, is endophthalmitis until excluded and is the complication the whole timetable is designed to prevent. Severe pain with nausea is a high intraocular pressure from blood or inflammation and needs measuring. Pain out of proportion on eye movement with proptosis suggests an orbital problem.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Pressing on the eye. The facemask, a hand steadying the head, a forehead strap, the laryngoscopist's wrist. Every person in the room is told which eye.
- Laryngoscopy before the relaxant has worked. The cough that follows is the event the old teaching blamed on suxamethonium.
- Awake intubation. Coughing during topicalisation is hard to avoid; for a predicted difficult airway with an open eye, the airway takes priority and is discussed with the surgeon beforehand. See [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html).
- Vomiting on the ward before theatre. Give the antiemetic at first contact, not at induction.
- The delayed case that is not fasted after all. Pain and opioids stop gastric emptying; decide on the history, and use a rapid sequence induction when in doubt.
- Intraocular foreign body and MRI. A metallic fragment is a contraindication to magnetic resonance imaging; this is established on CT.
- Older patients with rupture after a fall. On anticoagulants, with a head injury to exclude and often a previous cataract wound that has burst; the cause of the fall needs as much attention as the eye.
- Bilateral injuries and the only eye. Blast and airbag injuries. The same principles with higher stakes; expect a longer operation and a distressed patient afterwards.
- Secondary surgery. Vitrectomy, lens implantation or, for a blind painful eye, enucleation follow days to weeks later as planned procedures on the cards for those operations.
- Emergency and high-risk variant. This is the emergency. The highest-risk form is the unfasted patient with a difficult airway: the airway and the patient's life outrank the eye, and the surgeon should hear that said before induction. Invasive monitoring, blood and critical care are needed only for associated injuries.

## Key sources

The relaxant question rests on two small randomised trials of intraocular pressure in patients with intact eyes, a Cochrane review of intubating conditions, and two reviews of the teaching; no trial has compared relaxants in open globes with extrusion of contents as the outcome, and none will. The local anaesthesia meta-analysis is of retrospective series with obvious selection. The 24-hour repair window, the three-group pressure model, induction and emergence doses, and the approach to the child are consensus and author's judgement.
