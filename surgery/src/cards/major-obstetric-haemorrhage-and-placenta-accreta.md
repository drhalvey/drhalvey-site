## What the surgeon is doing and why

The uterus at term receives around 700 mL of blood a minute, and after the placenta separates the only thing that closes the open spiral arteries of the placental bed is the myometrium contracting around them. Every cause of major obstetric haemorrhage is a failure of that mechanism or a hole somewhere else, which is why obstetricians teach four causes: tone (the uterus will not contract: the commonest by far), trauma (a torn cervix, vagina, uterine angle or a ruptured uterus), tissue (retained placenta or membranes holding the uterus open, or a placenta that has invaded the wall), and thrombin (coagulopathy, either the cause, as in abruption and amniotic fluid embolism, or the consequence of the other three).

The surgeon's response is a ladder, and each rung is tried only as long as it is working:

```
 rub the fundus, empty the bladder, bimanual compression
        |  with oxytocin -> ergometrine -> carboprost -> misoprostol
 examination under anaesthesia: remove tissue, repair trauma
        |
 intrauterine balloon tamponade
        |
 laparotomy: uterine compression suture (B-Lynch)
        |
 devascularisation: uterine artery, then internal iliac ligation;
 or embolisation by interventional radiology if stable enough to move
        |
 hysterectomy (aortic compression buys time at any rung)
```

The skill is in climbing fast enough. Peripartum hysterectomy done early by a prepared team is a controlled operation; done late it is done in a cold, acidotic, coagulopathic patient.

Placenta accreta spectrum is the planned version of the same problem. Where the endometrium has been scarred, almost always by a previous caesarean, the placenta implants without a decidual layer and grows into the myometrium (accreta), through it (increta) or beyond the serosa into bladder and parametrium (percreta). It will not separate, and any attempt to remove it tears open a lower segment that is mostly placenta and new vessels. A placenta praevia over a caesarean scar is the classic setting, and the risk rises with each previous caesarean. When it is diagnosed antenatally the standard operation is a planned caesarean hysterectomy at 35-36 weeks in a centre with the full team: the baby is delivered through a uterine incision placed well away from the placenta, the cord is tied, the placenta is left untouched and the uterus is removed with it inside. Conservative alternatives (leaving the placenta to resorb, or resecting the invaded segment) exist for selected women.

For the anaesthetist these are one card because the physiology and the product strategy are identical; what differs is whether you had three weeks or three minutes to prepare. The pain mechanism, when there has been a laparotomy, is an abdominal wall incision plus a hysterectomy, in a patient whose coagulation limits the regional options. The general principles of massive transfusion are in [major haemorrhage, cell salvage and coagulation](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html).

## Steps

### 1. Recognition
**Why:** The obstetric team rubs up a contraction, empties the bladder and looks for the cause while calling for help.
**Anaesthesia:** Young, healthy, hypervolaemic women compensate until they have lost 30-40% of their blood volume, then decompensate abruptly. Visual estimation consistently under-reads large losses, so the first intervention is measurement: weigh everything, use a collecting drape, write the cumulative total on the board every 10-15 minutes. A heart rate higher than the systolic pressure is a late sign, not an early one. The Welsh national programme that paired measured loss with staged escalation and bedside clotting tests was associated with fewer women progressing from moderate to massive haemorrhage. Declare a major haemorrhage at 1,000 mL with ongoing loss, or earlier with signs of shock, and say the words that activate the protocol.

### 2. First ten minutes: access, tranexamic acid and the uterotonic ladder
**Why:** Most postpartum haemorrhages are atony, and most of those stop with drugs and compression if they are given promptly and in sequence.
**Anaesthesia:** Two large cannulae; full blood count, coagulation screen with Clauss fibrinogen, a venous gas and a cross-match, plus a viscoelastic test where available. Warmed crystalloid up to 2 L while blood is coming, then blood. TXA 1 g IV now: in the WOMAN trial it reduced death from bleeding by about a third when given within three hours of birth and did nothing for hysterectomy, because by the time that decision is made the window has closed. Uterotonics in order, without waiting long between them: oxytocin 5 IU slowly IV and an infusion of 40 IU over 4 hours; ergometrine 250 micrograms IM or slowly IV unless hypertensive; carboprost 250 micrograms IM, repeated every 15 minutes to a maximum of eight doses, unless asthmatic; misoprostol 800 micrograms sublingually or rectally. Each has a side effect you will be treating: hypotension, vomiting and hypertension, bronchospasm and desaturation, fever and shivering.

### 3. Moving to theatre and choosing the anaesthetic
**Why:** Examination under anaesthesia finds the two causes that drugs cannot treat, tissue and trauma, and gives access for a balloon.
**Anaesthesia:** A working epidural in a woman who is not shocked can be topped up cautiously in small fractions. A new spinal is reasonable only if loss has stopped, volume has been restored and clotting is known. Otherwise this is a general anaesthetic: rapid sequence induction with ketamine 1-1.5 mg/kg IV or a reduced dose of propofol, a videolaryngoscope first, and vasopressor in hand. Keep the volatile agent at or below 0.5 MAC with nitrous oxide or an opioid, or use propofol infusion, because volatile agents relax the uterus in a dose-dependent way and atony is the problem being treated. Place the arterial line as soon as someone has hands free; it gives the pressure and the serial gases.

### 4. Coagulation: fibrinogen first
**Why:** The surgeon can tie vessels but cannot suture a coagulopathy, and continued oozing from every surface defeats each rung of the ladder.
**Anaesthesia:** Pregnancy raises fibrinogen to 4-6 g/L, and in obstetric bleeding it falls earlier and further than the other factors, fastest of all in abruption and amniotic fluid embolism. A level under 2 g/L during ongoing bleeding predicts progression and is the national guideline's trigger for replacement. The OBS2 trial refines this: giving fibrinogen concentrate at a Fibtem A5 of 15 mm or less did not help, and the subgroup data suggest no benefit above 12 mm or a Clauss fibrinogen above 2 g/L. So do not give fibrinogen blindly and do not withhold it below those values: fibrinogen concentrate 4 g or the equivalent dose of cryoprecipitate, repeated against results. In atony and trauma with normal tests, red cells alone are often enough for the first four units; in abruption, amniotic fluid embolism or when results are unavailable after four units, give plasma 15 mL/kg and then fibrinogen. Platelets when the count is below 75 x 10^9/L. Calcium chloride 10% 10 mL IV with every four units, active warming, and a repeat set of bloods every 30 minutes or after each round of products.

### 5. Balloon tamponade and compression sutures
**Why:** A balloon inflated with 300-500 mL inside the cavity presses on the placental bed from within; if that fails at laparotomy, a brace suture folds the uterus on itself and compresses it from outside. Both preserve the uterus.
**Anaesthesia:** The balloon is placed from below and takes minutes; a positive test (bleeding stops) lets the patient go to high dependency care with an oxytocin infusion and the balloon in for up to 24 hours. Deflation is a moment for bleeding to restart, so it happens in daylight with a cannula and a valid cross-match. For a laparotomy, set up cell salvage if it is not already running: collection can begin with a second sucker once the liquor is gone. The salvage trial found no amniotic fluid embolism among 1,500 women allocated to it; its lack of cost-effectiveness applied to routine caesareans, not to this situation.

### 6. Devascularisation, embolisation and aortic compression
**Why:** Ligating the uterine arteries, and if necessary the anterior divisions of the internal iliacs, lowers the pulse pressure in the pelvis enough for clot to form. Embolisation does the same from within but requires a patient stable enough to move to radiology or a hybrid theatre.
**Anaesthesia:** Internal iliac ligation is slow, retroperitoneal and close to the ureter and the iliac vein; it is not a rescue for a patient in extremis. If the patient is deteriorating, ask the surgeon to compress the aorta against the sacral promontory with a fist or a swab on a stick while you catch up with volume and clotting: it is the most effective temporising manoeuvre in the room and it is the anaesthetist who usually has to ask for it. Do not allow transfer to radiology of a woman who is still requiring boluses of vasopressor.

### 7. The decision for hysterectomy
**Why:** When conservative measures have failed, or the cause is a ruptured uterus or an adherent placenta, removing the uterus removes the bleeding surface. A subtotal hysterectomy is faster and is enough unless the bleeding is from the lower segment or cervix.
**Anaesthesia:** A second senior obstetrician is called for the decision in most units; your contribution is the trend. Say the numbers aloud: cumulative loss, units given, last fibrinogen, lactate, temperature, vasopressor dose. If they are worsening despite everything, say that the patient will not tolerate another hour of trying. Noradrenaline by infusion is appropriate once volume is being replaced; it is not a substitute for volume.

### 8. Planned accreta surgery: the set-up
**Why:** The multidisciplinary plan fixes the date, the theatre, the surgeons (obstetric, gynaecological oncology or urology, sometimes vascular), the radiologist and the products.
**Anaesthesia:** Before the day: haemoglobin optimised with iron, antenatal anaesthetic review, cross-matched blood in the theatre refrigerator, cell salvage, a rapid infusion device primed, a critical care bed booked. On the day: two large cannulae and an arterial line before the anaesthetic, forced-air and fluid warming, calf compressors, TXA 1 g IV at skin incision (the author's practice, extrapolated from the treatment trial). Ureteric stents are placed by cystoscopy first in many centres, which is why the legs are in Lloyd-Davies supports. Prophylactic iliac balloon catheters are used in some centres; the international guideline does not find enough evidence to recommend them routinely, and they add time, a sheath in each groin and a small risk of arterial injury.

### 9. Planned accreta surgery: neuraxial or general
**Why:** The surgeon needs two to four hours of still, relaxed abdomen through a midline or high transverse incision, with possible bladder and ureteric dissection.
**Anaesthesia:** Both strategies are defensible. In the largest single-centre series, 95% of planned cases began under neuraxial anaesthesia, and of those who went on to hysterectomy under the block about one in five were converted to general anaesthesia, typically the women with the most complex surgery and the most transfusion. Starting with a combined spinal-epidural lets the mother be awake for the birth, avoids fetal exposure to general anaesthesia (the smaller cohort found more neonatal respiratory distress after it), and roughly halves postoperative opioid use. The author's default is a combined spinal-epidural for suspected accreta or increta with a stated plan to convert electively after delivery if the placenta is confirmed invasive and dissection is extensive, and general anaesthesia from the start for percreta with bladder or parametrial invasion on imaging. Converting electively, before the bleeding, is very different from converting during it with an oedematous airway and no free hands.

### 10. Delivery away from the placenta, then hysterectomy
**Why:** The uterine incision is fundal or high classical, placed with ultrasound to avoid the placenta. The baby is delivered, the cord is tied and cut short, the uterine incision is closed quickly for haemostasis, and the placenta is not touched. Hysterectomy proceeds with the bladder dissected off a lower segment covered in new vessels; this is where the blood is lost.
**Anaesthesia:** Surgeons vary on uterotonics at this point: contraction can shear part of the placenta off and start the bleeding the plan was designed to avoid, so the international guideline discusses oxytocin as a decision rather than a routine, and you give it only on request. Blood loss comes in two phases: a steady loss from the fundal incision, then sudden heavy loss during bladder dissection or if the placenta is breached. Have the first units checked and hanging before that dissection starts. Run salvage throughout, give products against viscoelastic or laboratory results as in step 4, and expect the bladder to be opened deliberately or accidentally in percreta, which adds an hour.

### 11. Closure and the handover to critical care
**Why:** The surgeon checks the pedicles, the bladder and the ureters, may leave a drain, and closes the sheath.
**Anaesthesia:** Before the drapes come down, check a full set of results: haemoglobin, platelets, fibrinogen, clotting, lactate, calcium, temperature. These decide three things. Extubation: a warm, non-acidotic patient who has stopped bleeding can be woken; a patient who has had more than a blood volume replaced, has facial and airway oedema or is still on vasopressor is ventilated for some hours. The epidural: use it if clotting and platelets are normal, leave it capped if they are not, and remove it only when they are. Destination: high dependency or intensive care for all hysterectomies and for any loss beyond 2,500 mL.

### 12. The first day
**Why:** Rebleeding, usually from a pedicle or from coagulopathy, declares itself in the first six hours.
**Anaesthesia:** Hourly observations, drain and vaginal loss, serial haemoglobin and fibrinogen. Transfusion-associated circulatory overload is common after a resuscitation run at speed, and a breathless woman with a rising oxygen requirement at four hours needs a chest radiograph and a diuretic more often than more fluid. Start pharmacological thromboprophylaxis as soon as haemostasis is secure, because bleeding, transfusion, surgery and immobility make these women among the highest-risk for venous thromboembolism in obstetrics. Explain what happened to the woman and her partner the next day, and again before discharge: an emergency hysterectomy ends her fertility and the debrief is part of the care.

## Pain plan

The analgesic strategy depends on which rung the surgeon reached. Haemorrhage controlled by drugs, examination or a balloon leaves afterpains and perineal discomfort only: paracetamol, and an NSAID once the bleeding has clearly stopped and urine output and creatinine are normal. A balloon in the uterus causes cramping that responds to the same drugs.

A laparotomy with hysterectomy is abdominal wall pain at the level of an open abdominal hysterectomy, score 4, and no PROSPECT guideline addresses it in this setting. The mechanism argues for neuraxial or truncal block; the coagulation decides whether one is allowed. For the planned accreta case with a combined spinal-epidural and normal clotting at the end, intrathecal morphine 100-150 micrograms given at the start covers the first day, and the epidural can be run for 24-48 hours as an alternative. For the unplanned case, or any case that ends coagulopathic, analgesia is intravenous: opioid patient-controlled analgesia, regular paracetamol, and a low-dose ketamine infusion if needed, with bilateral TAP or rectus sheath blocks added once fibrinogen, platelets and clotting are back to normal, usually later the same day. NSAIDs are withheld for at least 24 hours after a major haemorrhage and until renal function is known to be normal, because hypovolaemia has already put the kidneys at risk. This is consensus practice and the author's judgement.

Rebound is moderate: it comes when intrathecal morphine fades at 18-24 hours or when an epidural is removed. Escalation triggers: increasing abdominal pain with a falling haemoglobin or rising heart rate is rebleeding until proven otherwise; loin pain or a rising creatinine after a difficult hysterectomy raises ureteric injury.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Antepartum haemorrhage. Abruption conceals blood behind the placenta, consumes fibrinogen early and may present with a dead fetus and a woody uterus: expect coagulopathy at the first blood test. Bleeding praevia is revealed loss and a category 1 caesarean under general anaesthesia if the mother is unstable.
- Amniotic fluid embolism. Sudden collapse, hypoxia and then profound coagulopathy around delivery. Treat as cardiac arrest plus massive haemorrhage; fibrinogen falls to unmeasurable levels within minutes.
- Undiagnosed accreta. Found at caesarean as a bulging, vascular lower segment, or at manual removal as no plane. The safest surgical response is to stop: if the uterus is not yet opened, close and transfer or wait for the team; if it is, leave the placenta and move to hysterectomy with help.
- Uterine rupture. Previous caesarean, pain breaking through an epidural, fetal bradycardia, loss of station. General anaesthesia and laparotomy.
- Genital tract haematoma. Paravaginal and broad ligament haematomas bleed out of sight and present with pain and shock without visible loss.
- Carboprost in an asthmatic and ergometrine in a hypertensive woman are the two predictable drug errors.
- Over-resuscitation with crystalloid before blood dilutes fibrinogen and produces pulmonary oedema; pregnant women have low oncotic pressure to begin with.
- Recombinant factor VIIa is a last resort with a thrombosis risk, used only with fibrinogen and platelets corrected and on haematology advice.
- Jehovah's Witness and other women who decline blood. The plan is made antenatally: iron, cell salvage in continuity if accepted, early TXA, early recourse to hysterectomy.
- Rhesus status. Salvaged blood contains fetal red cells; the salvage trial found more fetomaternal haemorrhage in RhD-negative women, so a Kleihauer test and anti-D follow.

## Key sources

Tranexamic acid rests on a 20,000-woman trial conducted largely in low-resource settings, with a mortality benefit confined to early treatment. The fibrinogen threshold comes from observational work and one small randomised trial whose main result was negative; the 2 g/L figure is a guideline consensus. Cell salvage is supported by a large trial for safety rather than efficacy. Anaesthetic technique for accreta surgery is informed only by retrospective single-centre cohorts, and the default described here, like the timing of tranexamic acid before planned surgery and the analgesic plan, is the author's judgement.
