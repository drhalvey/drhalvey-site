## What the surgeon is doing and why

The pleural space is a potential space kept empty by two smooth membranes sliding on a film of fluid. Three things go wrong with it, and the three operations on this card are the three answers.

```
 Problem            What is in the space   The surgeon's answer
 -----------------  ---------------------  ---------------------------
 Pneumothorax       air, from a ruptured   staple the bleb, then make
                    bleb or bulla          the lung stick: pleurectomy,
                                           abrasion or talc
 Malignant or       fluid that keeps       drain, biopsy, then make
 recurrent          coming back            the lung stick: talc
 effusion
 Empyema or         pus or clot, then a    wash out; if a rind has
 clotted            fibrous rind that      formed, peel it off the
 haemothorax        cages the lung         lung: decortication
```

Pleurodesis is the common thread and the source of the pain. For the lung to adhere, the parietal pleura has to be injured: stripped off the chest wall from apex to about the sixth rib (pleurectomy), scrubbed raw with a pad (abrasion), or coated with sterile talc that sets up a chemical inflammation. The parietal pleura is the innervated layer, supplied by the intercostal nerves, and the operation works by inflaming all of it. The visceral pleura and the lung have no somatic sensation, which is why a wedge resection through the same ports hurts less.

Decortication is a different job. Infected or bloody fluid that is not drained organises over a few weeks into a fibrous peel on both pleural surfaces. The surgeon enters the space, breaks down loculations, and then finds the plane between rind and visceral pleura and peels the lung free so that it can expand to fill the chest. The plane is imperfect; every breach of the visceral pleura is an air leak and every strip of rind leaves an oozing surface. Early cases are thoracoscopic washouts; late cases are long, may convert to thoracotomy, and are done on patients who are septic and catabolic.

The patients are three different populations. Primary spontaneous pneumothorax is a disease of tall, thin, young smokers with apical blebs, fit and with a high pain response; secondary pneumothorax occurs in emphysema, cystic fibrosis and interstitial disease, in patients with no reserve. Malignant effusion means advanced cancer, breathlessness, poor nutrition and a wish to go home. Empyema follows pneumonia in the frail, the alcohol-dependent and the immunosuppressed.

The pain logic: chest wall mechanism, but driven by pleura more than by ribs. Ports still lever on intercostal nerves and the drains still rub, and on top of that the entire parietal surface is raw. The peak is on day 1, when the patient first breathes deeply against a drain on suction. It falls quickly once the drains are out, which is why the score is 4 and not 5.

## Steps

### 1. Establish what is in the space and whether it is draining
**Why:** The surgeon is operating on a space that may contain air under tension, litres of fluid, or pus that communicates with the airway.
**Anaesthesia:** Look at today's radiograph, not last week's. Three questions decide the induction. Is there a drain, and is it swinging and bubbling. Is there an air leak, which means a hole in the lung or a fistula. And how long has the lung been down, since days of collapse predict re-expansion oedema. For empyema, assess as for any sepsis: lactate, renal function, coagulation, and whether the patient coughs up large volumes on lying down, which suggests a bronchopleural fistula. For malignant effusion, ask whether breathlessness improved after the last tap; if it did not, the lung may be trapped and pleurodesis will fail.

### 2. Block and induction
**Why:** Nothing surgical yet.
**Anaesthesia:** Place the paravertebral or erector spinae block before induction where the patient can sit, with a catheter for pleurectomy or decortication. In sepsis or coagulopathy a deep paravertebral or epidural catheter is avoided and an erector spinae catheter or surgeon-delivered intercostal blocks are used. Induction then follows the answer to step 1. With a functioning drain, induce normally. With an undrained pneumothorax, positive pressure can convert it to tension within a few breaths: have the drain inserted first under local anaesthesia, or keep the patient breathing spontaneously until the lung is isolated or the chest is open. Avoid nitrous oxide in every case. With a large effusion the patient may only tolerate sitting; induce in that position. With a suspected fistula, see pitfalls.

### 3. Lung isolation
**Why:** The surgeon needs the lung down to see the apex, the diaphragmatic surface and the posterior gutter.
**Anaesthesia:** A left double-lumen tube or a blocker, checked by bronchoscope; see [one-lung ventilation and thoracic essentials](../a/a03-one-lung-ventilation.html). For empyema choose the double-lumen tube: pus squeezed out of the lung during decortication runs to the carina, and a blocker neither protects the dependent lung reliably nor lets you suction the operated side. A lung encased in rind or stuck by adhesions does not collapse however good the isolation, so do not chase a "poor collapse" with tube manipulation when the cause is on the surgical side.

### 4. Lateral positioning
**Why:** Standard thoracoscopic access with the rib spaces opened.
**Anaesthesia:** Recheck the tube after the turn. Keep a chest drain that is already in place unclamped and below the patient during the move. A patient with a litre or more of fluid may drop their pressure on turning as the fluid shifts onto the mediastinum; it corrects once the surgeon drains it.

### 5. Entry, drainage of fluid and inspection
**Why:** The first port goes in where imaging shows free space, fluid is suctioned, and the camera surveys both pleural surfaces. Biopsies of parietal pleura are taken for the undiagnosed effusion.
**Anaesthesia:** Stop ventilating the operative lung before the port goes in. Rapid evacuation of a large chronic effusion can drop the pressure briefly as the mediastinum swings back. Note the volume removed: several litres from a lung that has been compressed for weeks is the setting for re-expansion pulmonary oedema, and the timing of full re-expansion is in your hands at step 9.

### 6. Adhesiolysis and decortication
**Why:** Loculations are broken down and the cavity washed out. If a rind restricts the lung, the surgeon incises it, finds the plane on the visceral pleura and peels, working from the fissures outwards; the parietal rind is stripped off the chest wall as well if it restricts the ribs.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the long and bloody part. Blood loss is a steady ooze, easy to underestimate in a suction canister diluted with washout fluid; weigh swabs and track the haemoglobin. The surgeon will ask for the lung to be partly inflated to tension the rind and show the plane, then collapsed again, repeatedly: do it by hand on the operative lumen with low pressures. Bacteraemia from handling an infected cavity shows as tachycardia, fever and vasodilatation an hour in; have noradrenaline running early instead of repeated fluid boluses. Each tear in the visceral pleura is an air leak that will show when two-lung ventilation resumes.

### 7. Bullectomy or wedge of the leak site
**Why:** In pneumothorax surgery the apex is inspected under saline, blebs and bullae are stapled off, and any visible leak site is excised.
**Anaesthesia:** You may be asked for a gentle inflation with the apex under water to find the leak. Giant bullectomy in emphysema is a different case: a compliant bulla takes the tidal volume in preference to the stiff lung around it, so use low pressures, long expiratory times and one-lung ventilation of the good side as early as possible.

### 8. Pleurectomy or abrasion
**Why:** The parietal pleura is stripped from the apex down to the level the surgeon favours, or abraded with a scourer or diathermy pad until it bleeds, so that the raw surface adheres to the lung.
**Anaesthesia:** Stripping is fast and stimulating and can bleed from intercostal vessels, the internal mammary vessels anteriorly and, at the apex, the subclavian vessels; apical dissection can also bruise the sympathetic chain and produce a Horner syndrome. This step creates the postoperative pain. If intercostal blocks under vision are part of the plan, they are easier before the pleura is removed, because the pleura is what holds the injectate against the nerve.

### 9. Talc poudrage
**Why:** A few grams of sterile graded talc are insufflated over all pleural surfaces as a dry powder to provoke an inflammatory pleuritis. It is the standard method for malignant effusion and an adjunct or alternative to pleurectomy in pneumothorax.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing happens at the moment of insufflation. Over the next 12-48 hours expect fever, a rise in inflammatory markers and pleuritic pain; a systemic inflammatory response with hypoxaemia is uncommon and supportive care is the treatment. Whether an NSAID blunts the pleurodesis is the long-standing surgical concern. The TIME1 trial in malignant effusion found that NSAIDs were non-inferior to opiates for pleurodesis failure at three months (23% against 20%). Surgeons vary: some accept NSAIDs for all, some only for malignant effusion, some for none. Know which before you write the chart.

### 10. Re-expansion, leak check and drains
**Why:** The lung is inflated under vision to confirm that it reaches the chest wall everywhere. One or two drains are placed, apical and basal, and usually put on suction to hold the surfaces together.
**Anaesthesia:** Re-expand slowly and by hand, with the lowest pressure that opens the lung, especially after a long collapse or a large effusion. In 306 patients drained for spontaneous pneumothorax, radiographic re-expansion oedema followed in 16%, with risk rising with the size of the collapse; most cases are mild, and the severe ones present within an hour or two with frothy sputum and falling saturation from the operated lung. Treat with oxygen, CPAP or continued ventilation with PEEP, and patience; diuretics add little in a patient who is not overloaded. Tell the surgeon about a large leak on the ventilator now, while the ports are still in.

### 11. Emergence and extubation
**Why:** The surgeon wants a spontaneously breathing patient as soon as possible, because positive pressure prolongs air leaks from raw lung.
**Anaesthesia:** Extubate in theatre, sitting up, block topped up. Check the drains are connected, unclamped and swinging before the drapes come off. The septic decortication patient who is cold, acidotic or on a rising noradrenaline dose is the exception and goes to intensive care ventilated on the lowest pressures that work, after a change to a single-lumen tube.

### 12. The first days
**Why:** The surgeon is waiting for the air leak to stop and the drain output to fall.
**Anaesthesia:** Pain peaks on day 1 and is drain-dependent: suction on a large-bore drain against freshly stripped pleura. Regular paracetamol, the NSAID if permitted, the catheter running, and oral opioid charted. Opioid-related nausea is common in the young pneumothorax group and delays mobilisation. Fever on day 1-2 after talc is expected; fever with a rising oxygen requirement after empyema surgery is ongoing sepsis. A drain that stops swinging with increasing surgical emphysema is blocked or displaced and needs attention before it becomes a tension.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is an inflamed parietal pleura plus ports and drains, and the peak is early and steep. The plan has to be in place before the patient wakes: a hemithoracic block, regular simple analgesia with an anti-inflammatory if the surgeon allows it, and opioid as rescue.

PROSPECT's thoracoscopic guideline applies directly: paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started before or during surgery, a paravertebral or erector spinae plane block as first choice, serratus anterior plane block as second choice, and opioids for rescue. The author's practice is a paravertebral or erector spinae block at T4-T5 with ropivacaine 0.5% 20-25 mL before incision, with a catheter (ropivacaine 0.2% at 8-12 mL/h or as intermittent boluses) for pleurectomy and for any decortication. The extension from single shot to catheter is judgement based on the mechanism: the pain generator stays in the chest for as long as the drains do, which is days longer than a single injection lasts. There is one anatomical caveat. After a full parietal pleurectomy the paravertebral space on that side has lost its anterior wall, so a surgeon-placed paravertebral catheter leaks into the pleural cavity and out of the drain; an erector spinae catheter, which sits behind the transverse processes, is the more reliable choice in that operation. That is reasoning from anatomy, not trial evidence. See [analgesia for the trunk](../a/a02-truncal-blocks-epidural.html).

The NSAID question matters more here than anywhere else in thoracic surgery, because it is the most effective single drug for pleuritic pain and the one surgeons may forbid. The only randomised evidence, in talc pleurodesis for malignant effusion, found no loss of efficacy with NSAIDs. It has not been repeated for pleurectomy in pneumothorax, so a surgeon's refusal there is not unreasonable, and the plan must compensate: the catheter becomes more important, and low-dose ketamine or a dexmedetomidine infusion intraoperatively are reasonable additions.

For open decortication through a thoracotomy, PROSPECT's thoracotomy guideline applies: a paravertebral catheter or thoracic epidural as first-line. In a septic or coagulopathic patient neither may be safe, and an erector spinae catheter with intercostal blocks under vision is the fallback.

Rebound risk is high after a single shot. The typical failure is a young patient who is comfortable in recovery, arrives on the ward as the block fades, and meets the first physiotherapy session with a PCA that has only just been set up. Escalation triggers: sudden worsening of pain with breathlessness is a displaced or blocked drain and a recurrent pneumothorax; pain with fever and a rising white count beyond day 2 after talc deserves a look for infection.

Persistent chest wall pain and paraesthesia in the stripped area are reported after pleurectomy. No incidence figure was verified for this card.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Bronchopleural fistula and empyema with an air leak. Positive pressure escapes through the fistula and pus is driven into the good lung. Sit the patient up, tilted affected side down. Make sure the drain is working. Isolate before ventilating: a double-lumen tube into the healthy side, placed under bronchoscopic vision, either awake or after an induction that keeps spontaneous breathing until the cuff is up. Then ventilate the good lung alone. The same rules apply to the stump fistula described under [pneumonectomy](pneumonectomy.html).
- Tension pneumothorax on induction. Rising airway pressure, falling saturation and pressure, a hyper-resonant side. Disconnect, decompress with a needle or let the surgeon open the pleura, and do not wait for a radiograph.
- Secondary pneumothorax in severe emphysema or interstitial disease. One-lung ventilation may be impossible; the alternatives are intermittent apnoea, a lobar blocker, or talc through a single port under local anaesthesia and sedation.
- Awake and non-intubated surgery. Biopsy and talc under paravertebral or epidural block with sedation and spontaneous breathing is an established option for the patient too frail for general anaesthesia. The open pneumothorax collapses the lung without a tube; coughing, hypercapnia and conversion in the lateral position are the costs.
- Trapped lung. If the lung will not expand after drainage, pleurodesis cannot work and the surgeon places a tunnelled pleural catheter. The case is short and the analgesic need is small.
- Clotted haemothorax after trauma. Decortication physiology with rib fractures underneath; the earlier the clot is evacuated, the less rind there is to peel.
- Catamenial and bilateral pneumothorax, and the patient who will fly or dive. These change the surgeon's threshold, not your technique.
- For the resection that sometimes follows a pleural biopsy, see [lobectomy and wedge resection](video-assisted-and-open-lobectomy-and.html).

## Key sources

The analgesic skeleton comes from the two PROSPECT guidelines, which were built largely on lung resection trials; pleurectomy and decortication are under-represented in them, and the card's stronger push towards a catheter is author's judgement from the mechanism. TIME1 is a good randomised trial but answers the NSAID question only for talc in malignant effusion. The re-expansion oedema figure comes from one retrospective series of chest drain insertion and counts radiographic changes; clinically important oedema is much rarer. The induction rules for undrained pneumothorax and for fistula are consensus built on physiology and case reports. Statements about the inflammatory response to talc and about chronic pain after pleurectomy are given without numbers because none was verified.
