## What the surgeon is doing and why

A glioma in or beside the speech areas, the motor strip or the tracts beneath them cannot be removed by anatomy alone. Functional cortex is not where the textbook puts it in a brain that has been displaced and reorganised by a slowly growing tumour, and the tumour has no edge. The surgeon therefore finds function by testing it: a bipolar or monopolar stimulator is applied to the exposed cortex for a few seconds at a time while the patient counts, names pictures, reads or moves a hand. Stimulation that arrests speech or produces movement marks tissue that must stay. The surgeon tags those sites, resects between and beneath them, and repeats the stimulation in the white matter as the cavity deepens, with the patient performing the task continuously. A new hesitation or a weak grip is the signal to stop.

The reason for doing it this way is in the outcomes. In the pooled glioma literature, resections done with stimulation mapping carried a late severe neurological deficit in 3.4% of patients against 8.2% without it, while achieving more gross total resections (75% against 58%). Motor mapping can be done asleep with evoked potentials, but language cannot. So the operation exists for the patient with a tumour in the dominant frontal, temporal or parietal lobe, and increasingly for motor and other functions.

Everything else is an ordinary [supratentorial craniotomy](craniotomy-for-supratentorial-tumour.html): pins, scalp flap, bone flap, dural opening, resection, closure. The surgical requirement is the same slack brain. What changes is who delivers it. Without a tracheal tube you cannot hyperventilate or paralyse, so brain relaxation rests on the head-up position, an unobstructed airway, avoiding hypercapnia from over-sedation, and osmotic therapy. A sedated patient who obstructs and retains carbon dioxide produces a tight brain at exactly the moment the dura is opened. That single causal chain explains most of the technique.

Two anaesthetic patterns exist. In asleep-awake-asleep the patient has general anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway for the exposure, is woken and the airway removed for mapping, and is re-anaesthetised for closure. In monitored anaesthesia care the patient is sedated with a native airway for exposure and closure and fully awake between. A systematic review of 47 studies found both feasible and safe, with no relevant difference in failure, seizures or new deficit. Overall, awake craniotomy failed in 2% and needed conversion to general anaesthesia in 2%, and intraoperative seizures occurred in 8%. The choice follows the patient and the team: asleep phases suit a long exposure and an anxious patient; sedation suits a short one and avoids the wake-up transition.

Patient selection is the first anaesthetic act. The operation needs a person who can cooperate for an hour with their head fixed: severe anxiety, claustrophobia, dysphasia already too dense to test, a chronic cough, obstructive sleep apnoea with obesity, and an inability to lie still are relative contraindications that are discussed with the surgeon in clinic, not on the day.

The pain logic is unchanged from the asleep operation: scalp, muscle, periosteum and dura. The difference is that the block is the anaesthetic. A scalp block that misses the auriculotemporal nerve is discovered by the patient.

## Steps

### 1. Clinic visit and the morning of surgery
**Why:** The surgeon and speech therapist or neuropsychologist record baseline naming, counting and motor tasks, and choose the tests that will be used during mapping.
**Anaesthesia:** Meet the patient before the day. Describe each phase in the order it will happen, including the noise of the drill, the urinary catheter, a dry mouth, and the possibility of a seizure that will be treated. Rapport is the most effective anxiolytic available and a patient who trusts the voice beside them tolerates far more. Continue anticonvulsants and dexamethasone. Avoid benzodiazepine premedication, which blurs mapping and produces disinhibition; if anything is needed, a low-dose dexmedetomidine infusion once monitored is the better choice.

### 2. Lines, sedation and the scalp block
**Why:** The surgeon needs complete anaesthesia of the scalp from pin sites to incision for several hours.
**Anaesthesia:** Arterial line under local anaesthetic, two cannulae, and sedation started before the block because twelve scalp injections are unpleasant: propofol and remifentanil by target-controlled infusion (effect-site targets of the order of 1-2 micrograms/mL and 1-2 ng/mL), or dexmedetomidine 0.5-1 microgram/kg over 10-15 minutes then 0.2-0.7 micrograms/kg/h. In the randomised comparison the two gave the same quality of mapping, but respiratory adverse events occurred in 20% of the propofol-remifentanil group and none of the dexmedetomidine group. The block is bilateral: supraorbital, supratrochlear, zygomaticotemporal, auriculotemporal, greater and lesser occipital, with ropivacaine 0.375-0.5% or levobupivacaine with adrenaline. Calculate the total against weight and include the surgeon's infiltration: this operation uses more local anaesthetic into a more vascular bed than almost any other, and the early signs of toxicity (tinnitus, perioral tingling, agitation, a seizure) overlap with what the surgery itself can cause. Lipid emulsion is in the room.

### 3. Positioning and pins
**Why:** The head is fixed for navigation and turned so that the lesion is uppermost. The drapes are arranged as a tent so that the patient's face, one arm and one leg are visible to the tester.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient lies semi-lateral on a soft mattress with every pressure point padded, because discomfort in the shoulder or hip at hour three ends more awake craniotomies than pain in the head. Pin sites are infiltrated in addition to the block. Before the drapes go on, establish three things: you can see and reach the face; you have a clear route for a supraglottic airway or a videolaryngoscope from where you will be sitting; and the table can be levelled quickly. See [positioning](../a/a14-positioning.html) for the lateral position. A urinary catheter or sheath is placed, since mannitol in an awake patient without one is a predictable problem.

### 4. Exposure: asleep or sedated
**Why:** Scalp flap, muscle dissection, burr holes and craniotomy are the noisy, stimulating part and need no cooperation.
**Anaesthesia:** In the asleep-awake-asleep pattern, induce with propofol and remifentanil, place a supraglottic airway and ventilate with pressure support or controlled ventilation to keep carbon dioxide normal. In the sedation pattern, deepen to a level where the patient is asleep but rousable and breathing with capnography on the nasal cannulae. In either, give mannitol 0.5 g/kg IV or hypertonic saline at skin incision and keep the head up. The risk in this phase is hypoventilation: an end-tidal trace that flattens or climbs under sedation means a brain that will be tight when the dura opens, and the remedy is less sedation and a jaw thrust, not more opioid.

### 5. Dural infiltration and opening
**Why:** The dura is innervated by trigeminal branches running with the meningeal vessels, and by the vagus and upper cervical nerves near the skull base. The surgeon infiltrates around the middle meningeal artery or lays lidocaine-soaked patties on the dura before cutting it.
**Anaesthesia:** Begin the wake-up before the dura is opened, so that the patient is not coughing or straining against an open dura. Stop propofol and reduce remifentanil to a very low rate or stop it; continue dexmedetomidine at 0.1-0.2 micrograms/kg/h if it is in use. Expect 10-20 minutes to a testable patient. Dural traction and work near the temporal base can still hurt and may produce nausea or a vagal bradycardia despite a perfect scalp block: ask the surgeon to pause and add local anaesthetic.

### 6. Removing the airway and waking
**Why:** The surgeon cannot map until the patient can speak clearly and follow commands.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the transition named in the trap. Remove the supraglottic airway while the patient is still deep enough not to cough on it, or wait until fully awake and removing it on command; the half-awake state between is when coughing, biting and head movement in pins happen. Speak to the patient before they open their eyes, tell them where they are and that the head is held still. Emergence agitation is managed by voice, by time and by a small dose of dexmedetomidine, and almost never by re-sedating with propofol unless the decision has been made to abandon the awake phase.

### 7. Cortical mapping
**Why:** The surgeon stimulates the cortex at increasing current until a response or an after-discharge appears, then maps site by site while the tester runs naming, counting or motor tasks.
**Anaesthesia:** Your tasks are silence, comfort and seizure readiness. No sedative boluses during testing; remifentanil at 0.01-0.03 micrograms/kg/min is tolerated by some teams for a patient in pain, but every sedative degrades the test. Wet the lips, adjust a limb, keep talking. Stimulation-induced seizures are usually focal and brief and occurred in 8% of patients in the pooled series. The sequence is fixed and agreed beforehand: the surgeon stops stimulating and irrigates the cortex with iced crystalloid, which terminates most within seconds; if it continues, propofol 20-30 mg IV; if it generalises, protect the airway, give a further propofol dose or midazolam 1-2 mg IV, and accept that mapping may be finished for the day because the patient will be post-ictal. Load with levetiracetam if not already given.

### 8. Resection with continuous testing
**Why:** The tumour is removed between the mapped sites while the patient keeps performing. Subcortical stimulation identifies the tracts at the depth of the cavity.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the longest awake period, 30-90 minutes, and fatigue is the limiting factor. A patient who becomes drowsy, slow or tearful is tiring, hypercapnic, post-ictal or developing a deficit, and the surgeon needs to know which. Check the capnograph and glucose, and say what you see. Blood pressure is kept near the patient's normal: hypertension from discomfort or a full bladder increases bleeding in the cavity, and labetalol 5-10 mg IV is better than sedation. Nausea is treated at once. If the patient cannot continue, the choice is between closing awake under light sedation and converting to general anaesthesia.

### 9. Conversion and airway rescue
**Why:** Uncontrolled seizures, loss of airway, a tight brain, severe agitation, bleeding or air embolism can each make the awake technique untenable with the dura open.
**Anaesthesia:** The rehearsed sequence, as author's practice: call it early, ask the surgeon to cover the brain and stop, level the table, give propofol, and place a supraglottic airway from the side, which is achievable with the head rotated and fixed. A second-generation device with a gastric channel is the default; tracheal intubation by videolaryngoscope or through the device follows if ventilation is inadequate or the case will be long. Removing the pins is a last resort because it loses the field and the navigation. Venous air embolism in a spontaneously breathing head-up patient presents as a cough, chest tightness and desaturation: flood the field, lower the head.

### 10. Closure
**Why:** The dura is closed, the bone flap plated and the scalp closed, 45-60 minutes that need a still patient but no cooperation.
**Anaesthesia:** Many patients are content to stay awake with a low-dose remifentanil or dexmedetomidine infusion; others are re-sedated or re-anaesthetised with a supraglottic airway replaced from the side. The scalp block is still working. Give paracetamol 1 g IV. Remove the pins before the final wake-up if the patient has been re-anaesthetised.

### 11. Recovery
**Why:** The surgeon already has the neurological examination, which is the main advantage of the technique afterwards.
**Anaesthesia:** A deficit that appeared during resection is often transient oedema or supplementary motor area syndrome and is expected to recover; one that appears later in recovery is a haematoma until scanned. New neurological dysfunction of some degree was recorded in 17% in the pooled series. Seizures can recur in the first hours. The patient has been awake through a remarkable experience and is usually exhausted and hungry: fluids, food and regular simple analgesia.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is scalp, muscle, periosteum and dura, and the regional technique is doing double duty. The scalp block and the surgeon's infiltration are the anaesthetic for the awake phase and the analgesic for the first 6-12 hours afterwards. That fits the PROSPECT recommendation for craniotomy (paracetamol, NSAID, dexmedetomidine infusion, scalp block or infiltration, opioid as rescue) and the meta-analysis showing lower pain scores and opioid use with a scalp block.

During the operation, pain that breaks through is a block or infiltration problem and is treated with more local anaesthetic by the surgeon, inside the dose limit you have calculated, before it is treated with opioid. The predictable sites are the temporalis muscle, the dura near the middle meningeal artery and the pin sites. Positional pain in the shoulder and hip is treated by moving the limb and by paracetamol.

Afterwards the plan is regular paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor when the surgeon accepts it (the same haematoma caution as for any craniotomy, and the timing is author's judgement), and small doses of oral opioid. These patients typically need little. Dexmedetomidine, if it was the sedative, carries some analgesic benefit into recovery. There is no rebound problem: the block wears off into a pain that is already modest.

Headache with drowsiness, vomiting or a new deficit is not a pain problem and is scanned.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Over-sedation during exposure. Hypercapnia gives a tight brain, a difficult dural opening and a slow, confused wake-up. Light and breathing is better than deep and obstructed.
- The obese patient with sleep apnoea. The sedation pattern is hazardous; an asleep phase with a supraglottic airway and controlled ventilation is safer, or the surgeon reconsiders an asleep operation with motor mapping.
- Awake throughout. Some units use scalp block with no sedation at all. It needs a selected patient and a fast surgeon, and removes the transition.
- Local anaesthetic toxicity. Large doses, vascular scalp, repeated infiltration over hours. A seizure during the exposure, before any cortical stimulation, is toxicity until shown otherwise.
- The failed map. If no positive site is found, the surgeon may proceed on negative mapping or stop. Either way the anaesthetic has not failed.
- Dysphasia worsening during resection. Stop, wait, irrigate. If it does not recover in minutes the resection ends.
- Children and adolescents. Possible in a motivated teenager with a parent in clinic beforehand; otherwise asleep with evoked potentials.
- Motor mapping asleep. Total intravenous anaesthesia with no neuromuscular block after intubation, and a bite block: see [neuroanaesthesia essentials](../a/a08-neuroanaesthesia-essentials.html).
- Related awake neurosurgery. Electrode placement for [deep brain stimulation](deep-brain-stimulation-and-epilepsy-surgery.html) shares the frame, the scalp block and the sedation rules.

## Key sources

The case for mapping rests on a large meta-analysis of observational series, not on randomised trials, but the effect is large and consistent. The choice between asleep-awake-asleep and sedation rests on a systematic review of 47 mostly observational studies that found no relevant difference; the one signal, more conversions with the asleep pattern, came from a single retrospective study and disappeared in sensitivity analysis. The sedative choice has one randomised trial of 50 patients. The seizure sequence, the conversion drill and the infusion doses are consensus practice and author's judgement; the doses given are typical ranges, not trial regimens.
