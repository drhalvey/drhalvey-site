## What the surgeon is doing and why

Most breast cancers are now found small, and for a small cancer the survival after removing the tumour with a rim of normal tissue and irradiating the rest of the breast equals the survival after mastectomy. So the operation has two separate jobs. The first is to take out the cancer with a clear margin and leave a breast that still looks like one. The second is to find out whether the cancer has reached the axilla, by removing the first one to four nodes that drain the tumour (the sentinel nodes) rather than clearing the whole axilla. A negative sentinel node spares the patient an axillary dissection and most of its arm morbidity.

The surgeon's problems are finding things that cannot be felt. A screen-detected tumour is impalpable, so it is marked before surgery with a hookwire placed under imaging that morning, or with a magnetic, radar or radioactive seed placed days earlier. The sentinel node is found with a tracer: technetium-99m colloid injected into the breast hours before, a blue dye (patent blue V, isosulfan blue or methylene blue) injected after induction, a magnetic iron oxide tracer, indocyanine green, or two of these together. The dual technique of isotope plus blue dye has the highest identification rate and is why the dye is still in use.

The sequence is short:

1. Inject the blue dye, massage the breast, and wait a few minutes for it to reach the axilla.
2. Open the axilla through a 3-4 cm incision below the hairline, go through the clavipectoral fascia and take the hot, blue or palpably abnormal nodes.
3. Excise the tumour as a cylinder of breast from skin or subcutaneous fat down to pectoral fascia, orientate it with clips or sutures, and X-ray the specimen to prove the lesion and its marker are in it.
4. Take cavity shaves if the X-ray margin is close, mark the cavity with clips for the radiotherapist, and close, either simply or by moving local breast tissue (oncoplastic closure) to fill the defect.

What is left alone matters to you. Pectoralis major is not cut, the chest wall is not opened, and in a sentinel biopsy the axillary contents are sampled and not stripped. The pain logic follows: this is skin and soft tissue, two small wounds closed without tension, and nothing that movement loads. The axillary wound hurts more than the breast wound because the arm moves over it and because the intercostobrachial nerve crosses the field and is stretched or divided. Acute pain is mild. The long tail is a different matter: in the Danish national cohort nearly half of women reported pain in the operated area two to three years after breast cancer treatment, with young age and radiotherapy as risk factors and sentinel biopsy carrying lower odds than axillary clearance. Acute analgesia for this operation is easy; the chronic pain is driven by nerve handling and radiotherapy and has not been shown to be prevented by any block.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative localisation and tracer
**Why:** The lesion is marked and the isotope injected in radiology or nuclear medicine before the patient reaches you, so that the surgeon can find an impalpable tumour and a node with a gamma probe.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient arrives late, anxious, sometimes fasted for longer than planned, and with a wire protruding from the breast under a dressing or cup. Do not let the wire be moved, taped flat or caught during transfer: a displaced wire means a failed excision. The radiation dose to staff from technetium-99m is negligible and needs no precautions beyond routine. Ask about previous reactions to dyes, and note that cosmetics and some medicines contain related triarylmethane dyes, which is the proposed route of sensitisation in patients who react on first surgical exposure.

### 2. Induction and positioning
**Why:** The surgeon needs the breast and axilla exposed together, the arm out, and sometimes the patient sat up to judge symmetry.
**Anaesthesia:** A supraglottic airway with propofol and a short-acting opioid suits almost every case; there is no need for neuromuscular block. Site the cannula in the contralateral arm. That convention is sound for access and for the surgeon's convenience; the evidence that a cannula or a cuff on the operated side causes lymphoedema after sentinel biopsy alone is weak, and in bilateral surgery the arm with the lesser axillary procedure is the sensible choice. Abduct the arm to less than 90 degrees with the forearm supinated or neutral, and secure the patient if the table will be broken to sitting. Give dexamethasone 8 mg IV now: PROSPECT recommends it for analgesia and this is a high-risk group for nausea.

### 3. Blue dye injection
**Why:** One to two millilitres of dye is injected under the areola or around the tumour and massaged, so that lymphatics carry it to the sentinel node and stain it for the surgeon to see.
**Anaesthesia:** Note the time on the chart. Three things follow. First, the oximeter reading falls by a few percent within minutes and can stay down for an hour or more with patent blue and isosulfan blue, because the dye absorbs at the red wavelength; methylene blue produces a deeper, shorter dip. The patient is not hypoxic, and the artefact is the reason you cannot use saturation to reassure yourself about the second thing. Second, anaphylaxis: a meta-analysis of 61,951 sentinel procedures put the rate at 0.083% for breast surgery, highest with isosulfan blue (0.16%) and lower with small volumes and intradermal injection. Onset is often slower than with an intravenous drug, typically 15-30 minutes after injection, when the surgeon is in the axilla and nobody is thinking about the dye. Hypotension is the usual first sign; urticaria may be blue-tinged wheals. Treat as any perioperative anaphylaxis with adrenaline and fluid, and expect a protracted or biphasic course because the dye depot stays in the breast. Third, the skin takes a grey-blue cast and the urine turns green for a day, which the recovery staff and the patient should be told about.

### 4. Axillary incision and sentinel node retrieval
**Why:** The surgeon follows the gamma probe and the blue lymphatic through the clavipectoral fascia to the node, removes it, and checks the background count is low. Some units send the node for intraoperative assessment and proceed to clearance if it is positive.
**Anaesthesia:** Brief stimulation as the fascia is opened and the node is pulled on. Diathermy close to the thoracodorsal and long thoracic nerves and to pectoralis produces muscle twitches; the surgeon uses them as a guide, so paralysis is unhelpful. If the plan includes an intraoperative node result, the operation may become an [axillary clearance](mastectomy-axillary-clearance-and-implant-reconstruction.html) with 30-45 minutes added and a different pain picture; know before induction whether that is possible.

### 5. Tumour excision
**Why:** The surgeon incises over or near the lesion (or around the areola for cosmesis), raises thin flaps and excises a full-thickness cylinder of breast down to the pectoral fascia around the wire tip or seed, guided by palpation, a probe or intraoperative ultrasound.
**Anaesthesia:** The stimulus is modest and constant. Adrenaline-containing local anaesthetic infiltrated by the surgeon before incision reduces bleeding and volatile requirement; count it towards the total dose if a block has also been done. Blood loss is trivial.

### 6. Specimen radiograph and cavity shaves
**Why:** The orientated specimen is X-rayed in theatre or sent to radiology to prove the target and its marker are inside it and to show which margin is close. A close margin is re-shaved there and then to avoid a second operation.
**Anaesthesia:** A wait of 5-15 minutes with the wound open and no stimulation. Lighten the anaesthetic to match rather than treating the blood pressure, and use the pause to give the NSAID and paracetamol if they were not given orally beforehand.

### 7. Oncoplastic reshaping and closure
**Why:** A defect of more than about a fifth of the breast volume deforms the breast after radiotherapy, so the surgeon mobilises adjacent glandular flaps, or performs a therapeutic mammoplasty using breast reduction pedicles, sometimes with a symmetrising reduction on the other side. Haemostasis is meticulous because the cavity is a dead space.
**Anaesthesia:** A simple closure takes ten minutes. A therapeutic mammoplasty turns the case into a two to three hour [breast reduction](breast-reduction-augmentation-and-abdominoplasty.html): both arms out, the patient sat up for assessment, more warming, a urinary catheter rarely, and a stronger case for a Pecs II or serratus plane block. The surgeon infiltrates both wounds with long-acting local anaesthetic at closure; make sure it happens.

### 8. Emergence and discharge
**Why:** The cavity is closed without a drain in most hands, and a firm dressing or supportive bra is applied.
**Anaesthesia:** Avoid coughing and a pressure surge at emergence: a cavity haematoma is the commonest reason for a same-day return to theatre. A patient who has had blue dye stays long enough to cover the window for a delayed reaction; the author's practice is not to discharge within an hour of injection, which the length of the operation normally guarantees. Send her home on regular paracetamol and an NSAID with a small supply of a weak or low-dose opioid.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is skin and soft tissue with no tension and no loaded structure, so the plan is systemic and simple, and the "block" is the surgeon's infiltration. PROSPECT's guideline for oncological breast surgery recommends paracetamol and an NSAID given before or during surgery and continued afterwards, with pre-operative gabapentin and dexamethasone, opioids kept for rescue, and regional techniques (paravertebral or pectoral nerves block) or wound infiltration considered for major breast surgery. A wide local excision is not major breast surgery in that sense.

The defensible default is therefore paracetamol 1 g and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor orally before surgery, dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction, short-acting opioid intraoperatively, and infiltration of both wounds by the surgeon with ropivacaine or bupivacaine within the weight-based maximum. Gabapentinoids carry the PROSPECT recommendation, but for a day-case patient who needs to walk out clear-headed the author's judgement is that the sedation and dizziness outweigh a small analgesic gain; that is opinion, not evidence. A Pecs II or serratus plane block is reasonable for therapeutic mammoplasty, for a patient on long-term opioids, or where sedation rather than general anaesthesia is planned. It is not needed for the standard case, and it adds local anaesthetic to a field the surgeon is about to infiltrate.

Expect worst pain of 2-3 out of 10 on the first evening and day 1, mostly axillary and on arm movement, falling to little by day 3. In the 179-procedure cohort the lesson for operations like this one is that "minor" surgery scores badly when the analgesia is treated as optional, so the regular simple analgesics are the plan and not a courtesy. Escalation triggers: a tense, enlarging, painful breast in the first hours is a haematoma; burning pain and numbness on the inner upper arm is the intercostobrachial nerve and is managed with explanation and time; pain that is still present at three months is common and worth naming early to the surgeon or a pain clinic.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Blue dye anaphylaxis. The emergency variant of this card. Have adrenaline to hand from the moment of injection, think of it at 15-30 minutes, and do not wait for skin signs under the drapes. Refer for testing afterwards: there is demonstrated cross-reactivity between isosulfan blue and methylene blue, so "use the other dye next time" is not safe advice without a result.
- Spurious desaturation. A fall to the low 90s after dye with unchanged ventilation, capnography and colour (allowing for the grey-blue tinge) is artefact. A blood gas settles the question if there is real doubt. Methylene blue also carries the serotonin toxicity interaction with serotonergic antidepressants at intravenous doses; the small subcutaneous dose used for mapping makes this unlikely.
- Conversion to axillary clearance or mastectomy. Decided by an intraoperative node result or an unexpected finding. The consent form tells you whether it is on the table.
- Neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Anthracyclines and trastuzumab can impair ventricular function, taxanes cause neuropathy, and the patient may be anaemic or recently neutropenic. An echocardiogram is normally on file. A venous port is usually on the opposite side and can be used with a non-coring needle and full asepsis.
- Pregnancy-associated cancer. Surgery proceeds in any trimester; isotope alone is used for mapping and blue dye is avoided.
- Re-excision of margins. A shorter repeat of step 5 two to four weeks later, through an inflamed cavity that bleeds more and hurts slightly more.
- Men and augmented breasts. An implant beneath the field can be punctured; an implant is not a reason to change the anaesthetic.
- Day-case failure. Almost always nausea or a haematoma, seldom pain. The antiemetic plan matters more to discharge than the block does.
- No invasive monitoring, blood or critical care is needed for this operation in its own right; the only route to the intensive care unit is the dye.

## Key sources

The analgesic recommendations rest on PROSPECT's 2020 guideline, which pooled mastectomy and conserving surgery and whose regional recommendations apply to the major end of the range. The anaphylaxis figures come from a large meta-analysis of heterogeneous reports and are likely to understate mild reactions. The timing of dye reactions, the oximeter artefact, the cannula convention and the advice on gabapentinoids for day cases are consensus or author's judgement and are not drawn from the listed sources.
