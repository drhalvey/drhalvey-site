## What the surgeon is doing and why

The uterus is held in the pelvis by its blood supply and its ligaments. On each side, from the top down, it is attached by the round ligament (to the groin), the tube and utero-ovarian ligament (to the ovary) or, if the ovary is going too, the infundibulopelvic ligament carrying the ovarian vessels from the pelvic brim; then the broad ligament, a double sheet of peritoneum; then the uterine artery, which arrives at the level of the internal cervical os having crossed over the ureter about 1.5-2 cm lateral to the cervix; then the cardinal and uterosacral ligaments. In front, the bladder is stuck to the lower uterus and cervix. Below, the cervix is continuous with the vagina. A hysterectomy is the division of each of those attachments in turn, from the top down, followed by a circular cut around the top of the vagina that frees the specimen.

Doing that through ports instead of a laparotomy trades a 12 cm wound through the abdominal wall for four or five punctures and a longer operation in a harder position. The surgeon needs a deep, empty pelvis for two hours or more, which means pneumoperitoneum and a head-down tilt steep enough to keep the small bowel in the upper abdomen. A manipulator with a cup around the cervix is fixed in the uterus so that an assistant can push it up and away from the ureters and so that the rim of the cup marks where to cut the vagina. The specimen comes out through the vagina, whole or cut into strips inside a bag, and the vault is sutured from above (total laparoscopic and robotic) or from below (laparoscopically assisted vaginal hysterectomy, where the upper pedicles are taken laparoscopically and the rest is finished as a [vaginal hysterectomy](vaginal-hysterectomy-and-prolapse-repair.html)).

The robot changes the ergonomics, not the operation. Wristed instruments and a stable three-dimensional camera make suturing and dissection in a deep, obese pelvis easier, which is why endometrial cancer in women with a body mass index of 40 or 50 has become the typical robotic case. For the anaesthetist the consequences are a steeper tilt (often 25-40 degrees), a longer case, arms and face buried under equipment, and a patient who is mechanically connected to a machine that must be undocked before the table can move or the chest can be reached.

Three structures are at risk and each has a moment. The ureter is vulnerable at the infundibulopelvic ligament, at the uterine artery and at the vaginal angle. The bladder is vulnerable when it is dissected off the cervix, especially after a caesarean. The bowel is vulnerable at entry and from stray energy. Many surgeons finish with a cystoscopy to see both ureteric jets and an intact bladder.

The pain logic is laparoscopic with a bigger visceral component than the short cases on the [laparoscopic gynaecology](laparoscopic-gynaecology.html) card. Port wounds and peritoneal stretch behave as they always do. Added to them is a pelvic wound of divided pedicles, a raw bladder base and a sutured vault, which produces a deep central ache that lasts two to three days and is the main reason these patients need more opioid on the first night than a sterilisation does. It is still nothing like the [abdominal operation](abdominal-hysterectomy-and-myomectomy.html), because nothing is loaded when the patient coughs or sits up. PROSPECT accordingly recommends no block: paracetamol, an NSAID and dexamethasone, with opioid for rescue.

## Steps

### 1. Induction, airway and lines you will not see again
**Why:** The surgeon needs two to four hours of complete stillness with the abdomen inflated and the patient steeply head down.
**Anaesthesia:** Tracheal tube, secured as for a prone case, with the cuff pressure checked. Two cannulae on extension lines brought to the head, because both arms will be wrapped at the sides and a tissued cannula under the drapes is not discovered until the drugs stop working. Give dexamethasone 8 mg IV, paracetamol 1 g and the NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor at induction, antibiotic within the hour, and decide on TIVA against volatile on nausea risk. Protect the eyes with tape and padding: instruments and the camera arm pass over the face, and corneal abrasion is one of the commoner injuries of head-down robotic surgery. If intrathecal morphine is part of the plan it goes in before induction.

### 2. Positioning and the slide test
**Why:** Low lithotomy for vaginal access and the manipulator, arms tucked so the surgeon and assistant can stand at the shoulders or the robot arms can swing, and a mattress that grips the patient so she does not slide towards the head when tilted.
**Anaesthesia:** Sliding is the mechanism of the brachial plexus injury: the shoulders stop against a brace while the body keeps moving, or the tucked arm is dragged. Use a non-slip surface in contact with skin, avoid shoulder braces (or place them over the acromioclavicular joints, never the root of the neck), keep the head neutral, and ask for a full test tilt before draping while you watch the shoulders, the tube and the airway pressure. Boots take the weight on the heels; calves must be free, because hours of lithotomy with head-down tilt lowers leg perfusion and compartment syndrome is reported after long cases. An obese patient who cannot be ventilated at an acceptable pressure in the test position will not be ventilated better with gas in the abdomen: settle the maximum tilt with the surgeon now.

### 3. Manipulator, entry and pneumoperitoneum
**Why:** The manipulator and colpotomy cup are fixed to the cervix; the abdomen is entered at the umbilicus or the left upper quadrant and inflated; accessory ports are placed under vision.
**Anaesthesia:** The cervix and peritoneal stretch are both vagal stimuli: bradycardia or a pause at first insufflation is treated by stopping, releasing the gas and giving glycopyrrolate 200-400 micrograms IV. Hypotension with a falling end-tidal carbon dioxide after the first trocar is a major vessel injury or gas embolism, and is the one moment to call for the haemorrhage protocol on suspicion. An orogastric tube deflates the stomach for a left upper quadrant entry.

### 4. Steep Trendelenburg and docking
**Why:** The bowel is swept out of the pelvis and the table is fixed in position; the robot is brought in and its arms attached to the ports.
**Anaesthesia:** From here the physiology is set for the case. Venous return rises and so does afterload from the pneumoperitoneum: a normal heart tolerates it, a failing or stenotic one may not, and pulmonary hypertension is worsened by the hypercapnia. The diaphragm moves cephalad, compliance halves in the obese, and the tube can enter the right main bronchus: listen again. Intracranial and intraocular pressures rise and stay raised. Restrict crystalloid to roughly 1-1.5 L for the case, since every extra litre ends up as oedema of the face, conjunctivae and larynx. Once the robot is docked the table cannot be levelled without undocking unless table and robot are an integrated pair; rehearse with the team how long an emergency undock takes, because that is your delay to chest compressions or a re-intubation.

### 5. Upper pedicles: round ligaments, tubes and ovaries
**Why:** The round ligaments are divided to open the broad ligament; the tubes are removed; the ovaries are either conserved (dividing the utero-ovarian ligament) or removed (dividing the infundibulopelvic ligament after identifying the ureter beneath it).
**Anaesthesia:** A quiet phase haemodynamically. Use it to settle ventilation: pressure control, PEEP 5-10 cmH2O, a rate increase to hold end-tidal carbon dioxide, and an accepted mild hypercapnia if plateau pressure approaches 30 cmH2O. Maintain and measure the neuromuscular block; with rigid arms fixed through the abdominal wall, a cough or a buck moves the patient against the instruments.

### 6. Bladder flap and ureters
**Why:** The peritoneum between bladder and uterus is opened and the bladder pushed down off the cervix and upper vagina; the ureters are seen or dissected so that the next step is safe.
**Anaesthesia:** Haematuria or gas in the catheter bag is a bladder injury and should be reported from the head end the moment it is seen. Previous caesareans make this step slow and bloody. Surgeons vary in asking for a dye to see the ureteric jets at cystoscopy later; fluorescein, methylene blue and indigo carmine have all been used, methylene blue interferes with the pulse oximeter reading for a few minutes and is avoided in patients on serotonergic drugs.

### 7. Uterine arteries and cardinal ligaments
**Why:** The uterine vessels are skeletonised and sealed at the level of the internal os, then the cardinal ligaments are divided down to the cup. This devascularises the uterus and is the step closest to the ureter.
**Anaesthesia:** The one point at which brisk arterial bleeding can occur in an otherwise dry operation. A slipped uterine artery retracts towards the ureter and the surgeon may want to control it slowly instead of blindly; support the pressure and keep the field still. Conversion to laparotomy for bleeding means undocking, levelling and a Pfannenstiel or midline incision in a patient whose analgesic plan was built for ports: see the pain plan.

### 8. Colpotomy and delivery of the uterus
**Why:** The vagina is cut circumferentially on the cup and the uterus is delivered through it; a uterus too large to pass is cut up within a containment bag.
**Anaesthesia:** Opening the vagina vents the pneumoperitoneum unless a balloon or the specimen itself occludes it, so the view and the pressure fluctuate for a few minutes and the surgeon may ask for the insufflator flow to be raised. Extraction of a large uterus can take 20-30 minutes. This is the contaminated step and the reason some protocols add anaerobic cover.

### 9. Vault closure and cystoscopy
**Why:** The vaginal cuff is closed with a running barbed suture, incorporating the uterosacral ligaments for support; haemostasis is checked at low insufflation pressure; cystoscopy confirms two ureteric jets and an intact bladder.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon will drop the gas pressure to 6-8 mmHg to look for venous bleeding that the pneumoperitoneum was tamponading; keep the patient relaxed for it. Give the last analgesic doses now. If the ureteric jets are slow to appear the surgeon may request a small fluid bolus or furosemide 10 mg IV.

### 10. Undocking, levelling and deflation
**Why:** Instruments and ports are removed, the gas released and the port sites infiltrated and closed; fascial closure at any port of 10 mm or more.
**Anaesthesia:** Before the gas is fully out and with the patient still head down, give a recruitment manoeuvre (several slow inflations to 30-40 cmH2O) with the port valves open: the Cochrane review found that a specific gas-release technique reduced shoulder-tip pain at 24 hours. Then level the table and wait. Venous pooling on levelling drops the pressure in a fluid-restricted patient; a small vasopressor dose is usually all that is needed. Look at the face, tongue and conjunctivae. After a long steep case, marked chemosis and a swollen tongue predict a swollen larynx: sit the patient 30 degrees head up, check for a cuff leak, and delay extubation if there is none. Reverse the neuromuscular block against a quantitative monitor.

### 11. Recovery and discharge
**Why:** The surgeon wants early mobilisation, an early trial of void and, on a same-day pathway, discharge within hours.
**Anaesthesia:** Pain in recovery is central pelvic ache plus port sites; fentanyl 25-50 microgram IV increments and an early oral opioid deal with it, and the first regular paracetamol and NSAID doses should already be charted. Nausea threatens same-day discharge more than pain does. Pain that is escalating, with distension, a falling pressure or a rising heart rate, is bleeding from a pedicle or the vault. A patient who cannot pass urine after catheter removal goes home with a plan, not an indefinite wait. Flank pain or a fever in the following days is a ureteric injury until imaging says otherwise.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is laparoscopic plus a pelvic visceral wound, moderate for two days and gone by a week, so the plan is systemic. PROSPECT's review of 56 trials recommends paracetamol, an NSAID and dexamethasone as the baseline, with opioid for rescue. It found the evidence for TAP blocks and pregabalin inconsistent, no evidence to support intraperitoneal local anaesthetic or port-site infiltration, and a reduction in shoulder but not abdominal pain from lower insufflation pressure and conditioned gas. Port-site infiltration is kept on this card because it is harmless and cheap, not because it is proven.

The defensible default: paracetamol 1 g and a COX-2 inhibitor or NSAID before incision, dexamethasone 8 mg IV, intraoperative short-acting opioid with a modest longer-acting dose before waking, the gas-release manoeuvre, port infiltration, then regular paracetamol and NSAID for five to seven days with an oral opioid for the first two or three nights. That plan supports same-day discharge in a selected patient.

Regional techniques are options, not routine. A later meta-analysis of quadratus lumborum block (8 trials, 540 patients) found a 24-hour opioid saving of 4.6 mg morphine equivalents, below the threshold for clinical importance, with no effect on nausea. Intrathecal morphine is more powerful: in a randomised trial of 96 robotic hysterectomies, morphine 100 micrograms with bupivacaine reduced intravenous opioid from 22.8 to 9.4 mg and the highest recovery pain score from 5.3 to 2.0. Against that stand pruritus, urinary retention, the need for overnight respiratory observation and incompatibility with same-day discharge. The author's practice is to reserve intrathecal morphine 100-150 micrograms for the patient staying overnight in whom the baseline will fall short: opioid tolerance, chronic pelvic pain, or a contraindication to NSAIDs. A bilateral TAP or quadratus lumborum block is a reasonable lesser step for the same patients on a day-case pathway.

Chronic pain deserves a sentence before surgery. In the Danish national cohort a third of women had pain one year after benign hysterectomy and 14% had it on more than two days a week; preoperative pelvic pain, pain as the indication and pain elsewhere each roughly tripled the odds. The woman having her uterus removed for pain is therefore the one most likely to still have pain, and to have a difficult first night. The association of spinal anaesthesia with less chronic pain in that cohort is observational and is not a reason on its own to add a spinal.

Rebound is not a feature. If the operation is converted to laparotomy, the mechanism is abdominal wall and a pre-induction spinal is no longer available: place bilateral TAP or quadratus lumborum blocks or rectus sheath catheters before waking and start a PCA.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Severe obesity. The patient who benefits most from avoiding a laparotomy is the one least able to tolerate the position. Ramped intubation, a test tilt, PEEP and recruitment, an agreed tilt limit, an arterial line when ventilation is marginal, and extubation sitting up. Untreated sleep apnoea plus opioid plus a swollen airway earns a monitored bed.
- Emergency undocking. Cardiac arrest, a lost airway or uncontrollable bleeding require the instruments out and the robot away before anything else can be done. Know the time and the roles.
- Long cases. Beyond three to four hours head down: airway oedema, corneal abrasion, pressure injury to the occiput and shoulders, brachial plexus neuropraxia, calf compartment syndrome and, rarely, ischaemic optic neuropathy. A five-minute levelling break partway through is a reasonable request in a very long case.
- Subcutaneous emphysema and hypercapnia. Carbon dioxide tracking into the tissues from a port raises the end-tidal value progressively and can reach the neck and face; crepitus at the end plus a high carbon dioxide means a period of ventilation before extubation.
- Laparoscopically assisted vaginal hysterectomy. A shorter laparoscopic phase and a vaginal finish; the pain is the same and the position alternates.
- Subtotal hysterectomy and morcellation. The cervix is left and the body removed in strips within a bag; shorter, with no vault wound, and slightly less pain.
- Large fibroid uterus. More bleeding, a long extraction and a higher conversion rate; have blood grouped and the conversion plan agreed.
- Malignancy. Endometrial cancer staging adds sentinel or pelvic nodes and time. Radical hysterectomy for cervical cancer is done open in current practice and is covered on the [gynaecological oncology](gynaecological-oncology.html) card.
- Ureteric and bladder injury. Usually found at cystoscopy or days later; from the anaesthetic side the only signs are haematuria and gas in the catheter bag.
- Vault haematoma and dehiscence. Late presentations with pain, fever or bleeding; a return to theatre is short and done in lithotomy.

## Key sources

The analgesic baseline rests on a PROSPECT review of 56 randomised trials and is solid. The evidence that fascial plane blocks add little comes from a meta-analysis of eight small trials; the evidence for intrathecal morphine is one moderate-sized randomised trial in robotic surgery plus cohort data, so its place on this card is the author's selective reading. The positional physiology and the injury list are drawn from a narrative review and from standard teaching, and the fluid restriction figure is a convention with no trial behind it. The chronic pain figures are from a single national questionnaire study of benign hysterectomy by any route.
