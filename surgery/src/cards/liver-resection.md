## What the surgeon is doing and why

The liver is the only solid organ that grows back, so a surgeon can remove up to about 70-75% of a healthy liver, or much less of a diseased one, provided the piece left behind has an artery, a portal vein, a hepatic vein and a bile duct. Resection is done for colorectal metastases (the commonest indication, usually after chemotherapy), hepatocellular carcinoma (usually in a cirrhotic liver), cholangiocarcinoma, and a few benign lesions. The anatomy that makes it possible is segmental: eight segments, each with its own inflow pedicle, drained by three hepatic veins that run between segments and empty straight into the vena cava just under the diaphragm.

The surgical problem is that the liver is a sponge of blood with two inflows and a low-pressure outflow that cannot be clamped without clamping the cava. Bleeding during transection comes mostly from the hepatic veins and their tributaries, and it comes backwards, from the vena cava. So the operation is built around two controls.

```
 Bleeding source          Who controls it   How
 -----------------------  ----------------  -----------------------------
 Inflow (hepatic artery   surgeon           Pringle manoeuvre: clamp the
 and portal vein)                           porta hepatis, 15 min on,
                                            5 min off
 Backflow (hepatic veins  anaesthetist      low central venous pressure
 from the vena cava)                        until the specimen is out
```

The sequence is: open and assess (intraoperative ultrasound to map tumours against vessels), mobilise the liver off the diaphragm and the cava, control the inflow and outflow to the part being removed, divide the parenchyma with an ultrasonic aspirator or energy device while clipping every vessel and duct that crosses the plane, deliver the specimen, and secure haemostasis and bile leaks on the cut surface. A major hepatectomy (three or more segments) follows the same logic as a wedge, with more at stake at each step.

What is left matters as much as what is taken. The remnant must cope with the whole portal flow and the whole metabolic load on the first postoperative night. Chemotherapy-injured, steatotic or cirrhotic liver tolerates a smaller resection, and post-hepatectomy liver failure (rising bilirubin and INR, lactate that will not clear, hypoglycaemia, encephalopathy) is the complication that determines mortality. Everything you do to the remnant's perfusion, oxygen delivery and drug load is judged against that.

The pain logic: for open surgery this is the upper abdominal incision at its worst. The right subcostal incision, often extended up the midline (reverse-L or "hockey stick") or across to the left (rooftop), cuts rectus and the oblique muscles transversely beneath the costal margin, and a fixed retractor then lifts the right ribs towards the ceiling for the duration. The wall hurts with every breath and cough for a week, the costal margin and costochondral joints ache for weeks, and the diaphragm, stripped of its attachments to the liver, refers pain to the right shoulder. That duration and re-provocation earn the 5. Laparoscopic and robotic resections leave ports and a Pfannenstiel or small midline extraction wound and behave like a 3.

## Steps

### 1. Preassessment
**Why:** The surgeon is calculating the future liver remnant and may have arranged portal vein embolisation weeks earlier to grow it.
**Anaesthesia:** Two different patients. The metastasis patient has a structurally normal liver that has had oxaliplatin or irinotecan, and the usual comorbidity of a 65-year-old. The hepatocellular carcinoma patient has cirrhosis: grade it (Child-Pugh, MELD, platelet count as a marker of portal hypertension), look for varices, ascites, renal dysfunction and cardiomyopathy. For both, establish the planned extent of resection, because the analgesic technique depends on it.

### 2. Regional technique and induction
**Why:** Nothing surgical yet.
**Anaesthesia:** Decide the technique against the expected coagulopathy. After major hepatectomy the INR rises to a peak on day 1-3 and platelets fall, which is the day an epidural catheter would be due out; removal is then delayed or covered with plasma, and that is the case against the epidural for big resections. Intrathecal morphine 150-300 micrograms before induction carries no catheter. A normal preoperative INR and a planned minor resection make an epidural straightforward. Routine induction with a tracheal tube; arterial line; two large cannulae; a central line for major resection. Check blood is available before knife to skin.

### 3. Incision, retractor and intraoperative ultrasound
**Why:** A subcostal incision with a costal margin retractor gives the surgeon the dome of the liver; ultrasound defines the resection.
**Anaesthesia:** Begin fluid restriction from induction: maintenance at about 1 mL/kg/h, no pre-loading, and treat anaesthetic vasodilatation with a vasopressor infusion. The surgeon may find more disease than expected and change the plan; ask.

### 4. Mobilisation of the liver
**Why:** The falciform, triangular and coronary ligaments are divided and the right lobe is rotated up and to the left off the diaphragm and the vena cava; short hepatic veins entering the cava directly are ligated.
**Anaesthesia:** Rotating the liver kinks or compresses the vena cava: venous return falls and the arterial trace drops within seconds, recovering when the liver is let down. Tell the surgeon, because they cannot see your monitor. A torn short hepatic vein or caval tear here is the first opportunity for sudden large bleeding, with no inflow clamp able to help.

### 5. Inflow control and the Pringle manoeuvre
**Why:** The hepatic artery and portal vein to the segment or lobe are dissected and divided at the hilum, or the whole porta hepatis is intermittently clamped to stop inflow during transection.
**Anaesthesia:** Clamping the portal triad raises systemic vascular resistance and mean arterial pressure by a small amount and reduces venous return by a small amount; it is usually well tolerated. Note the clamp times aloud and on the chart: 15 minutes on and 5 off is typical, with a shorter limit in cirrhosis. Release returns cold, acidotic, potassium-rich splanchnic blood and a brief fall in pressure.

### 6. Parenchymal transection under low central venous pressure
**Why:** The liver is divided along the planned plane; every crossing vein is a side hole in the vena cava, and the pressure behind it decides how much it bleeds.
**Anaesthesia:** This is your step. Keep the central venous pressure under about 5 mmHg, or the cava visibly slack, by having given little fluid; add slight head-down tilt, a low tidal volume with minimal PEEP, and if needed a low-dose glyceryl trinitrate infusion or a small dose of furosemide, though methods vary and none is proven best. Hold arterial pressure with noradrenaline. The meta-analysis of eight trials found low central venous pressure reduced blood loss by about 310 mL without changing morbidity. Accept a low urine output for this hour. The two dangers are stated in the trap: a vein open to air at sub-atmospheric pressure entrains it (a fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide, hypotension, a new murmur: flood the field, head down, 100% oxygen, aspirate the central line), and a sudden bleed in a deliberately empty patient needs rapid transfusion at once.

### 7. Hepatic vein division and specimen out
**Why:** The hepatic vein draining the resected part is stapled close to the cava and the specimen is delivered.
**Anaesthesia:** The stapler firing next to the cava is the moment of maximum risk for torrential bleeding. Have blood in the room and the rapid infuser primed. Once the surgeon says the specimen is out and the surface is dry, refill: 500-1,000 mL of balanced crystalloid or albumin against stroke volume, wean the vasopressor, and send a gas for haemoglobin, lactate, calcium and glucose.

### 8. Total vascular exclusion (selected cases)
**Why:** For tumours on the caval confluence the surgeon clamps the portal triad and the vena cava above and below the liver, isolating it completely.
**Anaesthesia:** The opposite of low central venous pressure: caval clamping removes up to half of venous return, so volume load beforehand, test-clamp for a few minutes, and support with vasopressor. Patients who do not tolerate the test clamp need veno-venous bypass. Unclamping brings acid, potassium and cold. This variant needs a central line, cardiac output monitoring and a second anaesthetist.

### 9. Haemostasis, bile leak test and closure
**Why:** The raw surface is sealed with argon, sutures and topical agents; the bile ducts are tested; a drain is sometimes left; the muscle layers are closed.
**Anaesthesia:** Correct coagulopathy by viscoelastic test or laboratory result: fibrinogen first, platelets and plasma as indicated. Tranexamic acid is not the answer by default: in a trial of 1,245 liver resections it did not reduce transfusion or blood loss and complications were more frequent. Catheters for the wound go in now if they are the plan, placed by the surgeon in the transversus plane or the rectus sheath on both sides of the incision, or by ultrasound after closure.

### 10. Emergence
**Why:** The surgeon wants the patient awake, extubated and assessable.
**Anaesthesia:** Extubate in theatre unless transfusion has been massive or the patient is cold and acidotic. Drugs cleared by the liver last longer after a major resection: titrate opioid to effect, avoid benzodiazepines, and reverse neuromuscular block against a monitor. A patient who is slow to wake after major hepatectomy needs a glucose before anything else.

### 11. The first 72 hours
**Why:** The surgeon is watching for bleeding, bile leak and liver failure.
**Anaesthesia:** Lactate, INR, bilirubin, glucose and phosphate tell the story: lactate should fall within 12-24 hours; INR peaks on day 1-3 and then recovers; phosphate is consumed by the regenerating liver and needs replacing. Paracetamol is continued at a reduced dose (for example 1 g IV eight-hourly, less in cirrhosis or a small remnant) and held if the remnant is failing; NSAIDs depend on kidneys and platelets. Start low molecular weight heparin on schedule even with a raised INR: the laboratory number overstates the bleeding tendency, and in the trial cohort venous thromboembolism occurred in 4.1%, and in 6.7% after the largest resections.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a transverse muscle-cutting incision under the ribs, severe on movement for a week, in a patient whose ability to clear analgesic drugs and to make clotting factors has just been reduced. A continuous regional technique is indicated; the argument is over which.

PROSPECT's 2021 review of open liver resection recommends paracetamol and an NSAID, combined with either thoracic epidural analgesia or bilateral oblique subcostal TAP blocks, with systemic opioid as rescue. It found only limited procedure-specific evidence for intrathecal morphine, quadratus lumborum and paravertebral blocks and continuous wound infiltration, and none for intravenous lidocaine, ketamine, dexamethasone or gabapentinoids in this operation. The enhanced recovery literature in liver surgery has been more cautious about routine epidurals than PROSPECT, because of hypotension and fluid loading in a patient you are trying to keep dry, and because of catheter removal during the postoperative coagulopathy.

The author's reading is as follows. For a minor or moderate open resection in a healthy liver, an epidural at T7-T9 gives the best dynamic analgesia and the INR will not obstruct its removal: it is a sound choice where the ward manages epidurals well. For a major hepatectomy, a cirrhotic liver or any case in which the INR is expected to exceed 1.5 for several days, the defensible default is intrathecal morphine 150-300 micrograms before induction plus bilateral subcostal TAP or rectus sheath catheters (ropivacaine 0.2% by intermittent bolus, at a dose reduced for a small remnant because amide local anaesthetics are cleared by the liver) and a fentanyl or oxycodone PCA. The intrathecal dose covers the first 18-24 hours and the visceral component; the catheters cover the wall for the days after. For laparoscopic resection, intrathecal morphine or single-shot subcostal TAP blocks with port infiltration suffice.

Systemic drugs need adjusting to the remnant. Paracetamol remains appropriate at a reduced dose after most resections; an NSAID is recommended by PROSPECT and is reasonable for a short course when renal function and platelets are normal and the resection was not major, but many units omit it. Low-dose ketamine is useful for the opioid-tolerant patient despite the absence of procedure-specific trials.

Rebound is high for the reason common to all catheter techniques: when a catheter fails on day 1 or 2 the wound is at its worst. Chart the rescue PCA from the start. Escalation triggers: increasing pain with a falling haemoglobin or a tense abdomen is bleeding from the cut surface; right shoulder pain with fever and bile in the drain is a subphrenic collection or bile leak; drowsiness on a modest opioid dose after major resection is liver failure or hypoglycaemia until excluded.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Cirrhosis. Smaller resections, shorter clamp times, portal hypertension with collateral bleeding in the abdominal wall, thrombocytopenia, ascites afterwards and a real mortality from decompensation. Avoid epidurals when platelets or INR are marginal; avoid NSAIDs; be sparing with sodium.
- Laparoscopic and robotic resection. Pneumoperitoneum tamponades venous bleeding and replaces some of the benefit of a low central venous pressure, but an open hepatic vein now entrains carbon dioxide under pressure: gas embolism is commoner, usually transient, and treated by lowering the insufflation pressure and supporting the circulation. Conversion for bleeding is abrupt.
- Air embolism. The combination of low venous pressure, an open vein near the cava and a head-up or flat table. Slight head-down tilt and a surgeon who keeps the field flooded are the prevention.
- Massive haemorrhage. Caval or hepatic vein injury. The surgeon packs and compresses; you abandon the low-pressure strategy, transfuse by ratio, give calcium, warm the patient and call for help.
- Ischaemia-reperfusion. Prolonged or repeated inflow clamping produces a lactic acidosis and a transaminase rise the next day that is not failure by itself; the trend is what matters.
- Post-hepatectomy liver failure. Rising bilirubin and INR on or after day 5, with lactate, hypoglycaemia and encephalopathy: supportive critical care, a search for sepsis and vascular thrombosis, and removal of every hepatotoxic or sedating drug.
- Bile duct or vascular reconstruction (hilar cholangiocarcinoma). A longer operation in a jaundiced, often stented and colonised patient: broader antibiotics, more renal risk, and a biliary anastomosis that makes the surgeon wary of NSAIDs.
- Renal protection. Deliberate fluid restriction produces transient oliguria; acute kidney injury after hepatectomy is nonetheless usually mild when the circulation is refilled promptly after transection. This is consensus, and the threshold at which restriction becomes harmful is not defined.

## Key sources

Liver resection has a PROSPECT guideline, a 2022 ERAS Society guideline and, unusually, a large negative drug trial that changes practice: HeLiX removes tranexamic acid from the routine. The low central venous pressure strategy is supported by meta-analysis for blood loss but not for morbidity, and the method of achieving it is opinion. The choice between epidural and intrathecal morphine with fascial plane catheters is not settled by any trial in this operation; PROSPECT recommends epidural or subcostal TAP blocks and rates intrathecal morphine as having limited procedure-specific evidence, so the stratification by extent of resection in the pain plan is author's judgement. The statement that enhanced recovery guidance is cautious about routine epidurals reflects the author's understanding of that literature and was not verified against the guideline's full text during drafting.
