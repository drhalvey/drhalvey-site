## What the surgeon is doing and why

Bladder cancer is a disease of the urothelium that presents with painless haematuria, is found at flexible cystoscopy, and is then both diagnosed and, for most patients, treated by the same operation. Three quarters of new tumours have not invaded the detrusor muscle. For those, complete transurethral resection is the treatment, and the patient then enters years of surveillance cystoscopies and repeat resections. For the quarter that have invaded muscle, the resection is the staging biopsy that sends the patient on to [cystectomy](radical-cystectomy-and-urinary-diversion.html) or radiotherapy.

That dual purpose explains what the surgeon does. The tumour is resected with a diathermy loop in layers: the exophytic part first, then the base, then a separate deep bite that must contain detrusor muscle, because a specimen without muscle cannot tell non-invasive from invasive disease. The surgeon is therefore deliberately cutting to the full working thickness of a wall that is only a few millimetres thick when the bladder is full. A bimanual examination under anaesthesia, before and after, tells whether a mass is still palpable (invasive) or mobile. For tumours that look low grade, a single dose of intravesical chemotherapy within hours of resection reduces recurrence by killing floating cells before they implant.

The anatomy sets the trap. The obturator nerve runs along the pelvic side wall directly against the inferolateral bladder wall. Diathermy current on that wall depolarises the nerve, the adductors of the thigh contract with full force, the pelvis jerks towards the loop, and the loop, which is already cutting to muscle depth, goes through. A spinal anaesthetic does not prevent this, because the current stimulates the nerve distal to the block. Only two things do: local anaesthetic on the obturator nerve itself, or neuromuscular block at the adductor end plate.

The pain logic is modest. There is no wound. The pain is a mucosal burn in a distensible organ, a catheter balloon, detrusor spasm, and sometimes chemical cystitis from the instilled drug. A score of 2 with a flat, short course: simple analgesia, with an antimuscarinic doing more than an opioid.

## Steps

### 1. The patient and the tumour map
**Why:** The flexible cystoscopy report or imaging says where the tumour is and how big. Lateral wall and tumours near the ureteric orifices are the ones over the obturator nerve.
**Anaesthesia:** Read the location before choosing the anaesthetic; "lateral wall" on the booking changes the plan. The population is older smokers: chronic lung disease, coronary and peripheral vascular disease, and often anaemia from months of haematuria. Many are on antithrombotics, which the surgeon will usually want stopped for a resection but not for a check cystoscopy. Repeat attenders know what worked last time; ask.

### 2. Choice of anaesthetic
**Why:** The surgeon needs a still pelvis, a bladder that can be filled and emptied at will, and for lateral wall tumours a guarantee that the leg will not move.
**Anaesthesia:** Two sound plans exist for a lateral wall tumour. General anaesthesia with complete neuromuscular block for the duration of the lateral wall resection: quick, reliable, and the author's default, with the block monitored and reversed. Or spinal anaesthesia with an obturator nerve block on the tumour side (both sides if the tumour crosses or the location is uncertain): in pooled trials adding the block to a spinal cut the obturator reflex and bladder perforation to roughly a quarter of the rate with spinal alone, with no local anaesthetic toxicity recorded. The comparison no one has done is spinal plus block against general anaesthesia with full paralysis. For tumours on the dome, posterior wall or trigone either technique works unmodified. One retrospective study found lower five-year recurrence after spinal than general anaesthesia (42% against 53%); it is unrandomised and is not a reason to push a spinal on a patient who would do better asleep.

### 3. Lithotomy and examination under anaesthesia
**Why:** With the bladder empty the surgeon palpates it between a finger in the rectum or vagina and a hand on the abdomen, to feel for a mass and whether it is fixed.
**Anaesthesia:** A tense abdominal wall makes the examination useless; under general anaesthesia be deep or relaxed at this moment, and under a spinal the block does it. Position both legs together. Keep the thigh on the tumour side uncovered so that an adductor twitch can be seen by you and the scrub nurse.

### 4. Obturator nerve block (spinal cases)
**Why:** To stop the adductor response at source for the 10-20 minutes the loop will be on the lateral wall.
**Anaesthesia:** Place it after the spinal, with ultrasound, at the inguinal crease (anterior and posterior divisions between the adductor fascial planes) or more proximally in the plane between pectineus and obturator externus. The inguinal approach has a higher success rate and fewer needle passes than the classic pubic tubercle approach in pooled trials, and a network meta-analysis ranked the ultrasound-guided proximal approach best for preventing perforation. Confirm it: loss of adductor strength cannot be tested under a spinal, so the evidence is spread on ultrasound and, if a stimulator is used, loss of twitch. A block is not a guarantee. Ask the surgeon to test with a brief low-power touch before committing the loop.

### 5. Cystoscopy and mapping
**Why:** All tumours are counted and located, the ureteric orifices identified, and suspicious flat areas noted for biopsy. Some units use blue-light fluorescence to show flat disease.
**Anaesthesia:** Bladder filling is stimulating under a light general anaesthetic: laryngospasm on a supraglottic airway at first distension is avoidable by being deep before the scope passes.

### 6. Resection of the tumour and its base
**Why:** The tumour is shaved to the wall, then the base and a muscle sample are taken. The surgeon keeps the bladder only half full during deep cuts so the wall is thicker and further from the nerve.
**Anaesthesia:** When the loop moves to the lateral wall, the neuromuscular block must be complete: check the train-of-four count is zero at the ulnar nerve and remember that the adductors are no more sensitive than the hand. Give the top-up before the surgeon gets there, not after the first jerk, and say aloud that the patient is paralysed. If the leg kicks, the surgeon stops; ask whether the wall is intact. Signs of perforation are a bladder that will not distend, irrigant going in and not coming back, a tense lower abdomen, rising airway pressure, and in the awake patient sudden lower abdominal pain, or shoulder-tip pain and nausea if the breach is intraperitoneal. Resection near a ureteric orifice may end with a stent.

### 7. Haemostasis, chip evacuation and catheter
**Why:** The crater is coagulated, chips are washed out and sent as separate specimens, and a catheter is left if the resection was large or deep. Small resections go home without one.
**Anaesthesia:** Reverse the neuromuscular block fully against a quantitative monitor; a short case with a late top-up is the setting for residual paralysis in a patient with poor lungs. Paracetamol IV now; an NSAID if renal function and the surgeon's view of bleeding permit.

### 8. Recovery and intravesical chemotherapy
**Why:** The single post-operative instillation works only if given early, so it is often run into the catheter in recovery and left for an hour before draining.
**Anaesthesia:** The drug is a cytotoxic: the catheter is clamped, the bag and any leak are handled with cytotoxic precautions, and the patient needs to stay dry and reasonably still. It causes burning and urgency. It is contraindicated after a known or suspected perforation, because extravasated mitomycin causes pelvic necrosis; if the resection was eventful, make sure recovery staff know the instillation is cancelled. Catheter discomfort and spasm are treated as on the [prostate card](transurethral-resection-of-the-prostate-and.html): check drainage first, then an antimuscarinic.

### 9. Discharge
**Why:** Most patients go home the same day, with or without a catheter.
**Anaesthesia:** Standard day-case criteria. The patient who has had a spinal and an obturator block has a weak adductor on that side for some hours; the first walk is supervised. Heavy haematuria with clots, inability to void, or fever is the instruction to return.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a mucosal burn and a catheter, so the plan is paracetamol, an NSAID where allowed, and an antimuscarinic for spasm and urgency, with one or two doses of oral opioid on the day at most. If a spinal was used it covers the first hours. Chemical cystitis after mitomycin adds two or three days of dysuria that responds to fluids and time. Pain scores above 3 are unusual, and the trajectory is flat.

The obturator block is on this card as a safety device, not an analgesic: it has no sensory territory in the bladder. No catheter technique applies. Intrathecal opioid is not justified in a day case with a trial of void.

Escalation triggers are surgical. Severe lower abdominal pain after resection is perforation with extravasation or clot retention until excluded. Extraperitoneal perforation is usually managed with a catheter for one to two weeks; intraperitoneal perforation needs laparoscopy or laparotomy and repair, and presents to you as an acute abdomen in recovery in a patient who was meant to be a day case.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The obturator jerk. Lateral wall, inferolateral and peri-orifice tumours. Complete paralysis or a confirmed block, plus the surgeon's own measures (bipolar energy, lower power, intermittent bursts, a half-full bladder). None alone is fully reliable; combine them.
- Spinal alone for a lateral tumour. The classic error. The block is proximal to where the current hits the nerve.
- Perforation. Extraperitoneal is commoner and is suspected from poor distension and a tense suprapubic area. Intraperitoneal gives diaphragmatic irritation and a rising abdominal girth, and with glycine irrigation a delayed absorption syndrome from the peritoneal surface, so check sodium.
- Bleeding and the emergency variant. Large tumours and patients on antithrombotics can bleed into clot retention; the patient returning for a washout is the same hypovolaemic, anaemic, full-bladder emergency described on the prostate card. Bleeding from radiation cystitis and advanced tumour brings frail patients for repeated washouts and is the scenario most likely to need blood and an arterial line.
- Large-volume disease. Staged resection is safer than a two-hour single sitting; ask about duration.
- Re-resection at two to six weeks. Same operation on an inflamed bladder that perforates more easily.
- Repeat surveillance. Small recurrences are fulgurated in minutes under a brief general anaesthetic or even under local anaesthetic gel with flexible instruments.
- BCG and chemotherapy history. Contracted, irritable bladders with more spasm afterwards.
- Residual neuromuscular block. A deep block for a 20-minute case in a patient with chronic lung disease needs quantitative monitoring and an appropriate reversal dose.
- Pacemakers. Monopolar current in the pelvis; see the device plan.

## Key sources

The obturator block evidence is a set of small randomised trials, mostly from centres where spinal anaesthesia is the norm, pooled in overlapping meta-analyses that agree on a large reduction in adductor jerk and perforation compared with spinal alone. There is no trial against general anaesthesia with full neuromuscular block, which is the usual alternative in Australian practice, so the choice between them is the author's judgement. The anaesthetic-technique and recurrence signal comes from retrospective data and should not drive decisions. There is no PROSPECT guideline for this operation.
