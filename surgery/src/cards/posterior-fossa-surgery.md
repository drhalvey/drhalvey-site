## What the surgeon is doing and why

The posterior fossa is the small compartment below the tentorium that holds the cerebellum, the brainstem, cranial nerves V to XII and the outflow of the fourth ventricle. It has the least spare volume of any intracranial space and the most concentrated function. Three facts follow and they shape the whole anaesthetic. A small amount of swelling or blood obstructs the fourth ventricle and causes hydrocephalus, or compresses the medulla and stops breathing. The structures the surgeon works beside are the cardiovascular and respiratory centres and the nerves that protect the airway. And the approach is from behind, through thick muscle, in positions that put the head above the heart.

The common operations are: resection of a cerebellar tumour (metastasis and haemangioblastoma in adults, medulloblastoma and astrocytoma in children); a retrosigmoid approach to the cerebellopontine angle for vestibular schwannoma or meningioma, where the tumour is dissected off the facial nerve and the brainstem; microvascular decompression, where a pad is placed between an arterial loop and the trigeminal or facial nerve root; and foramen magnum decompression for Chiari malformation, where bone and the arch of C1 are removed and the dura is patched to make room.

The exposure decides the position. Midline lesions need the occiput and craniocervical junction presented with the neck flexed: prone, or sitting. Lateral lesions need the mastoid uppermost: park bench or supine with the head turned hard. The sitting position gives the surgeon a field that drains blood and cerebrospinal fluid by gravity, a slack cerebellum and an anatomical orientation, and it gives you a patient whose open veins sit well above the right atrium. The dural sinuses and the diploic and emissary veins of the occiput are held open by their bony and dural attachments and cannot collapse, so when venous pressure at the wound is below atmospheric, air enters. Venous air embolism is therefore the signature hazard of the position, and it also occurs, less often, in the park bench and prone positions with head-up tilt. The details of each position are in [positioning](../a/a14-positioning.html).

Whether the sitting position is used is a matter of unit tradition. Where it is, large series support it as safe with the right monitoring: in 600 consecutive cases air was detected in 19% but had physiological consequences in 3.3% and stopped the operation in 0.5%.

The pain logic: this is a craniotomy through muscle. The trapezius, splenius, semispinalis and the small suboccipital muscles are detached from bone and retracted, and the patient feels that as occipital headache and neck spasm for several days, more than after a supratentorial flap. The nerves are the greater and lesser occipital, which are easy to block. The constraint on opioids is tighter here than anywhere, because the lesion sits beside the respiratory centre and the nerves of swallowing.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative assessment
**Why:** The scan shows the surgeon the lesion; it shows you whether the fourth ventricle is obstructed and which cranial nerves are already involved.
**Anaesthesia:** Look for hydrocephalus (headache, vomiting, drowsiness), which means raised intracranial pressure before you start and often dehydration from vomiting. Examine and record swallow, voice, gag, facial movement and hearing: a patient with a pre-existing bulbar palsy is at risk of aspiration before and after surgery. For a planned sitting case, screen for a patent foramen ovale with a bubble contrast echocardiogram. The systematic review of sitting-position series found paradoxical embolism reported in up to 14% and recommended screening, with closure or a different position if a shunt is found; some experienced units operate sitting despite a known foramen ovale under transoesophageal monitoring, which is a unit-level decision.

### 2. Induction, lines and monitoring
**Why:** The surgeon wants facial nerve monitoring for any cerebellopontine angle case and evoked potentials for brainstem work.
**Anaesthesia:** Induce as for any patient with possible raised intracranial pressure (see [neuroanaesthesia essentials](../a/a08-neuroanaesthesia-essentials.html)). Use a single intubating dose of relaxant and let it wear off, or reverse it, so that facial electromyography reads true; maintain with propofol and remifentanil when motor or somatosensory potentials are monitored, and keep the patient still with remifentanil rather than relaxant. Arterial line with the transducer at the external auditory meatus. For the sitting position add a multi-orifice central venous catheter with its tip at the junction of the superior vena cava and right atrium, positioned by ECG guidance or echocardiography, and either precordial Doppler over the right heart or transoesophageal echocardiography, which is the most sensitive detector. End-tidal carbon dioxide is the monitor every case has.

### 3. Positioning
**Why:** The neck is flexed to open the craniocervical junction and the head is fixed in pins; in the park bench position the upper shoulder is taped caudally out of the surgeon's way.
**Anaesthesia:** Blunt the pin response with a remifentanil bolus or local infiltration. Then check the consequences of flexion yourself: two finger breadths between chin and sternum, because over-flexion kinks the tube, obstructs jugular drainage, swells the tongue and, over hours, can stretch the cervical cord to the point of quadriparesis. Recheck tube depth, since flexion advances the tip towards the carina. Sitting: raise the back in stages with a vasopressor running, legs elevated and knees flexed, compression stockings on, and accept that mean pressure at the head is some 15-25 mmHg below that at the heart. Park bench: axillary roll, dependent arm supported, the taped shoulder not under traction that will injure the brachial plexus. Prone: free abdomen, eyes clear of the headrest.

### 4. Incision, muscle dissection and craniotomy
**Why:** The muscles are stripped from the occiput and the bone is removed with a drill or craniotome, sometimes as a craniectomy that is not replaced. The transverse and sigmoid sinuses border the opening and emissary veins cross the bone.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the peak period for venous air embolism: open bone veins, a sinus edge and a negative venous pressure. Detection order by sensitivity is transoesophageal echocardiography, precordial Doppler (a change to a turbulent, mill-wheel sound), a fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide, then desaturation, hypotension and arrhythmia, which are late. On suspicion, say it aloud. The surgeon floods the field with saline and waxes the bone edges; you compress both jugular veins briefly to raise venous pressure and show the surgeon the bleeding point, give 100% oxygen, aspirate from the central line, support the circulation with fluid and vasopressor, and lower the head if the fall in pressure persists. Nitrous oxide is not used. Give the osmotic agent during this step if the surgeon expects a tight cerebellum.

### 5. Dural opening and cerebrospinal fluid release
**Why:** The dura is opened and the cisterna magna or the cerebellopontine cistern is opened to drain cerebrospinal fluid, which lets the cerebellum fall away without retraction.
**Anaesthesia:** A tight cerebellum at this point with hydrocephalus is solved by the surgeon with an [external ventricular drain](ventriculoperitoneal-shunt-and-external-ventricular-drain.html), sometimes placed before the main operation. Your contribution is the usual list: neck veins, airway pressure, carbon dioxide at 30-35 mmHg, propofol in place of volatile. Rapid drainage from a previously obstructed system can produce bradycardia or hypertension as the brainstem shifts.

### 6. Dissection beside the brainstem and cranial nerves
**Why:** The surgeon debulks the tumour and peels its capsule off the pons, the medulla and the nerves, or lifts a vessel off a nerve root. Traction on these structures is unavoidable.
**Anaesthesia:** The cardiovascular signs are the monitor. Trigeminal stimulation produces bradycardia, sometimes asystole, with hypotension (the trigeminocardiac reflex). Traction on the vagus or the floor of the fourth ventricle produces bradycardia, hypotension or ectopy. Pressure on the pons and medulla produces hypertension with bradycardia or tachycardia and irregular breathing in a patient who is breathing spontaneously. In each case tell the surgeon immediately and let them release traction: the sign resolves within seconds when the cause stops, and that resolution is the confirmation. Give glycopyrronium or atropine only if it persists or recurs and the surgeon needs to continue, because pre-treating removes the warning. Do not deepen anaesthesia or give a beta-blocker to tidy the trace.

### 7. Neuromonitoring-guided resection
**Why:** The facial nerve is identified by stimulation and its spontaneous electromyographic activity is watched for the bursts that mean irritation. Auditory evoked potentials guide hearing preservation.
**Anaesthesia:** No neuromuscular block, which means immobility depends on depth and remifentanil: a cough in pins with the brainstem exposed is an injury. Keep the infusion rate adequate and anticipate stimulating moments. Stable temperature, pressure and anaesthetic depth matter because evoked potentials change with all three, and an unexplained change that coincides with a propofol bolus is yours to own up to. These are long cases: check pressure areas, glucose, haemoglobin and gases on a schedule.

### 8. Haemostasis and closure
**Why:** The surgeon asks for a normal blood pressure and sometimes a Valsalva manoeuvre or jugular compression to reveal venous bleeding that the sitting position hides. The dura is closed with a graft if needed and the muscles are reapproximated in layers.
**Anaesthesia:** Restore pressure to the patient's baseline for the haemostasis check. In the sitting position air entrainment can recur as the muscles are closed. Give paracetamol 1 g IV and antiemetics; ask for the wound and muscle to be infiltrated with long-acting local anaesthetic, or place occipital nerve blocks. Lowering a sitting patient to supine returns pooled blood to the heart and is occasionally followed by a pressure surge.

### 9. Emergence and the extubation decision
**Why:** The surgeon wants a neurological examination, and both of you want to know whether the airway reflexes survived.
**Anaesthesia:** The decision is specific to this operation. Extubate awake, with the patient obeying, after a short uncomplicated operation with no brainstem handling. Delay extubation after prolonged dissection around cranial nerves IX, X and XII or the medulla, after significant intraoperative brainstem signs, after a very long prone or flexed case with facial and tongue swelling (no cuff leak), or if spontaneous ventilation is irregular. Avoid coughing and hypertension as for any craniotomy, with low-rate remifentanil through pin removal and an antihypertensive drawn up. Tension pneumocephalus after a sitting case presents as failure to wake.

### 10. The first night
**Why:** A clot or swelling in this compartment compresses the brainstem and obstructs the fourth ventricle within minutes to hours.
**Anaesthesia:** The presentation is hypertension, bradycardia, a falling conscious level and then apnoea, sometimes with no pupillary warning. The response is reintubation, an urgent scan or direct return to theatre, and ventricular drainage. A new hoarse voice, a wet cough or drooling means a lower cranial nerve palsy and nil by mouth until swallow is assessed. Nausea and vertigo are expected after cerebellopontine angle surgery and need two antiemetics from different classes. Facial weakness means an unprotected cornea: tape and lubricant.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is muscle stripped from bone plus the craniotomy itself, in the territory of the occipital nerves, so the strategy is occipital nerve block or infiltration, regular non-opioids, and opioid as cautious rescue. PROSPECT's craniotomy guideline recommends paracetamol, NSAIDs, dexmedetomidine infusion and scalp block or incision infiltration; its trials are mostly supratentorial, and applying it here is reasonable extrapolation, not direct evidence.

Greater and lesser occipital nerve blocks with ropivacaine 0.5%, placed after induction or at closure, cover the incision and most of the muscle attachment. A full scalp block is used when pins and a larger retrosigmoid flap extend forward. Paracetamol is regular. An NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor is added once the surgeon is satisfied with haemostasis, usually after the first scan or 24 hours, as author's judgement balancing a well-evidenced analgesic against a haematoma in the least forgiving compartment. Muscle spasm is a real component and is helped by positioning, heat and early gentle movement; a short course of a low-dose benzodiazepine is used by some units and sedates, so it is not a first-day drug.

Opioid rescue is titrated in small intravenous or oral doses with close observation. The arguments for restraint are stronger than after supratentorial surgery: carbon dioxide retention raises pressure in a full compartment, sedation hides the first sign of a haematoma, and nausea is already severe. Rebound is not a problem.

Escalation: occipital headache that worsens with drowsiness, vomiting or hypertension is hydrocephalus or haematoma until scanned. Headache with neck stiffness and fever some days later is chemical or bacterial meningitis. A positional headache with a soft swelling under the wound is a cerebrospinal fluid leak or pseudomeningocoele.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Sitting position contraindications. A known right-to-left shunt (relative, by unit), severe cervical stenosis, uncontrolled hypertension or hypovolaemia, and extremes of age are the usual exclusions.
- Paradoxical air embolism. Air crossing to the left heart causes stroke or myocardial ischaemia; it can cross pulmonary vessels when the volume is large even without a foramen ovale.
- Macroglossia and airway oedema. Hours of neck flexion with an oral airway or a large probe in the mouth; inspect before extubation.
- Quadriparesis. Cord ischaemia from flexion and low perfusion pressure in the sitting position; somatosensory potentials during positioning detect it.
- Microvascular decompression. A short operation in a well patient; the hazards are the trigeminocardiac reflex, post-operative nausea and, rarely, a cerebellar haematoma.
- Chiari decompression. Often young, sometimes with syringomyelia or sleep-disordered breathing; avoid neck extension at intubation and expect marked neck pain.
- Children. Medulloblastoma and other midline tumours present with hydrocephalus and vomiting; the same principles apply with the dosing and fluid rules in [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).
- Emergency variant. Cerebellar haemorrhage or infarction with brainstem compression: rapid sequence induction, ventricular drain and decompression, and planned post-operative ventilation. The principles overlap with [traumatic brain injury](traumatic-brain-injury.html).
- Supratentorial lesions are covered in [craniotomy for supratentorial tumour](craniotomy-for-supratentorial-tumour.html).

## Key sources

The air embolism figures come from a systematic review of observational series and one large single-centre series; incidence depends heavily on the detector used, which is why the range is wide. Management of air embolism is physiology and expert review, not trial evidence. The response to brainstem signs and the extubation criteria are consensus practice. The pain recommendations are extrapolated from craniotomy trials that were mostly supratentorial, and the statement that posterior fossa surgery hurts more is the author's reading of clinical experience, not a sourced comparison.
