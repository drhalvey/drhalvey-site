## What the surgeon is doing and why

Skin cancer surgery is two operations in sequence: remove the tumour with a margin, then close the hole. The margin is set by the pathology. A basal cell carcinoma needs a few millimetres, a squamous cell carcinoma a little more, and a melanoma is re-excised after its diagnostic biopsy with a margin of 1-2 cm determined by its thickness, often with a sentinel node biopsy at the same sitting. On the face, where every millimetre costs function or appearance, the surgeon may wait for frozen sections or receive the patient after Mohs surgery with a defect that is already known to be clear.

Closing the hole follows the reconstructive ladder, and the rung chosen decides your anaesthetic:

```
 direct closure        ellipse, edges undermined and sutured
 full-thickness graft  small disc of skin from behind the ear, the
                       neck or the groin; donor site sutured shut
 split-skin graft      shaving of epidermis and part of the dermis
                       from the thigh; donor heals by itself
 local flap            neighbouring skin moved on its own blood
                       supply: advancement, rotation, transposition
 regional, free flap   beyond this card
```

A graft has no blood supply of its own. It survives for two days on fluid from the bed, then vessels grow in, and for that it needs a vascular bed (not bare bone, tendon or cartilage), close contact and no movement. Blood or serum under it, or shear across it, kills it. That is why the surgeon dries the bed obsessively, meshes or fenestrates the graft so fluid can escape, and fixes it with a tie-over bolster, a foam dressing or negative pressure, sometimes with a splint and bed rest. A flap brings its own circulation and tolerates poorer beds, but it is tissue under some tension with a pedicle that can be kinked or compressed by a haematoma.

A split-skin graft is cut with a powered dermatome at 0.2-0.3 mm, usually from the lateral or anterior thigh. The donor site is left as a controlled partial-thickness wound that heals from the skin appendages in 10-14 days.

The pain logic is skin and soft tissue throughout, and mostly mild: an ellipse or a flap is a sutured wound. The exception is the split-skin donor site, where the dermatome exposes a wide field of superficial dermal nerve endings. It burns, it sticks to its dressing, and patients consistently rate it above the grafted wound. In a small randomised trial of thigh harvests, median donor site pain was 6 out of 10 without a block and 0 with a fascia iliaca block. Plan for the donor site and the rest takes care of itself.

## Steps

### 1. Choosing the anaesthetic
**Why:** Most of these operations are small, superficial and done on elderly patients with long medical histories; the surgeon can do nearly all of them under infiltration.
**Anaesthesia:** Local anaesthesia with or without sedation is the default and for the frail patient it is the safer choice by a distance. You are asked to attend for sedation, for monitoring of the patient with significant cardiac or respiratory disease, or for general anaesthesia when the field is large, the sites are multiple, the position is prone, the dose of local anaesthetic would be excessive, or the patient is a child or cannot cooperate. Dementia is the difficult case: sedation tends to disinhibit, and a brief general anaesthetic with a supraglottic airway is often kinder than a struggle. Antiplatelet and anticoagulant drugs are usually continued for simple excisions; ask the surgeon what has been arranged for a flap or graft. A pacemaker or defibrillator matters when monopolar diathermy is used on the chest or head: bipolar is the simple answer.

### 2. Positioning, the airway and oxygen
**Why:** The lesion and any donor site must both be exposed, and for the face the surgeon will be standing where you would like to be.
**Anaesthesia:** For facial and scalp lesions under sedation, keep the patient conversational and on air. If oxygen is needed, give the least that works through nasal prongs with scavenging of the space under the drapes, stop it a minute before diathermy is used, and let alcohol-based preparation dry fully. If more than that is needed, convert to a sealed airway: a flexible supraglottic device or a tube taped to the side away from the lesion. Protect the eyes with ointment and, when the surgeon is working on the lids or nose, corneal shields. For the back and posterior scalp, prone positioning under general anaesthesia with a tracheal tube is standard; lateral with a supraglottic airway suits many flank and shoulder lesions.

### 3. Infiltration
**Why:** Lidocaine with adrenaline gives anaesthesia, a dry field and hydrodissection of the plane; the surgeon marks margins and flap design first because infiltration distorts the skin.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the dose that matters. Lidocaine with adrenaline up to 7 mg/kg is the conventional ceiling, and several sites plus a donor field reach it sooner than the surgeon expects; long cases may add bupivacaine. Agree the budget, keep the tally, and reduce it for the small elderly patient. Injection pain is the worst part of the operation for an awake patient: warm, buffered solution injected slowly through a fine needle, with a bolus of short-acting opioid or propofol timed to the injection, makes the rest uneventful. Adrenaline produces a brief tachycardia; allow the several minutes it needs to work before incision.

### 4. Excision
**Why:** The lesion is cut out to the planned margin and depth (to fat, fascia or periosteum), orientated with a marking suture and sent. Where margin control is intraoperative, the wound is dressed and everyone waits.
**Anaesthesia:** Little stimulation if the infiltration is sound. Frozen section adds 20-40 minutes of nothing: lighten the sedation and let the patient rest, since re-sedating for closure is easy. Scalp wounds bleed briskly from vessels held open by the galea, and in an anticoagulated patient a scalp excision is the one site where measured blood loss can reach a few hundred millilitres.

### 5. Sentinel node biopsy (melanoma)
**Why:** After radiocolloid lymphoscintigraphy, the surgeon injects blue dye intradermally around the scar and removes the draining nodes from the axilla, groin or neck through a second incision.
**Anaesthesia:** This usually converts the case to a general anaesthetic, and the node basin may dictate the position. Blue dye lowers the oximeter reading for an hour or more and can cause anaphylaxis: the pooled rate for melanoma surgery in a large meta-analysis was very low (0.0043%), attributed to small intradermal volumes, though one centre has reported allergic reactions in 0.84% of 715 patients. Treat hypotension within half an hour of injection as a dye reaction until shown otherwise. See [wide local excision and sentinel node biopsy](wide-local-excision-and-sentinel-node.html) for the detail.

### 6. Closure or flap
**Why:** The surgeon undermines the edges and closes directly, or raises adjacent skin on a planned pedicle and moves it into the defect, closing the secondary defect behind it.
**Anaesthesia:** Flap raising extends the field beyond the infiltrated area, so expect a request for more local anaesthetic or a moment of patient discomfort. The flap needs a normal blood pressure and no venous congestion: avoid a hypertensive, coughing emergence that fills the space under it with blood. A forehead flap for nasal reconstruction is a two- or three-stage operation with the pedicle left attached across the nasal bridge for three weeks; at the later stages the pedicle sits where a face mask does, so plan the airway around it.

### 7. Graft harvest
**Why:** A full-thickness graft is cut as an ellipse from spare skin and the donor closed. A split-skin graft is shaved from the thigh with the dermatome, then meshed or fenestrated.
**Anaesthesia:** The dermatome pass is the most stimulating moment of the operation and the origin of the postoperative pain. Three options cover it, in ascending order of effect: surgeon infiltration of the donor area with dilute local anaesthetic and adrenaline, which also firms the skin for the blade and reduces ooze; a lateral femoral cutaneous nerve block, which covers the lateral thigh with 5-10 mL and no motor effect; or a fascia iliaca block, which also covers the anterior thigh at the cost of some quadriceps weakness. The author's default is the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve block for a lateral harvest in a day-case patient who must walk, and the trial evidence is for the fascia iliaca block. The donor dressing (alginate, film or foam, often soaked in local anaesthetic) is chosen by the surgeon.

### 8. Graft inset and dressing
**Why:** The graft is sutured or stapled to the bed and fixed against shear with a tie-over bolster, foam or negative pressure dressing; a limb may be splinted.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient must not move as the dressing is secured, and must not thrash on waking. Under sedation this is a matter of warning; under general anaesthesia it means a smooth emergence, particularly for grafts on the face and neck, and leaving a supraglottic airway until the patient is calm.

### 9. Recovery and aftercare
**Why:** The graft is at its most vulnerable for the first five days; the surgeon limits movement of the part and dependency of a grafted leg.
**Anaesthesia:** Regular paracetamol and, where renal function and bleeding risk allow, a short course of an NSAID, with a few doses of oral opioid for the donor site. Tell the patient that the donor site will be the sore part and that its first dressing change at one to two weeks is uncomfortable. Patients kept on bed rest for a lower limb graft need thromboprophylaxis and are at risk of delirium and deconditioning, so the decision to immobilise an 85-year-old is not a small one.

## Pain plan

For excision and direct closure or a local flap, the mechanism is a sutured skin wound and the plan is the surgeon's local anaesthetic followed by paracetamol, with an NSAID if suitable. Adding bupivacaine to the infiltration at the end extends the comfortable period to the evening. No block is needed, and for most patients no opioid.

For a split-skin graft the plan is built around the donor site, because that is the mechanism that differs: a broad superficial dermal wound whose nerve supply on the thigh is the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve laterally and the femoral nerve's cutaneous branches anteriorly. A block of those nerves fits the mechanism exactly, and in the one small randomised trial of fascia iliaca block for thigh donor sites the median pain score in the postoperative phase was 0 against 6, and 0 against 4.5 the next day. The trial was in burn patients with 20 participants; the extrapolation to elective skin cancer surgery is the author's. The defensible default is a lateral femoral cutaneous nerve block (or a fascia iliaca block when the harvest is anterior or large) with ropivacaine 0.2-0.375%, or thorough tumescent infiltration of the donor field when a block is impractical. No catheter.

There is no PROSPECT guideline for these operations. In the 179-procedure cohort, small operations ranked surprisingly high for day 1 pain because analgesia was treated as unnecessary; the donor site is the local example. Expect worst pain of 2-3 out of 10 on the first three days when a graft has been taken, mostly stinging at the donor site and worse on walking as the dressing moves, settling to 1 by a week. Without a graft the figures are a point lower from day 1. Rebound after a single-shot thigh block is modest because the stimulus is superficial.

Escalation triggers: throbbing, increasing pain under a flap or a bolster in the first 24 hours is a haematoma and is a surgical problem; pain, redness and offensive discharge at a donor site after the first week is infection; severe pain is unusual enough in this surgery that it should prompt a look at the wound.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Airway fire. The most serious avoidable event in this card. Facial surgery, sedation, an oxygen-enriched space under the drapes, alcohol preparation and diathermy are the full set of ingredients.
- Local anaesthetic toxicity. Multiple lesions on a small elderly patient, a donor field infiltration and a top-up for the flap add up. Perioral tingling, tinnitus or agitation in a sedated patient is toxicity before it is anything else.
- The very old and very frail. They tolerate local anaesthesia well and general anaesthesia and hospital admission poorly. The best anaesthetic contribution is often to make local anaesthesia work: position, warmth, a pillow under the knees, a hand to hold and a small dose of something at the time of injection.
- Anticoagulation. Continued for most excisions; a haematoma under a graft or flap is the cost, a stroke off anticoagulation the alternative. The decision is the surgeon's and the prescriber's and should be made before the day.
- Pretibial lacerations and trauma. The semi-urgent variant: an elderly patient on steroids or anticoagulants with a flap laceration of the shin, grafted on a trauma list under spinal, block or local anaesthesia.
- Large and deep tumours. Neglected scalp or trunk tumours can involve bone or need wide excision with real blood loss, a group and screen and occasionally a free flap; these are the cases where a second cannula and invasive monitoring become relevant, and they are no longer day cases.
- Children. Naevi and scars are excised under general anaesthesia; infiltration after induction and paracetamol are sufficient.
- Staged flaps. Forehead and cross-finger flaps return for division of the pedicle; the second stage is short and usually done under local anaesthesia.
- Prone cases. A back lesion with an axillary or groin sentinel node may need a turn mid-operation: plan the airway for both positions.
- Burn grafting is a different operation in scale and physiology: see [burns: debridement and grafting](burns-debridement-and-grafting.html).

## Key sources

The evidence base for anaesthesia in elective skin surgery is thin. The donor site block recommendation rests on one 20-patient randomised trial in burn patients and on anatomy. The blue dye figures for melanoma come from a meta-analysis and a single-centre letter that disagree by two orders of magnitude, which is why the card advises vigilance and not reassurance. Fire precautions, local anaesthetic dose limits and anticoagulant practice are consensus; block selection for the day-case patient is the author's judgement.
