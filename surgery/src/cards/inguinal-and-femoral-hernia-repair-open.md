## What the surgeon is doing and why

The groin is a designed weakness. In men the testis descended through the abdominal wall dragging its vessels and the vas behind it, leaving an oblique tunnel (the inguinal canal) from the deep ring, lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels, to the superficial ring above the pubic tubercle. An indirect hernia is a peritoneal sac following the cord down that tunnel. A direct hernia is a bulge straight through the weakened posterior wall of the canal, medial to the epigastric vessels. A femoral hernia leaves by a different door, the femoral canal below the inguinal ligament and medial to the femoral vein, through a rigid ring the width of a fingertip; that narrow neck is why femoral hernias strangulate far more often than inguinal ones and why they are repaired promptly. All three openings lie in one oval area of the lower abdominal wall, the myopectineal orifice, covered only by transversalis fascia.

Every modern repair reduces the sac and reinforces that area with mesh. The difference is which side of the wall the mesh goes on.

- Open anterior repair (Lichtenstein). A groin incision, the external oblique aponeurosis opened along its fibres, the cord lifted, the sac dissected off it and reduced or excised, and a flat mesh laid on the posterior wall, sutured to the inguinal ligament below and the conjoint tendon above, with a slit around the cord. Three sensory nerves cross the field: the ilioinguinal on the cord, the iliohypogastric above it, and the genital branch of the genitofemoral within it. They are identified and either preserved or deliberately divided; a nerve caught in a suture or scarred to mesh is the main cause of chronic pain.
- Laparo-endoscopic posterior repair. The mesh is placed behind the muscle, in the preperitoneal space, where abdominal pressure holds it against the defect and it covers the inguinal and femoral openings at once. TEP (totally extraperitoneal) creates the working space between the rectus and the peritoneum without entering the abdomen; TAPP (transabdominal preperitoneal) enters the abdomen, incises the peritoneum above the hernia, places the mesh and closes the peritoneum over it. Both suit bilateral and recurrent hernias and are increasingly done robotically.

Surgeons vary, and guidelines accept either approach according to expertise. The anaesthetic difference is large: the open repair can be done entirely under local anaesthesia in a patient of any fitness, while the laparoscopic repair needs general anaesthesia with a controlled airway.

Pain logic. Acute pain is that of a modest abdominal wall wound: aching and sharp on movement for three to five days, well controlled with non-opioids. In a cohort of open repairs under local anaesthesia, 59.5% of patients took no opioid at all. The laparoscopic repair hurts a little less early. The outcome that matters is chronic groin pain, persisting beyond three months in a minority of patients after any technique and roughly half as often after laparoscopic as after Lichtenstein repair in pooled data. Its mechanism is nerve, not wound, and nothing in the acute plan has been shown to prevent it.

## Steps

### 1. Choosing the anaesthetic with the operation
**Why:** The surgeon has chosen open or laparoscopic on the hernia (primary or recurrent, one side or both, size, femoral component), their expertise and the patient's fitness.
**Anaesthesia:** For an open repair there are three options and the evidence ranks them. Local infiltration with light sedation gives the least pain, the least urinary retention (0.1% in a network meta-analysis, against 1.4% for general and 8.6% for regional anaesthesia) and the fastest discharge; it depends on a surgeon who is practised at it and a hernia that reduces. General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway and a field block is the common default. Spinal is the least attractive: PROSPECT notes more retention and slower home-readiness. For a laparoscopic repair, general anaesthesia with neuromuscular block. Ask every patient to pass urine immediately before coming to theatre.

### 2. Field block and infiltration for the open repair
**Why:** The groin is supplied by the ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric nerves running between internal oblique and transversus abdominis medial to the anterior superior iliac spine, with the genitofemoral nerve supplying the cord and the sac neck supplied by peritoneal fibres.
**Anaesthesia:** PROSPECT's central recommendation is a field block, with or without wound infiltration, either as the anaesthetic or as an adjunct to general anaesthesia. An ultrasound-guided ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric block before incision plus infiltration by the surgeon in layers (subcutaneous, under the aponeurosis, around the cord and the sac neck) is the complete version. The block does not cover the sac neck or the cord, so traction there under local anaesthesia needs a warning and a top-up from the surgeon, or a small dose of short-acting opioid. Calculate the total local anaesthetic dose across both operators and both sides. A dense block can spread to the femoral nerve under the fascia iliaca and leave a weak quadriceps: test knee extension before the patient stands.

### 3. Incision and opening the canal
**Why:** Skin and Scarpa's fascia are divided, the external oblique aponeurosis is opened through the superficial ring, and the ilioinguinal nerve is found lying on the cord.
**Anaesthesia:** PROSPECT's surgical review favours cutting diathermy over scalpel for this incision on pain grounds. Under sedation keep the patient responsive enough to cough on request, which the surgeon uses to demonstrate the sac and test the repair. Target-controlled propofol at a low effect-site concentration, or small doses of midazolam, is enough; a deeply sedated patient who obstructs and strains pushes the hernia into the surgeon's hands.

### 4. Cord mobilisation and dealing with the sac
**Why:** The cord is encircled at the pubic tubercle and lifted, the cremaster is split, and the indirect sac is separated from the vas and vessels up to the deep ring, then reduced or ligated; a direct bulge is imbricated.
**Anaesthesia:** Traction on the cord and peritoneal sac is the vagal moment of the operation: bradycardia, nausea and a faint feeling under local anaesthesia, occasionally a marked bradycardia under general. Ask for the traction to be released and treat with an anticholinergic if it recurs. A large scrotal hernia involves long dissection and a postoperative haematoma risk. A sliding hernia contains bladder or colon in its wall.

### 5. Mesh placement and closure
**Why:** The mesh is tailored, sutured or glued to the inguinal ligament and the conjoint tendon, the tails crossed around the cord, and the aponeurosis closed over it.
**Anaesthesia:** No relaxation is needed. Under general anaesthesia, avoid coughing on emergence, which tests the fixation. Give paracetamol and the NSAID intravenously before the end so that they are working when the infiltration fades.

### 6. Laparoscopic repair: set-up and creating the space
**Why:** For TEP a port is placed below the umbilicus into the plane behind the rectus muscle, and a balloon or the telescope opens the preperitoneal space down to the pubis; two further ports go in the midline. For TAPP the abdomen is insufflated in the usual way.
**Anaesthesia:** Tracheal tube and neuromuscular block are the usual choice: the working space is small and a cough or a tense abdominal wall closes it. Both arms tucked, head-down tilt, the screen at the feet. The bladder must be empty, by voiding for a short unilateral case or by catheter for a long bilateral or recurrent one, because it sits directly under the dissection. In TEP the CO2 is in a tissue plane, not a cavity: absorption is brisk, end-tidal CO2 rises more than in a short pneumoperitoneum, and subcutaneous emphysema of the abdominal wall, scrotum and occasionally chest and neck is common and usually harmless. A tear in the peritoneum lets gas into the abdomen, which narrows the surgeon's space and can splint the diaphragm; they may vent it with a needle.

### 7. Laparoscopic dissection, mesh and exsufflation
**Why:** The sac is reduced off the cord structures, the whole myopectineal orifice is cleared, and a large mesh (about 10 by 15 cm) is unrolled over it, fixed with glue, a few tacks or nothing at all. The space is deflated under vision so the mesh does not fold.
**Anaesthesia:** The danger areas have names for a reason: the external iliac vessels lie between the vas and the gonadal vessels, and lateral to them run the lateral femoral cutaneous and genitofemoral nerves, where tacks are not placed. Bleeding from the inferior epigastric vessels or the pubic branch over the pectineal ligament is rare and awkward. Check for emphysema extending to the neck before extubation; if the chest and neck are tight with gas and the end-tidal CO2 is still elevated, ventilate until it clears. Port sites are infiltrated.

### 8. The strangulated or obstructed hernia
**Why:** An irreducible, tender hernia with obstruction or skin changes contains bowel or omentum at risk. The surgeon opens the sac, inspects the contents, and either returns viable bowel and repairs the defect or resects, through the groin wound or a laparotomy. Femoral hernias in elderly women are the classic presentation and are often missed until the bowel is dead.
**Anaesthesia:** This is a bowel obstruction with a small incision: full stomach, hypovolaemia, electrolyte loss, possible sepsis. Nasogastric tube, resuscitation, rapid sequence induction with a tracheal tube, arterial line in the frail or septic. Local anaesthesia alone is not appropriate when bowel may need resection. Under anaesthesia the hernia may reduce spontaneously with relaxation, taking a dead loop back into the abdomen, so the surgeon may ask you to delay the relaxant or will plan a laparoscopy to inspect the bowel. Be ready for conversion to a laparotomy ([small bowel obstruction](small-bowel-obstruction-and-adhesiolysis.html)).

### 9. Recovery and discharge
**Why:** The repair is strong immediately; surgeons encourage normal walking from day 0 and vary in how long they restrict lifting.
**Anaesthesia:** Before discharge: the patient has stood and walked (femoral spread of the block), the scrotum and wound are not expanding, and older men after bilateral or laparoscopic repair have passed urine. The discharge script is non-opioid first.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a small abdominal wall wound in a superficial, easily blocked territory, so the plan is local anaesthetic at the site plus two non-opioids. PROSPECT recommends a field block with or without wound infiltration for open repair, and paracetamol with a conventional NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor given early enough to be effective in recovery, with a weak opioid for moderate pain and a strong opioid only on request. For laparoscopic repair there is no separate procedure-specific guideline; port-site infiltration and the same oral regimen is the defensible default, and TAP blocks add little.

The evidence on how little opioid is needed is worth acting on. After open repair under local anaesthesia with sedation, six in ten patients took none and most of the rest took four tablets or fewer. A discharge supply of a few doses for the first two nights is enough, and a week of regular paracetamol and NSAID does more than a box of oxycodone. Ice and scrotal support help the swelling that alarms patients on day 2-3.

Rebound is mild: the field block wears off in the evening with the oral regimen already on board. If the block was a spinal, the oral regimen must start before sensation returns.

Chronic pain needs honest framing rather than a prescription. It follows a minority of repairs after any technique, pooled data favour the laparoscopic approach by about half, and the recognised risk factors (younger age, severe early postoperative pain, pain before surgery out of proportion to the hernia, recurrent repair) are worth noting beforehand. Severe pain in recovery in the distribution of a single nerve, with numbness, is worth telling the surgeon about that day, while a trapped nerve can still be released.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Local anaesthesia in the obese patient, the anxious patient, or the irreducible scrotal hernia. The technique has limits; convert early and without fuss.
- The anticoagulated patient. Open repair under local anaesthesia with meticulous haemostasis is the usual choice; preperitoneal bleeding after TEP has nowhere to tamponade.
- Bilateral and recurrent hernias. Usually laparoscopic if the previous repair was open, and open if it was laparoscopic, so that the surgeon works in an unscarred plane.
- Previous prostatectomy or lower abdominal surgery. The preperitoneal plane is scarred; TEP becomes TAPP or open.
- Femoral hernia. Repaired electively by a low groin, transinguinal or laparoscopic approach; as an emergency, expect a high preperitoneal incision or a laparotomy if bowel is compromised.
- Severe cardiorespiratory disease. The argument for the open repair under local anaesthesia, which guidelines support; the operation should not be denied because the patient cannot have a general anaesthetic.
- Ascites and cirrhosis. Hernias are large and symptomatic; ascites control comes first, and leak through the wound is the complication.
- Testicular pain and swelling. Ischaemic orchitis from cord dissection presents on day 2-5; it is not an analgesic problem.
- Young adults. A persistent processus without a muscular defect; some surgeons repair without mesh. Children are covered in the paediatric section.

## Key sources

The open operation has a mature evidence base: a PROSPECT review of 79 trials, several meta-analyses comparing anaesthetic techniques that agree with each other, and an international guideline. The PROSPECT review dates from 2012 and predates routine ultrasound-guided blocks, though its conclusions have held. For laparoscopic repair the analgesic evidence is thinner and the recommendation here is extrapolated. The chronic pain comparison between techniques is graded low certainty by its own authors.
