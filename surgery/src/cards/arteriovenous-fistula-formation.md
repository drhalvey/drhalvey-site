## What the surgeon is doing and why

Haemodialysis needs a vessel that can be needled three times a week and deliver 300-400 mL/min to the machine. No native vein has that flow and no artery tolerates that needling. The solution is to join a superficial vein to an artery. Arterial pressure and flow then remodel the vein over six to twelve weeks: it dilates, its wall thickens, and it becomes a high-flow conduit lying under the skin where it can be cannulated. That process is called maturation, and its failure is the main problem of the operation. A substantial proportion of fistulae, more at the wrist than at the elbow, either thrombose early or never mature enough to use, and each failure sends the patient back for another operation at a more proximal site while they dialyse through a central venous catheter with its infection risk.

The surgeon works from distal to proximal and from the non-dominant arm, preserving options: a radiocephalic fistula at the wrist first, then a brachiocephalic fistula at the elbow, then a brachiobasilic fistula, in which the deep-lying basilic vein is mobilised along the upper arm and transposed into a superficial tunnel so it can be needled. When the veins are exhausted, a prosthetic graft is looped between artery and vein in the forearm or upper arm.

The operation itself is small. Through a short incision the vein is mobilised and divided, its distal end tied, and its proximal end swung over to the artery. Both are controlled with slings or fine clamps, a 6-10 mm arteriotomy is made, and the vein is sewn end to side with fine monofilament under loupes. On release the surgeon feels for a thrill, the buzz of turbulent flow, which is the sign of a working fistula. A pulse without a thrill means outflow obstruction; nothing at all means spasm, thrombosis or a technical fault.

What determines early success is flow through the anastomosis in the first hours, and flow depends on the calibre of the vessels. A cold, anxious patient with a small radial artery and a constricted cephalic vein gives the surgeon 2 mm vessels to sew and a low-flow fistula that clots. This is where the anaesthetic enters the surgical result. A brachial plexus block produces sympathetic blockade of the arm: the veins dilate, the artery dilates, and flow through the new fistula is higher. In the one adequately conducted randomised trial, 126 patients having wrist or elbow fistulae, primary patency at three months was 84% under brachial plexus block against 62% under local infiltration, and the difference held at twelve months (79% against 59%), with better functional patency as well. A meta-analysis of four small trials points the same way. A larger multicentre trial was designed because that evidence is from few centres, and its protocol states plainly that practice still varies. The reading that fits the evidence today is that a block is the default anaesthetic for a primary fistula because it probably improves the surgical result, not only because it is convenient.

The pain logic is brief. A short skin incision and superficial dissection produce minimal pain. A basilic vein transposition, with its long medial arm wound and tunnel, is more uncomfortable for a few days. The block outlasts the worst of either.

The patient is the difficult part. End-stage renal disease brings hypertension, coronary and peripheral vascular disease, diabetes with autonomic neuropathy and gastroparesis, anaemia, platelet dysfunction, hyperkalaemia, fluid overload or post-dialysis depletion, and altered handling of most drugs. Avoiding general anaesthesia in this group is the second reason the block is preferred.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative check: potassium, volume and timing of dialysis
**Why:** The surgeon wants the operation done before the patient needs a dialysis line, or soon after starting on one. Many patients are not yet dialysing; others arrive the day after a session.
**Anaesthesia:** For the dialysing patient: when was the last session, what is today's weight against dry weight, and what is this morning's potassium. A potassium above 6.0 mmol/L postpones elective surgery for dialysis. The best timing is the day after dialysis, when volume and potassium are corrected and the heparin has worn off. For the pre-dialysis patient, residual renal function is to be protected: no NSAIDs, no hypotension. Note the existing access (a tunnelled line, a failed fistula in the other arm) and do not use it. Record which antiplatelets and anticoagulants are current, since they bear on the block site.

### 2. Cannula, cuff and monitoring
**Why:** The surgeon and the nephrologist have mapped the patient's arm veins as a finite resource.
**Anaesthesia:** One small cannula in the dorsum of the hand on the non-operative side. Not the cephalic vein at the wrist, not the forearm, not the antecubital fossa, in either arm: a thrombosed or stenosed vein is a lost access site. If the other arm has a working fistula, it takes neither cannula nor cuff, and the cuff goes on a leg. Subclavian central lines are avoided in this population for life because central venous stenosis ruins the whole arm.

### 3. Brachial plexus block
**Why:** The surgeon needs an anaesthetised, still arm and benefits from dilated vessels at the moment of choosing and sewing them.
**Anaesthesia:** Any approach that covers the surgical site and produces sympathetic block serves. An axillary block with separate musculocutaneous injection covers forearm and elbow surgery, spares the phrenic nerve entirely and is at a compressible site, which suits the fluid-overloaded, breathless or anticoagulated patient. Supraclavicular and infraclavicular blocks are quicker and more reliable for upper arm surgery, with a phrenic palsy rate that matters in a patient with pulmonary oedema or pleural effusions. The medial upper arm and axilla (intercostobrachial nerve, T2, and the medial cutaneous nerve of the arm) are not covered by any plexus block and need a subcutaneous infiltration for brachiobasilic surgery. Use a moderate dose, for example ropivacaine 0.5% 20-25 mL or a lidocaine and ropivacaine mixture for faster onset: acidosis and low protein binding raise the free fraction of local anaesthetic in uraemia, and seizure threshold is lower, so the maximum is not the target. Tell the surgeon when the block is in, because the veins are visibly larger 15-20 min later and that is when they should be assessed.

### 4. Position and sedation
**Why:** The arm is abducted and supinated on a hand table, and the surgeon uses loupes for a 2-3 mm anastomosis.
**Anaesthesia:** Keep the patient and the limb warm: a forced-air blanket over the trunk and the other arm, and a warm theatre, since cold undoes the vasodilatation the block was placed for. Sedation is optional and light. Midazolam 1-2 mg IV or a low-dose propofol infusion is enough; remember that the active metabolite of midazolam and of morphine accumulates in renal failure, and that delayed gastric emptying makes an unprotected airway under deep sedation a real aspiration risk. Fluid is minimal: 250-500 mL in an anuric patient, with no potassium-containing solutions if the potassium is high.

### 5. Exposure of vein and artery
**Why:** The vein is mobilised over a few centimetres with its side branches ligated, and the artery exposed beneath the deep fascia. For a brachiobasilic fistula the basilic vein is dissected free along the whole upper arm, with the medial cutaneous nerve of the forearm running across it.
**Anaesthesia:** A patchy block declares itself here. Supplement with surgeon infiltration early; converting to general anaesthesia in a uraemic patient with a full stomach because of a missed musculocutaneous or medial cutaneous territory is avoidable. The surgeon may distend the vein with heparinised saline to test its calibre.

### 6. Heparin, clamps and anastomosis
**Why:** The artery is controlled proximally and distally, opened, and the vein sewn on. Clamp time is 15-25 min.
**Anaesthesia:** Heparin practice varies from none, through local flushing, to 2,000-5,000 units IV; uraemic platelets already bleed. Maintain the blood pressure near the patient's normal, which is often high: a fistula sewn at a systolic of 90 mmHg under sedation may not run, and hypotension after clamp release is the commonest anaesthetic contribution to early thrombosis. Treat with metaraminol in small doses, not fluid. Keep the patient still during the back-wall sutures.

### 7. Clamp release and assessment of flow
**Why:** The surgeon feels for the thrill and may measure flow. Poor flow leads to a search for a kink, a twisted vein, a competing branch or spasm, and occasionally to revision at a more proximal level under the same anaesthetic.
**Anaesthesia:** A block that covers the elbow as well as the wrist allows that proximal revision without re-anaesthetising, which is an argument for a plexus block over a forearm block or infiltration. Systemic haemodynamic change from opening a new fistula is negligible at this stage; the cardiac effects of a high-flow fistula develop over weeks.

### 8. Closure and transposition tunnelling
**Why:** A simple fistula is closed in two layers. In a one-stage basilic transposition the vein is passed through a subcutaneous tunnel on the anterior arm before being joined to the brachial artery.
**Anaesthesia:** Tunnelling is the most stimulating moment and tests the intercostobrachial infiltration. Ask the surgeon to infiltrate the wound for when the block recedes. No drains and no constrictive dressings are used on the arm.

### 9. Recovery and discharge
**Why:** The surgeon wants a warm arm, a palpable thrill and a hand that is not ischaemic.
**Anaesthesia:** Before discharge check the thrill, the radial pulse, and the colour, warmth and movement of the fingers. A blocked arm cannot report ischaemic pain, so steal has to be looked for: a cold, pale hand after a brachial artery-based fistula in a diabetic patient is the pattern. Send the patient home with the arm in a sling until the block has gone, written advice not to allow blood pressure measurement, venepuncture or tight clothing on that arm, and paracetamol. Arrange dialysis as scheduled; the operation does not change the timetable.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a small superficial wound, so the block is the anaesthetic and the vasodilator, and the analgesic strategy afterwards is paracetamol alone. A long-acting block with ropivacaine gives 8-14 h of analgesia, by which time wound pain is mild. Surgeon infiltration at closure covers the transition. A few doses of an opioid suitable for renal failure (oxycodone in reduced dose, or buprenorphine or hydromorphone where preferred) are reasonable to supply after a basilic vein transposition and unnecessary after a wrist fistula.

Drug choice follows the kidneys. NSAIDs are contraindicated in the pre-dialysis patient and avoided in the dialysing patient with residual function or bleeding risk. Morphine and codeine are avoided because their active metabolites accumulate; tramadol needs dose reduction and lowers seizure threshold. Gabapentinoids, if the patient already takes them, are continued at their renally adjusted dose.

Whether to block at all is settled, as far as the evidence goes, by the patency data and not by pain: local infiltration provides adequate anaesthesia for a wrist fistula, and the reason to prefer a plexus block is the 20 percentage point difference in patency in the randomised trial. Where a block is declined or contraindicated, infiltration with light sedation is acceptable anaesthesia, and the surgeon should know that the vessels will be smaller. General anaesthesia vasodilates as well, but brings the airway, haemodynamic and drug-handling problems of renal failure and is the third choice. That ordering is the author's judgement applied to limited trial data.

Rebound is not a feature. Escalation triggers are surgical. Severe hand pain with pallor, coolness and weakness in the hours or days after surgery is arterial steal; a rare, early, very painful hand with profound weakness and little visible ischaemia after a brachial artery fistula in a diabetic patient is ischaemic monomelic neuropathy, which needs the fistula closed urgently to save nerve function. A tense, enlarging swelling is haematoma, and in the upper arm it can compress the fistula and the median nerve.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Hyperkalaemia on the day. The commonest reason for cancellation. Suxamethonium raises potassium by a further 0.5 mmol/L, which is one more reason to avoid needing a rapid sequence induction.
- Fluid overload. Orthopnoea, a raised venous pressure and basal crackles in a patient who has missed dialysis: lying flat is difficult, a phrenic palsy is poorly tolerated, and the case should follow dialysis, not precede it.
- Post-dialysis depletion. A patient dialysed that morning is volume-depleted, may still be heparinised, and drops their pressure with sedation or sympathetic block. A gap of at least 4-6 h after dialysis, and preferably the next day, avoids both.
- Bleeding tendency. Uraemic platelet dysfunction, antiplatelets and residual dialysis heparin favour a compressible block site and ultrasound guidance.
- Brachiobasilic transposition. One-stage or two-stage; longer, with a long medial arm wound. A supraclavicular or infraclavicular block plus intercostobrachial infiltration, or general anaesthesia if the patient cannot lie for 2-3 h.
- Prosthetic grafts. Forearm loop or upper arm straight grafts are tunnelled subcutaneously, need antibiotic prophylaxis, and can be needled within days to weeks. Tunnelling pain and post-operative swelling are greater than for a native fistula.
- Lower limb access. Thigh loop grafts in patients with no arm options are done under spinal anaesthesia with the usual checks on coagulation and volume.
- Revision, ligation and thrombectomy. Fistula thrombectomy and angioplasty are often done in the angiography suite under local infiltration; open revision for aneurysm or steal uses the same block. A bleeding fistula aneurysm is a surgical emergency with direct pressure applied until theatre.
- Existing high-flow fistula. A mature fistula carrying 1.5-2 L/min is a left-to-right shunt and can contribute to high-output heart failure; it matters when the patient returns for other surgery more than on the day it is made.
- The emergency variant. Urgent access for a patient presenting in established renal failure is a dialysis catheter, not a fistula. The semi-urgent fistula cases are thrombectomy of a clotted access and ligation for steal or bleeding, in patients who may have missed dialysis: potassium and volume come first.

## Key sources

The central claim on this card, that the choice of anaesthetic changes the surgical outcome, rests on one well-conducted three-centre randomised trial with twelve-month follow-up and a meta-analysis of four small trials whose authors noted methodological flaws. The effect is large and the mechanism is plausible, but a definitive multicentre trial was thought necessary by the same investigators. The renal pharmacology and the vein preservation rules are consensus. There is no procedure-specific pain literature, and none is needed for a wound of this size.
