## What the surgeon is doing and why

Most gastrectomies are for adenocarcinoma. The stomach has a rich submucosal lymphatic plexus that drains to nodes along its four named arteries and then to the coeliac axis, so a curative operation is two operations in one: remove enough stomach to clear the tumour with a margin, and remove the nodes along the left gastric, common hepatic and splenic arteries and the coeliac trunk (a D2 lymphadenectomy). Then restore continuity. Most patients in Australian and European practice have had perioperative chemotherapy and are anaemic, have lost weight and may have a stomach that empties poorly.

How much stomach goes depends on where the tumour is.

- Distal or subtotal gastrectomy for tumours of the antrum and body. The duodenum is divided just beyond the pylorus and stapled closed (the duodenal stump); the stomach is divided high, leaving a small proximal pouch on the short gastric vessels. Reconstruction joins the pouch to the duodenum (Billroth I), to a loop of jejunum (Billroth II) or to a Roux limb.
- Total gastrectomy for proximal or diffuse tumours. The oesophagus is divided at the hiatus and a Roux limb of jejunum is brought up and joined to it. The oesophagojejunostomy is the high-risk anastomosis: it lies at the hiatus, under some tension, on bowel with a mesenteric blood supply, and a leak there drains into the mediastinum.
- Wedge or sleeve resections for gastrointestinal stromal tumours, which need no nodal dissection and are much smaller operations.

The node dissection is the long, bloody and physiologically active part. The surgeon strips lymphatic tissue off the hepatic artery, the splenic artery along the top of the pancreas, and the coeliac trunk, working on the surface of the pancreas and next to the splenic hilum. Pancreatic injury (later a fistula), splenic injury (bleeding now, sometimes splenectomy) and chyle leak come from here.

The approach is the variable that changes your plan most. In the KLASS-01 trial of 1,416 patients with early distal cancers, laparoscopic resection had fewer complications overall than open (13.0% against 19.9%) and fewer wound complications. Laparoscopic and robotic gastrectomy are now standard for distal resections in many units and increasingly for total gastrectomy. The open operation, through an upper midline or bilateral subcostal (rooftop) incision, remains for bulky tumours, multivisceral resection, hostile abdomens and emergencies.

The pain logic: for open surgery the dominant mechanism is abdominal wall, and it is the worst version of it. The incision sits immediately below the costal margin, the upper recti and the costal attachments of the diaphragm move with each breath, and a fixed retractor has held the costal margins apart for hours. Pain is severe on coughing for five to seven days and the consequence is basal atelectasis and pneumonia. A rooftop incision cuts both recti transversely and is the most painful version. Open upper abdominal surgery is one of this book's anchors for a score of 5, and the open gastrectomy is scored 5 here, in line with open liver and pancreatic resection through the same incisions: the early peak is controlled by the catheter, but the pain outlasts 72 hours and is re-provoked by every cough. Under the wall is a visceral and retroperitoneal component from the coeliac dissection, felt as deep epigastric and back pain. The laparoscopic operation removes most of the wall component and leaves a moderate, short course that scores 3, with a trajectory nearer [4, 4, 3, 2, 1].

## Steps

### 1. Preassessment
**Why:** The surgeon is staging, restaging after chemotherapy and deciding the extent of resection.
**Anaesthesia:** Haemoglobin and iron (chronic blood loss is the rule), albumin and weight loss, the cardiac and marrow effects of chemotherapy, and whether the stomach empties: early satiety and vomiting mean a reservoir of food and fluid on the day whatever the fasting time. Intravenous iron and nutritional support in the weeks before are worth more than anything done on the day. Discuss the epidural or its alternative.

### 2. Regional technique and induction
**Why:** Nothing surgical yet.
**Anaesthesia:** Thoracic epidural at T7-T9 awake for an open operation; intrathecal morphine for laparoscopic surgery if that is the plan. Rapid sequence induction for obstruction, bleeding or poor emptying, with the nasogastric tube aspirated first where one is in place. Tracheal tube, arterial line for open, total and long robotic cases, warming, urinary catheter. Antibiotics before incision.

### 3. Incision or ports, and staging laparoscopy
**Why:** The surgeon looks for peritoneal or liver metastases before committing to resection; unexpected disease ends the operation in 20 minutes.
**Anaesthesia:** Hold long-acting drugs until the surgeon has confirmed that the resection is going ahead. For laparoscopic and robotic surgery the patient goes steeply head up with legs apart; secure against sliding, protect the arms and face from the robot arms, and settle lines and tube length before docking because access is limited afterwards.

### 4. Mobilisation of the greater curve and omentum
**Why:** The greater omentum is detached from the transverse colon, the gastro-epiploic vessels are divided and the short gastric vessels are taken for a total gastrectomy.
**Anaesthesia:** Traction on the stomach and omentum pulls on the spleen; a capsular tear is the commonest cause of brisk bleeding in this phase. Bowel and mesenteric handling cause a period of vasodilatation and hypotension early in open surgery. Fluid by stroke volume response, not by habit.

### 5. Duodenal division
**Why:** The first part of the duodenum is cleared and stapled across; the stump is the join most likely to blow out later if the afferent limb obstructs.
**Anaesthesia:** No physiological event, but confirm with a look at the screen or a word that nothing of yours has passed through the pylorus.

### 6. D2 lymphadenectomy
**Why:** Nodal tissue is cleared from the hepatic artery, the coeliac trunk and the splenic artery, and the left gastric artery is divided at its origin.
**Anaesthesia:** The longest phase, with steady ooze and occasional abrupt bleeding from the left gastric or splenic vessels. Dissection around the coeliac plexus produces swings in heart rate and pressure, usually hypotension and bradycardia with traction and hypertension with direct stimulation, that do not track depth of anaesthesia. Keep the patient warm and the neuromuscular block deep for laparoscopic work at the back of the lesser sac.

### 7. Proximal division of the stomach or oesophagus
**Why:** The stomach is stapled across below the cardia for a distal resection, or the oesophagus is divided at the hiatus for a total gastrectomy.
**Anaesthesia:** Before the stapler closes, withdraw the nasogastric tube, temperature probe and any bougie to 25-30 cm or out, and say aloud that the oesophagus is clear. For a total gastrectomy the hiatal dissection can open the pleura or the mediastinum to carbon dioxide during laparoscopy: a rising end-tidal carbon dioxide and airway pressure at this point is capnothorax and responds to PEEP and a lower insufflation pressure.

### 8. Reconstruction
**Why:** Roux-en-Y: the jejunum is divided 20-40 cm beyond the duodenojejunal flexure, the distal end is brought up to the gastric pouch or oesophagus, and the proximal end is rejoined 40-60 cm downstream. The oesophagojejunostomy is made with a circular stapler (the anvil passed from above or below), a linear stapler or by hand.
**Anaesthesia:** Some techniques need you to pass the anvil of the circular stapler through the mouth on a carrier tube: lubricate, deflate the tube cuff briefly if it will not pass the cricopharyngeus, and never force it. Hold the mean arterial pressure near baseline while the joins are made; if the epidural is the cause of hypotension, treat with noradrenaline, not litres of fluid, because oedematous jejunum heals badly. A leak test with air or dye through a tube you advance under vision is common.

### 9. Drains, feeding access and closure
**Why:** Drains near the duodenal stump and the anastomosis; a feeding jejunostomy or nasojejunal tube in malnourished patients and after total gastrectomy in some units.
**Anaesthesia:** If the plan is fascial plane catheters, they go in now: rectus sheath catheters under vision for a midline wound, subcostal TAP catheters for a rooftop. Keep relaxation until the fascia is closed. For laparoscopic surgery ask for infiltration of the extraction wound and ports.

### 10. Emergence and the first days
**Why:** The surgeon wants early mobilisation, sips on day 1 and no vomiting against a fresh oesophagojejunostomy.
**Anaesthesia:** Extubate in theatre, sitting up, fully reversed. Test the epidural before leaving theatre. The complications arrive in order: atelectasis and pneumonia from day 2 if coughing is painful; anastomotic or duodenal stump leak from day 3-7, announced by tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, new pain or a change in the drain; pancreatic fistula with amylase-rich drain fluid. Hypotension on the ward with an epidural running is managed with a reduced rate and a vasopressor before fluid boluses.

## Pain plan

The plan follows the approach, because the approach decides whether the mechanism is abdominal wall or laparoscopic.

Open gastrectomy. A high upper abdominal incision needs a continuous technique that makes coughing possible for five days. Thoracic epidural at T7-T9 is the technique with the longest record in open upper abdominal surgery and the ERAS Society gastrectomy guideline, like its sister guidelines for open abdominal surgery of that era, was written around it. There is no PROSPECT guideline for gastrectomy; the nearest procedure-specific review, for open liver resection through the same kind of incision, recommends paracetamol and an NSAID with either thoracic epidural or bilateral oblique subcostal TAP blocks, and opioid for rescue. The defensible default is therefore an epidural run dilute (ropivacaine 0.2% or bupivacaine 0.1-0.125% with fentanyl) for three to four days, with regular paracetamol. The alternative, increasingly chosen because epidurals fail in a meaningful fraction and cause hypotension that complicates fluid management, is intrathecal morphine 150-300 micrograms plus bilateral rectus sheath catheters (midline) or subcostal TAP catheters (rooftop) and a PCA. No trial in gastrectomy shows which is better; the choice is author's judgement and depends on which technique the ward can look after.

Both techniques carry conditions. Intrathecal morphine, in open or laparoscopic surgery, commits the ward to watching for delayed respiratory depression: sedation score, respiratory rate and oximetry at a frequency and for a duration matched to the dose and the patient's risk (the [truncal blocks and epidural chapter](../a/a02-truncal-blocks-epidural.html) describes the risk window), a PCA with no background infusion, and no other long-acting opioid or sedative charted during that window. Use the lower end of the dose range in the elderly, the frail and the patient with sleep apnoea, and do not give it at all if the destination ward cannot provide that observation. The epidural has to coexist with a month of thromboprophylaxis: site it before any heparin, and separate each dose of low molecular weight heparin from catheter removal by the minimum intervals in the current ASRA guideline or the local policy built on it.

Laparoscopic and robotic gastrectomy. The wall component is ports and a small extraction wound. Intrathecal morphine before induction, or bilateral subcostal TAP blocks at the start, with port and wound infiltration, regular paracetamol and a PCA or oral opioid for 48 hours is sufficient; an epidural adds hypotension and immobility for little gain. An IV lidocaine infusion during the case is a reasonable adjunct when no block is used.

Adjuncts in both: NSAIDs are effective and are withheld by many surgeons for the first days because of concern about anastomotic healing and the kidneys; where the surgeon is comfortable and renal function is normal, a short course from day 1 or 2 reduces opioid. Low-dose ketamine for the opioid-tolerant. Gabapentinoids are not routine.

Rebound is moderate: the predictable failure is an epidural that falls out or goes one-sided on day 1 with no systemic opioid charted. Chart the rescue PCA at the outset. Escalation triggers: pain that increases after day 2, shoulder-tip or back pain, tachycardia or new atrial fibrillation mean leak, collection or pancreatitis until imaging says otherwise.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Emergency gastrectomy for bleeding. A failed endoscopic and radiological control of a bleeding ulcer or tumour: stomach full of blood, haemorrhage protocol running, rapid sequence induction with the surgeon scrubbed. Under-running the vessel is preferred to resection where possible. Critical care afterwards.
- Perforated gastric ulcer or tumour. Managed as an emergency laparotomy; biopsy and patch or resection.
- Gastric outlet obstruction. Days or weeks of vomiting give hypochloraemic, hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis and dehydration. Correct with sodium chloride 0.9% and potassium before elective surgery and decompress the stomach with a wide-bore tube; the aspiration risk at induction is very high.
- Total gastrectomy with splenectomy or distal pancreatectomy. More bleeding, a left subphrenic collection risk, and post-splenectomy vaccination and antibiotic prophylaxis to arrange.
- Transhiatal extension or a thoracoabdominal approach for junctional tumours turns this into an oesophagectomy: see [oesophagectomy](oesophagectomy.html).
- Robotic surgery. Long, steeply head up, with fixed access: facial and airway oedema are less of a problem than in head-down robotic surgery, but calf compartment pressure, nerve injury from arm boards and hypothermia are not.
- Malnutrition and refeeding. A patient who has eaten little for weeks and is then fed by jejunostomy can drop phosphate, potassium and magnesium within two days.
- Late consequences that return to you. Vitamin B12 and iron deficiency, dumping, and a patient with no stomach who is not safely fasted by the clock: after total gastrectomy the Roux limb refluxes freely.
- Stromal tumour wedge resection. A one-hour laparoscopic case with no nodal dissection; treat as a laparoscopic upper abdominal operation with a score of 2-3.

## Key sources

The ERAS Society gastrectomy guideline dates from 2014 and acknowledges that only eight of its 25 items had procedure-specific evidence. KLASS-01 is strong evidence that the laparoscopic approach reduces morbidity for early distal cancers, and it is the reason the analgesic plan divides by approach. There is no PROSPECT review for gastrectomy and no adequate trial comparing epidural with intrathecal morphine and fascial plane catheters in this operation; that section is extrapolated from open liver resection and is author's judgement. The haemodynamic effects of coeliac dissection, the stapler precautions and the management of outlet obstruction are standard teaching and are unsourced. The intrathecal morphine monitoring and anticoagulant timing statements are consensus principles; the intervals are in the ASRA guideline and local policy.
