## What the surgeon is doing and why

Pectus excavatum is a sternum pushed backwards by overgrown, misdirected costal cartilages. The depression narrows the space between sternum and spine, displaces and compresses the right ventricle, and restricts the chest wall. Most patients are adolescent boys, tall and thin; some have a connective tissue disorder. The complaints are exertional breathlessness, reduced exercise capacity, chest pain and distress about appearance, and severity is expressed on CT as the ratio of the transverse chest diameter to the narrowest sternum-to-spine distance.

There are two ways to bring the sternum forward.

The minimally invasive repair (Nuss) changes nothing in the bone or cartilage. A convex steel bar, bent to the shape the chest should have, is passed from one mid-axillary line to the other behind the sternum with its curve facing backwards, and then rotated through 180 degrees so that the curve pushes the sternum out. The chest wall remodels around it over two to three years, after which the bar is removed.

```
   before flip                    after flip
   (bar concave to sternum)       (bar convex, sternum lifted)

      ___   sternum   ___             _____________
     /   \___     ___/   \           /   sternum   \
    | rib    \___/    rib |         | rib ========= rib |
    |    ====bar=====     |         |      bar          |
     \       heart       /           \      heart      /
```

Everything that makes this operation hard for you is in that picture. The bar is passed blind or under thoracoscopic vision through the narrowest part of the chest, between the back of the sternum and the pericardium. And once it is flipped, it is a spring holding the anterior chest wall away from where every cartilage and joint wants to sit.

The open repair (Ravitch) removes the deformed cartilages subperichondrially through an anterior incision, breaks the sternum forward with a wedge osteotomy and holds it with a strut or plate. It is used for asymmetric and complex deformities, for carinatum, and for some adults. In pooled comparisons the bar procedure is quicker with less blood loss and the hospital stay is the same, which reflects the pain.

The pain logic: this is a 5 by the definition the book uses, tissue held under tension and loaded by movement. The mechanism is chest wall, but not the nerve-in-a-rib-space mechanism of thoracotomy. Force is applied continuously to the sternum and through it to every costochondral junction, to the ribs where the bar rests laterally, and to the costovertebral joints behind. The pain is bilateral, anterior and posterior, severe for three to five days, and re-provoked for weeks by breathing deeply, coughing, sneezing, sitting up and rolling over. The older and stiffer the chest, the greater the force and the pain.

## Steps

### 1. Preoperative assessment
**Why:** The surgeon selects on severity and symptoms and chooses the number of bars.
**Anaesthesia:** Most patients are well. Look for the exceptions. Marfan syndrome and related disorders bring aortic root dilatation and mitral valve prolapse: an echocardiogram is part of the work-up. A deep deformity may show right ventricular compression and reduced exercise capacity on testing, which improves when the sternum is lifted. Scoliosis may complicate an epidural. Ask about metal allergy. Then spend time on the pain conversation with the patient and the family, because expectations set here determine the first week: what the plan is, that the chest will feel tight and sore whatever is done, that numbness after cryoablation is intended and temporary, and that walking on day 1 is expected. Preoperative gabapentin was the one adjunct associated with lower opioid use in a 20-centre cohort; some units give it on the morning of surgery.

### 2. Induction and lines
**Why:** Nothing surgical.
**Anaesthesia:** Intravenous induction, single-lumen tube, two cannulae. Drug doses are by weight in the adolescent; see [the child: doses, fluids, airway and the neonate](../a/a13-the-child.html). A deeply depressed sternum can reduce venous return further under positive pressure, so hypotension after induction in a severe case responds to volume and to lower airway pressures more than to vasopressor alone. Defibrillator pads go on before draping in a position that will not be under the bar or the incisions.

### 3. Regional technique before incision
**Why:** Nothing surgical. The correction will be made within the hour and the pain begins the moment the bar is flipped.
**Anaesthesia:** Where cryoablation is planned, place bilateral erector spinae plane or paravertebral blocks at about T5 after induction, with ropivacaine 0.2-0.375% to a total dose within weight limits, to cover the operation and the first night. In children a randomised trial found the erector spinae block non-inferior to paravertebral block for pain and opioid use at 24 hours, and it is the simpler bilateral technique. Where the plan is an epidural, site it at T5-T7, awake in a cooperative adolescent or asleep according to unit practice, and test it. See [analgesia for the trunk](../a/a02-truncal-blocks-epidural.html).

### 4. Positioning
**Why:** The surgeon needs both lateral chest walls and a clear run across the front.
**Anaesthesia:** Both arms are out or up for the whole case in a tall, thin patient with little soft tissue: keep abduction at or under 90 degrees, forearms supported, elbows padded, head neutral. Brachial plexus stretch is the avoidable injury here. After positioning, check that the cannulae still run and that you can reach them.

### 5. Thoracoscopy and insufflation
**Why:** A camera through the right chest shows the retrosternal space, the pericardium and the point of deepest depression. Carbon dioxide at low pressure pushes the lung down.
**Anaesthesia:** Insufflation at 5-8 mmHg raises airway pressure and can reduce venous return; reduce tidal volume, raise the rate, and treat a fall in blood pressure by asking for a lower pressure first. End-tidal carbon dioxide climbs by absorption. A sudden fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide with hypotension is gas embolism or a large fall in output and needs the surgeon to stop.

### 6. Intercostal nerve cryoablation
**Why:** A cryoprobe is applied under vision to the intercostal nerve in each of four or five spaces on each side, centred on the bar, posteriorly, so that the whole nerve distal to it is affected. Freezing produces axonal degeneration with the nerve sheath intact, so the axon regrows along it over the following months.
**Anaesthesia:** This adds time: 46.5 minutes in the randomised trial. Both sides are done, which means insufflation or lung retraction on the left as well. In that trial of 20 patients, cryoablation against epidural shortened the median stay from five days to three and reduced opioid by 416 mg of oral morphine equivalent per patient with equivalent pain scores, and a meta-analysis of 196 patients and an 898-patient multicentre cohort point the same way. Two things to plan for. The effect is widely reported to be incomplete on the first night, which is why the block in step 3 exists. And cryoablation covers the anterior and lateral chest wall, not the costovertebral joints or the paraspinal muscles, so back pain is not treated by it.

### 7. Retrosternal tunnel
**Why:** Through small lateral incisions the surgeon passes a curved introducer into the right chest, across the mediastinum directly behind the sternum at its deepest point, and out through the left chest. Many surgeons first lift the sternum with a crane, a bone clamp or a subxiphoid finger to widen the gap.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the dangerous minute. The introducer tip passes between sternum and pericardium with millimetres to spare. Turn the ECG tone up and watch the trace and the arterial or plethysmograph waveform: ectopics are common as the pericardium is pressed and settle at once; sudden loss of output, a new tamponade picture or blood in the camera view is cardiac or great vessel injury. In a survey-based review, life-threatening complications were estimated at under 0.1% of about 50,000 procedures, with cardiac perforation heading the list of injuries and previous chest surgery, severe deformity and inexperience as risk factors. The response is immediate sternotomy with the introducer left in place, transfusion through the cannulae you already have, and cardiac surgical help. Blood grouped and a sternal saw in the room are the preparation. Hold ventilation briefly on request as the tip crosses.

### 8. Bar passage and the flip
**Why:** A tape drawn through the tunnel pulls the pre-bent bar across, concave side up. It is then rotated with flipping tools so that the convexity lifts the sternum. Deep or stiff chests take two or three bars.
**Anaesthesia:** The flip is abrupt. Chest wall mechanics change in a second: the anterior wall is now fixed outward, compliance usually improves, and the compressed right heart fills better, so blood pressure often rises. It is also the moment the pain generator is switched on; deepen analgesia before it, not after the heart rate climbs. Ask the surgeon to look with the camera at the pericardium and both internal mammary pedicles after the flip.

### 9. Fixation, evacuating the pneumothorax and closure
**Why:** A bar that rotates back loses the correction and can injure what lies behind it, so each end is fixed with a stabiliser plate, pericostal sutures or both. Carbon dioxide and air are then expelled from both pleural spaces before the last port site is closed. A drain is not usually left.
**Anaesthesia:** Pericostal sutures pass around a rib beside the intercostal bundle and are a source of both bleeding and later focal pain; the surgeon needs stillness while they are tied, so no coughing on a light plane. Then, on request, give a slow sustained inflation to 25-30 cmH2O and hold it while the surgeon closes around a catheter or the port held under water until bubbling stops. A small residual apical pneumothorax on the recovery radiograph is common and resolves. A larger one, or one that grows, needs a drain. Give paracetamol and the NSAID now.

### 10. Emergence
**Why:** The surgeon wants a patient who wakes without bucking against a bar whose fixation is hours old.
**Anaesthesia:** Establish analgesia before waking: the block or epidural working, a long-acting opioid on board if no neuraxial opioid was given, and antiemetics in. Extubate smoothly, deep or awake according to the airway, but not through a coughing fit. A low-dose dexmedetomidine or clonidine supplement helps both emergence and early analgesia in adolescents. In recovery, sit the patient up without letting them pull with their arms or twist.

### 11. The first week
**Why:** The surgeon wants the patient walking on day 1, a check radiograph showing the bar in position, and discharge once oral analgesia holds.
**Anaesthesia:** With cryoablation the course is usually two or three nights: regular paracetamol and NSAID, a short course of a muscle relaxant such as diazepam for spasm in some units, oral opioid on request, and a clear weaning plan for home. Without it the course is longer. An epidural runs for about three days and brings a urinary catheter, itching and the step-down problem when it stops; a PCA regimen brings nausea and constipation, both of which matter in a patient who must not strain. The back and shoulder pain that appears on day 2-3 is postural and from the costovertebral joints; it responds to positioning, heat and the NSAID. Constipation prophylaxis starts on day 0.

### 12. Bar removal, two to three years later
**Why:** The bar is freed from scar and its stabiliser at one or both ends, straightened and pulled out through the original incision.
**Anaesthesia:** A short day-case anaesthetic with little pain, and one rare, lethal complication: the bar may have become adherent to pericardium, lung or an internal mammary vessel, and pulling it tears them. Two of the deaths in the international survey occurred at removal. Prepare as for insertion with reliable access, blood grouped and the means to open the chest, and watch the monitor as the bar slides.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a chest wall held under tension, bilateral, with a peak that lasts days and a tail that lasts weeks. No single-shot technique outlasts it. The plan needs something that works for days over the anterior chest, a bridge for the first night, and a multimodal oral regimen that the patient goes home on.

There is no PROSPECT guideline for pectus repair. The evidence is specific to the operation and has shifted practice in one direction. Thoracic epidural was the standard for two decades; a meta-analysis found it about one point better than PCA for pain in the first 12 hours with no difference in other outcomes, and concluded that either was acceptable. Intercostal cryoablation has since been compared with epidural in one small randomised trial and several cohorts: equivalent pain scores, a hospital stay two to three days shorter and much less opioid, with no increase in complications in an 898-patient multicentre series. The quality of that evidence is low to moderate, the randomised trial had 20 patients, and long-term sensory outcomes are less well described than the short-term gains.

The defensible default where the surgeon offers it is therefore cryoablation of the intercostal nerves at the levels around the bar, bilateral erector spinae or paravertebral blocks before incision as the bridge, and a multimodal regimen: preoperative gabapentin where the unit uses it, paracetamol and an NSAID regularly, dexamethasone, low-dose ketamine intraoperatively, and oral opioid on request. Where cryoablation is not available, the author's preference is a thoracic epidural at T5-T7 for three days in a patient and unit suited to it, and otherwise bilateral erector spinae catheters with a PCA. That ordering is judgement: the comparative data between epidural, catheters and PCA do not separate them clearly.

Cryoablation has costs to state plainly. The anterior chest is numb for some months, and the nipples with it. Sensation returns as the axons regrow, and a period of tingling or hypersensitivity during recovery is described; persistent neuropathic pain has been reported, more in adults, and some surgeons restrict the technique to adolescents for that reason. No incidence figure was verified for this card.

Rebound risk is high if the bridge is missing or short. The typical failure is the first night after cryoablation with no block: the nerve freeze has not taken full effect and the patient is on a PCA at maximum. The other is the day the epidural stops. Start the oral opioid and continue the NSAID before the infusion is turned off, and stop it in the morning.

Escalation triggers: sudden new sharp pain with a visible change in chest shape or a clunk is bar displacement and needs a radiograph; pleuritic pain with breathlessness is a pneumothorax or effusion; chest pain with fever in the first weeks is pericarditis or infection around the bar; pain that is worsening at three weeks instead of improving deserves a surgical review.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Adults. A calcified, rigid chest wall needs more force, more bars and sometimes sternal osteotomy or an open hybrid repair. Pain is worse and longer. Cardiac compression is more often symptomatic, so an arterial line is reasonable.
- Open (Ravitch) repair and pectus carinatum. An anterior incision with muscle flaps raised, cartilages resected and a sternal osteotomy: incisional, muscle and bone pain across the front of the chest. Thoracic epidural or bilateral paravertebral catheters are the conventional choice, with a drain under the flaps for some days.
- Connective tissue disorders. Aortic root protection means avoiding hypertension at laryngoscopy and at the flip; spontaneous pneumothorax is more likely; healing is slower.
- Redo surgery and previous sternotomy. Adhesions between sternum and heart make the retrosternal passage far more dangerous; these cases are done with full invasive monitoring and sometimes with a subxiphoid incision to pass a finger behind the sternum.
- Sternal elevation devices. A crane or suction device lifting the sternum before the tunnel is made increases the safe space; ventilation is briefly easier while it is on.
- Epidural in the anaesthetised adolescent. Practice varies between awake and asleep placement; the thoracic level and a thin back make ultrasound or a careful landmark technique worthwhile, and a failed epidural discovered on the ward at midnight is the commonest reason for a bad first night.
- Urinary retention. Common with epidural opioid and with PCA in this age group; a catheter for the duration of the epidural is usual.
- Cardiopulmonary resuscitation with a bar in place. Compressions are less effective and need more force; defibrillator pads go anteroposterior. The patient should carry this information.
- Other paediatric thoracoscopic work is covered under [paediatric laparoscopic and thoracoscopic surgery](paediatric-laparoscopic-and-thoracoscopic-surgery.html), and adult thoracoscopic analgesia under [lobectomy and wedge resection](video-assisted-and-open-lobectomy-and.html).

## Key sources

The analgesic evidence is unusually procedure-specific but small: one 20-patient randomised trial of cryoablation against epidural, a meta-analysis dominated by observational studies, and a large retrospective multicentre cohort, all pointing the same way on length of stay and opioid use. The epidural against PCA comparison is older and inconclusive. The life-threatening complication figures come from a survey with an estimated denominator and are certainly incomplete, as the authors say. Statements on the delayed onset of cryoanalgesia, the duration of numbness, neuropathic symptoms during nerve recovery and the greater pain in adults are widely reported clinical experience for which no figure was verified during drafting. Drug doses by weight are standard paediatric practice.
