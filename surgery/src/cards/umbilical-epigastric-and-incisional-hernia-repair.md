## What the surgeon is doing and why

The midline of the abdominal wall is the linea alba, a strip of interwoven aponeurosis where the two rectus sheaths meet. It has one natural scar, the umbilicus, and it is where surgeons make most of their incisions. Hernias here are holes in that strip. An umbilical hernia comes through or beside the umbilical cicatrix; an epigastric hernia is a small defect above it, usually containing only preperitoneal fat and painful out of proportion to its size; an incisional hernia is a failure of a previous closure, which may be one hole or several along the length of the scar (the "Swiss cheese" defect), and a port-site hernia is the small version. This card covers the common, small-to-moderate end. Wide defects, loss of domain and component separation are on the [complex abdominal wall reconstruction](complex-abdominal-wall-reconstruction.html) card.

Whatever the name, the surgeon reduces the contents, closes the hole, and reinforces the closure with mesh, because suture alone recurs. The joint European and American guideline recommends mesh for umbilical and epigastric hernias and favours an open repair with a flat mesh in the preperitoneal plane for most, reserving laparoscopy for larger defects or patients at high risk of wound complications. The options differ in which layer holds the mesh:

- Open suture repair. For a defect under about 1 cm. A curved incision by the umbilicus, sac reduced, defect closed transversely. Twenty minutes.
- Open mesh repair. The same incision, slightly larger, with a flat mesh placed behind the defect (preperitoneal or retromuscular) or a patch device, and the fascia closed over it. For incisional hernias the old scar is reopened, the sac and adherent bowel are dissected free, the posterior rectus sheaths are opened and closed behind the muscle, mesh is laid on them (the Rives-Stoppa or sublay repair) and the linea alba is closed in front.
- Laparoscopic intraperitoneal mesh. Ports are placed laterally, adhesions to the wall are taken down, the contents reduced, the defect ideally sutured shut, and a coated mesh fixed to the inside of the abdominal wall with a ring or two of tacks and often transfascial sutures. No large wound, and a much lower wound infection rate in obese patients.
- Laparoscopic or robotic retromuscular repair. The mesh goes behind the rectus without a large incision; technically demanding, long, and increasingly common.

Pain logic. The dominant mechanism is the abdominal wall under tension: a closed defect in a structure that is loaded by every increase in abdominal pressure. Open repair of a small hernia concentrates this in a few centimetres, and a rectus sheath block covers it well. The laparoscopic intraperitoneal repair hurts more than its small wounds suggest, and the usual explanation is the fixation: tacks driven through peritoneum into muscle around a 15-20 cm circle, and sutures that pass through the full thickness of the wall and are tied over muscle. Trials comparing tack types, glue and sutures have not shown that any fixation method hurts less than another, so the pain appears to belong to the operation and not the device. The author's working figure is worst pain on movement of about 5 on day 1 with blocks in place, and noticeably sore for a week.

## Steps

### 1. Reading the booking: defect size and patient
**Why:** The surgeon's choice of technique follows defect width, previous repairs, body habitus and wound risk.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask for the defect width from the imaging or the surgeon. Under 2 cm in a slim patient is a day case with a supraglottic airway or even local infiltration. Above 4 cm, recurrent, or incisional means a tracheal tube, relaxation and probably a night in hospital. The population carries risk factors that caused the hernia: obesity, diabetes, smoking, chronic cough, constipation, previous laparotomies, and sometimes cirrhosis with ascites, in which the umbilical hernia is a pressure valve and the perioperative problem is the liver.

### 2. Anaesthetic and block
**Why:** The surgeon needs a relaxed wall to reduce the contents and close fascia without tension.
**Anaesthesia:** For open umbilical and epigastric repair place bilateral rectus sheath blocks after induction and before incision: a randomised trial found lower pain scores throughout the first 24 hours and less morphine. For laparoscopic and incisional repairs use bilateral TAP blocks matched to the level of the defect (subcostal for upper midline, lateral for peri-umbilical and lower), or ask the surgeon to place them under vision; a rectus sheath block also reduced pain after laparoscopic intraperitoneal mesh repair in one trial. Neuromuscular block for laparoscopy and for any fascial closure of size.

### 3. Open repair: incision, sac and contents
**Why:** The sac is dissected off the umbilical skin and the fascial edges are defined. Contents are usually omentum or preperitoneal fat, reduced or excised.
**Anaesthesia:** Brief and minimally stimulating once the block is working. In incisional repair the sac often has bowel adherent inside it, and the dissection is an adhesiolysis with the usual risk of enterotomy; an unrecognised bowel injury under a mesh is the worst complication of the operation.

### 4. Mesh placement and fascial closure
**Why:** The preperitoneal or retromuscular space is developed for a few centimetres around the defect, the mesh is laid flat, and the fascia is closed over it with a slowly absorbed or permanent suture.
**Anaesthesia:** Relaxation matters at the moment of closure. Closing a moderate defect raises intra-abdominal pressure only slightly; if peak airway pressure rises by more than a few cmH2O the defect was bigger than booked and the patient needs watching. Infiltration of the wound by the surgeon complements the block at the skin.

### 5. Laparoscopic repair: entry and adhesiolysis
**Why:** The first port goes in as far from the hernia and old scars as possible, often the left upper quadrant, and adhesions between bowel or omentum and the anterior wall are divided sharply, without energy near bowel.
**Anaesthesia:** Entry into a previously operated abdomen risks bowel and vessel injury; a sudden fall in end-tidal CO2 or pressure at insufflation needs the usual differential. In obese patients, a level or slightly head-up table, PEEP and recruitment keep the lungs open. The surgeon works on the ceiling of the abdomen, so the ports are lateral and the arms are best tucked.

### 6. Defect closure and mesh fixation
**Why:** The pneumoperitoneum is lowered to bring the edges together, the defect is sutured, and the mesh is unrolled, centred with at least 5 cm of overlap and fixed.
**Anaesthesia:** Expect a request to drop the pressure and to keep the patient completely still while sutures are passed through the wall with a needle device. Each transfascial suture and each tack is a stimulus that a TAP block only partly covers; this is the step that generates the postoperative pain. Deep block until fixation is finished.

### 7. Open incisional retromuscular repair
**Why:** Through the old scar, the posterior rectus sheath is incised a centimetre from the midline on each side and separated from the muscle out to the lateral border, where the intercostal nerves and vessels enter. The posterior sheaths are sutured together, mesh is placed on them behind the muscle, drains are left, and the anterior sheath is closed.
**Anaesthesia:** One and a half to three hours, an open abdomen with heat loss, and modest blood loss from perforating vessels. Preserving the neurovascular bundles at the lateral edge is what keeps the rectus innervated. If the anterior fascia will not close without tension, the surgeon moves to a component separation and the case moves to the complex reconstruction card, with its airway pressure check before and after closure.

### 8. Emergence
**Why:** The repair is at its weakest before any tissue has grown into the mesh.
**Anaesthesia:** Plan the emergence around not coughing: full reversal, an opioid on board, lidocaine intravenously or a remifentanil taper if a tube is in place, or removal of a supraglottic airway deep in a suitable patient. Two antiemetics. Binder on if the surgeon uses one, which helps comfort on movement and limits seroma.

### 9. The incarcerated or strangulated hernia
**Why:** A tender, irreducible hernia with obstruction or skin changes needs the contents inspected and non-viable bowel or omentum resected. In a contaminated field the surgeon may suture alone, use a different mesh or stage the repair.
**Anaesthesia:** A bowel obstruction through a small incision: resuscitation, nasogastric tube, rapid sequence induction, arterial line in the frail. In cirrhosis, an umbilical hernia that ruptures or strangulates is a high-mortality emergency, with coagulopathy, ascites drainage, albumin replacement and hepatology input all needed.

### 10. First days
**Why:** The surgeon wants early walking and no straining; seroma under the skin or over the mesh is common and mostly left alone.
**Anaesthesia:** Regular paracetamol and NSAID, oral opioid for three to five days after laparoscopic or incisional repair, and a laxative with it, because straining is the mechanical enemy of the repair. Increasing pain with distension and tachycardia after laparoscopic repair is a missed bowel injury until proved otherwise.

## Pain plan

No PROSPECT guideline covers ventral hernia; the nearest is the open inguinal hernia review, whose pattern (local anaesthetic block or infiltration, paracetamol, NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, opioid as rescue) transfers directly. The block should match the wound. For a midline open repair of a primary hernia, bilateral rectus sheath blocks cover the anterior cutaneous branches of T9-T11 where they pierce the muscle, and are supported by a small randomised trial. For laparoscopic intraperitoneal mesh, the pain comes from a wide circle of fixation, so TAP blocks cover more of it than rectus sheath blocks alone; either reduced pain in the trials available. Infiltration by the surgeon at each port and suture site is additive, within the dose limit.

Scale the oral plan to the operation, not the booking name. Small open umbilical or epigastric repair: paracetamol and an NSAID for five days, a few opioid doses. Laparoscopic mesh repair or open incisional repair: the same base plus oral opioid for three to five days, and for larger incisional repairs a night or two of PCA. Dexamethasone 8 mg at induction helps both nausea and pain. NSAIDs are appropriate (there is no anastomosis) unless renal function or bleeding risk says otherwise.

Catheters are rarely needed. The exception is a longer open incisional repair in a patient with chronic pain, opioid tolerance or significant lung disease, where bilateral rectus sheath or TAP catheters for 48 hours are reasonable; this is the author's judgement.

Rebound is moderate after laparoscopic repair: single-shot TAP blocks wear off during the first night, and the patient should be told that day 1 is the worst day. Persistent point tenderness at a transfascial suture site beyond six to eight weeks is a recognised cause of chronic pain after these repairs and is sometimes treated by injection or suture removal.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Obesity. The commonest comorbidity and the reason for choosing laparoscopy; it is also what makes the pneumoperitoneum and the airway harder. Body mass index above 40 often leads to deferral for weight loss in elective cases.
- Cirrhosis and ascites. Elective repair only after ascites control; expect coagulopathy and postoperative ascitic leak.
- Pregnancy and the post-partum umbilical hernia. Repair is deferred until after childbearing when possible.
- Divarication of the recti. A stretched linea alba with no hole is not a hernia; some surgeons plicate it at the same operation, which lengthens the repair and adds tension.
- Chronic cough, constipation, prostatism. Each one loads the repair; optimise before, and treat cough and straining actively after.
- Local anaesthesia. A small epigastric or umbilical hernia in a thin patient can be repaired under infiltration and sedation; traction on the sac and peritoneum is the uncomfortable moment.
- Robotic repair. Longer, with docking constraints, and less fixation pain when the mesh is sutured or placed retromuscular.
- Parastomal hernia. A special case of incisional hernia with bowel running through the defect; laparoscopic mesh repair or relocation, longer and with enterotomy risk.
- The recurrence. Each re-repair is longer, with more adhesions, and moves the patient towards the complex reconstruction card.

## Key sources

The surgical guidance is consensus on weak evidence, as the guideline itself states. The analgesic evidence consists of small single-centre block trials and the fixation trials, which were designed to compare devices and incidentally show that none of them is painless. The statement that laparoscopic intraperitoneal mesh repair is more painful than its incisions suggest is the author's reading of clinical experience and the fixation literature, not the finding of a head-to-head trial.
