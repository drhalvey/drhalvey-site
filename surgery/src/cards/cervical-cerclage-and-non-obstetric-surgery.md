## What the surgeon is doing and why

Two different situations share this card because they share a patient: a woman with a continuing pregnancy who needs an operation that is not a delivery. In one the operation is on the pregnancy itself; in the other the pregnancy is a bystander.

Cervical cerclage treats a cervix that opens painlessly and too early. The cervix is meant to stay closed and firm against the weight of the pregnancy until term. When it shortens and dilates in the second trimester, from intrinsic weakness, previous surgery to the cervix or previous mid-trimester loss, the membranes prolapse and the pregnancy is lost or delivers very preterm. A cerclage is a non-absorbable suture or tape placed around the cervix to hold it shut. There are three settings. A history-indicated cerclage is placed electively at 12-14 weeks in a woman with previous losses: closed cervix, no urgency. An ultrasound-indicated cerclage is placed when surveillance shows the cervix shortening before 24 weeks. A rescue cerclage is placed when the cervix is already open and the membranes are visible or bulging into the vagina, at any point up to about 24 weeks: the surgeon has to push the membranes back without rupturing them and close the cervix under them.

Technique varies with the surgeon and the anatomy. A McDonald suture is a purse-string placed in four or five bites around the cervix at the level of the vaginal reflection, with no dissection, in ten minutes. A Shirodkar suture is placed higher, after incising the vaginal mucosa and pushing the bladder up, and is buried. A transabdominal cerclage, at the level of the internal os between the uterine vessels and the cervix, is for women in whom a vaginal suture has failed or the cervix is too short to hold one; it is placed laparoscopically or by a small laparotomy, before conception or at 10-14 weeks, and the baby is later delivered by caesarean.

Non-obstetric surgery is needed in a small proportion of pregnancies: appendicectomy, cholecystectomy, adnexal torsion or mass, trauma and fracture fixation, and occasionally cancer, neurosurgical or cardiac surgery. The surgeon does the same operation, by the same approach where possible. The largest dataset, 47,628 operations among 6.5 million English pregnancies, estimated one additional stillbirth for every 287 operations, one preterm delivery for every 31 and one low birthweight baby for every 39, and could not distinguish the effect of surgery and anaesthesia from the effect of the disease that prompted it. The authors' conclusion was that surgery in pregnancy is generally safe. The obstetric and anaesthetic colleges in the United States state jointly that no randomised data exist, that necessary surgery is never withheld because of pregnancy, and that purely elective surgery waits until after delivery.

The pain logic for cerclage is brief: traction on and a suture through the cervix give visceral, period-like cramps for some hours. For non-obstetric surgery the pain is that of the operation, found on its own card, with one pharmacological restriction and one diagnostic one: NSAIDs are avoided late in pregnancy, and abdominal pain after surgery has to be distinguished from contractions.

## Steps

### 1. Cerclage: which kind, and how urgent
**Why:** The obstetrician is balancing the benefit of mechanical support against the risk of rupturing membranes or provoking contractions by touching the cervix.
**Anaesthesia:** An elective cerclage at 12-14 weeks is day surgery in a well woman: the physiological changes of pregnancy are present but modest, aortocaval compression is not yet relevant, and aspiration risk is close to the non-pregnant baseline unless she has reflux. A rescue cerclage is a semi-urgent case in an anxious woman at 18-24 weeks who may have been lying head-down for hours. Ask about contractions, bleeding and fever, because infection or labour makes the procedure pointless and the obstetrician will want to know. Document the fetal heart beforehand.

### 2. The spinal for an elective cerclage
**Why:** The surgeon needs a still perineum, a relaxed vagina and no pain on cervical traction, for 15-30 minutes.
**Anaesthesia:** The afferents are sacral (vagina, perineum) and low thoracic (cervix and uterus, T10-L1), so the target is a block to T10. In the survey of obstetric anaesthetists spinal anaesthesia was the clear preference, with hyperbaric bupivacaine 10-12 mg or hyperbaric prilocaine 2% 40-60 mg, usually without opioid; prilocaine gave the faster recovery. The author's practice follows that: prilocaine where it is stocked for a day-case McDonald suture, bupivacaine 7.5-10 mg with fentanyl 10-15 micrograms for a Shirodkar or a longer case. Morphine is not added. Hypotension is less marked than at term but still deserves treatment, because uterine flow is pressure-dependent at any gestation.

### 3. Position and exposure
**Why:** Lithotomy with head-down tilt lets the uterus and membranes fall away from the cervix; retractors or a speculum expose it and the surgeon grasps the cervical lips with atraumatic forceps.
**Anaesthesia:** Position after the block has fixed, to avoid a high block from early head-down tilt with a hyperbaric solution. Lithotomy precautions apply. Traction on the cervix occasionally produces a vagal bradycardia; ask the surgeon to release and give glycopyrronium 200 micrograms IV if it persists.

### 4. Placing the suture
**Why:** The bites must be deep enough to hold in cervical stroma, clear of the bladder in front, the rectum behind and the uterine vessels at three and nine o'clock, and the knot is tied firmly enough to close the canal without strangling it.
**Anaesthesia:** A quiet period. If the woman feels sharp pain on traction with a block below T10, alfentanil 250-500 micrograms IV covers the few minutes involved. Bleeding from a cervical branch is usually controlled by tying the suture.

### 5. Rescue cerclage with bulging membranes
**Why:** The surgeon has to reduce the membranes back through the cervix (with head-down tilt, a filled bladder, a moist swab or a balloon) and close the cervix beneath them. Any rise in intrauterine pressure pushes the membranes back out or ruptures them.
**Anaesthesia:** Your task is a patient who does not cough, strain or retch at any point. Steeper head-down tilt, antiemetic prophylaxis, and no sudden movement. Surgeons vary on technique: some ask for general anaesthesia so that a volatile agent relaxes the uterus; others prefer a spinal because it avoids coughing at intubation and extubation. Comparative evidence is limited, as the survey authors note, and neither choice is wrong. The author's default is a spinal with a slightly larger dose (bupivacaine 10-12 mg) and a low threshold for glyceryl trinitrate 50-100 micrograms IV if the uterus is felt to be contracting. If general anaesthesia is chosen at 20 weeks or more, it is a tracheal tube with a smooth induction and a deep or opioid-covered extubation. Tocolysis with indomethacin or nifedipine and antibiotics are the obstetrician's decisions.

### 6. Transabdominal cerclage, recovery and removal
**Why:** The abdominal tape sits at the internal os and stays for life; a vaginal suture is removed at 36-37 weeks or when labour starts.
**Anaesthesia:** Laparoscopic transabdominal cerclage is a general anaesthetic with a tracheal tube and the laparoscopy precautions in step 11; when done before conception it is ordinary gynaecological day surgery. After a vaginal cerclage, check the fetal heart, give paracetamol for cramps, give anti-D if the woman is RhD-negative according to the unit's protocol, and discharge once the block has gone and she has passed urine. Removal of a McDonald suture needs no anaesthetic; a buried Shirodkar suture may need a short spinal. A woman who arrives in labour with a cerclage in place needs it cut promptly, because contractions against a closed suture tear the cervix.

### 7. Non-obstetric surgery: whether, when and where
**Why:** The surgeon needs to treat the disease; the obstetrician needs to know the gestation and the plan if the pregnancy is threatened.
**Anaesthesia:** Urgent surgery proceeds at any gestation. Semi-elective surgery is best placed in the second trimester, when organogenesis is complete, miscarriage risk has fallen and the uterus is not yet in the way; this is convention with observational support. Purely elective surgery waits until six weeks after delivery. From viability (around 23-24 weeks) the operation should happen where there are obstetric and neonatal services, and the conversation beforehand should settle what would be done for a fetal bradycardia: if nobody would perform a caesarean in the middle of the operation, continuous fetal monitoring only generates anxiety. Offer a pregnancy test to any woman of childbearing age whose history leaves it in doubt.

### 8. Preparation by gestation
**Why:** The pregnancy changes the mother's reserve more than it changes the operation.
**Anaesthesia:** Before about 16-18 weeks the anaesthetic is close to a non-pregnant one, apart from lower anaesthetic requirements and a degree of vasodilatation. From the mid second trimester add acid prophylaxis and plan a tracheal tube for general anaesthesia. From 20 weeks displace the uterus to the left in every position. At any gestation fit calf compressors and plan pharmacological thromboprophylaxis afterwards, because pregnancy and surgery multiply each other's risk. Antenatal corticosteroids for fetal lung maturity between about 24 and 34 weeks are an obstetric decision when preterm delivery is a real possibility. Document the fetal heart before induction.

### 9. Regional or general, and the induction
**Why:** The surgical approach decides whether a regional technique is possible.
**Anaesthesia:** A regional or neuraxial technique is preferred when the operation allows, because it avoids the airway and minimises fetal drug exposure; treat the hypotension it causes promptly. For general anaesthesia after 18-20 weeks: head-up pre-oxygenation to an end-tidal oxygen above 90%, rapid sequence induction, a tube half to one size smaller, a videolaryngoscope. Anaesthetic requirement is reduced by about a third; standard induction agents, opioids, volatile agents and neuromuscular blocking drugs have not been shown to be teratogenic in humans at clinical doses. Regulatory warnings about anaesthetic neurotoxicity to the developing brain rest on animal data and prolonged exposure, and are not a reason to delay necessary surgery. Avoiding nitrous oxide in the first trimester is tradition without human evidence of harm.

### 10. Maintenance: the mother's numbers are the fetal protection
**Why:** The surgeon wants the same conditions as in any patient.
**Anaesthesia:** The uteroplacental circulation is maximally dilated and passive. Keep maternal systolic pressure within 20% of baseline with phenylephrine or metaraminol and left displacement, not with fluid alone. Ventilate to the pregnant normal: an end-tidal carbon dioxide of 30-32 mmHg. Hyperventilation constricts uterine vessels and shifts the maternal oxygen dissociation curve to the left; hypercapnia crosses to the fetus as acidosis. Keep oxygen saturation high and haemoglobin adequate. Volatile agents relax the uterus, which is an advantage here, in contrast to their effect at caesarean. Loss of fetal heart rate variability under general anaesthesia is a drug effect and expected; a sustained bradycardia is a prompt to check pressure, oxygenation, position and surgical retraction on the uterus before anything else.

### 11. Laparoscopy in pregnancy
**Why:** The surgical guidelines support laparoscopy in any trimester for appendicitis, biliary disease and adnexal pathology, with open (Hasson) or optical entry adjusted for fundal height, insufflation pressure of 10-15 mmHg, and left tilt.
**Anaesthesia:** Pneumoperitoneum on top of a gravid uterus reduces venous return further and raises airway pressure against a reduced functional residual capacity; ask for the lowest pressure that gives a view and expect to increase minute ventilation to hold the carbon dioxide target. Capnography is an adequate guide in most cases; an arterial gas is useful in a long case or with lung disease. Head-up positions for upper abdominal surgery add to venous pooling. The details of gas insufflation are in [laparoscopy, robotics and the pneumoperitoneum](../a/a01-laparoscopy-pneumoperitoneum.html), and the operations themselves on the [appendicectomy](laparoscopic-and-open-appendicectomy.html) and [cholecystectomy](laparoscopic-cholecystectomy-and-bile-duct-exploration.html) cards.

### 12. Emergence, the first night and preterm labour
**Why:** The surgical risk to the pregnancy is greatest with peritonitis and pelvic manipulation, and it shows as contractions in the hours and days after the operation.
**Anaesthesia:** Reverse neuromuscular block fully. Sugammadex binds progestogens in vitro, and there is a theoretical concern in early pregnancy when progesterone maintains the gestation; human data showing harm are lacking. The author's practice is neostigmine with glycopyrronium when the block is shallow enough and sugammadex without hesitation when it is not, because residual paralysis in a pregnant woman with a full stomach is the certain harm. Extubate awake. Afterwards: fetal heart documented in recovery, uterine activity monitored for some hours after abdominal surgery from the mid second trimester, and a clear instruction to the ward that new rhythmic abdominal pain, bleeding or fluid loss is reported to the obstetric team. Prophylactic tocolytics are not given; contractions are treated if they occur.

## Pain plan

For cerclage the strategy is a spinal that is no longer than the procedure, then paracetamol. The mechanism (a suture in the cervix, no wound) produces mild cramping that settles within a day, so there is nothing for a long-acting block or neuraxial opioid to do, and both would delay a day-case discharge. Pain that increases after discharge, or becomes rhythmic, is not postoperative pain: it is contractions, infection or membrane rupture, and the woman is told to return.

For non-obstetric surgery, start from the pain plan on the operation's own card and make three adjustments. First, NSAIDs: they are avoided after 30 weeks because they constrict the fetal ductus arteriosus and reduce fetal urine output, and many obstetricians prefer to avoid them in the first trimester as well; in the second trimester a short course is generally accepted. Without an NSAID the pain score of an abdominal operation rises by about a point. Second, lean toward regional techniques to make up the difference: TAP or rectus sheath blocks for laparoscopy and appendicectomy, peripheral nerve blocks for limb surgery, a neuraxial technique for lower abdominal and perineal surgery, and a catheter where the parent card would only consider one. Local anaesthetic doses stay within the usual weight-based limits, calculated on booking weight. Third, opioids are used as needed and without undue restraint: paracetamol and short-acting opioids are compatible with pregnancy, untreated pain raises maternal catecholamines, and the fetal effects of a few days of maternal opioid are negligible. Gabapentinoids and tramadol have less reassuring data and are avoided as a matter of the author's judgement.

Rebound is low for cerclage and follows the parent operation otherwise. The escalation trigger particular to this card is diagnostic: before treating increasing abdominal pain after surgery in pregnancy with more analgesia, put a hand on the uterus.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The emergency variant. Trauma, a perforated appendix or a ruptured ectopic in a pregnant woman is resuscitated as a pregnant woman: left tilt from 20 weeks, blood early, and a low threshold for an arterial line because compensation hides the loss. In maternal cardiac arrest at 20 weeks or more, manual uterine displacement during compressions and resuscitative hysterotomy within about five minutes are for the mother's benefit.
- Critical care and invasive monitoring. Needed for the operation and the illness (sepsis from appendicitis, major trauma), not for the pregnancy; when a pregnant woman goes to intensive care, daily obstetric review and a delivery plan go with her.
- Appendicitis is the commonest general surgical emergency in pregnancy and the appendix moves upward with the uterus; delay causes perforation, and perforation causes fetal loss. The anaesthetist's contribution is not to be the reason for delay.
- Failed rescue cerclage. Membranes rupture during the procedure in a proportion of cases; the woman has been warned, and the anaesthetic did not cause it unless she coughed.
- Short-acting spinal agents. Hyperbaric prilocaine suits a ten-minute suture; it will not cover a difficult Shirodkar that runs to 45 minutes.
- High block from early head-down tilt. Let a hyperbaric spinal fix for a few minutes before tilting.
- Radiology. Necessary imaging is not withheld; shield where it does not interfere and keep fluoroscopy time short.
- Postoperative vomiting. More than discomfort after rescue cerclage or abdominal surgery in pregnancy; give two prophylactic antiemetics.
- The unrecognised pregnancy. Found on a pre-operative test before elective surgery: stop, tell her privately, and reschedule unless the surgery is necessary.

## Key sources

Anaesthesia for cerclage has almost no comparative evidence; the best description of practice is a survey with a 19% response rate, and the handling of rescue cerclage is the author's judgement. For non-obstetric surgery the outcome data are from administrative datasets that cannot separate disease from treatment, the college statement is explicit that it is opinion, and the physiological targets are standard teaching. The surgical guideline is cited for the acceptability and technique of laparoscopy. The positions taken here on sugammadex, nitrous oxide, gabapentinoids and the timing of aspiration precautions are judgement, not evidence.
