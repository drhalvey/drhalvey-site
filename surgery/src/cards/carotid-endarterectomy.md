## What the surgeon is doing and why

Atheroma at the carotid bifurcation causes stroke mainly by embolism: a plaque in the bulb ulcerates and sheds platelet aggregates and debris into the middle cerebral territory. Flow limitation is the minor mechanism. The operation therefore does not bypass a narrowing, it removes the embolic source. The surgeon opens the artery, peels the plaque out in the plane between the diseased intima and the outer media, leaves a smooth surface with a tapered or tacked distal end point, and closes the artery without narrowing it.

The benefit is front-loaded and so is the urgency. Stroke risk after a transient ischaemic attack or minor stroke is highest in the first days, so symptomatic patients are operated on within about two weeks of the index event, often during the same admission. That means you meet them recently infarcted, on dual antiplatelets and a new statin, with blood pressure deliberately left a little high, and with little time for optimisation. Asymptomatic patients are elective, and for them the operation's value depends entirely on a low perioperative stroke rate, to which the anaesthetic contributes.

To do the endarterectomy the surgeon has to stop flow through the internal carotid. For 20-45 minutes the ipsilateral hemisphere lives on the circle of Willis: the contralateral carotid and the vertebrals, driven by systemic pressure. Three things follow. First, your blood pressure during the clamp is the hemisphere's perfusion pressure. Second, somebody has to know whether collateral flow is enough, and if it is not, the surgeon places a shunt (a plastic tube from common to internal carotid through the arteriotomy), which protects flow at the price of possible embolism, dissection and a more awkward endarterectomy. Third, the method for detecting inadequate flow decides the anaesthetic: an awake patient who can talk and squeeze a toy with the contralateral hand is the most direct monitor there is, and every asleep method (stump pressure, EEG, near-infrared spectroscopy, transcranial Doppler) is a surrogate. Some surgeons avoid the question by shunting everyone.

The bifurcation is also a sensor. The carotid sinus baroreceptors sit in the wall being dissected, and their afferent, the sinus nerve, runs in the fork. Handling the bulb produces bradycardia and hypotension. Stripping the plaque denervates or resets the baroreceptor, and exposing a stiff wall to pulse pressure it has not felt for years changes its firing, so the first 24 hours are marked by labile pressure in both directions. The brain distal to a tight stenosis has been maximally vasodilated for a long time and cannot constrict promptly when normal pressure is restored, which is the mechanism of the hyperperfusion syndrome.

What is left alone: no muscle is divided (sternocleidomastoid is retracted, the facial vein is ligated), and no bone is involved. The pain logic is therefore simple. This is skin and soft tissue in the territory of C2-C4, plus a retractor against the angle of the mandible. It is a mild operation for pain, and that is useful: a patient who needs strong opioid after a carotid endarterectomy, or who has a severe ipsilateral headache, has a complication until proven otherwise.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative decisions: awake or asleep, and the baseline
**Why:** The surgeon needs a still neck, a hemisphere that survives the clamp, and a way of knowing that it is surviving.
**Anaesthesia:** The technique question is settled by evidence only to the extent that neither wins: in GALA, 3,526 patients randomised across 95 centres, stroke, myocardial infarction or death at 30 days occurred in 4.8% under general and 4.5% under local anaesthesia, with no difference in length of stay or quality of life. The choice is therefore the team's and the patient's. Awake surgery suits a cooperative patient who can lie flat and still for two hours, a surgeon who is gentle and quick, and a team that does it often. General anaesthesia suits the claustrophobic, the orthopnoeic, the patient with a high bifurcation or short neck, the patient with a language barrier or post-stroke dysphasia, and the team that does few. Record blood pressure in both arms and take the higher as the reference; record a ward baseline, not the anxious anaesthetic room figure. Document the existing neurological deficit yourself so that you can recognise a new one.

### 2. Arterial line and cervical plexus block
**Why:** Beat-to-beat pressure is the one monitor this operation cannot be done without, and the block covers the C2-C4 dermatomes of the incision.
**Anaesthesia:** Arterial line awake, in the arm that is accessible once both are tucked. A superficial or intermediate cervical plexus block (10-15 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% or levobupivacaine 0.375% along the posterior border of sternocleidomastoid) is the block of choice whether the patient is to be awake or asleep. In a systematic review of 69 papers, deep or combined blocks carried about twice the odds of serious needle-related complications (odds ratio 2.13) and about five times the odds of conversion to general anaesthesia (odds ratio 5.15). The block does not cover the carotid sheath (vagal and glossopharyngeal afferents) or the submandibular retractor (trigeminal territory), so warn an awake patient to expect jaw and ear discomfort and the surgeon to expect to infiltrate the sheath. Sedation for awake surgery is minimal by design: remifentanil 0.025-0.05 micrograms/kg/min or small doses of midazolam at most, because a sedated patient is a broken monitor.

### 3. Induction, airway and position (general anaesthesia variant)
**Why:** The head is turned away and extended to open the angle between mandible and clavicle, and the surgeon stands where you would normally stand to manage an airway.
**Anaesthesia:** The induction that matters is the one that does not drop pressure in a patient with a tight stenosis, a possible contralateral occlusion and treated or untreated hypertension. Titrate, and have metaraminol running or in hand from the start; the laryngoscopy response is blunted with remifentanil or a short-acting opioid, not with depth. A reinforced tube taped to the contralateral side is the secure choice. Volatile or propofol maintenance with remifentanil both work; what counts is that the technique allows a waking, examinable patient within minutes of the last stitch. Check the neck is not over-rotated, since in an arteriopath the contralateral carotid and the vertebrals are the collateral supply you are about to depend on. Place cerebral monitoring now if the unit uses it and record a pre-clamp baseline.

### 4. Incision and dissection to the carotid sheath
**Why:** The incision follows the anterior border of sternocleidomastoid. The surgeon divides platysma, retracts the muscle, ligates the common facial vein, opens the sheath and slings the common, external and internal carotid arteries, touching the bulb as little as possible to avoid dislodging plaque.
**Anaesthesia:** Bleeding is trivial. The nerves at risk are the surgeon's concern but present to you later: hypoglossal (crossing the internal carotid at the top of the wound), vagus and its recurrent laryngeal branch (in the sheath), marginal mandibular (retractor) and, with high dissections, glossopharyngeal. Handling the bulb produces abrupt bradycardia and hypotension; ask the surgeon to stop, which is usually sufficient, and give atropine 300-600 micrograms IV or glycopyrronium 200 micrograms IV if it persists. Infiltrating the sinus nerve with lidocaine 1% 1-2 mL stops the reflex and is done by some surgeons routinely; it may worsen post-operative hypertension, and practice varies.

### 5. Heparin and the test clamp
**Why:** Heparin prevents thrombus forming in the static column above the clamp. The clamps go on the internal carotid first, to trap any debris, then the common and external.
**Anaesthesia:** Give unfractionated heparin 70-100 units/kg IV (many surgeons ask for a fixed 5,000 units) and say the time aloud; the clamp follows about 3 min later. Before the clamp, raise the pressure to baseline or up to 20% above it with metaraminol or phenylephrine and hold it there for the whole clamp period, because flow through the collateral circle is pressure-passive. Maintain normocapnia: hypocapnia constricts the collaterals and hypercapnia steals from the ischaemic territory. In the awake patient the test is direct: talk, ask for a countdown, and have the squeaky toy in the contralateral hand for at least 2-3 min. Loss of speech, grip or consciousness is a failed test. The first response is more pressure and 100% oxygen while the surgeon prepares the shunt. In the asleep patient the unit's surrogate is read at the same moment: a stump pressure below about 40-50 mmHg, a fall in regional oxygen saturation of more than 15-20% from baseline, or ipsilateral EEG slowing are the usual triggers, all with meaningful false positive and false negative rates.

### 6. Arteriotomy, shunt if needed, and endarterectomy
**Why:** A longitudinal arteriotomy from common into internal carotid exposes the plaque. A shunt, if used, is placed now. The plaque is dissected out and the distal intima is feathered or tacked so that no flap lifts when flow returns. In the eversion variant the internal carotid is divided obliquely at its origin, turned inside out over the plaque and re-implanted, with no patch and usually no shunt.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the quiet, dangerous middle. Hold the pressure and do nothing clever. Shunt insertion and removal are each a short period of no flow and a chance of air or debris embolism. Late neurological change in an awake patient after an initially good test clamp is usually a pressure drift: restore it before anything else. Ask about clamp time at 20 min; a surgeon who knows the anaesthetist is counting tends to keep moving.

### 7. Patch closure and clamp release
**Why:** Primary closure narrows the artery, so most surgeons close with a patch of bovine pericardium, polyester or vein. The clamps come off in an order that sends the first flush up the external carotid: external and common first, internal last.
**Anaesthesia:** At release the target changes direction within a minute. The hemisphere now sees full systemic pressure through a wide artery with impaired autoregulation, and the suture line sees it too. Let the pressure come down to baseline and keep systolic under 150-160 mmHg from here on. Reflex vasodilatation and bradycardia are common for a few minutes as the baroreceptor is re-exposed. Protamine is the surgeon's call. In a registry of 72,787 elective endarterectomies, reoperation for bleeding was 0.7% with protamine and 1.4% without, with no difference in stroke; that is observational evidence and practice still varies, so have 25-50 mg ready and give it slowly if requested.

### 8. Haemostasis, closure and emergence
**Why:** A patch suture line under arterial pressure in a patient on aspirin, often clopidogrel, and heparin oozes. The surgeon waits, packs, and usually leaves a small suction drain.
**Anaesthesia:** The emergence is as much part of the operation as the clamp. Coughing and straining on the tube produce venous and arterial pressure surges into a fresh arteriotomy, so plan for it: remifentanil continued to extubation, lidocaine 1 mg/kg IV, or deep extubation onto a supraglottic airway in suitable patients. Treat pressure during emergence actively: labetalol 5-10 mg IV increments, hydralazine 5 mg IV, or a glyceryl trinitrate infusion are the usual agents; have one drawn up before the drapes come down. Examine the patient before leaving theatre: both hands, both legs, speech, face, tongue protrusion and voice. A new deficit found on the table goes straight back to the surgeon for duplex or re-exploration, because an occluded endarterectomy is reversible for a short time only.

### 9. The first six hours
**Why:** The surgeon's result is decided here: thrombosis at the endarterectomy site, embolism from it, bleeding around it, or hyperperfusion beyond it.
**Anaesthesia:** Keep the arterial line and written pressure limits set from the patient's own baseline, with named drugs for each direction: a floor no more than 20% below the awake baseline systolic (for a baseline of 150-160 mmHg that is 120-130 mmHg, not 100) and a ceiling of 150-160 mmHg. Hypotension with bradycardia is baroreceptor overactivity and responds to fluid, atropine and a short vasopressor infusion; exclude myocardial ischaemia, which is the leading non-neurological cause of death in this population. Hypertension is treated promptly, because in the pooled literature severe hypertension followed 19% of endarterectomies and the cases of hyperperfusion rose once post-operative systolic pressure exceeded 150 mmHg. The neck haematoma is the airway emergency of this operation: swelling, a change in voice or difficulty swallowing means opening the wound at the bedside, since the obstruction is mucosal and laryngeal oedema from venous and lymphatic congestion as much as external compression, and it does not resolve the moment the clot is evacuated. Reintubation in that setting is a difficult airway in an anticoagulated neck: call for help early, inhalational or awake technique, small tube.

### 10. Day 1 to day 7
**Why:** The endarterectomised hemisphere regains autoregulation over days to weeks.
**Anaesthesia:** Hyperperfusion syndrome is uncommon (about 1%, with intracerebral haemorrhage in 0.5%) but presents after discharge: median day 5, more than 90% within the first week, with ipsilateral headache, seizures or hemiparesis and a systolic pressure typically near 190 mmHg. The discharge plan therefore includes blood pressure control and a clear instruction about headache. Analgesia by this point is paracetamol alone.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a clean soft-tissue incision in a dermatome that a superficial cervical plexus block covers completely, so the analgesic strategy is the block plus simple oral analgesia, and that is the whole plan. Place the superficial or intermediate block in every patient, awake or asleep: under general anaesthesia it removes the need for long-acting opioid, which matters because the priority at the end of the case is an alert, examinable patient with an unobtunded airway and no retching. Paracetamol 1 g IV or oral before the end, then regular. Surgeon infiltration of the wound is a reasonable substitute where a block was not done.

NSAIDs are usually omitted, as an author's judgement and not a trial result: the patient is on one or two antiplatelets and has had heparin, a neck haematoma is an airway emergency, and renal disease is common in arteriopaths. The pain is mild enough that the trade is not worth making. Opioid requirement is small: a few doses of oral oxycodone 5 mg or equivalent on the first night for the minority who need it. Sore throat, jaw ache from the retractor and a numb ear lobe and jaw line (great auricular and transverse cervical nerves) are expected and settle.

The escalation rule is specific to this operation. Wound pain that is rising with swelling is a haematoma and goes to the surgeon. Headache is not treated as a pain problem: a severe ipsilateral frontotemporal or periorbital headache in the first week, especially with a high pressure, is hyperperfusion until imaging says otherwise, and the treatment is blood pressure control, not analgesia. Rebound after the block is not a clinical problem here because the block outlasts the worst of the wound pain.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Awake conversion. A patient who becomes agitated, obstructed or unable to lie still with the artery open has to be converted with the head turned away and draped. Decide beforehand how: a supraglottic airway placed from the side is the usual first move, with the surgeon protecting the field. Agitation during the clamp is cerebral ischaemia until the pressure and the shunt have been dealt with.
- Contralateral carotid occlusion. Collateral reserve depends on the vertebrals and the circle of Willis alone; shunt rates are higher and tolerance of hypotension lower. GALA showed no significant difference in this subgroup, so the technique remains a team choice.
- Recent stroke. Operated on within two weeks, these patients have an area of infarcted brain that is pressure-passive. Hyperperfusion and haemorrhagic transformation are more likely, and upper pressure limits are stricter.
- Dual antiplatelet therapy. Increasingly standard for symptomatic patients and continued through surgery in many units. Expect more ooze, a longer closure and a lower threshold for protamine and a drain. It is a relative argument against deep cervical blocks.
- Phrenic and recurrent laryngeal effects. A cervical plexus block can produce hemidiaphragmatic paresis, hoarseness and Horner's syndrome. A patient with a previous contralateral endarterectomy or neck dissection may already have a contralateral cord or phrenic palsy; ask about voice, and consider nasendoscopy before a second side.
- Bilateral disease and the second side. After bilateral endarterectomy both baroreceptors and both carotid bodies may be denervated: labile hypertension and loss of the hypoxic ventilatory response. Be cautious with opioids and with oxygen weaning.
- Carotid artery stenting and transcarotid revascularisation. Done under local anaesthesia and light sedation in the angiography suite. The stimulus is balloon inflation in the bulb, which produces profound bradycardia or asystole: glycopyrronium or atropine beforehand, arterial line, and the same post-procedure pressure rules. Hyperperfusion occurs after stenting as it does after surgery.
- Combined or staged carotid and cardiac surgery. The coronary disease that accompanies carotid disease is the main cause of perioperative death. Myocardial injury after endarterectomy is often silent, so a new ECG change or a troponin rise in recovery is taken seriously.
- The emergency variant. Crescendo transient ischaemic attacks, stroke in evolution, or re-exploration for acute occlusion or haematoma. All arrive with full anticoagulation or antiplatelet loading on board. Re-exploration for haematoma always needs a secured airway and is a difficult airway first and an operation second. Crescendo attacks and stroke in evolution are done under general anaesthesia in most units, and awake in units practised at awake surgery, where the patient remains the monitor; that choice is judgement, with no trial behind it.

## Key sources

The anaesthetic technique question has one large trial, GALA, which found no difference and is unlikely to be repeated. The block question rests on a systematic review of case series, which is weak in design but large and consistent: superficial is safer than deep. Blood pressure management after surgery rests on level-3 evidence from pooled observational studies; the thresholds are consensus drawn from where the hyperperfusion cases cluster. There is no PROSPECT guideline and no procedure-specific pain trial of note, because pain is not the problem this operation poses. The NSAID and opioid-sparing advice is the author's judgement.
