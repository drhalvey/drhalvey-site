## What the surgeon is doing and why

Six muscles move each eye. When their pull is unbalanced the eyes point in different directions: inwards (esotropia), outwards (exotropia), or vertically. In a child the brain suppresses the image from the deviating eye, which then fails to develop vision, so the operation is done early, commonly between one and eight years of age, for sight as well as appearance. In adults it is done for double vision after nerve palsy, thyroid eye disease or trauma, and for long-standing deviation.

The surgeon cannot change the nerve supply, so the correction is mechanical: change where a muscle attaches to the globe and its pull changes. A recession weakens a muscle by detaching it from the sclera and suturing it further back. A resection strengthens one by cutting out a length and suturing the shortened muscle to the original insertion. The inferior oblique is weakened by cutting or moving it. The amount is planned in millimetres from clinic measurements, usually two muscles on one eye or the medial or lateral rectus on both.

To reach a muscle the surgeon opens the conjunctiva and Tenon's capsule at the limbus or in the fornix, passes a squint hook under the tendon and lifts the muscle forward under tension, places sutures through it, cuts it from the sclera, and stitches it to the sclera at the measured position with a spatulated needle passed within the thickness of the wall of the eye, which is about a millimetre at that point. In cooperative adults one muscle may be left on an adjustable suture with a slip knot, to be tightened or loosened hours later with the patient awake.

Three things follow for the anaesthetist, each tied to a step. Tension on a muscle fires the oculocardiac reflex. Manipulating the eye muscles disturbs the link between vision and balance and, by a route not fully understood, makes this one of the most emetogenic operations in childhood. And the needle pass through sclera requires an eye that does not move.

The pain logic is muscle and conjunctiva: a detached and re-sutured muscle that hurts when the eye moves, and a raw conjunctival surface that feels like grit. It is mild to moderate, brief, and covered by a surgeon-placed sub-Tenon injection and simple analgesics. Opioids are worth avoiding, because here they cost more in vomiting than they buy in comfort.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment
**Why:** The surgeon has measured the deviation and fixed the plan; it is elective day surgery on a mostly healthy child.
**Anaesthesia:** Most children with a squint are otherwise well. A minority have the squint as part of something else: prematurity, cerebral palsy, hydrocephalus with a shunt, a craniofacial syndrome, or a myopathy. The historical teaching linking strabismus to malignant hyperthermia and masseter spasm is weak, and a family history is what matters. Score the vomiting risk and treat everyone as high risk regardless: age over three, surgery over 30 minutes, previous vomiting or motion sickness in the child or family, and the operation itself all count. Adults are older, may have thyroid eye disease or previous orbital surgery, and may be having an adjustable suture, which shapes the anaesthetic (step 9). General points for children are in [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).

### 2. Induction and airway
**Why:** The surgeon works at the head on one or both eyes and wants the airway device out of the way and the head still.
**Anaesthesia:** Inhalational or intravenous induction by the child's preference. A reinforced supraglottic airway taped to the chin suits most cases; a tracheal tube for infants, reflux and the long reoperation. Avoid suxamethonium (step 4). If a tube is wanted, intubate with propofol and a short-acting opioid or a non-depolarising relaxant. Intravenous access is needed before the first muscle is touched, however short the case, because the treatment of an asystolic pause is intravenous.

### 3. The oculocardiac reflex: prevent or treat
**Why:** The surgeon is about to pull on a rectus muscle, and the afferent arc of the reflex runs from stretch receptors in the muscle through the ciliary nerves and the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve to the brainstem, with the vagus as the efferent.
**Anaesthesia:** A review covering more than 15,000 patients found profound bradycardia in almost 10% of strabismus operations when no anticholinergic was given, and found that the reflex cannot be predicted for the individual. It is increased by rapid-acting opioids (remifentanil in particular) and decreased by anticholinergics, deeper inhaled anaesthesia, hypocarbia and an orbital block. There are two defensible policies. Prophylaxis: glycopyrronium 5-10 micrograms/kg IV or atropine 10-20 micrograms/kg IV at induction, which prevents most episodes at the cost of a tachycardia. Or watchful treatment: the drug drawn up, the pulse tone audible, and the surgeon told to expect a request to let go. The author's practice is prophylaxis in small children, in anyone having propofol and remifentanil, and in reoperations; and treatment on demand in adults under volatile anaesthesia.

### 4. Forced duction test
**Why:** Before incision the surgeon grasps the eye with forceps and rotates it in each direction to feel whether movement is limited by a tight or tethered muscle, which changes the operation.
**Anaesthesia:** The test needs muscles with no active tone. Suxamethonium causes a sustained contracture of the extraocular muscles lasting 15 to 20 minutes, which makes every muscle feel tight, so it is not used. A non-depolarising relaxant does not interfere. The test itself can trigger the reflex.

### 5. Conjunctival incision and hooking the muscle
**Why:** The conjunctiva and Tenon's capsule are opened, and a hook is swept under the tendon to isolate the whole muscle and pull it forward into view.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the moment. Bradycardia arrives within seconds of the pull: sinus slowing, a junctional rhythm, ventricular ectopics or a pause. The medial rectus is the usual culprit, though any muscle can do it. The sequence is: tell the surgeon, who releases the hook; the rate recovers in seconds; check that the child is deep enough and the carbon dioxide is not high; give the anticholinergic if none has been given; then the surgeon tries again, gently. The reflex fatigues with repeated traction. If asystole persists after release, it is treated as any arrest, with atropine 20 micrograms/kg IV and compressions; that is rare. Each new muscle starts the process again.

### 6. Sutures, disinsertion and reattachment
**Why:** Sutures are locked into the tendon, the muscle is cut from the sclera, and the needle is passed through partial-thickness sclera at the new position.
**Anaesthesia:** The scleral pass is the step where movement harms: a needle through the full thickness perforates the globe and can detach the retina. A supraglottic airway with spontaneous ventilation is adequate provided depth is; a cough or a breath-hold at a light plane is the usual cause of movement. Do not let the volatile or the infusion drift down because the case is nearly over; a two-muscle operation has two of these passes on each muscle.

### 7. Sub-Tenon local anaesthetic
**Why:** The space is already open, and the surgeon can instil local anaesthetic into it under direct vision before or after the muscle work.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask for it. A meta-analysis of randomised trials in children found that a sub-Tenon block reduced early postoperative pain, the number of children needing opioid (relative risk 0.59), postoperative vomiting (relative risk 0.31) and intraoperative oculocardiac events (relative risk 0.40), with no block-related complications reported. Given at the start it covers the reflex as well as the pain; given at the end it covers only the pain. Know the dose: 1-2 mL per eye of levobupivacaine 0.25-0.5% or lidocaine 2% stays far below the maximum even in a toddler having both eyes done. Pain scores at six hours were no different, so the oral regimen still matters.

### 8. The antiemetic plan
**Why:** Nothing the surgeon does can be changed to reduce vomiting; the whole burden falls on the anaesthetic.
**Anaesthesia:** In the pooled placebo arms of the strabismus trials, about two-thirds of children vomited. Dexamethasone halved that (relative risk 0.50), ondansetron did likewise (relative risk 0.58), and the combination was better than either alone, so every child gets both. On top of that: no long-acting opioid unless needed, the sub-Tenon block, paracetamol and an NSAID, and generous intravenous crystalloid. On maintenance the evidence leaves a choice. Propofol TIVA and volatile anaesthesia with one prophylactic antiemetic were equally effective against vomiting in a paediatric meta-analysis, and TIVA increased oculocardiac bradycardia in strabismus surgery. So either run propofol with an anticholinergic on board, or sevoflurane with two antiemetics. Do not force early drinking in recovery. See [nausea, vomiting and day-case anaesthesia](../a/a11-ponv-day-case.html).

### 9. Adjustable sutures in adults
**Why:** The muscle is tied with a slip knot so that, once the patient is awake and can look at a target, the surgeon can measure the alignment and tighten or loosen the suture at the bedside under topical anaesthesia.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient must be alert, cooperative and able to fix with both eyes within a few hours. That means short-acting drugs, no benzodiazepine premedication, no long-acting orbital block that paralyses the muscles being measured, and antiemetics that do not sedate. Adjustment itself pulls on the muscle: the oculocardiac reflex and vasovagal fainting both occur in the awake patient, so it is done lying down with monitoring and an anticholinergic to hand. Some adults have the whole operation under topical or sub-Tenon anaesthesia with the same precautions.

### 10. Emergence and recovery
**Why:** The eye is closed with ointment or a pad; the surgeon wants no rubbing and no vomiting.
**Anaesthesia:** Remove the airway deep and let the child wake undisturbed in the lateral position, or fully awake; not in between. Emergence agitation is frequent in preschool children after sevoflurane and is worse with blurred vision from ointment and a sore eye. A meta-analysis of dexmedetomidine in paediatric strabismus surgery found less agitation (relative risk 0.27), less vomiting and less analgesic use without a longer recovery stay. On the oculocardiac reflex the literature conflicts: that analysis found fewer events with dexmedetomidine, while the reflex review lists it among the drugs that augment the reflex. The author's practice is dexmedetomidine 0.3-0.5 micrograms/kg IV after the last muscle has been reattached, when the reflex no longer matters. Late vomiting happens in the car and at home: send rescue advice with the family.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is muscle and conjunctiva, modest and short, in an operation where opioid-induced vomiting is the larger threat to a good day. The plan is therefore built to avoid opioids: a sub-Tenon injection of long-acting local anaesthetic by the surgeon, paracetamol 15 mg/kg and an NSAID (ibuprofen 10 mg/kg orally beforehand, or an intravenous equivalent during the case), and dexamethasone, which serves both nausea and inflammation. Fentanyl 0.5-1 microgram/kg IV is held in reserve for a child in pain in recovery. The block evidence is from a meta-analysis of randomised trials; the remainder is consensus. There is no PROSPECT guideline.

Expect a worst pain of 2-3 out of 10 on the evening of surgery and the next day, as soreness, grittiness and pain on looking to the side, settling by day 3. A reoperation through scarred tissue and a resection hurt more than a first recession. Rebound when the block recedes is mild. Regular paracetamol and ibuprofen for two to three days is the discharge regimen. A cold compress helps, and so does a distracted child.

Escalation triggers. Severe pain with a swollen lid and a fall in vision in the first days is orbital cellulitis or, rarely, endophthalmitis after an unrecognised scleral perforation. Persistent vomiting beyond the first evening needs fluids, not more analgesia. A deviation that looks worse on day 1 is expected and is the surgeon's matter.

## Pitfalls and variants

- No intravenous access. A gas induction and a quick squint without a cannula removes the only treatment for a pause. Cannulate before the eye is touched.
- Remifentanil. It augments the reflex and adds nothing an eye this small needs. If it is used, give an anticholinergic first.
- Light anaesthesia at the scleral pass. Movement here perforates the globe.
- Suxamethonium before the forced duction test. Invalidates the test for about 20 minutes.
- Hypercarbia. A spontaneously breathing child under drapes drifts up, and a high carbon dioxide makes the reflex more likely. Assist ventilation if it climbs.
- Thyroid eye disease. Tight fibrotic muscles need more traction, so more reflex activity; check thyroid status and protect an eye that does not close.
- Botulinum toxin injection. A two-minute procedure under electromyographic guidance in adults awake, or a brief anaesthetic in children, without relaxant so that the muscle signal can be heard.
- Malignant hyperthermia and myopathy. Not predicted by the squint. A known myopathy changes the anaesthetic on its own account.
- Post-discharge vomiting. Common enough to plan for: a second dose of ondansetron as a wafer for the journey is reasonable.
- Emergency and high-risk variant. Strabismus surgery is elective; the urgent form of the same physiology is the entrapped muscle of a trapdoor orbital fracture, where the reflex is active before surgery (see [facial fracture fixation](facial-fracture-fixation.html)). Invasive monitoring, blood and critical care are not required.

## Key sources

The oculocardiac reflex data come from a large narrative review with pooled observational and trial data; its conclusion that the reflex cannot be predicted for the individual is the practical one. The sub-Tenon block, antiemetic and dexmedetomidine evidence are meta-analyses of small paediatric trials. The equivalence of TIVA and volatile-plus-antiemetic, with more bradycardia under TIVA, is from a paediatric meta-analysis. The anticholinergic doses, the duration of suxamethonium contracture, the adjustable suture advice and the timing of dexmedetomidine are consensus and author's judgement.
