## What the surgeon is doing and why

A saccular aneurysm is a thin-walled blister at a branch point of the circle of Willis. When it ruptures, arterial blood enters the subarachnoid space until intracranial pressure rises to meet arterial pressure and a clot seals the hole. The patient who survives that moment has three problems on a timetable. Rebleeding, most likely in the first hours and days and often fatal, is why the aneurysm is secured early, usually within 24 hours. Hydrocephalus, from blood obstructing cerebrospinal fluid pathways, is why many arrive with an [external ventricular drain](ventriculoperitoneal-shunt-and-external-ventricular-drain.html). Delayed cerebral ischaemia from vasospasm, peaking between days 4 and 14, is why they stay for weeks and why you may meet them a second time for angioplasty. Around those sit the systemic effects of the catecholamine surge at ictus: ECG changes, troponin release, stunned myocardium, neurogenic pulmonary oedema, and later hyponatraemia.

There are two ways to exclude the sac from the circulation. Coiling fills it from within: a microcatheter is navigated from the femoral or radial artery into the aneurysm and platinum coils are packed until it thromboses. Wide-necked aneurysms need a balloon or a stent to hold the coils in, or a flow-diverting stent across the neck, and anything left in the parent artery needs antiplatelet drugs. Clipping closes it from outside: through a pterional craniotomy the surgeon opens the Sylvian fissure, follows the parent artery to the aneurysm, dissects the neck free of perforating arteries, and applies a spring clip across it. For ruptured aneurysms suitable for either, ISAT found dependency or death at one year in 23.7% after coiling and 30.6% after clipping, so most are now coiled. Clipping remains for middle cerebral bifurcation aneurysms, wide necks, those with a haematoma to evacuate, and failed coiling.

One equation governs the anaesthetic before the aneurysm is secured. The stress on the wall is the transmural pressure: mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure. Raising arterial pressure increases it. So does lowering intracranial pressure abruptly, by hyperventilating hard, giving mannitol with the dura closed in a ruptured case, or opening a ventricular drain too far. The same patient needs enough perfusion pressure to supply a swollen, sometimes vasospastic brain. The target is therefore a narrow band near the patient's normal, held without excursions.

Clipping adds the surgical needs of any [craniotomy](craniotomy-for-supratentorial-tumour.html), with more emphasis: the brain must be very slack, because the aneurysm sits at the base and retraction on a swollen frontal lobe tears vessels. The surgeon also has a tool that changes your targets for a few minutes: a temporary clip on the parent artery, which softens the sac for dissection and makes the territory beyond it ischaemic.

The pain logic separates the procedure from the disease. Clipping is a craniotomy through temporalis. Coiling is a groin puncture. The subarachnoid blood produces a severe meningitic headache with neck stiffness that outlasts both, and it has to be treated without sedating a patient whose hourly neurological examination is the vasospasm monitor.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment of a ruptured aneurysm
**Why:** The clinical grade (World Federation of Neurological Surgeons I to V, built on the Glasgow Coma Score and motor deficit) and the amount of blood on the scan predict vasospasm, outcome and how tight the brain will be.
**Anaesthesia:** Read the ECG, the troponin and any echocardiogram: T-wave inversion and a long QT are common and usually benign, but regional wall motion abnormality with a low ejection fraction changes induction and vasopressor choice. Check sodium, the ventricular drain height and output, and whether nimodipine has started (oral 60 mg 4-hourly for 21 days, the regimen that reduced infarction from 33% to 22% in the British trial). Nimodipine lowers pressure; do not omit it, and expect to need more vasopressor. Good-grade patients are awake, in pain and frightened, and a calm explanation is worth more than a sedative.

### 2. Induction without a pressure spike
**Why:** The aneurysm has a fresh clot over a hole in its dome.
**Anaesthesia:** Arterial line first, under local anaesthetic. Induce with propofol titrated, an opioid dose that genuinely covers laryngoscopy (remifentanil 1 microgram/kg or fentanyl 3-5 micrograms/kg), and a full dose of relaxant confirmed by nerve stimulator. Have both a vasopressor and a short-acting agent to lower pressure (esmolol, or a propofol bolus) drawn up, and treat in either direction within seconds. Do not hyperventilate before the dura is open. If a ventricular drain is present, clamp it for transfer and positioning, then transduce it and reopen at the prescribed height; do not let it drain freely.

### 3. Coiling: set-up in the angiography suite
**Why:** The interventionalist needs a motionless head for roadmap imaging, which overlays live fluoroscopy on a stored angiogram; any movement invalidates the map while a microcatheter is inside the sac.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with a tracheal tube and neuromuscular block maintained and monitored. This is [remote site anaesthesia](../a/a12-remote-site-sedation.html): establish where help, blood, the difficult airway trolley and the defibrillator are before starting, run long extensions on every line, and place yourself where you can see the patient, the monitor and the angiography screens. The C-arms swing through the space above the head, so circuit and lines are routed and taped with that in mind. Heparin is given after arterial access to the interventionalist's target, typically an activated clotting time two to three times baseline; say the dose and time aloud and know where the protamine is. Contrast load and a groin sheath are the other constants.

### 4. Coiling: catheterising and packing the sac
**Why:** The microcatheter and each coil are advanced into a fragile sac. Perforation by a wire or coil, and clot forming on the coil mass or in a stent, are the two procedural complications.
**Anaesthesia:** Rupture in the angiography suite is seen as contrast leaving the sac, with an abrupt rise in arterial pressure, and often bradycardia, as intracranial pressure climbs. The interventionalist's response is to keep coiling, since the fastest way to stop the bleeding is to finish packing. Yours is to reverse heparin with protamine at once (1 mg per 100 units given), bring systolic pressure down to about baseline with propofol or esmolol, give mannitol 0.5-1 g/kg IV, and ventilate to low-normal carbon dioxide; a ventricular drain is opened or inserted urgently. Thromboembolism is the mirror image: the interventionalist asks for pressure to be raised to drive collateral flow while giving an intra-arterial or intravenous glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitor. Know which complication you are treating, because the pressure targets are opposite.

### 5. Clipping: position, pins and brain relaxation
**Why:** The pterional approach reaches the circle of Willis along the sphenoid ridge with the frontal lobe falling back under gravity.
**Anaesthesia:** Blunt pinning completely, with a remifentanil bolus and local anaesthetic or a scalp block: this is the pre-securing stimulus most likely to be forgotten. Maintain with propofol and remifentanil. Give mannitol 0.5-1 g/kg IV, but in a ruptured aneurysm time it to finish as the dura is opened and not before, so that intracranial pressure does not fall while the skull is closed. A lumbar drain or the ventricular drain is opened by the surgeon after dural opening for the same reason. Keep carbon dioxide low-normal. Crossmatched blood is in the room.

### 6. Clipping: dissection and the temporary clip
**Why:** The surgeon splits the Sylvian fissure, defines the neck and, for a tense or adherent sac, places a temporary clip on the feeding artery to deflate it. Each minute of temporary occlusion is ischaemia in the distal territory.
**Anaesthesia:** When the temporary clip goes on, note the time and call it every few minutes; raise mean arterial pressure to baseline or 10-20% above it with metaraminol or noradrenaline to drive collateral flow. Maintain normothermia: the IHAST trial of cooling to 33 degrees C in 1,001 good-grade patients showed no improvement in outcome. Burst suppression with propofol before temporary clipping is requested by some surgeons; it has no outcome evidence, lowers pressure when you are trying to raise it, and is the surgeon's preference to accommodate, not a standard. Evoked potentials, where monitored, give the surgeon a warning that the clip time is too long.

### 7. Intraoperative rupture
**Why:** The dome tears during dissection or clip application and the field fills with arterial blood. The surgeon needs to see for a few seconds to place a temporary or permanent clip.
**Anaesthesia:** Say the blood loss aloud and replace it; this is the point at which a clean operation needs the [major haemorrhage](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html) pathway. If proximal control is already in place, the surgeon applies the temporary clip and you raise the pressure as above. If it is not, the surgeon may ask for a brief fall in pressure or for adenosine. Adenosine as a rapid IV bolus, commonly quoted at 0.3-0.4 mg/kg, gives roughly half a minute of asystole or profound hypotension and a bloodless field; in a series of 40 aneurysms it was used for softening the sac and for rupture with only minor arrhythmias and two troponin rises. Have external pacing pads on before the start if this is planned, and avoid it in severe coronary disease or bronchospasm. Do not give tranexamic acid as a reflex: in ULTRA it did not improve outcome after subarachnoid haemorrhage.

### 8. Permanent clip and confirmation
**Why:** The clip is closed across the neck, the dome is punctured or checked with indocyanine green angiography and a Doppler probe to prove the sac is excluded and the parent and perforating arteries are still open.
**Anaesthesia:** Indocyanine green 12.5-25 mg IV on request causes a transient false fall in the pulse oximeter reading. Once the aneurysm is secured the pressure rule reverses: let the pressure rise to the patient's normal or a little above, restore normovolaemia, and stop any agent given to hold pressure down. If the surgeon repositions the clip because a branch is compromised, the temporary clip sequence repeats.

### 9. Emergence
**Why:** A prompt neurological examination distinguishes a clipped perforator or a clot from an anaesthetic hangover while something can still be done.
**Anaesthesia:** Good-grade and unruptured patients are woken and extubated at the end with the same smooth emergence as any craniotomy: low-rate remifentanil through pin removal or sheath management, full reversal, antihypertensive drawn up. After coiling, coughing and straining also threaten the groin puncture, and the leg stays straight. Poor-grade patients (those who were obtunded before treatment), and anyone after intraoperative rupture or a long temporary clip, stay intubated for neurocritical care. A new deficit on waking goes back to the surgeon or interventionalist immediately for angiography.

### 10. Days 4 to 14: vasospasm and the return visit
**Why:** Blood breakdown products around the basal arteries cause narrowing and delayed ischaemia. The interventionalist treats refractory cases with intra-arterial vasodilators or balloon angioplasty.
**Anaesthesia:** A patient who comes back for angioplasty has a secured aneurysm, a drowsy or fluctuating conscious level, and a brain that is pressure-dependent. The targets are euvolaemia and induced hypertension, often on noradrenaline already: induction must not drop the pressure, and intra-arterial verapamil or nimodipine given by the interventionalist will, so run the vasopressor ahead of it. Sodium is often low from cerebral salt wasting or inappropriate antidiuretic hormone and is corrected with sodium, not fluid restriction.

## Pain plan

The mechanism differs by route, and the analgesic strategy is systemic and deliberately light because conscious level is the monitor. After clipping, the pain is that of a pterional craniotomy: temporalis cut and retracted, so pain on chewing and jaw opening for days. A scalp block or incision and muscle infiltration, regular paracetamol and small rescue doses of oral or intravenous opioid follow the PROSPECT craniotomy recommendations. After coiling, the procedure contributes a sore groin and paracetamol is enough.

NSAIDs are the open question. PROSPECT recommends them after elective craniotomy, but most units avoid them after subarachnoid haemorrhage in the first days, and patients with stents are on dual antiplatelets already; as author's judgement, omit them until the surgical team asks otherwise. Dexmedetomidine is a reasonable intraoperative adjunct for clipping.

The subarachnoid headache is the harder problem and has little evidence. It is treated with regular paracetamol and titrated oral opioid, accepting an incomplete result, and avoiding doses that cloud the hourly examination or retain carbon dioxide. Dexamethasone and occipital nerve blocks are used in some units. Constipation and straining are prevented from day 1.

Escalation: a sudden new headache before the aneurysm is secured is a rebleed. A change in headache with drowsiness or a new deficit between days 4 and 14 is vasospasm or hydrocephalus and needs imaging, not analgesia. Groin or flank pain with hypotension after coiling is a retroperitoneal haematoma.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Unruptured aneurysms. Elective, no vasospasm, no swollen brain, and an intact patient with everything to lose: the same avoidance of pressure surges, with dual antiplatelet loading before stents and flow diverters.
- Intracerebral haematoma with a ruptured aneurysm. An emergency craniotomy in a patient with raised intracranial pressure: the transmural pressure rule and the [traumatic brain injury](traumatic-brain-injury.html) principles apply together.
- Stunned myocardium. Low ejection fraction and pulmonary oedema that recover over days; use noradrenaline with caution and consider inotropic support guided by echocardiography.
- Ventricular drain handling. Clamp to move, level at the tragus, reopen at the prescribed height, never leave clamped unmonitored and never let it siphon.
- Radiation and contrast. Long coiling cases deliver a significant dose; lead for the anaesthetist and distance from the tubes. Iodinated contrast and a dehydrated patient threaten the kidneys.
- Arteriovenous malformations. Embolisation and resection share the suite and the craniotomy, but the hazard after resection is normal perfusion pressure breakthrough, and pressure is kept low afterwards.
- Hyponatraemia. Treat with hypertonic saline or salt; fluid restriction risks ischaemia in the vasospasm window.
- Rapid ventricular pacing and balloon occlusion are alternatives to adenosine for flow arrest in a few centres.

## Key sources

Coiling against clipping rests on one large randomised trial and its long-term follow-up. Hypothermia has a definitive negative trial. Nimodipine has an old, positive, well-conducted trial and remains the one drug that improves outcome. The blood pressure thresholds before securing, the temporary clip targets and the rupture drills are consensus and guideline opinion, not trial results, and the adenosine dose is quoted from common practice with a single retrospective series for safety. The subarachnoid headache has almost no evidence and the advice is the author's judgement.
