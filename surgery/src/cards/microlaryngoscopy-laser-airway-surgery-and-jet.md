## What the surgeon is doing and why

The larynx is examined and operated on through a rigid tubular laryngoscope passed over the tongue, lifted to expose the glottis and then fixed in suspension on a support so that both of the surgeon's hands are free under the operating microscope. Through it the surgeon biopsies a suspicious cord, removes a polyp, cyst or nodule with cold instruments, debulks papilloma with a microdebrider or laser, injects a paralysed cord to medialise it, dilates a subglottic stenosis with a balloon after radial cuts, or resects an early tumour with a carbon dioxide laser. A panendoscopy for cancer staging adds pharyngoscopy, oesophagoscopy and sometimes bronchoscopy.

The glottis is about a centimetre wide in the adult. It is the surgeon's entire operative field and it is also the only way gas reaches the lungs. Every technique on this card is a way of dividing that centimetre between two people, and each gives something up. A small tube guarantees ventilation and protects the trachea but sits across the posterior commissure and hides part of the field. Removing the tube gives the surgeon everything and leaves the airway unprotected and ventilation dependent on apnoeic oxygenation, intermittent re-intubation or a jet of high-pressure gas. The operation requires a motionless larynx, which means cords that are either paralysed or very deeply anaesthetised, yet it is short and ends abruptly, and the patient must then breathe and protect an airway that has just been operated on.

The pain logic is trivial by comparison: a small mucosal wound and pressure from the laryngoscope on tongue, teeth and jaw. Score 1. The difficulty of this card is the airway, the laser and the jet. The general framework sits in [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html).

## Steps

### 1. Planning with the surgeon
**Why:** The surgeon has seen the larynx in clinic with a nasendoscope and knows where the lesion is, how big it is and how much airway remains.
**Anaesthesia:** Look at the clinic images or the nasendoscopy together. Three things decide the plan. Where the lesion sits: posterior glottic and subglottic work is obscured by a tube, anterior work is not. Whether a laser is planned. And whether the airway is already narrowed: stridor at rest, a voice change with breathlessness, or a CT showing a lumen of a few millimetres moves the case out of this card's routine and into the obstructed airway plan in [airway foreign body, epiglottitis and the obstructed airway](airway-foreign-body-epiglottitis-and-the.html). Agree the primary technique, the fallback, and who will call a change. These patients are often smokers with coronary and lung disease, and suspension laryngoscopy is a larger haemodynamic stimulus than intubation.

### 2. Induction
**Why:** The surgeon needs a deeply anaesthetised, paralysed patient within minutes and will be finished within the half hour.
**Anaesthesia:** Preoxygenate thoroughly, with high-flow nasal oxygen if a tubeless technique is planned. Propofol and remifentanil TIVA from the start, because the breathing system will be open. Give dexamethasone 8 mg IV. For relaxation, rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg with sugammadex at the end gives profound block that can be removed on demand; suxamethonium by bolus or mivacurium are the older alternatives. Monitor the block quantitatively: the cords must not move, and the reversal must be complete. Mask ventilate and confirm it is easy before committing to any technique that depends on it as the rescue.

### 3. Securing or sharing the airway
**Why:** The surgeon wants the least possible material in the glottis for the lesion in question.
**Anaesthesia:** The four options, with what each costs.

A microlaryngeal tube is long, narrow and has a full-size cuff; it is the default for anterior lesions, for reflux and obesity, and whenever you are unsure. Tubeless apnoeic oxygenation with high-flow nasal oxygen at 50-70 L/min, the jaw held forward or the laryngoscope in place, is established practice: in the two foundational series apnoea of 14 and 22.5 minutes was sustained without desaturation below 90%, while carbon dioxide rose at 0.15-0.24 kPa/min. That rise is the clock. Obesity shortens the time available, and the airway is unprotected throughout.

### 4. Suspension laryngoscopy
**Why:** The surgeon lifts the tongue base and epiglottis with a rigid tube and fixes it, with enough force to hold the view for the whole operation.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the peak stimulus: hypertension and tachycardia in most, and in a few a profound bradycardia or asystole from vagal afferents in the larynx, which stops when the surgeon releases the suspension. Give a remifentanil bolus or raise the target ahead of it and have atropine drawn up. Check the tube has not been pushed down or the cuff damaged as the scope passes. The teeth take the fulcrum: note loose teeth and crowns beforehand and see a guard placed. The surgeon sprays lidocaine onto the cords now; count it towards the dose.

### 5. Running a jet
**Why:** For posterior and subglottic work, and for stenosis, the surgeon needs the glottis empty; a jet delivers gas through a needle on the laryngoscope or a fine catheter through the cords.
**Anaesthesia:** A jet entrains air and inflates the lungs with a volume you cannot measure, at a pressure set by the driving source. Exhalation is passive, through the glottis around the jet. If that path closes (cords coming together as relaxant wears off, the scope slipping, a tight stenosis, a surgeon's instrument, laryngospasm) the next breath stacks on the last. In a ten-year series of 1,093 microlaryngoscopies, all seven cases of barotrauma occurred with jet ventilation, and a transtracheal cannula was the main independent risk factor. The rules that follow from the mechanism: start at a low driving pressure (1-1.5 bar in an adult, less in a child) and increase only to achieve visible chest movement; watch the chest fall completely before every manual breath; use an automated high-frequency ventilator with a pause-pressure alarm where there is one, since a national survey found the deaths in this field were all in departments using manual devices; keep the patient paralysed; and never jet through a transtracheal catheter without knowing the upper airway is open. Supraglottic jetting is the safest for barotrauma but blows blood and debris distally and moves the cords. Oxygenation is monitored by pulse oximetry and ventilation by intermittent end-tidal sampling during a pause or by transcutaneous carbon dioxide.

### 6. Cold-steel surgery
**Why:** Benign lesions are dissected off the superficial layer of the cord with microinstruments to preserve the vibrating edge; biopsies are taken with cup forceps.
**Anaesthesia:** Absolute stillness, including from ventilation: the surgeon may ask you to hold breaths during a cut. Adrenaline 1 in 10,000 on a patty is applied for haemostasis and is absorbed; a brief tachycardia follows. Blood and fragments in a tubeless airway go to the trachea unless the surgeon suctions them, which is another reason for a cuffed tube when bleeding is expected.

### 7. Laser
**Why:** The carbon dioxide laser cuts and seals mucosa precisely down the line of sight of the microscope, and is used for papilloma, stenosis and tumour resection.
**Anaesthesia:** An airway fire needs an oxidiser, a fuel and an ignition source, and here all three are within a centimetre of each other. The precautions are those set out in the operating room fire advisory. Oxidiser: inspired oxygen at the lowest concentration that maintains saturation, 30% or less where possible, in air and never in nitrous oxide; tell the surgeon the figure and wait for the expired concentration to fall before firing. Fuel: a laser-resistant tube with the cuff filled with saline (tinted, so a cuff strike is visible) and protected by wet swabs, or no tube at all. Ignition: the laser on standby whenever the surgeon is not looking down the microscope. Wet drapes around the face, protective glasses for staff and wet eye pads on the patient. Tubeless high-flow oxygen during laser work is controversial for the obvious reason that it floods the field with oxidiser; one series of 369 consecutive laser cases reported no fire using it within a strict laser protocol, though two-thirds also needed jet ventilation. The author's judgement is that it belongs only in teams who do it routinely.

If the tube ignites: the surgeon removes it and you stop all gas flow at the same moment, saline is poured into the airway, the patient is mask ventilated and reintubated, and the airway is examined bronchoscopically for the extent of the burn.

### 8. Dilatation, injection and steroid
**Why:** A stenosis is cut radially and dilated with a balloon; a cord is injected; steroid may be injected into scar.
**Anaesthesia:** A balloon inflated in the subglottis occludes the airway completely for the 30-60 seconds it is up: preoxygenate, stop any jet, and time it aloud. A dilated stenosis is oedematous afterwards and can be tighter in recovery than before.

### 9. Handing the airway back
**Why:** The surgeon removes the laryngoscope the moment the work is finished.
**Anaesthesia:** The stimulus disappears, the blood pressure falls, and the patient is paralysed with an unprotected airway. Take over with a face mask or place a laryngeal mask, which gives a calm bridge to waking; if a tube is in, either leave it or exchange it. Reverse the block fully (sugammadex 2-4 mg/kg IV according to depth) and confirm a train-of-four ratio above 0.9. Suction blood from the pharynx under vision.

### 10. Emergence and recovery
**Why:** The cords have been instrumented and are swollen; the patient needs to breathe through them unaided.
**Anaesthesia:** Follow the extubation guideline's logic for an at-risk airway: plan it, optimise position and oxygenation, and decide awake against deep by which risk is greater. Laryngospasm is common in the minutes after any tubeless or mask-bridged technique: positive pressure with oxygen, propofol, and relaxant if it does not break. Stridor in recovery is oedema or residual lesion; sit the patient up, give nebulised adrenaline 5 mg and humidified oxygen, and call the surgeon. The topical lidocaine means nothing by mouth for about two hours.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a small mucosal wound in an area already anaesthetised by the surgeon's topical lidocaine, so the plan is paracetamol and an NSAID in theatre, continued for two or three days as needed. Remifentanil covers the intraoperative stimulus, which is intense but ends when the laryngoscope comes out; no long-acting opioid is needed for a routine case, and avoiding it helps the patient wake with airway reflexes intact. Dexamethasone is given for oedema and contributes to analgesia.

Expect a sore throat of 2 out of 10 for a day or two. Tongue numbness or altered taste from compression of the lingual nerve under suspension is common after long cases and resolves over days to weeks; warn patients having long procedures. Jaw and dental pain reflect the fulcrum.

The exception is transoral laser or robotic resection of a supraglottic or tongue base tumour, which leaves a raw area that is re-provoked by swallowing. Treat that as the mucosal mechanism at tonsillectomy scale: regular paracetamol and NSAID for one to two weeks, a rescue opioid, and attention to swallowing and aspiration. See [adult and paediatric tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy](adult-and-paediatric-tonsillectomy-and-adenoidectomy.html) for the regimen. There is no PROSPECT guideline for laryngeal surgery.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The narrowed airway. A patient with stridor for biopsy or debulking is the high-risk variant: induction can convert partial obstruction to complete. Options are awake tracheal intubation with a small tube, awake tracheostomy under local anaesthesia, or induction with the surgeon scrubbed and a rigid bronchoscope and tracheostomy set open. Decide before the patient is in the room.
- Barotrauma. Surgical emphysema, pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum or sudden cardiovascular collapse during or just after jetting. Stop the jet, open the airway, intubate, and decompress the chest if the picture is tension.
- Airway fire. Rare and survivable when the response is immediate; the drill is rehearsed before the laser is switched on, not improvised.
- Papilloma in children. Repeated procedures, a narrow airway, spontaneous ventilation techniques with TIVA and topical lidocaine, and a real risk of total obstruction at induction. Paediatric centres only.
- Hypercapnia. Tolerated for the durations involved in fit patients; a reason to shorten apnoea in pulmonary hypertension, raised intracranial pressure and severe coronary disease.
- Cardiovascular disease. Suspension produces myocardial ischaemia in susceptible patients; an arterial line is justified for significant coronary or cerebrovascular disease and for long laser resections.
- Residual block. A short operation and a profound block is the recipe for it. Quantitative monitoring and sugammadex have removed the excuse.
- Dental damage. The laryngoscope pivots on the upper incisors for the whole case; document the teeth beforehand and tell the patient.
- Oesophagoscopy during panendoscopy. Rigid oesophagoscopy can perforate; chest or neck pain, surgical emphysema or fever afterwards is a perforation until imaged.
- Critical care is not needed for routine cases. It enters with the obstructed airway, a fire, barotrauma or a large tumour resection with oedema; a tracheostomy may be done at the same sitting, see [tracheostomy](tracheostomy.html).

## Key sources

The complication data for jet ventilation come from one large single-centre series and one national survey, both now old but not superseded. High-flow nasal oxygen for tubeless laryngeal surgery rests on two physiological case series and subsequent experience; neither was a comparison with another technique. The laser fire precautions are an advisory based on expert consensus and case reports. Driving pressures, relaxant choices, the lidocaine dose and the recovery management of stridor are consensus and author's judgement.
