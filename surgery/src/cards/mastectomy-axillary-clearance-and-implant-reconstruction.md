## What the surgeon is doing and why

Mastectomy removes all the breast tissue on one side: for a cancer too large or too multifocal for conservation, for a patient who cannot have radiotherapy, by choice, or as risk reduction in a gene carrier, in which case it is bilateral. The breast is a skin appendage lying on pectoralis major and it has no capsule, so the surgeon creates its boundaries. Skin flaps are raised in the plane between subcutaneous fat and gland, up to the clavicle, down to the inframammary fold, medially to the sternal edge and laterally to latissimus dorsi. The breast is then peeled off the muscle with the pectoral fascia. In a simple mastectomy an ellipse of skin with the nipple goes too and the flaps are closed flat. In skin-sparing and nipple-sparing mastectomy the envelope is kept for reconstruction, and the flaps are longer, thinner and more fragile.

The axilla is dealt with through the same wound. A sentinel node biopsy is the default for a clinically negative axilla (see [wide local excision and sentinel node biopsy](wide-local-excision-and-sentinel-node.html) for the dye). An axillary clearance removes the fat and nodes below and behind pectoralis minor (levels I and II, sometimes III) as a block bounded by the axillary vein above, the chest wall medially and latissimus behind. The surgeon must find and keep the long thoracic nerve to serratus and the thoracodorsal bundle to latissimus, and will usually sacrifice or stretch the intercostobrachial nerve.

Immediate implant reconstruction fills the empty envelope. There are two pockets:

```
 PREPECTORAL                      SUBPECTORAL (dual plane)
 skin flap                        skin flap
 [ implant wrapped in mesh ]      pectoralis major (lifted, origin cut)
 pectoralis major (untouched)     [ implant or expander ]
 ribs                             ribs      lower pole: mesh sling
 pain: skin, raw surface          pain: muscle stretch and spasm
```

In the prepectoral pocket the device sits on top of the muscle, wrapped in acellular dermal matrix or synthetic mesh. In the subpectoral pocket the surgeon lifts pectoralis major, divides its inferior and lower sternal origin, and slides the device underneath, with a mesh sling holding the lower pole. A tissue expander is part-filled with saline and inflated over weeks before exchange for a permanent implant; a direct-to-implant reconstruction goes in at final size.

The pain logic depends on which of these was done. Mastectomy alone is a large raw surface and a long wound with no muscle cut: skin and soft tissue, moderate, settling over days. Add a clearance and the axilla contributes arm-movement pain and a neuropathic element from the intercostobrachial nerve. Add a subpectoral device and the dominant mechanism becomes muscle: a pectoralis major detached at one end and stretched over a firm object, which goes into spasm and hurts with every reach and deep breath. That is the operation that earns the score of 4, and the nerves that serve it (medial and lateral pectoral, from the brachial plexus) are not thoracic nerves. Persistent pain is the other feature: nearly half of women in a national cohort had pain in the operated area at two to three years, with axillary clearance, young age and radiotherapy raising the odds.

## Steps

### 1. Block, induction and positioning
**Why:** The surgeon needs the whole chest wall and axilla exposed, the arm movable, and for reconstruction the ability to sit the patient up and compare the two sides.
**Anaesthesia:** Do the block before incision so that it reduces intraoperative opioid, which is where the pectoral block trials showed their benefit. Pecs II covers the pectoral nerves, the lateral cutaneous branches of the intercostals, the intercostobrachial and the long thoracic nerve; paravertebral block covers the intercostal nerves including their anterior branches near the sternum but not the pectoral nerves. Tell the surgeon which you have done: a block that includes the long thoracic nerve can abolish the serratus twitch they look for in the axilla. Propofol TIVA with remifentanil is the author's default for nausea reasons. The randomised trial of paravertebral block with propofol against sevoflurane with opioid found no difference in cancer recurrence, so technique is chosen for recovery quality and not for oncology. Secure the head, pad the arms, strap the pelvis and test the sitting position before draping.

### 2. Skin incision and flap elevation
**Why:** The surgeon raises flaps of even thickness in the subcutaneous plane, often after infiltrating dilute adrenaline solution to open the plane and reduce bleeding. Flaps that are too thin die; flaps that are too thick leave breast tissue behind.
**Anaesthesia:** The longest part of the mastectomy and a steady diathermy plume. Skin flap perfusion is the surgeon's main worry in skin-sparing and nipple-sparing operations and it depends on pressure: keep mean arterial pressure near baseline, keep the patient warm and normovolaemic, and treat hypotension rather than tolerating it for a dry field. A modest vasopressor infusion is not the enemy of the flap; hypotension and cold are. Count the adrenaline and any local anaesthetic in the tumescent solution.

### 3. Dissection of the breast off pectoralis major
**Why:** The gland is lifted from medial to lateral with the pectoral fascia, and the perforating vessels from the internal mammary artery near the sternal edge are controlled one by one.
**Anaesthesia:** The medial perforators are the bleeding point of the operation, both now and tonight: one that retracts into the intercostal space and reopens when the blood pressure recovers is the usual cause of a return to theatre. Diathermy on the muscle makes the shoulder jump; this is direct stimulation and paralysis does not stop it. Bradycardia or asystole from diathermy interference with the monitor is artefact far more often than event, so look at the plethysmograph trace before you treat.

### 4. Axillary surgery
**Why:** Sentinel node biopsy, or clearance of levels I and II between the axillary vein, chest wall and latissimus, with preservation of the long thoracic and thoracodorsal nerves.
**Anaesthesia:** No neuromuscular block from here: the surgeon confirms the motor nerves by the twitch of serratus and latissimus. If blue dye was given for a sentinel node, the window for anaphylaxis (about 0.08% in breast surgery) is now. The arm may be lifted and flexed across the face by the assistant to relax pectoralis major; protect the tube, the eyes and the brachial plexus from a prolonged abducted and externally rotated position. Clearance adds 30-45 minutes, a drain, and most of the long-term arm morbidity.

### 5. Creating the implant pocket
**Why:** For a subpectoral reconstruction the surgeon elevates pectoralis major from the chest wall and divides its inferior origin; for a prepectoral one the muscle is left and the device is wrapped in mesh. The pocket must be dry, because blood around an implant drives infection and capsular contracture.
**Anaesthesia:** The muscle elevation is the pain-generating step, and it happens close to the pleura: a pneumothorax from dissection at the rib origin of pectoralis is rare but presents to you as rising airway pressure and falling saturation. If no pectoral block was done, this is when the surgeon can place local anaesthetic between the pectoral muscles under direct vision. Asepsis tightens: glove changes, pocket irrigation and minimal theatre traffic are the surgeon's implant ritual, and a second antibiotic dose is due at four hours.

### 6. Sizers, sitting up and the device
**Why:** Temporary sizers are placed and the patient is sat up so the surgeon can judge volume, projection and symmetry before the definitive implant or expander is opened and inserted with a no-touch technique.
**Anaesthesia:** Sitting an anaesthetised, vasodilated patient to 45-60 degrees drops the blood pressure, so give the vasopressor as the table moves and not after the reading falls. Check the tube, the head and the arms each time; the cycle may be repeated three or four times in a bilateral case. Expander inflation tightens the pectoral envelope further and is a late stimulus.

### 7. Drains and closure
**Why:** One or two suction drains per side control the dead space under the flaps and in the axilla; the skin is closed in layers and, in some units, assessed with indocyanine green angiography for perfusion.
**Anaesthesia:** Give the NSAID if the surgeon is content with haemostasis (PROSPECT recommends it, and withholding it as routine is a surgical habit and not an evidence-based one). Give long-acting opioid in a modest dose before the remifentanil stops. Indocyanine green is safe in the doses used and transiently lowers the oximeter reading.

### 8. Emergence and the first night
**Why:** A haematoma under the flaps threatens flap survival and, with an implant, the whole reconstruction, so the surgeon wants a quiet wake-up and a pressure garment.
**Anaesthesia:** Extubate without coughing: deep extubation to a supraglottic airway or a remifentanil wake-up are both reasonable. Blood pressure will rise above the intraoperative level as the patient wakes; that surge is the test of haemostasis and it is better it happens before she leaves theatre. Observe drain output in recovery. A drain that fills quickly, a swelling flap or a tense, painful, expanding chest is a haematoma and goes back to theatre, usually within six hours.

## Pain plan

The mechanism decides the plan. For mastectomy alone or with a prepectoral device the pain is skin and soft tissue over a wide area, carried by the intercostal nerves T2-T6 and, in the axilla, the intercostobrachial nerve. For a subpectoral device add pectoralis major, supplied by the medial and lateral pectoral nerves. PROSPECT recommends paracetamol and an NSAID started before or during surgery and continued, pre-operative gabapentin and dexamethasone, a paravertebral or pectoral nerves block and/or wound infiltration for major breast surgery, opioids as rescue, and allows a paravertebral catheter to be continued. A meta-analysis of 13 trials found that Pecs II reduced 24 hour opioid use by about 14 mg of oral morphine equivalents against systemic analgesia and matched paravertebral block for opioid use and pain scores.

The defensible default follows the anatomy rather than a ranking of blocks. For mastectomy with or without clearance: a single-shot Pecs II or a paravertebral block, either is supported. For subpectoral reconstruction the author's practice is Pecs II, because it is the block that reaches the pectoral nerves, supplemented by surgeon infiltration medially where the anterior cutaneous branches escape it. For bilateral surgery, bilateral Pecs II blocks keep within local anaesthetic limits more easily with ropivacaine 0.2-0.375% than bilateral multi-level paravertebral injections do, and carry no risk of bilateral sympathetic block. A catheter is rarely needed: the single shot covers the first night, the peak is day 1-2, and the oral regimen is adequate afterwards in most patients.

Systemic: paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, dexamethasone 8 mg IV, and oral opioid for the first two to three days. Gabapentin carries the PROSPECT recommendation; the author's judgement is to use a single pre-operative dose in inpatients and to omit it in the elderly and in day cases. Pectoral spasm after subpectoral expansion responds poorly to opioid and better to a short course of a muscle relaxant such as diazepam at night, which is common surgical practice and not trial-based. Rebound when the block recedes on the first night is moderate: write the bedtime opioid as regular for that night.

Escalation triggers: pain out of proportion with a swelling flap is a haematoma; burning, hypersensitive skin on the inner arm and lateral chest is the intercostobrachial nerve and may merit early neuropathic treatment; breathlessness with pleuritic pain after a paravertebral block or a subpectoral dissection is a pneumothorax until a film says otherwise. Neither a block nor TIVA has been shown to prevent persistent pain: the large recurrence trial found the frequency and severity of incisional pain at 6 and 12 months unaffected by a paravertebral technique.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Prepectoral against subpectoral. Ask before induction. Prepectoral reconstruction removes the muscle mechanism and brings the card down to a 3; subpectoral is the painful one.
- Delayed and second-stage operations. Exchange of expander for implant is a short, mildly painful day case. Capsulectomy for contracture or implant-associated lymphoma is longer, bloodier and close to the pleura.
- Latissimus dorsi flap. The patient is turned lateral for the harvest and back supine for the inset: two positions, a long back wound with a large seroma cavity, and a strong case for a paravertebral or erector spinae block on that side. Abdominal free flaps are a separate card: [free flap breast reconstruction (DIEP)](free-flap-breast-reconstruction.html).
- Bilateral risk-reducing mastectomy. Young, healthy, anxious patients, long cases, double the local anaesthetic dose and the group with the highest odds of persistent pain.
- Neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Anthracycline and trastuzumab cardiotoxicity, anaemia, a venous port on the other side, and poor veins.
- Toilet or salvage mastectomy. A fungating, bleeding or infected tumour in a frail patient: anaemia, hypoalbuminaemia, a contaminated field, larger blood loss and skin closure with a graft or flap. This is the variant in which a second cannula, a blood sample for crossmatch and a higher-dependency bed are justified.
- Return to theatre for haematoma. The emergency variant. The patient is usually haemodynamically stable but may have lost 500 mL or more into the pocket; treat as a recent anaesthetic with a possibly full stomach, expect the same airway, and do not repeat a block into a field the surgeon is about to open.
- The arm. Avoid the operated side for cannulation and pressure measurement after clearance where there is a choice; in bilateral disease use the side with the lesser axillary surgery or a leg. The strength of the evidence behind this convention is modest.
- Smokers, diabetics and irradiated chests. Flap necrosis and implant loss are several times more likely; the surgeon may stage the reconstruction, and your contribution is pressure, temperature and glucose.
- Invasive monitoring, blood and critical care are not needed for the standard operation. They become relevant for the salvage mastectomy and for patients with chemotherapy-related cardiomyopathy.

## Key sources

PROSPECT's guideline and the Pecs II meta-analysis are the evidence for the block; neither separates subpectoral reconstruction from mastectomy alone, so the preference for a pectoral block in that setting is an anatomical argument and the author's judgement. The statements on flap perfusion and blood pressure, antispasmodics, tranexamic acid and the arm convention are consensus or opinion. The ERAS Society recommendations are a consensus review built largely on extrapolated data.
