## What the surgeon is doing and why

These are the two largest elective operations in abdominal surgery, and they share a principle: cancer that has spread beyond the organ of origin but not beyond a compartment can sometimes be cured by removing the compartment.

Cytoreductive surgery treats cancer that has seeded the peritoneum: pseudomyxoma from an appendiceal tumour, colorectal and ovarian peritoneal metastases, peritoneal mesothelioma. The peritoneal cavity is treated as one organ. The surgeon opens from xiphoid to pubis, scores the disease in 13 regions (the peritoneal carcinomatosis index, 0-39), and then removes every visible deposit. That means stripping the parietal peritoneum off the abdominal wall, the pelvis, the paracolic gutters and the undersurface of both hemidiaphragms, removing the greater and lesser omentum, and resecting whatever viscera are coated: commonly the right colon, the rectosigmoid, the spleen, the gallbladder, the uterus and ovaries, lengths of small bowel, and the capsule of the liver. The aim is no residual nodule larger than 2.5 mm, because that is roughly how far chemotherapy penetrates tissue from the surface. Then the abdomen is perfused with heated chemotherapy (HIPEC) at 41-43 degrees C for 30-90 minutes to treat what cannot be seen. Heat is cytotoxic in itself and increases drug penetration; intraperitoneal delivery gives high local concentration with limited systemic exposure. The agent depends on the tumour: mitomycin C, oxaliplatin or cisplatin are the usual three, and each has a different toxicity.

Pelvic exenteration treats locally advanced or recurrent rectal, gynaecological or urological cancer that has grown into neighbouring pelvic organs. Total exenteration removes the rectum, bladder, distal ureters and the reproductive organs in one block; anterior and posterior versions spare the rectum or the bladder. When tumour reaches the pelvic side wall or the sacrum the resection extends to internal iliac vessels, sciatic nerve roots, or the sacrum itself. The patient leaves with a colostomy, a urinary conduit fashioned from ileum, and a pelvic defect that is usually filled with a rectus or gluteal flap.

What both operations do to physiology follows from surface area and time. A stripped peritoneum is a burn-like raw surface the size of the patient's skin: it weeps protein-rich fluid for days, loses heat, absorbs nothing and bleeds diffusely. Six to twelve hours with the abdomen open multiplies that. The anaesthetic is a long exercise in replacing volume, protein, clotting factors and heat without drowning the anastomoses, with a pharmacological event (HIPEC) or a haemorrhagic one (pelvic side wall, presacral veins, sacrectomy) near the end when reserves are lowest.

Pain logic. The incision alone would score 4. The stripped parietal peritoneum makes the entire inner abdominal wall a wound; the diaphragmatic stripping adds shoulder-tip and pleuritic pain and chest drains; the patient cannot mobilise early; and recovery is prolonged by ileus and re-provoked by coughing with effusions. Exenteration adds the perineum and nerve resection. Pain persists well beyond 72 hours, which is the definition of a 5.

## Steps

### 1. Selection and preparation
**Why:** Morbidity is high and the benefit depends on complete cytoreduction, so patients are selected by a multidisciplinary meeting on disease extent and fitness.
**Anaesthesia:** Assess functional capacity objectively (cardiopulmonary exercise testing where available), nutrition and albumin, anaemia, and what previous chemotherapy has left behind: anthracycline and trastuzumab cardiotoxicity, platinum neuropathy and renal impairment, bleomycin lungs. Ascites and pleural effusions may need draining. Prehabilitation, iron and nutritional support are the modifiable items. The patient should have met the stoma nurse and should know they will wake in intensive care.

### 2. Epidural, induction and lines
**Why:** Every later step assumes access you cannot obtain once the drapes are on and the arms are surrounded by retractors.
**Anaesthesia:** Thoracic epidural at T7-T9 placed awake, tested, and used cautiously until the bleeding phase is past. Tracheal tube. Arterial line, central line for vasopressors and for the electrolyte replacement that follows HIPEC, wide-bore peripheral access attached to a fluid warmer and rapid infuser, cardiac output monitoring, nasopharyngeal or oesophageal and bladder temperature probes, nasogastric tube. Baseline gas, coagulation and viscoelastic test. Forced-air warming above and below, warmed fluids, and a plan to switch them off later.

### 3. Positioning for a twelve-hour case
**Why:** Lloyd-Davies gives access to the perineum for the stapler, for vaginal or perineal resection and for a second team.
**Anaesthesia:** Padding at the heels, calves, sacrum, occiput and elbows; arms abducted less than 90 degrees; eyes taped and lubricated. Well-leg compartment syndrome and pressure injury scale with time, so agree a schedule to level the legs. Fixed retractors on the costal margin restrict the chest wall: note the airway pressure before and after they go on.

### 4. Laparotomy, staging and the decision to proceed
**Why:** The surgeon assesses whether complete cytoreduction is achievable. Extensive small bowel or porta hepatis disease means an open-and-close or a palliative procedure.
**Anaesthesia:** Draining several litres of mucin or ascites on opening drops the intra-abdominal pressure and with it the preload: have volume running and a vasopressor in hand. This is also the point at which the planned operation may shrink to 90 minutes, so hold back long-acting drugs until the surgeon commits.

### 5. Peritonectomy and visceral resections
**Why:** Stripping proceeds quadrant by quadrant with high-power diathermy, which is slow, smoky and hot, with organ resections as needed. Bowel ends are usually left stapled and joined after HIPEC.
**Anaesthesia:** The long middle. Fluid loss from raw surfaces is continuous and invisible; blood loss is diffuse ooze punctuated by the spleen, the pelvis and the liver capsule. Guide volume with stroke volume and lactate trends, not a formula, and use a low-dose noradrenaline infusion for epidural and anaesthetic vasodilatation. The retrospective data link both blood loss and the total fluid given to worse outcomes, and RELIEF warns against the opposite error; aim for the middle. Albumin falls steeply and many units replace it, though without trial evidence. Coagulopathy is dilutional and consumptive: test every one to two hours and replace fibrinogen early. Glucose, potassium, magnesium and ionised calcium drift and need hourly attention.

### 6. Diaphragm stripping and upper abdominal work
**Why:** The peritoneum is peeled off both hemidiaphragms and the liver is mobilised; a full-thickness patch of diaphragm is sometimes resected.
**Anaesthesia:** Liver retraction kinks the vena cava and drops venous return abruptly. The pleura is often opened: watch airway pressure and oxygenation, and expect the surgeon to place chest drains, since HIPEC fluid and later effusions track into the chest. Ventilation after this step is that of a patient with bilateral pleural breaches and a splinted diaphragm.

### 7. HIPEC
**Why:** With the resection complete, inflow and outflow cannulae are placed, the abdomen is closed temporarily or held open as a reservoir (coliseum technique), and 3-5 L of carrier solution with the drug is circulated at 41-43 degrees C while the abdomen is agitated.
**Anaesthesia:** Stop warming and cool pre-emptively: cold fluids, forced air on ambient, ice packs to the head and axillae if the core passes 38.5 degrees C. Expect heart rate and cardiac output to rise, systemic resistance to fall, oxygen consumption and end-tidal CO2 to climb, and lactate to rise. With the closed technique the perfusate raises intra-abdominal pressure, which raises airway pressure and reduces venous return. Maintain a brisk urine output through this phase, the conventional protection against nephrotoxic agents. Agent-specific points: cisplatin is nephrotoxic and wastes magnesium, and an intravenous sodium thiosulfate protocol prevented the creatinine rise in an ovarian cohort (a 53% rise without it, none with it); oxaliplatin is carried in dextrose 5%, so several litres of glucose and free water are absorbed and blood glucose and sodium must be measured every 15-20 minutes with insulin running; mitomycin C causes delayed neutropenia. Staff wear chemotherapy-protective equipment, and body fluids are handled as cytotoxic for 48 hours.

### 8. Reconstruction and closure
**Why:** After the perfusate is drained the bowel is joined, stomas are made, drains and chest drains placed and the abdomen closed.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient is now at maximum vasodilatation with the largest accumulated deficit, and the surgeon is making anastomoses that depend on perfusion: restore volume and pressure before, not after. Core temperature falls quickly once perfusion stops, so warming goes back on. Recheck coagulation and platelets before deciding whether the epidural can be loaded and whether extubation is sensible. Extubate in theatre only if the patient is warm, not acidotic, not on escalating vasopressor and exchanging gas well; otherwise ventilate for a few hours.

### 9. Pelvic exenteration: the differences
**Why:** After mobilising the left colon the surgeon dissects outside the planes of a standard rectal resection, divides the ureters and urethra or the vagina, takes internal iliac branches for a side-wall resection, and may turn the patient prone to divide the sacrum. A urologist builds the conduit and a plastic surgeon the flap.
**Anaesthesia:** No hyperthermia and no chemotherapy, but more blood. The internal iliac venous system and the presacral plexus bleed fast and are hard to control; sacrectomy opens cancellous bone and epidural veins. Minimally invasive exenteration in selected patients loses about 600 mL less than open, which gives a sense of the scale. Run it as a planned massive transfusion: rapid infuser primed, products in theatre, calcium, viscoelastic testing every hour. Urine output is unmeasurable once the ureters are divided, so perfusion is judged by lactate and cardiac output. A prone turn at hour six in a swollen patient needs the same care as a spinal case with ten times the lines. Resected sacral roots mean expected, permanent deficits and neuropathic pain to plan for from day 0.

### 10. Intensive care and the first week
**Why:** The surgical team is watching for bleeding, leak, collections, ileus and, after HIPEC, marrow suppression at day 7-10.
**Anaesthesia:** Fluid loss from the raw surfaces continues for 48-72 hours through drains that may put out 1-2 L a day. Coagulopathy and thrombocytopenia often reach their worst on day 1-3, which is exactly when someone will want to remove or adjust the epidural: check the platelet count and coagulation before touching it, and time heparin around it. Hypophosphataemia and hypomagnesaemia are common. Pleural effusions and atelectasis make the epidural's contribution to coughing worth protecting.

## Pain plan

The mechanism (a maximal incision, a raw abdominal wall, chest drains, prolonged immobility) argues for a thoracic epidural run for four to six days, and this is the technique the ERAS guideline for cytoreductive surgery supports by consensus and that reduced opioid use and sped the return of bowel function in a before-and-after pathway study. The evidence is observational. The honest account of the epidural's costs here is longer than usual: hypotension in a vasodilated, hypovolaemic patient; a coagulopathy that develops after the catheter is in; and a catheter that may have to stay beyond its useful life until the platelets recover. The defensible default is still to place it, to run a dilute local anaesthetic and opioid mixture with a vasopressor available, and to remove it only with a normal coagulation screen and an adequate platelet count.

When an epidural is not possible, the next best combination is intrathecal morphine for the first day plus bilateral rectus sheath catheters for the midline wound, with a ketamine infusion and PCA. This covers the incision but not the stripped peritoneum or the diaphragm, and opioid requirements are substantial. Paracetamol is given throughout unless liver resection or dysfunction argues for a reduced dose. NSAIDs are avoided for the first days: the kidneys have been through hypovolaemia and often a nephrotoxic drug, platelets are low, and there are several anastomoses.

After exenteration with nerve or sacral resection, start a gabapentinoid or other antineuropathic agent early and involve the pain service before the patient leaves intensive care; ketamine is useful in the first days. Many of these patients arrive on opioids for tumour pain, and their baseline has to be continued, converted and added to.

Escalation: an epidural that covers the incision but leaves severe shoulder-tip or chest pain is working correctly, and the missing part needs systemic opioid. A sudden increase in pain after day 3 is a leak, a collection or bleeding.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Open and close. Up to a few hours of anaesthesia, an epidural in place and a patient with unresectable disease: the plan converts to comfort and early mobilisation.
- Low-volume disease and laparoscopic HIPEC. A shorter operation with modest shifts in selected patients; scale the monitoring down accordingly.
- Agent and carrier. Know before induction which drug, what dose, what carrier and what duration; the glucose, sodium, magnesium and renal plan follows from it.
- Early postoperative intraperitoneal chemotherapy. Some units continue chemotherapy through drains for several days on the ward, which prolongs the cytotoxic precautions and the ileus.
- Diaphragm resection and effusions. A proportion need non-invasive ventilation or reintubation on day 2-3 when fluid shifts back.
- Renal protection. Avoid hypovolaemia, nephrotoxic antibiotics, contrast and NSAIDs around cisplatin; follow the unit's thiosulfate protocol.
- Exenteration with sacrectomy. Often staged over two days or run with two teams; plan staffing, handovers and a second set of blood products.
- Flap reconstruction. The flap dislikes vasoconstrictors, hypothermia and hypovolaemia as much as the anastomoses do; the pedicle must not be compressed by positioning in intensive care.
- The redo patient. Recurrence after previous cytoreduction means adhesions, longer surgery and more blood for less benefit, and a careful conversation beforehand.

## Key sources

Almost everything here is consensus and observational data. The ERAS Society guideline for cytoreductive surgery is an expert Delphi process that itself calls for trials; the analgesic recommendation rests on cohort studies; the fluid strategy is extrapolated from general major abdominal surgery. The haemodynamic description of the HIPEC phase is consistent across case series and is standard teaching. The blood loss ranges given are the author's working figures from the published series and vary widely with disease extent.
