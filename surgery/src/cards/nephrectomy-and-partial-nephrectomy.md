## What the surgeon is doing and why

The kidney sits in the retroperitoneum inside a fat-filled envelope (Gerota's fascia), hung from the aorta and vena cava by a short artery and a short, thin-walled vein, with the adrenal on top, the diaphragm and pleura behind its upper pole, and the colon, duodenum, liver, spleen and pancreatic tail in front. Almost all nephrectomy is for a renal mass. What the surgeon does to it depends on how much kidney can safely be kept.

Radical nephrectomy removes the kidney within Gerota's fascia. The oncological and haemostatic rule is the same: control the hilum first. The artery is found and divided before the vein, because clipping the vein first leaves a kidney still being pumped full with nowhere to drain. Then the organ is mobilised and removed intact through a muscle-splitting incision. Laparoscopic or robotic access is standard for tumours up to about 10 cm; open surgery through a flank, subcostal or chevron incision is kept for very large tumours, invasion of neighbours and tumour thrombus in the vena cava.

Partial nephrectomy removes the tumour with a rim of normal kidney and repairs the defect. It is preferred for small tumours and essential when the other kidney is absent or poor. The two kidneys together take about a fifth of the cardiac output, so each receives roughly a tenth through a single short artery, and cutting into one unprotected is not possible: the renal artery is clamped, the tumour is cut out in a bloodless field against the clock (warm ischaemia, which surgeons try to keep under about 25 minutes), the collecting system and vessels in the bed are oversewn, the cortex is closed over bolsters, and the clamp comes off. The robot has made this suturing quick enough that most partials are now robotic.

Nephroureterectomy, for urothelial cancer of the renal pelvis or ureter, adds the whole ureter and a cuff of bladder, which means a second operative field in the pelvis and often a change of position.

For you the operation has three features. The position: lateral, flexed over the break of the table, which obstructs the vena cava, hides the patient, and makes access something you set up once. The vessels: bleeding in this operation is either trivial or sudden and from the aorta, cava or their first branches. And the organ: the patient leaves with fewer nephrons, so the analgesic you would most like to use is the one the nephrologist would least like.

The pain logic depends on the route. Minimally invasive nephrectomy is a laparoscopic mechanism with a meaningful incision added: port sites, a 6-8 cm extraction wound, a large raw retroperitoneal bed, and diaphragmatic irritation. It is worse than a cholecystectomy and peaks on day 1 when the patient first sits and coughs. The open flank incision cuts latissimus, the obliques and transversus, often resects or springs a rib, and stretches the subcostal nerve; it is one of the more painful abdominal wall incisions, with a recognised tail of chronic flank pain and bulge.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment: the tumour and the other kidney
**Why:** Imaging defines the tumour's size, whether it reaches the renal vein or cava, and how the contralateral kidney looks. Split function matters more than total creatinine.
**Anaesthesia:** Read the scan report for vein and caval involvement, because it changes the case from a ward-bed operation to one needing echocardiography and a blood bank plan. Estimate what the patient will be left with: a radical nephrectomy in someone with an eGFR of 50 produces chronic kidney disease stage 3b or worse. Renal tumours bring anaemia, hypertension, hypercalcaemia and occasionally polycythaemia or abnormal liver enzymes without metastases. Large left-sided tumours and upper pole tumours predict splenic, pancreatic and pleural events.

### 2. Induction, access and neuraxial block
**Why:** Once the patient is lateral and flexed, the dependent arm is compressed and the upper arm is on a support beside the surgeon.
**Anaesthesia:** Intrathecal morphine goes in first, sitting, before induction (see the pain plan for the reasoning and the monitoring it commits the ward to). If local anaesthetic is added to the spinal, as it was in the trial, its sympathetic block arrives just before a lateral, flexed position that already obstructs the vena cava: keep the dose small or omit it, and have the vasopressor running before the turn. Two large cannulae, at least one in the arm that will be uppermost. An arterial line for every partial nephrectomy, for large or hilar tumours, for open surgery and for limited cardiac or renal reserve: the reasons are sudden bleeding and the need to hold a perfusion pressure, both of which are easier to manage beat to beat. Tracheal tube, temperature probe, urinary catheter. Start low-dose vasopressor early so that pressure is not chased with litres.

### 3. Lateral position and flexing the table
**Why:** Breaking the table opens the space between the costal margin and the iliac crest so the kidney can be reached, and for laparoscopy lets the bowel fall away medially.
**Anaesthesia:** Flexion with a kidney rest kinks and compresses the vena cava, more in the right lateral position; expect a fall in pressure as the table breaks and ask for less flexion if it does not recover with a small fluid bolus and vasopressor. Check the tube after the turn, keep the neck neutral, place an axillary roll, pad the dependent peroneal nerve and both arms, and secure the patient so that the table can be rolled. Long cases in heavy patients on a flexed table cause gluteal and flank rhabdomyolysis; ask for the flexion to be reduced once the ports are in.

### 4. Pneumoperitoneum and reflecting the colon
**Why:** Transperitoneal access is usual: the colon is reflected medially to open the retroperitoneum, with the spleen and pancreatic tail on the left, or the liver and duodenum on the right, mobilised out of the way. Some surgeons go straight into the retroperitoneum with a balloon dissector.
**Anaesthesia:** Lateral pneumoperitoneum is tolerated better than head-down, but the dependent lung is already compressed; expect a need for PEEP and a modest rise in airway pressure. The retroperitoneal approach has no peritoneal barrier to gas, so carbon dioxide absorption and subcutaneous emphysema are greater and can track to the neck and mediastinum. Splenic capsular tears on the left are a cause of steady unexplained ooze.

### 5. The hilum
**Why:** The ureter and gonadal vein are followed up to the renal vein, the artery is found behind it and divided with clips or a stapler, then the vein. On the right the vein is short and enters the cava directly; on the left it receives the adrenal, gonadal and a lumbar vein, any of which can tear.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step to watch. Have blood available as planned, the vasopressor running, and neuromuscular block reliable. A stapler misfire or avulsed lumbar vein is immediate, large-volume bleeding in a patient you cannot easily turn; the surgeon will compress, raise the gas pressure, and convert. Your part is to call for help and blood early, give volume through the lines you placed, and say what the pressure is doing. Venous injury under pneumoperitoneum can also present as gas embolism: a sudden fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide with hypotension. Major haemorrhage principles are in [the haemorrhage chapter](../a/a05-major-haemorrhage.html).

### 6. Radical nephrectomy: mobilisation and extraction
**Why:** With the hilum divided the kidney is freed within Gerota's fascia, the adrenal is taken or spared depending on the tumour, the ureter is divided, and the specimen is bagged and removed through a Pfannenstiel, iliac fossa or extended port incision.
**Anaesthesia:** Upper pole dissection is against the diaphragm. A pleural breach shows as rising airway pressure, a billowing diaphragm on the surgeon's screen, and falling saturation; it is usually repaired with a suture while you hold a sustained inflation, and rarely needs a drain. If the adrenal is removed on one side nothing follows; in a patient with a previous contralateral adrenalectomy, steroid cover does. After specimen removal the case is 20-30 minutes from finished.

### 7. Partial nephrectomy: clamp, excise, repair, unclamp
**Why:** The artery (sometimes artery and vein) is clamped with a bulldog, the tumour is cut out with a margin, the base is oversewn in one or two layers, and the clamp is released to test the repair. Some surgeons operate off-clamp or with selective clamping for small exophytic tumours.
**Anaesthesia:** Before the clamp: the kidney should be well perfused and the patient normovolaemic, with mean arterial pressure near baseline. Mannitol before clamping is a long-standing ritual; in a blinded randomised trial of 199 patients with normal function, 12.5 g made no difference to eGFR at six months, and there is no reason to offer it. Note the clamp time and call it at 15 and 20 minutes if asked. At unclamping the repair is tested under systemic pressure: do not let the patient be hypotensive at this moment, because a repair that is dry at a mean of 55 mmHg bleeds on the ward at 90. Expect 100-300 mL quickly from the bed and occasionally much more. The surgeon may lower the gas pressure to look for venous bleeding.

### 8. Open surgery: flank, subcostal and chevron incisions
**Why:** A flank incision through or below the 11th or 12th rib gives extraperitoneal access to the kidney; subcostal and chevron incisions give transperitoneal access to the great vessels for large tumours.
**Anaesthesia:** A thoracic epidural at T8-T10 placed before induction, or a paravertebral or erector spinae catheter on the operated side, earns its place here (see [truncal analgesia](../a/a02-truncal-blocks-epidural.html)). The pleura is opened often in rib-resecting approaches; the surgeon closes it over a catheter on suction while you inflate the lung. Blood loss and fluid shifts are those of a laparotomy.

### 9. Tumour thrombus in the vena cava
**Why:** Renal cell carcinoma grows along the renal vein into the cava as a tongue of tumour. Below the hepatic veins it is milked back and the cava clamped and opened; at or above the hepatic veins the liver must be mobilised and the cava controlled above it; into the right atrium, cardiopulmonary bypass may be needed.
**Anaesthesia:** A different operation and the high-risk variant of this card. Large-bore access above the diaphragm, arterial line, central line placed with care not to dislodge thrombus, rapid infuser, cell salvage, and transoesophageal echocardiography to define the top of the thrombus, watch for embolisation during handling, and guide filling when the cava is clamped; a series of 55 such cases recommends echocardiography and cardiac anaesthetic involvement for thrombus at or above the hepatic veins. Caval clamping removes a large part of venous return (a test clamp tells you whether the patient tolerates it), and thrombus embolisation presents as abrupt right heart failure. Critical care afterwards.

### 10. Closure and emergence
**Why:** The extraction wound is closed in layers; a drain is usual after partial nephrectomy to detect urine leak and bleeding.
**Anaesthesia:** Infiltrate or block if no neuraxial opioid was given. Flatten the table before closure to take tension off the wound. Paracetamol IV; decide on the NSAID with the surgeon and the pre-operative eGFR in mind. If the pleura was opened, get a chest radiograph in recovery. Shoulder-tip pain in recovery is diaphragmatic gas and responds poorly to opioid.

### 11. Ward
**Why:** The surgeon is watching haemoglobin, creatinine and drain output.
**Anaesthesia:** Urine output from one kidney, or from a kidney just made ischaemic, is an unreliable guide for the first hours; avoid chasing it with fluid boluses in a patient whose pressure is adequate. Bleeding after partial nephrectomy is early from the repair, or delayed by one to two weeks from a pseudoaneurysm, presenting with haematuria and flank pain and treated by embolisation.

## Pain plan

For laparoscopic and robotic nephrectomy the plan with the best support is intrathecal morphine before induction, paracetamol, dexamethasone, and oral opioid rescue, with an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor for 48 hours only when the remaining renal function is good and the surgeon agrees. The reasoning is evidential, not habitual. A network meta-analysis of 12 trials found that TAP and quadratus lumborum blocks reduce 24-hour opioid by about 11 mg of morphine equivalents against systemic analgesia, a statistically real effect that did not reach the authors' threshold for clinical importance, with pain score differences of less than one point. A randomised trial then set the quadratus lumborum block directly against intrathecal morphine: the block failed non-inferiority, with 9.5 mg more opioid, higher pain scores and lower satisfaction, and no excess of nausea, pruritus or respiratory events with the spinal. Truncal blocks are therefore the alternative when a spinal is declined or contraindicated, not the equal of it. A dose of 100-150 micrograms is the author's practice; the trial used up to 200 micrograms, with bupivacaine 7.5 mg. The technique carries a condition. Delayed respiratory depression is uncommon at these doses but is the reason the patient goes to a ward that records sedation score and respiratory rate hourly for about the first 24 hours under a local intrathecal opioid protocol, with oxygen and naloxone prescribed, no background or long-acting opioid, and no sedatives charted alongside the rescue opioid. The older patient, the patient with sleep apnoea or obesity, and the patient left with poor renal function get the lower end of the dose range, and where that monitoring cannot be provided a truncal block is the better plan.

For open flank, subcostal and chevron incisions the mechanism is abdominal wall and the plan moves up: a thoracic epidural for 48-72 hours, or a unilateral catheter technique, on the same base. This is the author's judgement extrapolated from laparotomy evidence; there is no PROSPECT guideline for nephrectomy.

The NSAID question deserves a decision and not a reflex. After radical nephrectomy with a normal contralateral kidney and normal eGFR, a short course is widely given. After partial nephrectomy (an ischaemic kidney with a fresh repair) or with an eGFR under 60, most would withhold it. Gabapentinoids add sedation for little gain.

Rebound is moderate: intrathecal morphine fades at 18-24 hours as the patient starts to mobilise, so the day 1 score is the peak and the oral regimen must already be running. Escalation triggers: flank pain with a falling haemoglobin is bleeding; pain with fever and high drain output is a urine leak; pleuritic pain with breathlessness is a missed pneumothorax or, later, embolism.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Lateral flexion hypotension. Caval kinking from the table break. Reduce the flexion before reaching for more fluid.
- The emergency variants. Trauma nephrectomy, a bleeding angiomyolipoma, and nephrectomy for emphysematous pyelonephritis or an infected obstructed kidney that has failed drainage. All are laparotomies in shocked or septic patients, managed as on the [emergency laparotomy card](emergency-laparotomy.html).
- Conversion. A laparoscopic case that converts for bleeding does so with the patient lateral and already behind on volume. The pain plan converts too.
- Solitary kidney partial nephrectomy. Every minute of clamp time and every episode of hypotension is paid for in dialysis risk; arterial line, tight pressure control, no nephrotoxins, high dependency care.
- Nephroureterectomy. Longer, two fields, a bladder cuff with a catheter for a week, and sometimes intravesical chemotherapy afterwards.
- Simple nephrectomy for a non-functioning or chronically infected kidney. Dense inflammatory adhesions make it harder and bloodier than a cancer operation; xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis is the worst.
- Phaeochromocytoma is not on this card; see [adrenalectomy](splenectomy-and-adrenalectomy-including-phaeochromocytoma.html).
- Living donor nephrectomy. A healthy patient having the same operation for someone else; covered with [transplantation](renal-transplantation-and-donor-nephrectomy.html).
- Chronic flank pain. Subcostal nerve entrapment and flank bulge after open incisions; early neuropathic features are worth flagging.
- Rhabdomyolysis. Long lateral cases in muscular or obese patients; dark urine in a patient with one kidney matters.

## Key sources

The analgesic evidence for laparoscopic nephrectomy is recent and reasonably consistent: fascial plane blocks help a little, intrathecal morphine helps more. It comes from single-centre trials and a network meta-analysis of small studies, and the dose of morphine is not settled. The mannitol trial is the one high-quality piece of renal protection evidence and is negative. Management of caval thrombus is supported by case series only. Advice on perfusion pressure at unclamping and on NSAID selection is reasoning from mechanism and the author's judgement.
