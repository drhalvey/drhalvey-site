## What the surgeon is doing and why

Everything on this card is done through the urethra with no incision, and the operations form a ladder. Cystoscopy looks at the urethra and bladder: flexible, under lidocaine gel, for surveillance and stent removal, or rigid, under anaesthesia, when something has to be done (biopsy, diathermy, a retrograde pyelogram, a wire up a ureter). Ureteroscopy climbs the next rung: a semi-rigid scope passed over a guidewire into the ureter for a stone in the lower or middle third, or a flexible scope steered into the renal pelvis and calyces for stones in the kidney (retrograde intrarenal surgery). The stone is fragmented or dusted with a holmium or thulium laser fibre and the pieces are basketed or left to pass. A ureteric stent, a soft tube with a coil in the renal pelvis and a coil in the bladder, is the common end point: it is placed to drain an obstructed kidney, to protect a ureter that has just been instrumented and will swell, or exchanged every few months in the patient who lives with one.

The surgical problem is working inside a tube 3-4 mm wide that moves with respiration, through a scope that needs continuous irrigation to see. Three consequences follow, and they are the whole anaesthetic.

First, the irrigation is pressurised and the kidney is a closed, thin-walled, highly perfused chamber. Once pressure in the renal pelvis rises above roughly 30-40 mmHg, fluid and whatever is in it flow backwards into the renal veins and lymphatics. If the urine or the stone carries bacteria, that backflow is a bolus of endotoxin and organisms into the circulation. This is why post-ureteroscopy urosepsis exists (5.0% in a pooled 5,597 patients), why it is commoner with a positive culture, a pre-existing stent and a long case, and why surgeons use an access sheath to let irrigant drain around the scope.

Second, the ureter is fragile and the instruments are rigid or sharp. A cough, a buck or a leg movement with a semi-rigid scope or a laser fibre in the ureter can perforate or avulse it. The surgeon is not asking for much (the stimulus is modest for long periods, then intense at ureteric dilatation), but what is asked for is absolute.

Third, nothing is cut, so there is nothing for a nerve block to do. The pain is visceral: distension of the ureter and renal pelvis during the case, ureteric spasm and oedema afterwards, and above all the stent. Its lower coil sits on the trigone, the most sensitive part of the bladder, and because it holds the ureteric orifice open, every detrusor contraction sends a pressure wave to the kidney. Urgency, frequency, haematuria and loin pain on voiding last as long as the stent does. That is why the trajectory on this card does not fall to zero by day 7, and why the analgesics that work are an NSAID, an alpha blocker and an antimuscarinic.

## Steps

### 1. Triage before the list: culture, obstruction, fever
**Why:** The same booking ("cystoscopy, retrograde, stent") covers a well patient having a stent changed and a septic patient with pus behind an obstructing stone. The surgeon's plan for the second is drainage only, as fast as possible, with definitive stone surgery weeks later once the kidney is sterile.
**Anaesthesia:** Read the urine culture and the observations before anything else. A positive culture should have been treated; if it has not, expect the surgeon to give a targeted antibiotic and to shorten the case. Fever, tachycardia, rigors or a rising lactate with an obstructed system is urosepsis: large-bore access, blood cultures and antibiotics before induction, an arterial line and a noradrenaline infusion ready if the pressure is already soft, a tracheal tube, and a critical care bed asked for before the scope goes in, because the deterioration typically comes in the hours after the kidney is instrumented, not on the table. Check renal function and potassium in the patient with bilateral obstruction or a solitary kidney.

### 2. Choice of anaesthetic and airway
**Why:** The surgeon needs a still patient and, for flexible intrarenal work, a kidney that is not swinging 2-3 cm with each breath while a 200 micrometre laser fibre is aimed at a 6 mm stone.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway suits most cystoscopy, stent work and short ureteroscopy. A tracheal tube and neuromuscular block earn their place for long flexible intrarenal surgery, where controlled small tidal volumes or brief apnoea on request make the surgeon's targeting easier, and for the patient with reflux or obesity in head-down lithotomy. Spinal anaesthesia is a sound alternative: in a propensity-matched comparison of intrarenal stone surgery, stone-free rates were 70.0% under spinal and 67.5% under general anaesthesia with no conversions, and a retrospective series of 246 proximal ureteric stones found the same equivalence. The block needs to reach T10 for the bladder and T8 for the ureter and kidney; a block that stops at L1 leaves the patient feeling renal pelvic distension. The awake patient cannot be made apnoeic, so warn the surgeon. Flexible cystoscopy and stent removal need only lidocaine gel and a few minutes for it to work.

### 3. Lithotomy on the X-ray table
**Why:** The perineum must be at the table edge and the image intensifier must swing over the abdomen. For ureteroscopy the leg on the operative side is often dropped and the other raised to straighten the ureter.
**Anaesthesia:** Lift and lower both legs together to protect the lumbar spine and hips, pad the common peroneal nerve at the fibular head, and avoid hip flexion beyond 90 degrees. Short cases forgive a great deal; a two-hour stone case in asymmetric lithotomy does not, and the details are in [the positioning chapter](../a/a14-positioning.html). The table is usually head-down a little, which raises airway pressure through a supraglottic device in the obese. Lead apron on before the patient is asleep.

### 4. Cystoscopy and retrograde pyelogram
**Why:** The bladder is inspected, the ureteric orifice found, and contrast injected up the ureter to map the stone, the stricture or the filling defect. A guidewire is then passed to the renal pelvis; it is the lifeline for every later step.
**Anaesthesia:** Urethral instrumentation and bladder filling are the first stimuli. In a lightly anaesthetised patient on a supraglottic airway this is where laryngospasm happens, so be deep before the scope goes in, not in response to it. Bladder distension occasionally produces a vagal bradycardia. In the patient with a spinal cord lesion at or above T6 the same distension triggers autonomic dysreflexia: severe hypertension with reflex bradycardia. A spinal prevents it reliably by blocking the afferent limb; a general anaesthetic prevents it only if it is deep, and a small epidural series reports dysreflexia under general anaesthesia that did not recur with neuraxial block. Have a short-acting vasodilator drawn up, and ask the surgeon to empty the bladder as the first treatment.

### 5. Ureteric dilatation and access sheath
**Why:** The intramural ureter is the narrowest point. It is dilated with a balloon or graduated dilators, and for flexible work a 10-14 Fr access sheath is slid over the wire to the upper ureter so the scope can pass repeatedly and irrigant can drain around it, which keeps the pressure in the renal pelvis down.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the most stimulating minute of the case and the one where a ureter is torn if the patient moves. Deepen or give an opioid bolus before it, and if the patient is paralysed make sure the block has not worn off. A tight ureter that will not accept the sheath is a reason for the surgeon to place a stent and come back in two weeks, once the ureter has passively dilated; the case ends abruptly, so do not have long-acting drugs running.

### 6. Ureteroscopy and laser lithotripsy
**Why:** The stone is fragmented into pieces small enough to basket or dusted into particles that wash out. The laser's energy is absorbed within a fraction of a millimetre in water, so it is safe unless the fibre is touching urothelium when it fires.
**Anaesthesia:** Stillness, and attention to the clock and the irrigation. Hand-pumped or pressure-bagged saline pushed through a scope in a closed system can generate pressures far above the backflow threshold, and urosepsis risk rises with operating time; most surgeons stop at around 90 minutes and stage the rest. Absorption of even isotonic saline is possible: a 25-minute stent change with pressurised irrigation in a tetraplegic patient produced symptomatic hyponatraemia and acidosis four hours later. Warm the fluid, since cold irrigant through the renal pelvis cools the core quickly. If the surgeon asks for the ventilator to be paused, give 30-60 seconds of apnoea at a time and say when you are restarting. A sudden rise in airway pressure, a tense abdomen or shoulder-tip pain in an awake patient means irrigant is outside the collecting system.

### 7. Stent insertion, exchange or removal
**Why:** After ureteroscopy the ureter swells; a stent holds it open for a few days to two weeks. Some surgeons leave a stent on a string for the patient to pull at home, some leave none after an uncomplicated case, and some place a ureteric catheter overnight instead. Long-term stents are exchanged every 3-12 months.
**Anaesthesia:** A stent exchange is 5-10 minutes of intense stimulation at the start and almost nothing after; plan for a patient who is awake two minutes after the scope comes out. The encrusted, forgotten stent is the exception and can become a long case. Give the NSAID now if the kidney function allows (rectal or IV), because ureteric spasm is prostaglandin-driven and the stent symptoms start as soon as the patient voids.

### 8. Emergence and recovery
**Why:** The surgeon wants the patient voiding and home.
**Anaesthesia:** Emergence is uneventful. Recovery problems are three. Loin pain from ureteric spasm or a stent: NSAID first, small opioid doses second. Bladder spasm and urgency in a patient who has just had a scope and a stent: recognise it as detrusor irritation and not incisional pain, since opioids do little and an antimuscarinic helps. And the early signs of bacteraemia: a rigor, a temperature and a pressure drifting down in recovery after stone surgery is the start of urosepsis until proved otherwise, and the patient stays, has cultures, antibiotics and fluid, and is not discharged because the list has finished.

### 9. Discharge with a stent
**Why:** The stent is doing its job while it irritates the bladder; the surgeon's plan for its removal (string at home, flexible cystoscopy in clinic, or a second procedure) sets how long the symptoms last.
**Anaesthesia:** Send the patient home with the mechanism explained: frequency, urgency, blood in the urine and an ache in the flank on voiding are the stent, not a complication. In a meta-analysis of 11 randomised trials an alpha blocker and an antimuscarinic each reduced stent symptoms and the combination was better than either alone; the prescription is usually the surgeon's, but a patient discharged without one will be back. Fever or rigors at home is the instruction to return.

## Pain plan

There is no wound, so the plan is systemic and aimed at the three visceral mechanisms. For ureteric spasm and oedema: an NSAID, given before the end of the case and continued for two to three days where renal function, age and the surgeon's view of bleeding permit, with paracetamol alongside. For the stent: an alpha blocker to relax the lower ureter and trigone and an antimuscarinic to damp detrusor contractions, for as long as the stent is in. For the breakthrough: a few doses of oral opioid, which most ureteroscopy patients use on day 0 and 1 and few need beyond that. Diagnostic cystoscopy alone is a score of 1 and needs paracetamol if anything; the score of 2 on this card is the stented ureteroscopy patient.

Neuraxial anaesthesia is an option for the operation, not an analgesic strategy: it wears off before the stent symptoms peak. Its real indications are the patient with a cord lesion at or above T6, the patient with bad lungs, and the patient who prefers it. Intrathecal opioid is not worth its side effects for a day case of this size. Nerve blocks have no role; the author's judgement is that the fashion for adding fascial plane blocks to endoscopic stone surgery treats a pain the operation does not produce.

Escalation triggers: loin pain that is severe and unrelenting after ureteroscopy with no stent is an obstructed ureter (oedema or a fragment) and is a surgical problem; pain with fever is infection behind obstruction; pain with a rigid abdomen is extravasation or perforation. None of these is treated by increasing the opioid.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The infected obstructed kidney. The emergency variant of this card and the one that kills. Drain only (retrograde stent, or percutaneous nephrostomy by radiology if the patient is too unstable to anaesthetise or the stent will not pass), invasive pressure monitoring and vasopressor early, critical care afterwards. The pressure falls after decompression, not before.
- Pre-stented patients and positive cultures. The two strongest predictors of post-operative urosepsis (odds ratios 3.94 and 3.56). A stent-change list is a list of colonised urinary tracts.
- Spinal cord injury. Frequent attenders for stent and bladder stone work. Autonomic dysreflexia with lesions at or above T6; spinal anaesthesia is the reliable prevention, with latex allergy, pressure areas, spasms and a full bowel as the supporting problems.
- Pregnancy. Stones in pregnancy are stented or have ureteroscopy with minimal fluoroscopy; left tilt after 20 weeks, and obstetric advice on monitoring.
- The solitary or transplanted kidney. Any obstruction is anuria; check potassium, and keep the NSAID out of the plan.
- Long flexible cases. Beyond 90 minutes the sepsis risk, the fluid absorption and the lithotomy injuries all climb; the surgeon stages the rest.
- Ureteric perforation or avulsion. Presents to you as a sudden change of plan: a stent if the wire is still across, an open or laparoscopic repair if it is not.
- Laser safety. Eye protection for everyone in the room, including the patient's eyes taped and covered.
- The stent on a string. Pulled out accidentally in recovery by a confused patient or a catheter bag change; tape it to the thigh.
- Bladder stone lithotripsy (cystolitholapaxy). Same card, longer, more irrigation, and in the cord-injured patient the highest dysreflexia risk of all.

## Key sources

The evidence here is observational and mostly urological. The urosepsis meta-analysis is the most useful single paper for the anaesthetist because it identifies who deteriorates. Spinal against general anaesthesia has been compared only in cohorts, which agree that stone clearance is the same. Stent symptom pharmacology is supported by many small randomised trials of modest quality that point the same way. The autonomic dysreflexia advice rests on physiology and case series, and the preference for spinal anaesthesia in high cord lesions is consensus practice, not trial evidence. There is no PROSPECT guideline for endoscopic urology.
