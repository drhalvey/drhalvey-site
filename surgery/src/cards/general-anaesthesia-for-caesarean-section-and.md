## What the surgeon is doing and why

The surgery is the caesarean section of the [elective](elective-caesarean-section.html) and [emergency](emergency-caesarean-section-by-category.html) cards. This card is about the anaesthetic that is now used for a small minority of caesareans, which is exactly why it is dangerous: it is done rarely, usually at night, usually in a hurry, by someone who may have done very few.

The reasons a caesarean ends up under general anaesthesia fall into four groups. There is no time: a category 1 delivery for a bradycardia that has not recovered, a cord prolapse, a major abruption. Neuraxial block is contraindicated: coagulopathy, a platelet count too low, therapeutic anticoagulation, uncorrected hypovolaemia, sepsis with shock. The block has failed: an epidural that will not extend, a spinal that has not risen, or pain during surgery. Or the mother declines to be awake. Each of these arrives with its own problem attached (a compromised fetus, a bleeding mother, a patient who is already in pain and frightened), so the general anaesthetic is rarely given to the well patient.

The surgeon adapts in one respect: because every minute of anaesthesia before delivery transfers drug to the fetus, the abdomen is prepared and draped and the surgeon stands scalpel in hand before induction, and the incision is made as soon as the airway is confirmed. From then the operation is the same, with a slightly less contracted uterus because volatile agents relax myometrium in proportion to dose.

The pregnant airway at term is harder for anatomical and physiological reasons. Capillary engorgement swells the mucosa of the pharynx and larynx, more so after hours of labour, pushing, pre-eclampsia or intravenous fluid. Enlarged breasts obstruct the laryngoscope handle. Functional residual capacity is down by a fifth and oxygen consumption is up by more than that, so apnoea is tolerated for two or three minutes at best. Gastric emptying is delayed in labour and after opioids, and the lower oesophageal sphincter is lax. The literature review of failed obstetric intubation found the incidence unchanged over four decades at about 1 in 390 general anaesthetics; the national surveillance study found 1 in 224. What has changed is the response, from waking the patient to continuing on a supraglottic airway.

The pain logic is that of any caesarean, less the neuraxial opioid. That gap, the first 12-24 hours, is what the pain plan on this card has to fill. General airway principles are in [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html) and the physiology in [obstetric essentials](../a/a09-obstetric-essentials.html).

## Steps

### 1. The decision and the 30-second brief
**Why:** The obstetrician has declared a category and an indication; the choice of general anaesthesia is yours.
**Anaesthesia:** Confirm that it is still necessary on arrival in theatre: a fetal heart that has recovered buys time for a spinal. If it is, say aloud to the team the three things the obstetric airway guideline asks to be settled in advance: who is coming to help and how they are called, what the rescue device is, and whether a failed intubation would mean waking the mother or proceeding. The last is a joint decision with the obstetrician, weighing the factors the guideline sets out in its table: maternal condition, fetal condition, the anaesthetist's experience, obesity, surgical factors, aspiration risk, whether neuraxial anaesthesia or an awake airway would be feasible as an alternative, and how good the airway is with the rescue device. For a dying fetus or a bleeding mother the answer is proceed; for a category 3 case in a woman with a good back it is wake.

### 2. Position, antacid and monitoring
**Why:** The surgeon preps and drapes with the patient awake so that incision follows intubation immediately.
**Anaesthesia:** Sodium citrate 0.3 M 30 mL orally now; intravenous H2 antagonist or proton pump inhibitor if not already given, though it will not act in time for induction and is for extubation. Ramp to 20-30 degrees head-up with the ear level with the sternal notch: this lengthens safe apnoea, improves the view and takes the breasts out of the way. Keep the left tilt. Full monitoring including capnography and a nerve stimulator; suction on and under the pillow; videolaryngoscope switched on; tube with a stylet or bougie; second-generation supraglottic airway opened; scalpel, bougie and 6.0 mm tube within reach. A trained assistant for cricoid pressure, who knows they may be asked to reduce or release it.

### 3. Pre-oxygenation
**Why:** Nothing surgical; the drapes are going on.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step that buys the margin for everything that follows, and it is commonly inadequate. In the crossover study of women at term, standard facemask pre-oxygenation reached an end-tidal oxygen of 90% in fewer than half; high-flow nasal oxygen did better at 71%. The practical conclusions are a tight two-handed seal, a fresh gas flow of at least 10 L/min, three minutes of tidal breathing or eight vital-capacity breaths if there is no time, and watching the end-tidal oxygen number rather than the clock. The obstetric airway guideline advises nasal cannulae at 5 L/min under the mask from the start, turned up after induction to keep oxygen flowing during apnoea. Do not skip this for urgency: a fetus with a bradycardia is not helped by a mother with a saturation of 80%.

### 4. Rapid sequence induction
**Why:** The surgeon is waiting for the word.
**Anaesthesia:** Propofol 2-2.5 mg/kg has replaced thiopental in Australian practice, and the awareness cohort gives a reason beyond availability: thiopental and ketamine inductions were associated with more awareness, probably through under-dosing and unfamiliar syringes. Give a full dose based on actual body weight in the well patient; use ketamine 1-1.5 mg/kg or a reduced propofol dose in the hypovolaemic one. Suxamethonium 1-1.5 mg/kg, or rocuronium 1-1.2 mg/kg provided sugammadex 16 mg/kg is in the room and someone other than you can draw it up. Opioid before induction is not routine because it crosses to the fetus, but is mandatory in pre-eclampsia and significant cardiac disease (see [that card](pre-eclampsia-eclampsia-and-the-parturient.html)), with the neonatal team informed. Cricoid pressure is applied at 10 N awake and 30 N after loss of consciousness. The guideline supports gentle mask ventilation after induction, with inflation pressures under 20 cmH2O, to extend the time to desaturation.

### 5. Laryngoscopy: the first attempt is the best attempt
**Why:** The surgeon cannot cut until the airway is secure and anaesthesia confirmed.
**Anaesthesia:** Videolaryngoscope first, a 6.5-7.0 mm tube, and a bougie or stylet loaded. If the view is poor, the commonest fixable cause is the cricoid hand: ask for it to be reduced or released. The guideline allows two attempts, with a third only by a more experienced colleague, because each attempt adds oedema and bleeding to a mucosa that had little reserve, and the deaths in the literature review came from hypoxaemia and aspiration during prolonged attempts. Confirm with capnography, tell the surgeon to start, and tell the midwife the time.

### 6. Failed intubation: declare it and oxygenate
**Why:** The surgeon stops and waits; an assistant maintains uterine displacement.
**Anaesthesia:** Say the words "failed intubation" so that the help you arranged in step 1 comes. The priority changes from intubating to oxygenating.

```
 2 attempts failed -> declare, call for help
        |
 oxygenate: second-generation supraglottic airway (2 attempts,
 cricoid released for insertion) or two-person facemask with adjuncts
        |
   oxygenation adequate              oxygenation failing
        |                                   |
 wake or proceed (step 7)        declare CICO: 100% oxygen, full
                                 paralysis, scalpel cricothyroidotomy;
                                 perimortem caesarean if arrest
```

In the national case-control study a laryngeal mask rescued the airway in most of the 57 cases, there was one surgical airway, and nobody died or suffered hypoxic brain injury. A second-generation device with a gastric channel is preferred so that the stomach can be drained.

### 7. Wake or proceed
**Why:** If the mother can be oxygenated, the obstetrician can deliver the baby in two minutes; if she is woken, delivery waits for a spinal or an awake intubation.
**Anaesthesia:** Apply the decision made in step 1 unless the facts have changed. To proceed: keep the supraglottic device, maintain cricoid pressure if it does not impair ventilation, keep her paralysed and deep, ask for the most experienced surgeon and minimal fundal pressure (it raises gastric pressure against an unprotected airway), pass a gastric tube down the drain channel, and consider intubating through the device with a fibrescope once the baby is out and help has arrived. To wake: reverse rocuronium with sugammadex or let suxamethonium wear off, head-up or left lateral, keep oxygen on, expect laryngospasm and a period of can't-ventilate on the way up, and then choose between a spinal and an awake intubation. The literature review found that since the late 1990s the majority of reported cases have proceeded, with outcomes no worse than waking.

### 8. Can't intubate, can't oxygenate
**Why:** There is no surgical step until there is oxygen, with one exception: if the mother arrests, delivering the baby improves the chance of resuscitating her.
**Anaesthesia:** If neither the supraglottic device nor the facemask oxygenates, declare it, give full neuromuscular block if not already given (to exclude laryngospasm and optimise the last mask attempt), and perform a scalpel cricothyroidotomy: transverse stab through the cricothyroid membrane, bougie, 6.0 mm cuffed tube. The review found front-of-neck access in about 3 per 100,000 general anaesthetics for caesarean, usually done late and with poor outcomes; the lesson drawn in the guideline is that the delay is the lethal part. If maternal cardiac arrest occurs, a perimortem caesarean starts immediately on the theatre table without waiting for the airway.

### 9. Knife to delivery: depth and the awareness window
**Why:** The surgeon is moving as fast as safety allows; uterine incision to delivery should be under three minutes because uterine incision disturbs placental flow.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the period of greatest awareness risk: the induction dose is redistributing, the volatile has not yet equilibrated, and the temptation is to keep it low for the baby. In the prospective cohort the incidence was 1 in 256 on direct questioning, three-quarters of episodes were at induction or emergence, and most of the women were distressed by it. Use overpressure to reach an end-tidal volatile concentration of 1 MAC (with 50% nitrous oxide contributing if used) before the skin incision; pregnancy reduces requirement by about a third, and that is your margin, not a reason to go lower. Processed electroencephalography is reasonable. Ventilate to an end-tidal carbon dioxide of 30-32 mmHg. Tell the neonatal team the induction time and any opioid given.

### 10. After delivery: change the anaesthetic
**Why:** With the cord clamped the surgeon needs a contracted uterus and haemostasis.
**Anaesthesia:** Give the uterotonic as on the emergency card (oxytocin 3 IU slowly and an infusion, or carbetocin 100 micrograms). Now give the opioid that was withheld: fentanyl 100-200 micrograms and morphine 10 mg IV in divided doses, or equivalent. Reduce the volatile to 0.5-0.75 MAC with nitrous oxide or a propofol infusion to limit uterine relaxation, without going so low that the awareness problem returns; if the uterus is atonic, changing to propofol maintenance is one of the treatments. Antibiotic if not already given, paracetamol 1 g IV, an NSAID unless contraindicated, dexamethasone and ondansetron. Check the neuromuscular monitor.

### 11. Closure, blocks and extubation
**Why:** Closure is standard; the surgeon may infiltrate the wound.
**Anaesthesia:** Bilateral TAP or quadratus lumborum blocks before emergence with ropivacaine up to 3 mg/kg in total, using booking weight; or ask the surgeon for wound infiltration or a wound catheter. Pass an orogastric tube, empty the stomach and remove it. Extubation is the second high-risk moment and the commonest time for aspiration in a patient who was intubated without difficulty: reverse fully against a quantitative train-of-four ratio, wait for her to be awake and obeying, and extubate head-up or in the left lateral position. If intubation was difficult or the airway is swollen, test for a cuff leak and have the same help present as for induction.

### 12. Recovery and the follow-up visit
**Why:** The obstetric priorities are those of any emergency caesarean: tone, loss, and reuniting mother and baby.
**Anaesthesia:** Start the patient-controlled analgesia or the regular oral regimen before the blocks have had a chance to wear off. Visit the next day and ask directly what she remembers between going to sleep and waking up; awareness is under-reported unless asked about, and early acknowledgment and referral change the psychological outcome. Explain why general anaesthesia was needed. If intubation was difficult or failed, tell her, write to her general practitioner, and record an airway alert, because her next anaesthetic may be another emergency caesarean.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is the abdominal wall incision and the contracting uterus of any caesarean, and the difference is that the 12-24 hours normally covered by neuraxial morphine are uncovered. PROSPECT excluded caesareans under general anaesthesia from its review, but its recommendation for women who have not had intrathecal opioid applies by direct analogy: wound infiltration, continuous wound infusion, or TAP or quadratus lumborum blocks, on a base of paracetamol, an NSAID and dexamethasone, with opioid for rescue.

Fascial plane blocks treat the wall and not the uterus. That is why they are a substitute for intrathecal morphine and not its equal, and why the NSAID matters even more here: it is the only regular drug acting on afterpains. A quadratus lumborum block may add some visceral cover through paravertebral spread; the evidence that this is clinically important after caesarean is not strong enough to make it the required choice, and the author's default is whichever of the two blocks the operator performs reliably in a supine patient at the end of an emergency. The defensible default for the first night is intravenous opioid patient-controlled analgesia, converted to oral opioid the next morning; a woman with a short, straightforward operation and good blocks can go straight to regular oral opioid for 24 hours. A labour epidural catheter still in place can be given morphine 2-3 mg if clotting and platelets are normal, which restores the standard plan.

Rebound is moderate and comes at 12-18 hours when a single-shot block recedes, usually in the early hours of the morning. Escalation triggers are those of the emergency caesarean card. One is particular to general anaesthesia: a sore throat is expected, but pain on swallowing with neck or chest pain, fever or surgical emphysema after a difficult intubation is an airway or oesophageal injury and needs imaging.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The high-risk variants. Morbid obesity, pre-eclampsia with facial oedema, a known difficult airway, and haemorrhage. For the first three, an early labour epidural is the prevention; for a known difficult airway with time available, an awake videolaryngoscopic or fibreoptic intubation is the planned route, and for one without time, the decision analysis suggests rapid sequence induction with a videolaryngoscope remains acceptable, with ultimate failure of airway control estimated at about 21 per 100,000.
- Invasive monitoring and critical care. An arterial line before induction for severe pre-eclampsia, cardiac disease and active haemorrhage; critical care after aspiration, a failed intubation with hypoxia, a surgical airway, or massive transfusion.
- Aspiration. Occurred in 8% of failed intubations in the review and the national study. If it happens: head-down, suction, intubate, bronchoscopy for particulate matter, and ventilate; antibiotics and steroids are not routine.
- Conversion from a failed neuraxial block mid-surgery. The abdomen is open, the patient is in pain and may have a partial high block: pre-oxygenate properly even so, reduce the induction dose if she is hypotensive, and warn the surgeon to stop until the tube is in.
- Magnesium prolongs non-depolarising block; suxamethonium fasciculations may be absent.
- Suxamethonium apnoea and anaphylaxis present at the worst time; rocuronium with sugammadex avoids the first and not the second.
- Uterine atony from volatile agents is dose-dependent and reversible; above 1 MAC after delivery is avoidable.
- The neonate. General anaesthesia is associated with lower early Apgar scores, mostly through the indication and partly through drug transfer; an opioid-based induction requires someone ready to ventilate the baby.
- Skill decay. The falling rate of general anaesthesia means few trainees have done many; simulation and using a videolaryngoscope for every obstetric intubation are the practical answers.
- Mother and partner. Neither sees the birth. Photographs by the midwife and early skin-to-skin contact in recovery matter to how the anaesthetic is remembered.

## Key sources

The airway management here follows a national guideline built on observational data, case series and expert consensus; no randomised trial of wake against proceed exists or could. Incidence figures come from a 45-year literature review and a two-year national surveillance study and differ by a factor of nearly two, which reflects definition and reporting. Awareness data are from a large prospective cohort with direct questioning. Pre-oxygenation evidence is from volunteers at term, not from emergency inductions. The analgesic plan is extrapolated from the PROSPECT recommendation for women without intrathecal opioid, and the opioid doses, the choice between TAP and quadratus lumborum block and the volatile targets after delivery are standard teaching and the author's judgement.
