## What the surgeon is doing and why

An infected tooth root, most often a lower molar, breaks through the thin bone of the jaw. Where the pus goes depends on where the root apex lies in relation to the muscle attachments. Above the buccinator or mylohyoid it points into the mouth as a gum boil: a vestibular abscess that is drained through the mucosa under local anaesthesia. Below those attachments it enters the fascial spaces of the face and neck: buccal, submasseteric, pterygomandibular, submandibular, sublingual, submental, and from there backwards to the parapharyngeal and retropharyngeal spaces, which lead down to the mediastinum.

Ludwig angina is the name for a rapidly spreading cellulitis of the submandibular, sublingual and submental spaces on both sides. The floor of the mouth is a closed box bounded by the mandible in front and at the sides and the hyoid below. Oedema and pus inside it have one direction to go: the tongue is pushed up against the palate and back into the oropharynx. The neck in front becomes brawny and hard. The patient sits forward, drools because swallowing is too painful, speaks with a muffled voice, and holds the airway open with active muscle tone. Stridor is late.

The operation is simple. For a single-space abscess the surgeon makes a small incision in the skin below the mandible (or in the mouth), spreads bluntly with a clip into the space, lets the pus out, irrigates, leaves a corrugated or tube drain and removes the causal tooth. For multi-space infection there are several incisions with through-and-through drains connecting the spaces across the midline; in Ludwig angina there may be little pus and the purpose is decompression.

The pain logic is unusual: the disease is the pain and the operation relieves it. Afterwards there are skin incisions and drains in inflamed tissue. That is a mild-to-moderate, short, systemically treated pain. Everything difficult about this card happens before the knife. In the fourth national audit of airway complications in the United Kingdom, airway management was judged good in only one in five of the major events reviewed, and obstructing head and neck pathology managed with an optimistic general anaesthetic is the pattern the audit was written to stop.

## Steps

### 1. Grading the airway at the bedside
**Why:** The surgeon books "incision and drainage of dental abscess" for everything from a swollen cheek to Ludwig angina; the booking does not grade the airway.
**Anaesthesia:** See the patient yourself, early. Sort into three.

```
A. Localised (buccal, canine space)      Mouth opening fair, floor of
                                         mouth soft, swallowing normal
B. Trismus, single deep space            Opening under 2-3 cm, floor of
   (submasseteric, pterygomandibular,    mouth soft, tongue mobile,
   one submandibular)                    swallows saliva, lies flat
C. Multi-space, Ludwig, parapharyngeal   Raised firm floor of mouth,
                                         tongue elevated, drooling,
                                         muffled voice, cannot lie
                                         flat, neck swelling
```

Group A is a routine anaesthetic. Group C is an awake tracheal intubation or an awake tracheostomy. Group B is where judgement is needed, and the information that settles it is the CT (airway calibre and deviation, which spaces, gas) and a flexible nasendoscopy done sitting up by the surgeon or by you, which shows the tongue base, the pharyngeal walls and the larynx. Fever, tachycardia, rising lactate and a swelling that is advancing by the hour move a patient up a group.

### 2. The team plan before anything else
**Why:** The surgeon is the rescue airway and has to be in the room and ready, not on the way.
**Anaesthesia:** For groups B and C the plan is spoken and agreed with the whole team before the patient is touched, as the awake intubation guideline requires: the primary technique, who does what, what counts as failure, and what happens then (postponement is not available here, so the options are front-of-neck airway by the surgeon, or a high-risk general anaesthetic that everyone understands to be a last resort). Do it in theatre, with a second anaesthetist, the surgeon scrubbed or gloved, the tracheostomy set open, and the cricothyroid membrane marked. In a swollen neck find the membrane with ultrasound and mark it with the head in the position you will use; a neck in which the membrane cannot be found or reached is an argument for an elective awake tracheostomy under local anaesthesia as the first plan.

### 3. Preparing the patient
**Why:** The surgeon needs the infection treated and the patient fit to survive the next hour; drainage is urgent but minutes spent here are well spent.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient stays sitting. No sedative premedication on the ward. Antibiotics confirmed given; fluid resuscitation started, because these patients have not swallowed for a day or two and are septic; dexamethasone 8 mg IV for oedema; an antisialagogue (glycopyrronium 0.2 mg IV) twenty minutes ahead to dry the mucosa for topical anaesthesia. Nebulised adrenaline buys time in a patient with stridor while theatre is prepared. High-flow nasal oxygen goes on as the patient arrives and stays on throughout: the guideline reports desaturation in 12-16% of awake intubations with low-flow oxygen and 0-1.5% with high flow. Explain the procedure plainly; a cooperative patient is the largest factor in success.

### 4. Topicalisation and sedation
**Why:** The patient must keep breathing and keep their airway tone until a cuffed tube is confirmed in the trachea.
**Anaesthesia:** Topical lidocaine to the nose, oropharynx and larynx by atomiser and spray-as-you-go, tested before proceeding, with a total dose not exceeding 9 mg/kg lean body weight. Inflamed, acidic mucosa takes local anaesthetic poorly and absorbs it quickly, so expect patchier anaesthesia than usual and stay well within the limit. Nasal vasoconstrictor to the wider nostril. Sedation is minimal or none: the guideline advises a single agent, remifentanil or dexmedetomidine, ideally run by a second anaesthetist, and never as a substitute for topical anaesthesia. In the largest series of awake bronchoscopic intubation in deep neck infection the only notable complications apart from mild epistaxis were four oversedations with hypoxaemia. A remifentanil effect-site target of 1-3 ng/mL is the author's ceiling, and for a patient who cannot lie flat the right dose is often none.

### 5. Awake tracheal intubation
**Why:** A tube placed with the patient awake converts an obstructing airway into a secure one before any tone is lost.
**Anaesthesia:** Nasal flexible bronchoscopy is the usual route, because the mouth does not open and the tongue fills it: in the case series of 26 patients, 19 of 25 successful intubations were nasal. Sitting up, operator facing the patient. A small tube (6.0-6.5 mm, reinforced or a soft-tipped design) loaded on the scope. Expect a displaced, asymmetrical view: the tongue base pushed back, one pharyngeal wall bulging, the larynx off the midline and oedematous. Go slowly and do not push through a bulge, because rupturing an abscess into the pharynx of a patient with topicalised cords is aspiration of pus. An awake videolaryngoscopic intubation is an alternative only when mouth opening allows a blade. Limit attempts to three, plus one by a more experienced operator. Confirm with the two-point check, tracheal rings seen through the scope and a capnography trace, and only then induce anaesthesia.

### 6. If the awake intubation fails or cannot be done
**Why:** The surgeon can place a tracheostomy under local anaesthesia in a sitting, breathing patient; they cannot do it well in a hypoxic arrest.
**Anaesthesia:** Unsuccessful awake intubation, a patient who cannot cooperate, an airway that obstructs with topical anaesthesia, or bleeding that hides the view: stop while the patient is still breathing and move to awake tracheostomy under local infiltration, with high-flow oxygen running. If the airway is lost, the unanticipated difficult intubation algorithm applies in compressed form, because supraglottic airway and facemask ventilation are unlikely to work in this anatomy: one optimised attempt at each is enough before scalpel cricothyroidotomy. Neck swelling can make that hard, which is why the membrane was marked beforehand.

### 7. Why not an inhalational or intravenous induction
**Why:** The surgeon may suggest that the trismus will relax once the patient is asleep.
**Anaesthesia:** Sometimes it does. In group A and in carefully selected group B patients (pain-related trismus of short duration, soft floor of mouth, normal nasendoscopy, comfortable lying flat) an intravenous induction with a videolaryngoscope, a bougie and a tested facemask seal is reasonable, and one series of 29 deep neck abscesses managed with such techniques recorded no surgical airways and no deaths. It is unreasonable in group C for three mechanical reasons. Trismus from days of inflammation in the masseter and pterygoids is partly fixed and does not release with relaxant. Laryngoscopy works by displacing the tongue into the submandibular space, and that space is full. Loss of pharyngeal tone lets the swollen tongue base meet the posterior wall, and the hard neck prevents jaw thrust. A gas induction does not escape this; it reaches the same obstruction more slowly, in a patient who is now neither awake nor deep.

### 8. Incision and drainage
**Why:** Skin incisions two finger-breadths below the mandible protect the marginal mandibular nerve; blunt dissection opens each involved space; drains keep them open; the tooth comes out.
**Anaesthesia:** With the tube secured the operation is short and undemanding. Pus may drain into the mouth: a throat pack around the tube and suction protect the lower airway, and the pack is counted out. Septic vasodilatation shows itself after induction; noradrenaline by infusion and an arterial line are appropriate for anyone with a raised lactate or hypotension. Send pus for culture. Take the opportunity to pass a nasogastric tube under vision for feeding if the patient will stay intubated.

### 9. The extubation decision
**Why:** Drainage starts recovery but the oedema peaks a day or two later; the surgeon may plan a second look.
**Anaesthesia:** Extubate at the end only in group A, and in group B when the swelling was limited, the intubation was easy and the airway looks normal on laryngoscopy at the end. Everyone else leaves theatre with a secured airway. The choice between a tracheal tube for several days and a tracheostomy is made with the surgeon and the intensivists. In a retrospective comparison of 85 multi-space infections, tracheostomy was associated with less time in intensive care (1.1 against 3.1 days) and lower cost; against that stand an operation in an infected field and a scar. A tube is reasonable when the infection is expected to turn within two to three days; a tracheostomy when the swelling is massive, the infection is descending, or the intubation was so difficult that an accidental extubation would be fatal. A nasal tube is better tolerated than an oral one for a patient who will be woken daily.

### 10. Critical care and delayed extubation
**Why:** The surgical team are watching for re-collection, spread to the parapharyngeal space and mediastinum, and sepsis.
**Anaesthesia:** Hand over the grade of the airway, how it was secured, and that reintubation would be as hard as the first time or harder. Extubation is planned, in daylight, with the original team: a cuff leak present, swelling visibly receding, trismus improving, a look with a nasendoscope alongside the tube, extubation over an airway exchange catheter, and the tracheostomy set in the room. Worsening sepsis, chest pain or a widening mediastinum on imaging is descending necrotising mediastinitis and needs the cardiothoracic surgeons.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is pressure relieved, then incisional pain in inflamed skin and soft tissue. Paracetamol and an opioid cover it; fentanyl or morphine by infusion or bolus for the intubated patient, oral or sublingual opioid for two to three days for the extubated one. NSAIDs are usually withheld in the first days in a septic, fluid-depleted patient with kidneys at risk, and added once renal function and sepsis are settled. Local infiltration by the surgeon is of limited use in infected tissue. No block and no catheter has a role, and there is no procedure-specific guideline. This plan is consensus and author's judgement.

Expect worst pain of about 3 out of 10 on the evening of surgery and day 1, less than before drainage, improving daily. Drain removal and dressing changes are briefly painful.

Escalation triggers are surgical. Pain, swelling or trismus that increases after the first 48 hours is a re-collection or spread into another space and needs repeat imaging and usually a return to theatre. Neck or chest pain with fever is mediastinal extension. New pain on swallowing with a changed voice in an extubated patient is an airway review, sitting up, before it is an analgesic request.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The under-graded booking. "Dental abscess" at the end of an evening list with a junior surgeon. See the patient before agreeing a slot and a location.
- Trismus. Assume it will not release until shown otherwise. Mouth opening under two finger-breadths with any group C sign is an awake technique.
- Sedation before the airway is secure. The reported harm in awake intubation for these patients was oversedation. Anxiolysis is explanation and a calm room.
- Lying the patient flat for CT. A group C patient who cannot lie flat does not go to the scanner without an airway plan and an anaesthetist; some go to theatre without the scan.
- Blind techniques. Blind nasal intubation and supraglottic rescue devices risk rupturing the abscess and seldom work.
- The child. Deep neck infections in children are more often retropharyngeal or parapharyngeal and seldom Ludwig angina; awake techniques are not feasible, so the plan is an inhalational induction with spontaneous ventilation, an ENT surgeon scrubbed and a rigid bronchoscope open. See [airway foreign body, epiglottitis and the obstructed airway](airway-foreign-body-epiglottitis-and-the.html) and [the child](../a/a13-the-child.html).
- Pregnancy, obesity and diabetes. All three are over-represented, each narrows the margin, and diabetes worsens the infection. Check the glucose and ketones.
- Tracheostomy is covered on its own card: [tracheostomy](tracheostomy.html). The principles of shared and obstructed airways are in [the shared and difficult airway](../a/a04-shared-difficult-airway.html).
- Localised abscess with a normal airway (group A). A day-surgery-length anaesthetic with a tracheal tube or reinforced supraglottic airway, a throat pack protocol, and the same emergence care as [dental extractions and wisdom teeth](dental-extractions-and-wisdom-teeth.html), though the patient usually stays a night for antibiotics.
- Invasive monitoring and critical care. Arterial line and vasopressor for sepsis; critical care for every patient who leaves theatre intubated or with a new tracheostomy, and for multi-space infection even when extubated.

## Key sources

The awake intubation recommendations (oxygen, lidocaine dose, sedation, attempt limits, the two-point check, the pre-agreed failure plan) are from the Difficult Airway Society guideline, itself largely expert consensus. The disease-specific evidence is three small retrospective series: one of awake bronchoscopic intubation, one arguing that a surgical airway is seldom needed, one comparing tracheostomy with prolonged intubation. The three-group bedside grading, the argument against asleep induction, the remifentanil ceiling and the extubation criteria are author's judgement built on those and on the national audit.
