  circulations, so the anaesthetic is planned around the heart, brain and kidney and not around the leg or

  routine coronary revascularisation before vascular surgery did not improve outcome. Most perioperative

## One disease, many beds

Atherosclerosis is a systemic disease that the surgeon treats one artery at a time. The patient booked for a [femoral endarterectomy](../c/lower-limb-bypass-and-femoral-endarterectomy.html), an [aneurysm repair](../c/open-abdominal-aortic-aneurysm-repair.html) or a [carotid endarterectomy](../c/carotid-endarterectomy.html) has plaque in every bed, whether or not it has declared itself. The claudicant cannot walk far enough to get angina, so a clean cardiac history means only that the heart has never been tested.

| Bed | What is usually there | What it does to the anaesthetic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coronary | Fixed stenoses, often silent; previous stents | Supply-demand ischaemia with tachycardia, hypotension or anaemia; antiplatelet decisions |
| Cerebral | Carotid and intracranial stenosis; autoregulation shifted to the right | Stroke with hypotension; pressure kept near the patient's own baseline |
| Renal | Renal artery stenosis, hypertensive and diabetic nephropathy | Injury from contrast, clamping and hypotension; drug accumulation |
| Aorta and limbs | Aneurysm, occlusive disease | Unequal arm pressures: measure both and use the higher |
| Fellow travellers | Smoking-related lung disease, diabetes, heart failure, atrial fibrillation | Postoperative respiratory failure; glucose, anticoagulants |

The operations themselves run from the trivial ([varicose veins](../c/varicose-vein-surgery-and-endovenous-ablation.html), [fistula formation](../c/arteriovenous-fistula-formation.html) under a block) to the largest physiological insult in elective surgery (the supracoeliac clamp). The patient is the constant.

## Cardiac risk: what to assess and what testing changes

Three questions sort the patient, and only the third needs a test.

```
  1. Is there an ACTIVE cardiac condition
     (unstable angina, recent infarct, decompensated failure,        yes -> treat that
      severe valve disease, uncontrolled arrhythmia)                         first
                     | no
  2. How big is the surgery and how many risk factors
     (Revised Cardiac Risk Index 0-6)
                     |
  3. Would a test change what is done, independent of the operation
     (a murmur or breathlessness -> echocardiogram;
      symptoms that would justify revascularisation anyway -> cardiology)
                     | no
              proceed, with the plan below
```

The Revised Cardiac Risk Index counts six things: high-risk surgery (which includes all suprainguinal vascular surgery), ischaemic heart disease, heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, insulin-treated diabetes and a creatinine above 177 micromol/L. In the validation cohort major cardiac complications occurred in 0.4%, 0.9%, 7% and 11% of patients with none, one, two, and three or more. Most patients for open aortic or bypass surgery score two or three before they sit down. The index underestimates risk in vascular surgery and does not capture frailty, but it is quick and it ranks patients correctly.

Functional capacity is the traditional second filter, and the METS study showed that the way it is usually done does not work. Among patients with cardiac risk factors having major surgery at 25 hospitals, the anaesthetist's subjective estimate identified only about one in five of those who could not reach four metabolic equivalents on formal exercise testing, and it did not predict death or infarction. The Duke Activity Status Index questionnaire did. If functional capacity is going to gate a decision, use the questionnaire or a measured test, not an impression at the bedside.

What testing changes is less than it seems. The CARP trial took patients with stable coronary disease who were due for aneurysm repair or leg revascularisation and randomised them to coronary revascularisation first or not. Revascularisation did not improve survival and it tripled the wait for the vascular operation. The lesson is that stress testing and angiography are for patients who would warrant them if no surgery were planned. Current European and American guidelines agree, and add biomarkers: a baseline troponin and natriuretic peptide in higher-risk patients make later values interpretable and add prognostic information to the clinical index.

Recent coronary stents are the exception that does change the plan. Elective surgery is deferred until dual antiplatelet therapy can be reduced safely, the period being set by the stent, the indication and the cardiologist, and time-critical surgery proceeds on aspirin at least.

## Perioperative myocardial injury

The VISION investigators measured troponin for three days after surgery in 15,133 unselected patients aged 45 or over and changed how the problem is understood. A raised troponin was common (11.6% had a peak troponin T of 0.02 ng/mL or more) and graded in its meaning: 30-day mortality was 1.0% with a normal value and 4.0%, 9.3% and 16.9% across the three bands above it. Myocardial injury after non-cardiac surgery, defined as a troponin rise judged to be ischaemic, occurred in 8.0%, carried an adjusted hazard ratio for death of 3.87, and in more than half of cases would not have met the standard definition of myocardial infarction because there was no chest pain and no diagnostic electrocardiogram. The high-sensitivity analysis in 21,842 patients confirmed that the rise predicts death with or without an ischaemic feature.

The mechanism is mostly supply and demand. A fixed stenosis that copes at rest fails when the heart rate is 110, the haemoglobin is 80 g/L and the diastolic pressure is 45 mmHg on the first postoperative night, and the patient, on opioids and with a wound, reports nothing. That is why the injury clusters in the first 48 hours, why it is missed without surveillance, and why the preventive measures are haemodynamic: avoid tachycardia, avoid hypotension, transfuse the bleeding arteriopath earlier than the fit patient, treat pain and hypoxia.

What to do when the troponin comes back raised is less certain. MANAGE randomised 1,754 such patients to dabigatran 110 mg twice daily or placebo and found fewer major vascular complications (11% against 15%) without a significant excess of major bleeding. Observational data support aspirin and a statin. The practical sequence is an electrocardiogram, a search for a correctable cause (bleeding, hypoxia, tachyarrhythmia, sepsis), cardiology review, and secondary prevention before discharge. The defensible default for open aortic, bypass and amputation surgery is troponin on days 1 and 2.

Two POISE-3 results complete the picture. A strategy of withholding antihypertensives and targeting a higher intra-operative pressure did not reduce vascular complications compared with the opposite strategy (13.9% against 14.0%). And tranexamic acid reduced bleeding (9.1% against 11.7%) with a small difference in cardiovascular events that did not meet the non-inferiority margin, which makes it reasonable in open vascular surgery with expected blood loss and a decision to be weighed in the patient with a fresh stent or graft.

## Beta blockers and statins

POISE is the trial to remember when someone suggests a beta blocker in the anaesthetic room. Extended-release metoprolol started two to four hours before surgery in 8,351 patients cut myocardial infarction (4.2% against 5.7%) and increased death (3.1% against 2.3%) and stroke (1.0% against 0.5%). The mechanism of harm was hypotension and bradycardia: a patient who cannot raise the heart rate cannot compensate for bleeding, sepsis or a vasodilating anaesthetic, and a brain behind a carotid stenosis infarcts.

- A patient already taking a beta blocker continues it, including on the morning of surgery, because withdrawal causes rebound ischaemia.
- A beta blocker is not started on the day of surgery.
- Where there is an independent indication, it is started weeks ahead and titrated, which is a physician's decision.
- Intra-operative tachycardia is treated by finding the cause first, then with a short-acting agent (esmolol 10-20 mg IV increments) while the pressure is watched.

Statins are the opposite story: plaque stabilisation with almost no haemodynamic cost. In 100 vascular surgery patients atorvastatin 20 mg started before surgery cut cardiac events at six months from 26% to 8%. Every vascular patient should be on one, it is taken on the morning of surgery and restarted the same evening, and a patient who arrives without one is started at the pre-assessment visit.

## Antiplatelets and anticoagulants around surgery

Aspirin for all-comers does not prevent perioperative infarction: in POISE-2 (10,010 patients) death or infarction was 7.0% with aspirin and 7.1% with placebo, and major bleeding rose from 3.8% to 4.6%. Vascular patients are not all-comers. In the subgroup with a previous coronary intervention aspirin halved the primary outcome, and carotid, bypass and endovascular surgeons want aspirin on board to protect the endarterectomy surface, graft or stent. Aspirin therefore continues through nearly all vascular surgery.

| Drug | Usual handling for vascular surgery |
| --- | --- |
| Aspirin | Continue |
| Clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor | Often continued for carotid and endovascular work; stopped 5-7 days (ticagrelor 3-5) before open aortic surgery if the cardiac indication allows; never stopped for a recent stent without the cardiologist |
| Warfarin | Stop 5 days; check INR the day before; no bridging for atrial fibrillation |
| Dabigatran, apixaban, rivaroxaban | Omit 2 days before high-bleeding-risk surgery (longer for dabigatran with reduced renal function); no bridging; restart at 48-72 h |
| Mechanical valve, recent venous thrombosis | Individual plan; these are the patients who still get bridged |

The no-bridging rule rests on two studies. In BRIDGE, patients with atrial fibrillation who simply stopped warfarin had the same rate of arterial thromboembolism as those bridged with low-molecular-weight heparin (0.4% against 0.3%) and less than half the major bleeding (1.3% against 3.2%). In PAUSE, 3,007 patients on a direct oral anticoagulant followed a fixed interruption schedule with no bridging and no coagulation testing; major bleeding was 0.9-1.85% and thromboembolism 0.16-0.6%. The chest physicians' guideline makes its recommendation against bridging in atrial fibrillation one of only two strong ones.

## Antithrombotics and neuraxial or deep blocks

An epidural for open aortic surgery, a spinal for a bypass, and a deep block for an amputation all sit inside an antithrombotic regimen, with systemic heparin given between insertion and removal. The American (fifth edition) and European guidelines agree on the structure: the intervals apply to neuraxial procedures and to deep, non-compressible blocks (paravertebral, lumbar plexus, proximal sciatic); superficial, compressible blocks are managed on bleeding risk; and the interval depends on the drug, the dose (prophylactic or therapeutic), renal function and whether the puncture was traumatic. The table gives the conventional intervals; the current edition of the guideline is the reference for any dose at the margin.

| Drug | Last dose before needle or catheter removal | Next dose after |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aspirin, NSAIDs | No restriction | No restriction |
| Clopidogrel | 5-7 days | After catheter removal |
| Ticagrelor | 5-7 days | After catheter removal |
| Prasugrel | 7-10 days | After catheter removal |
| Warfarin | 5 days and a normal INR | After catheter removal, or remove at INR below 1.5 |
| Unfractionated heparin, prophylactic subcutaneous | 4-6 h | 1 h |
| Unfractionated heparin, intravenous | 4-6 h and normal coagulation | 1 h |
| Low-molecular-weight heparin, prophylactic | 12 h | 4 h after removal |
| Low-molecular-weight heparin, therapeutic | 24 h | 24 h after surgery; catheter out first |
| Apixaban, rivaroxaban, therapeutic dose | 72 h, or a drug level | 6 h after removal |
| Dabigatran | 72-120 h by renal function | 6 h after removal |

For the intra-operative heparin of vascular surgery the working rules are: place the needle or catheter at least one hour before the heparin, avoid the technique in a patient with another coagulopathy, remove the catheter four to six hours after the last heparin dose with coagulation checked, and examine leg power regularly afterwards. A bloody tap does not mandate cancellation but is discussed with the surgeon before heparin is given. The analgesic alternatives when the timing does not work are in [analgesia for the trunk](a02-truncal-blocks-epidural.html).

## Heparin and protamine in theatre

Heparin binds antithrombin and multiplies its inhibition of thrombin and factor Xa, which protects the static column of blood beyond a clamp and the thrombogenic surface of a wire, sheath or graft. The usual dose is 70-100 units/kg IV (5,000 units in the average adult) given three minutes before the clamp, and the surgeon is told when it is in. The activated clotting time, measured after three to five minutes, confirms an effect and guides re-dosing in long cases, because the half-life is about an hour and is dose-dependent. Resistance (a low antithrombin after days of heparin infusion) shows as a clotting time that will not rise.

Protamine is a cationic protein that binds heparin one for one. Whether to give it is a surgical preference that varies: many do not reverse after an uncomplicated endarterectomy or bypass, some give a half dose, and most reverse after open aneurysm repair with oozing. When it is given:

- Dose to the heparin that remains, not to the heparin that was given: 1 mg per 100 units, reduced for elapsed time. Excess protamine is itself an anticoagulant, and a ratio above 1:1 increases bleeding.
- Give it slowly, over at least five minutes, peripherally. Fast injection releases histamine and drops the pressure.
- Expect three reactions: transient hypotension (rate-related), anaphylaxis (more likely after previous exposure, including protamine-containing insulin), and a rare catastrophic pulmonary vasoconstriction with right heart failure.
- Tell the surgeon before the first millilitre: clamps must be off and the graft flowing.

## Contrast and the kidney

[Endovascular aneurysm repair](../c/endovascular-aneurysm-repair.html) and [angioplasty](../c/peripheral-angioplasty-and-stenting.html) deliver iodinated contrast to patients with poor renal reserve, and for 20 years the response was bicarbonate, acetylcysteine and litres of saline. The trials have removed most of that. PRESERVE found neither bicarbonate nor acetylcysteine better than saline or placebo in about 5,000 high-risk patients. AMACING found no hydration non-inferior to prophylactic saline at an eGFR of 30-59 (nephropathy 2.6% against 2.7%), with complications of the fluid itself in 5.5%. The radiology and nephrology consensus is that the risk from contrast alone has been overstated, that saline prophylaxis is indicated at an eGFR below 30 or in acute kidney injury, and that it may be considered at 30-44.

For the anaesthetist the kidney is protected by the same things that protect the heart: no hypovolaemia, no hypotension, no NSAID, no aminoglycoside without a level, and the smallest contrast volume the operator can manage, with carbon dioxide angiography for the worst kidneys. No drug (mannitol, dopamine, furosemide) has been shown to protect the kidney from a suprarenal clamp; short clamp time and perfusion pressure are what count.

## Clamp and unclamp

The aortic clamp is a sudden rise in afterload with a shift of blood volume, and its effects depend on the level.

```
  CLAMP ON                                     CLAMP OFF
  afterload up (more the higher the clamp)     resistance falls: the legs are
  blood moves from below to above the clamp    dilated, acidotic and empty
    supracoeliac: splanchnic volume            venous return falls (blood pools
      displaced centrally, preload up,           in the reperfused bed)
      wall tension up, ischaemia               lactate, potassium, CO2 and cold
    infrarenal: volume can move into             blood return to the heart
      the splanchnic bed, modest change        -> hypotension, sometimes
  renal blood flow falls even with an             profound; rise in end-tidal CO2
    infrarenal clamp
  -> treat proximal hypertension:              -> prepare: fill before release,
     deepen, GTN, do not overfill                 stop vasodilators, pressor in
  -> watch ST segments and filling                hand, raise ventilation, ask for
                                                  a staged release, one limb
                                                  at a time
```

Two points of technique. The fluid given during the clamp is aimed at the unclamp: a patient kept dry to control clamp hypertension will collapse on release, so the last 10 minutes before release are spent filling while the vasodilator is weaned. And the unclamp is a negotiation: if the pressure falls too far the surgeon re-applies the clamp partially, which works within seconds and is better than a large dose of vasopressor into an acidotic circulation. Calcium is given if citrate load or ionised hypocalcaemia is contributing, and bicarbonate is rarely needed.

The carotid clamp is a different problem: the brain beyond it lives on collateral flow through the circle of Willis, so pressure is held at or a little above the patient's baseline for the duration of the clamp, and the surgeon shunts selectively or routinely. General and local anaesthesia gave the same rate of stroke, infarction and death in the GALA trial (4.8% and 4.5%). After the clamp comes off the baroreceptor is disturbed and the pressure can swing either way for hours; hypertension risks hyperperfusion and a neck haematoma, and it is treated.

The [ruptured aneurysm](../c/ruptured-abdominal-aortic-aneurysm.html) inverts the sequence: the clamp is the resuscitation, and induction is delayed until the surgeon is scrubbed (see [major haemorrhage](a05-major-haemorrhage.html)).

## Spinal cord protection

The anterior two-thirds of the cord depend on the anterior spinal artery, which is reinforced in the lower thoracic region by a variable number of segmental arteries from the aorta. Thoracoabdominal repair and long thoracic stent grafts cover or sacrifice those feeders. Cord perfusion then depends on collaterals (subclavian and hypogastric) and on a pressure gradient:

```
  cord perfusion pressure = mean arterial pressure - CSF pressure (or venous
                                                     pressure, if higher)
```

Both terms can be manipulated. In the randomised trial of extent I and II thoracoabdominal repairs, a lumbar cerebrospinal fluid drain cut paraplegia or paraparesis from 13.0% to 2.6%. The usual protocol drains to keep cerebrospinal fluid pressure at about 10 mmHg while the mean arterial pressure is held at 80-90 mmHg or higher, with haemoglobin and cardiac output maintained, for 48-72 hours. Drainage volume is capped, because over-drainage causes intracranial haemorrhage, and the drain is a neuraxial catheter for the purposes of every anticoagulant rule above.

Deficit can be delayed by hours or days and is usually provoked by hypotension. A new leg weakness after thoracic aortic surgery is treated at once by raising the mean pressure and lowering cerebrospinal fluid pressure; some of these recover. Infrarenal surgery rarely threatens the cord, but a patient with a previous aortic repair and occluded hypogastric arteries has lost collateral supply and deserves the same care with pressure.

## Diabetes and renal failure

Diabetes is present in a large minority of vascular patients and changes four things. Autonomic neuropathy removes the warning signs of ischaemia and hypoglycaemia, slows the stomach, and makes the pressure labile at induction. Glucose is kept in a moderate range (about 5-10 mmol/L) with an insulin infusion for major surgery, since both hyperglycaemia and hypoglycaemia worsen outcome. Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors are omitted the day before and the day of surgery because fasting on these drugs produces ketoacidosis with a normal glucose; a sick postoperative patient who takes one has blood ketones measured. And the diabetic foot is the commonest reason for amputation and debridement, usually in a patient with renal impairment, on anticoagulants, with sepsis.

The patient on dialysis presents a set of fixed rules:

- Dialyse the day before, not the day of, surgery, and measure potassium on the morning. Suxamethonium is acceptable with a normal potassium.
- Protect the fistula arm and the future fistula arm: no cannula, no arterial line, no cuff.
- Drugs: morphine metabolites accumulate, so use fentanyl, hydromorphone or cautious oxycodone; avoid NSAIDs if there is residual urine output to preserve; low-molecular-weight heparin accumulates, so prophylaxis is unfractionated heparin.
- Uraemic platelets work poorly whatever the count, which lowers the threshold for avoiding a neuraxial technique.
- Fluid has nowhere to go. A litre too much is pulmonary oedema until the next dialysis.

For [fistula surgery](../c/arteriovenous-fistula-formation.html) itself, a brachial plexus block has a surgical argument as well as an anaesthetic one: the sympathetic block dilates the vein and artery while the surgeon is choosing and sewing them.
