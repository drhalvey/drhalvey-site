## Why these two problems share a chapter

The shared airway and the difficult airway are the same problem seen from two sides. In the first, the surgeon needs the space the airway device would occupy; in the second, disease or anatomy has already taken it. Both are solved by the same small set of tools (a narrower tube, a different route, no tube with oxygen delivered another way, the patient kept breathing, a hole in the neck), and both go wrong for the same reason: the plan covered the first step and not the failure of it.

The national audit of major airway complications is the reference point. It identified 133 major events during general anaesthesia, about one per 22,000 anaesthetics, with a mortality of one per 180,000, and the reviewers judged airway management to have been good in only 19% of the cases they could assess. The audit estimated that perhaps a quarter of events were reported. Its recurring findings were poor assessment, the absence of a strategy (a sequence of plans, as opposed to a single plan), persistence with a failing technique, and failure to use awake intubation when it was indicated; head and neck surgery, obesity and emergence featured heavily. The rest of this chapter is organised around avoiding those.

## The shared airway

### Negotiating the design

For any operation in or near the airway the questions are fixed, and the answers come from a conversation with the surgeon before the patient is in the room:

1. Where is the surgeon working, and what must be out of the way: the mouth, the nose, the larynx, the trachea.
2. Does the airway need protecting from blood, pus or debris, and until when.
3. Will an ignition source be used in the airway.
4. Will the airway be better, the same or worse at the end.

| Operation | Usual airway | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Tonsillectomy](../c/adult-and-paediatric-tonsillectomy-and-adenoidectomy.html), palate and oral surgery | Preformed south-facing oral tube or reinforced supraglottic airway, under the tongue blade of the gag | Tube lies in the midline groove of the gag; the gag can kink or dislodge it when opened |
| [Dental](../c/dental-extractions-and-wisdom-teeth.html), [orthognathic](../c/orthognathic-surgery.html) and [mandibular fracture](../c/facial-fracture-fixation.html) surgery | Preformed north-facing nasal tube | The surgeon needs the mouth and the occlusion |
| [Nasal and sinus surgery](../c/septoplasty-turbinate-surgery-and-functional-endoscopic.html) | Oral tube or reinforced supraglottic airway | The supraglottic airway shields the larynx from blood from above and gives a smoother emergence |
| [Microlaryngoscopy](../c/microlaryngoscopy-laser-airway-surgery-and-jet.html) | Microlaryngoscopy tube (5.0-6.0 mm, long, with a full-size cuff), or tubeless | Posterior glottis hidden by a tube; anterior lesions usually accessible around a small one |
| Laser surgery to the larynx or trachea | Laser-resistant tube or tubeless | Fuel removed from the beam path |
| [Rigid bronchoscopy](../c/rigid-bronchoscopy-stents-and-tracheal-resection.html) | The bronchoscope is the airway | Ventilation through its side arm or by jet |
| [Tracheostomy](../c/tracheostomy.html), [laryngectomy](../c/laryngectomy-neck-dissection-and-free-flap.html) | Oral tube, then a tube through the stoma | A planned handover of the airway from one device to another mid-operation |

Once the drapes are on, the head is a metre away, often turned through 90 or 180 degrees, and the tube is under the surgeon's instruments. The connections are therefore taped, the tube position is rechecked after the gag is opened or the head is extended, and disconnection or kinking is assumed when the capnograph changes. Agree the words for "stop, I need the airway".

### Throat packs

A pack absorbs blood above the cuff and is the classic retained object: left in at extubation it obstructs the airway of a patient who has just been made unable to protect it. If a pack is used it needs a reason, a visible reminder (a tail out of the mouth, a label on the tube or the forehead), an entry in the swab count, and removal confirmed aloud before extubation. Suction under vision at the end does the same job for most operations, and a cuffed tube already protects the trachea.

### Working without a tube

When even a small tube is in the way, there are four ways to oxygenate, and they can be combined in one case.

| Technique | How gas exchange happens | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intermittent apnoea | Tube or mask ventilation, then the tube is removed and the surgeon works until saturation or time runs out | Short windows; repeated instrumentation |
| High-flow nasal oxygen during apnoea | Oxygen drawn down by mass flow as it is absorbed; some carbon dioxide clearance from flow-dependent flushing | Carbon dioxide still rises; needs a patent upper airway; oxygen-rich field |
| Spontaneous ventilation with IV anaesthesia and high-flow nasal oxygen | The patient breathes; no tube | Depth is a narrow target: too light and the cords move, too deep and breathing stops |
| Jet ventilation (supraglottic, subglottic or transtracheal) | High-pressure gas through a narrow cannula entrains air and inflates the lungs; expiration is passive through the open glottis | Barotrauma if the way out is blocked; no reliable capnography; dries and cools |

Apnoeic oxygenation with high-flow nasal oxygen has made the tubeless field routine for short laryngeal procedures. In the original series of patients with difficult airways the median apnoea time was 14 min with no desaturation below 90%, and carbon dioxide rose at 0.15 kPa/min (about 1.1 mmHg/min). In a physiological study during laryngeal surgery, 31 patients were apnoeic for a mean of 22.5 min with saturation never below 91%, while arterial carbon dioxide rose at 0.24 kPa/min (about 1.8 mmHg/min), twice the rate suggested by the end-tidal value. The lesson in those numbers is that oxygenation is not the limit; carbon dioxide and acidosis are, and they cannot be seen on the monitor. About 20-30 min is a sensible ceiling in a patient with a normal starting point, and less in obesity (where desaturation comes first because the reservoir is small), pulmonary hypertension, raised intracranial pressure and sickle cell disease. It requires a patent airway from nose to trachea, so jaw thrust or the surgical laryngoscope has to be holding it open, and it is an oxygen-enriched field (see airway fire below).

Jet ventilation remains the technique for longer subglottic and tracheal work. Its hazard is mechanical: gas goes in under pressure and can only leave passively, so anything that closes the exit (laryngospasm, a tumour, the surgeon's instrument, a closed mouth around a transtracheal catheter) traps each breath, and the result is pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum or cardiovascular collapse within a few breaths. A ten-year review of endoscopic laryngeal surgery found the complications clustered in jet ventilation, with the transtracheal route the major independent risk factor. A national survey found deaths and major complications with manual jet devices and none of the deaths in departments using an automated jet ventilator, and noted that two-thirds of respondents started manual ventilation at pressures above 2 atmospheres. The rules: an automated ventilator with a pause-pressure alarm and cut-off where one exists; the lowest driving pressure that moves the chest; watch the chest fall after every breath; full neuromuscular block so that the cords cannot close; total IV anaesthesia because there is no circuit for a volatile; and an immediate stop if the chest does not fall.

## The obstructed airway

A patient with stridor has lost most of the cross-section of the airway and is holding the rest open with muscle tone and effort. General anaesthesia removes both. The decisions depend on where the narrowing is and how much room is left, so the minimum dataset before any drug is given is a history of the tempo and of what the patient cannot do (lie flat, sleep), a look with a nasendoscope by someone who can interpret it, and cross-sectional imaging if the patient can lie down for it. Stridor at rest, inability to lie flat, a muffled voice with drooling, and exhaustion are signs that there is no margin.

| Level | Typical causes | What tends to work | What tends to fail |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Oral cavity, tongue base, pharynx | [Dental and neck abscess](../c/dental-and-neck-abscess.html), tongue base tumour, angioedema, [bleeding tonsil](../c/bleeding-tonsil.html) | Awake tracheal intubation, nasal route where the mouth will not open; videolaryngoscopy if the cause is trismus from pain alone | Facemask and supraglottic rescue (the obstruction is at or above the device) |
| Supraglottis and glottis | Laryngeal tumour, [epiglottitis](../c/airway-foreign-body-epiglottitis-and-the.html), bilateral cord palsy | Awake tracheostomy under local anaesthesia for severe fixed narrowing; spontaneously breathing induction with the surgeon scrubbed; small tube under videolaryngoscopy for moderate lesions | An awake bronchoscope that completes the obstruction of a slit-like glottis, or bleeds a friable tumour |
| Subglottis and trachea | Stenosis, tumour, retrosternal goitre | Small tube or rigid bronchoscope past the lesion; jet from above for short stenoses | Front of neck access above an obstruction that lies below it |
| Lower trachea and carina | [Anterior mediastinal mass](../c/mediastinoscopy-and-anterior-mediastinal-mass.html) | Spontaneous ventilation, position change, rigid bronchoscope, extracorporeal support arranged in advance | Paralysis and positive pressure with no rescue below the lesion |

Three principles carry across the table. Rescue has to work below the obstruction, so the usual Plans B, C and D are tested against the anatomy before they are relied on. The surgeon is part of the plan: for a glottic or supraglottic lesion the rigid bronchoscope and tracheostomy set are open, and the surgeon gowned, before induction. And temporising buys time for a proper setup: sitting position, high-flow humidified oxygen, nebulised adrenaline, dexamethasone IV for oedema, heliox if it is to hand.

The spontaneously breathing induction deserves a note because its reputation exceeds its reliability. A slow volatile or IV induction keeps the patient breathing until the airway is seen, but obstruction commonly worsens as tone is lost, and the plan for that moment has to exist. High-flow nasal oxygen has improved the margin: in 30 patients with obstructed airways, most of them stridulous or dyspnoeic, IV induction with preserved spontaneous ventilation and high-flow nasal oxygen produced no apnoea and no complete obstruction, with a median lowest saturation of 100% over a median of 44 min. That is one observational series from one unit and describes what is achievable, not what is guaranteed.

## Awake tracheal intubation

The argument for intubating awake is simple: the patient keeps the airway, the breathing and the option to stop. The Difficult Airway Society guideline describes it as having a high success rate and a favourable safety profile and as underused, and structures it as four components under the mnemonic sTOP, with the lower-case s a reminder that sedation is the optional one.

| Component | Recommendation | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sedation | Minimal, cautious, ideally given by a second anaesthetist; a single agent (remifentanil or dexmedetomidine) is easier to control than a combination | Over-sedation is the commonest way an awake intubation becomes an emergency; sedation does not substitute for poor topicalisation |
| Topicalisation | Lidocaine to the nose or mouth, pharynx, larynx and trachea, tested before instrumenting; total not above 9 mg/kg lean body weight; lidocaine with phenylephrine for the nose in preference to cocaine | A cough or gag on contact means the block is incomplete; toxicity is the dose-limiting risk |
| Oxygenation | Supplemental oxygen throughout, high-flow nasal oxygen where available | Sedation, obstruction and a scope in the glottis all desaturate |
| Performance | Flexible bronchoscope or videolaryngoscope by the operator's skill and the anatomy; patient sitting; a tube chosen to pass easily; no more than three attempts plus one by a more experienced operator | Repeated attempts cause bleeding, oedema and loss of cooperation |

After the tube is in, anaesthesia is induced only after a two-point check: the tube seen in the trachea and a capnograph trace. A plan for failure is agreed before starting: stop and postpone, awake front of neck access, or, only where that has been judged the lesser risk, induction with a prepared rescue. In a prospective cohort from a centre with a large awake practice, 11.0% of cases had a complication, most often multiple attempts (4.2%), over-sedation (2.2%) and desaturation (1.5%), and complications were fewer with experienced operators.

Awake intubation fails in predictable patients: the one with blood or pus in the airway that hides the view, the one whose glottis is so narrow that the scope completes the obstruction, the one who cannot cooperate, and the one in whom local anaesthetic will not work through infected tissue. Those are the patients for an awake tracheostomy or for the surgeon-scrubbed induction described above.

## The unanticipated difficult airway: the algorithms

The guidelines differ in layout and agree on substance. The Difficult Airway Society algorithm, in its 2015 form and its 2025 revision, is linear.

```
 Plan A  tracheal intubation      best attempt first: position, full
         max 3 attempts + 1       block, videolaryngoscope, bougie or
              |                   stylet; oxygen running throughout
              v  fail: say so
 Plan B  supraglottic airway      second-generation device; then
         limited attempts         stop and think: wake, proceed,
              |                   intubate through it, or go surgical
              v  fail
 Plan C  facemask ventilation     two hands, adjuncts, full block
              |                   if oxygenating: wake the patient
              v  fail: declare cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate
 Plan D  emergency front of       scalpel, bougie, tube
         neck airway
```

The 2015 guideline introduced the features that matter in practice: a limit of three attempts at laryngoscopy plus one by a more experienced colleague, a declaration of failure out loud at each stage, second-generation supraglottic devices for rescue, waking the patient as the default after failed intubation, and scalpel cricothyroidotomy as the rescue. The 2025 revision keeps the same linear structure and, in its own summary, shifts the emphasis from managing failure to maximising first-attempt success, with continuous oxygen delivery throughout (which it terms peroxygenation), confirmation by waveform capnography, and attention to the physiologically difficult airway, obesity, human factors and ultrasound. A Cochrane review supports the first-attempt emphasis: videolaryngoscopes of all designs likely reduce failed intubation and raise first-attempt success compared with direct laryngoscopy.

The American and Canadian guidelines add a stronger decision step before induction (awake or asleep, based on predicted difficulty with intubation, ventilation, front of neck access and the patient's physiological reserve), and the Canadian document makes a useful point for the patient with predicted difficulty who is nonetheless managed asleep: brief the triggers for moving from one technique to the next before starting. The Vortex is a different kind of tool: a cognitive aid for the crisis itself, built around three upper airway lifelines (facemask, supraglottic airway, tracheal tube), a best effort at each, and a move to front of neck access when all three have failed. Use the guideline to plan and the simpler aid to act.

The drugs matter to the algorithm. Full neuromuscular block makes facemask ventilation, supraglottic airway placement, laryngoscopy and front of neck access all easier, and the guidelines are clear that a patient who cannot be intubated or oxygenated should be paralysed, not left half-relaxed in the hope of spontaneous recovery. Waking up is a real option only when oxygenation has been maintained.

## Front of neck access

The decision is harder than the procedure. Cases reviewed after the event show delay: more attempts at intubation, another device, a falling saturation watched instead of acted on. The trigger is failure to oxygenate by tube, supraglottic airway and facemask in a paralysed patient, declared aloud, and from that moment the neck is the only plan.

The Difficult Airway Society technique is deliberately minimal: a scalpel with a number 10 blade, a bougie and a cuffed 6.0 mm tube. The neck is extended, the larynx is stabilised with the laryngeal handshake, and with a palpable cricothyroid membrane a transverse stab is made through skin and membrane, the blade is turned through 90 degrees with the sharp edge caudal, the bougie is passed along the blade into the trachea, and the tube is railroaded over it. Where the membrane cannot be felt, a vertical midline skin incision comes first, then blunt dissection with the fingers to find the airway. Oxygen continues from above throughout.

Cannula techniques with jet oxygenation are taught alongside the scalpel in Australian practice, where the published training algorithm includes both and stresses regular rehearsal. The caution is the evidence from real emergencies: in a systematic review, transtracheal jet ventilation failed in 42% of uses in a cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate crisis, against none in other emergency uses, with a high rate of barotrauma, because in exactly these patients the upper airway is obstructed and the gas cannot get out. Whichever technique a department teaches, the equipment has to be within reach of every anaesthetic location and the operator has to have done it on a model recently.

Preparation makes the difference in the neck that is hard to read. Ultrasound identifies the cricothyroid membrane in difficult necks with about double the success of palpation after brief training, and marking it before induction in any patient with predicted difficulty, obesity or neck pathology converts a search into a known target. In the patient with a fixed obstruction at the glottis, the logical extension is to have front of neck access performed electively, awake, before anything else.

## Extubation

Intubation problems happen with the team assembled and the patient preoxygenated. Extubation problems happen at the end of the list, with a swollen airway, blood in the pharynx, residual drugs, and the difficult airway trolley already pushed back against the wall. The extubation guideline treats it as a procedure with its own plan, preparation and aftercare, and asks one sorting question: is this a low-risk or an at-risk extubation.

At risk means either that reintubation would be difficult (it was difficult at the start, or the airway has changed: oedema after long head-down or prone surgery, a neck fixed or wired, a free flap in the mouth, a haematoma) or that the patient may not tolerate extubation (aspiration risk, poor respiratory reserve, a physiology that will not survive a period of hypoxia). For those patients the choices are:

1. Is it safe to extubate now at all. If the airway is likely to be worse in six hours than it is now, leave the tube in or ask for a tracheostomy. That is a surgical decision made jointly, common after major [head and neck resection](../c/laryngectomy-neck-dissection-and-free-flap.html).
2. Optimise first: full reversal with a train-of-four ratio above 0.9, a cuff-leak test where oedema is possible, suction under vision, pack out, sitting up, preoxygenated, with help and equipment present.
3. Extubate awake by default. The advanced options are for specific problems: exchange of the tube for a supraglottic airway under deep anaesthesia when coughing must be avoided; a low-dose remifentanil infusion to suppress coughing while the patient wakes; and an airway exchange catheter left in the trachea as a conduit for oxygen and for reintubation when getting back in would be difficult.
4. Continue the plan into recovery: where the patient goes, what was difficult, what the triggers for calling are, and who has the wire cutters, the stitch cutter or the tracheostomy dilators at the bedside.

The neck haematoma is the post-extubation emergency that rewards a prepared plan. After [thyroid](../c/thyroidectomy-and-parathyroidectomy.html), [carotid](../c/carotid-endarterectomy.html) and anterior cervical surgery, a haematoma under the strap muscles obstructs venous and lymphatic drainage of the larynx, and the airway closes from oedema, not from external compression alone. The consensus guideline for thyroid haematoma puts the steps in order: oxygen and evaluation, evacuation of the haematoma at the bedside by opening the wound through all its layers, and then intubation, which is easier after decompression but may still meet a swollen larynx. Agitation, difficulty swallowing, a change in voice and a rising respiratory rate precede stridor; waiting for stridor is waiting too long.

## Airway fire

A fire needs an oxidiser, an ignition source and fuel, and in airway surgery the anaesthetist controls the first, the surgeon the second, and both share the third.

| Element | In the airway | Control |
| --- | --- | --- |

The highest-risk moments are laser surgery to the larynx or trachea, diathermy entry into the trachea during tracheostomy in a patient on a high inspired oxygen, and diathermy in the mouth with a leaking uncuffed tube. A tubeless technique removes the main fuel but not the risk: tissue, swabs and eschar still burn, and apnoeic oxygenation with high-flow nasal oxygen puts close to 100% oxygen at the ignition point, so the same discipline of announcing, pausing and using the minimum energy applies, and many units reduce the oxygen fraction or pause the flow during laser activation.

If the tube ignites, the blowtorch effect of gas flow through a burning tube drives the injury down the trachea, so the sequence is aimed at stopping flow and removing fuel, done together by surgeon and anaesthetist:

1. Remove the tracheal tube and stop all gas flow, disconnecting the circuit.
2. Remove packs and any other burning material and flood the airway with saline.
3. Ventilate by facemask, avoiding supplemental oxygen and nitrous oxide if the patient's saturation allows until the fire is certainly out.
4. Examine the airway, with a rigid bronchoscope for the trachea, remove fragments, and reintubate; the airway will swell.
5. Plan for a ventilated bed, a repeat bronchoscopy and the possibility of a tracheostomy.

The response is rehearsed in the team brief for every laser case: who pulls the tube, who has the saline, where it is.

## The rule set

1. Before every shared airway case, agree where the surgeon is working, whether the airway needs protecting, whether there will be an ignition source, and how the airway will look at the end.
2. A plan is a sequence. State Plans A to D, the limit on attempts, and the trigger for each move before induction.
3. Awake intubation when difficulty is predicted and the patient can cooperate: minimal sedation, tested topicalisation within 9 mg/kg lean body weight of lidocaine, oxygen throughout, three attempts plus one, two-point check before induction.
4. In the obstructed airway, locate the level, test every rescue against it, and have the surgeon scrubbed with the rigid bronchoscope and tracheostomy set.
5. Tubeless techniques are limited by carbon dioxide, which you cannot see; set a time limit. Jet ventilation is limited by the exit path; watch the chest fall.
6. Oxygen from the first moment to the last, a videolaryngoscope and full neuromuscular block for the first attempt, and capnography to confirm.
7. Cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate is declared aloud and treated with a scalpel, bougie and 6.0 mm cuffed tube, or the technique the department has trained, without further attempts from above.
8. Mark the cricothyroid membrane, with ultrasound where it cannot be felt, before induction in any predicted difficult airway.
9. Sort every extubation into low risk or at risk; if the airway will be worse later, do not extubate now.
10. Neck haematoma: oxygen, open the wound, then intubate.
11. Laser or diathermy in the airway: lowest oxygen, no nitrous oxide, saline-filled cuff, wet swabs, and a rehearsed fire drill.
