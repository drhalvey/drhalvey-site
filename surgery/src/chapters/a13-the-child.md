title: "The child: doses, fluids, airway and the neonate"
summary: >
  A child is not harder to anaesthetise than an adult, but the margins are narrower and the errors are
  different: a decimal point, a tube one size wrong, ninety seconds of laryngospasm, a litre of the wrong
  fluid. Weigh the child and write the doses before induction. Let clear fluids run until an hour before.
  Use isotonic fluid, with glucose for the small and the starved. Choose a cuffed tube by formula, expect
  laryngospasm in the child with a cold, and treat it by a fixed sequence. The neonate and the former
  premature infant are a separate population with apnoea, hypothermia and hypoglycaemia as the default
  complications. Single brief anaesthetics in infancy have not been shown to harm development. Pain after
  surgery is treated with regular paracetamol and ibuprofen and small doses of opioid, without codeine.

## The shape of the problem

The European APRICOT audit counted a severe critical event in 5.2% of 31,127 paediatric anaesthetics, three-fifths of them respiratory, and found that the risk fell with every year of experience of the senior anaesthetist. Children's general surgery reflects that: the operations are short and kind ([hernia, orchidopexy and circumcision](../c/paediatric-inguinal-hernia-orchidopexy-and-circumcision.html), [grommets](../c/grommets-and-minor-paediatric-ent.html), [appendicectomy](../c/paediatric-appendicectomy-and-intussusception.html)), and the complications are those of the airway, the dose and the fluid, not of the surgery. This chapter is the general-surgical companion to the cards; the specialist neonatal operations are on the [neonatal emergencies](../c/neonatal-emergencies.html) and [pyloromyotomy](../c/pyloromyotomy.html) cards.

The college guideline defines what a generalist's training covers: children over two years of age without significant comorbidity having surgery of moderate complexity. Neonates, former premature babies, infants and children with heart, airway or neuromuscular disease go to a paediatric centre unless the problem cannot wait.

## Weight, and the sheet written before induction

Every dose is a multiplication, so every dose can be wrong by a factor of ten. Weigh the child. When that is impossible, the old formula of twice (age plus four) underestimates modern children by a third; three times age plus seven is within 7% between 1 and 13 years.

```
  under 1 year    (age in months / 2) + 4 kg        term newborn about 3.5 kg
  1 to 13 years   (3 x age in years) + 7 kg         1 y: 10   5 y: 22   10 y: 37
```

The calculated doses in milligrams and millilitres go on a sheet before the child is in the room. The first table is the set for the first five minutes and the emergency; the second is analgesia. Obese children are dosed on ideal weight for paracetamol, local anaesthetic and opioid, and no dose exceeds the adult dose.

## Fasting

Long fasts make children miserable, ketotic, hypotensive at induction and hard to cannulate, and they do not prevent aspiration. The British, European and French-language paediatric societies moved in 2018 to clear fluids until one hour before anaesthesia, after audits showed that a written two-hour rule delivered fasts of six or seven hours. The 2022 European guideline confirmed it. The Australian and New Zealand college says the same: clear liquids, up to 3 mL/kg per hour, are encouraged until one hour before; solids stop at six hours; under six months breast milk stops at three hours and formula at four; over six months breast milk stops at four and formula counts as a solid.

Emergencies are different. The child with [appendicitis or intussusception](../c/paediatric-appendicectomy-and-intussusception.html), bowel obstruction or a painful injury has a stomach that stopped emptying when the illness began, and the baby with [pyloric stenosis](../c/pyloromyotomy.html) has a stomach that cannot empty at all. These are managed with a nasogastric tube aspirated before induction where one is in place, an intravenous induction with a full dose of relaxant, gentle mask ventilation at low pressure while it works (a small child desaturates before any relaxant acts if left apnoeic), and a cuffed tube.

## Fluids and glucose

The 4-2-1 rule (100 mL/kg/day for the first 10 kg, 50 for the next 10, 20 thereafter) was derived in 1957 from the energy use of healthy children, and it was paired with hypotonic fluid. A child who is ill or has had surgery secretes antidiuretic hormone in response to pain, nausea, opioid and hypovolaemia and cannot excrete free water. Hypotonic fluid at the full rate then dilutes the plasma, and children have died of hyponatraemic encephalopathy after tonsillectomy and appendicectomy. In the PIMS trial of 690 hospitalised children, isotonic fluid cut hyponatraemia from 11% to 4% compared with a solution containing 77 mmol/L of sodium.

| Phase | Fluid | Rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Resuscitation | Balanced isotonic crystalloid, no glucose | 10 mL/kg boluses to effect |
| Intra-operative background | Balanced isotonic crystalloid with 1-2.5% glucose in infants and the at-risk | About 10 mL/kg/h for the first hour, then by losses |
| Replacement of losses | Balanced isotonic crystalloid; blood by volume | Titrated |
| Postoperative, until drinking | Isotonic fluid with glucose | Two-thirds of the 4-2-1 rate |

The German guideline's intra-operative recommendation is the simplest way to avoid both errors: a balanced isotonic solution with 1-2.5% glucose as the background infusion keeps the glucose normal without the hyperglycaemia of 5% dextrose, and the same solution without glucose is given for hypovolaemia. Healthy children over about a year who drank an hour ago do not need glucose for a short case. Those who do are neonates and young infants, children on parenteral nutrition or a glucose infusion on the ward (which continues), small or malnourished children, and anyone fasted for many hours. Measure the glucose in any infant and in any long case. A day-case child who was drinking until called and will drink in recovery often needs no intravenous fluid beyond the drugs.

Blood loss is judged against a circulating volume of about 80-90 mL/kg in the neonate and 70-75 mL/kg in the child: 100 mL from a 5 kg infant is a quarter of the blood volume.

## The airway: what is different

| Difference | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| Large occiput, short neck | The head flexes on a flat table: a roll under the shoulders for infants, no pillow |
| Large tongue, small mandible | Obstructs with the first breath of sevoflurane; an oral airway early |
| Larynx high, epiglottis long and floppy | A straight blade lifting the epiglottis under about a year |
| Narrow subglottis, cricoid ring cannot expand | Tube size matters; oedema of 1 mm in a 4 mm airway cuts the area by three-quarters |
| Short trachea (4-5 cm in the newborn) | Endobronchial intubation and accidental extubation with head movement; check after every position change |
| Oxygen use per kg twice the adult's, small reserve | Saturation falls within seconds of apnoea |
| Compliant stomach, diaphragmatic breathing | Gas blown into the stomach splints the diaphragm: decompress it |

Cuffed tubes are now standard from term. The objection was subglottic injury; the evidence removed it. In 2,246 children from birth to five years randomised to cuffed or uncuffed tubes, post-extubation stridor was the same (4.4% and 4.7%), while the tube had to be changed for size in 2.1% of the cuffed group and 30.8% of the uncuffed. A cuff also allows low flows, reliable capnography and protection during laparoscopy. The conditions are a tube designed for children and a cuff pressure measured and kept at 20 cmH2O or less.

```
  cuffed tube, internal diameter (mm)     (age / 4) + 3.5 from 2 years
                                          (the original trial formula was
                                           age / 4 + 3 for older tube designs)
  term to 8 months  3.0       8 months to 2 years  3.5
  depth at the lips (cm)                  (age / 2) + 12
  supraglottic airway by weight           under 5 kg: 1     5-10 kg: 1.5
                                          10-20 kg: 2       20-30 kg: 2.5
                                          30-50 kg: 3
```

Have the size above and below open on the trolley.

## Laryngospasm

Laryngospasm is reflex closure of the cords that outlasts its stimulus. It is the commonest serious event in children's anaesthesia because the reflex is brisk, the stimulus (secretions, blood, a tube removed at the wrong depth) is common, and the oxygen reserve is small. The risk is higher in infants, after airway surgery, with a recent cold, with a history of asthma or eczema, with exposure to smoke at home, and with a less experienced anaesthetist. In the Perth cohort of 9,297 children a positive respiratory history carried a relative risk of 4.13 for laryngospasm and 8.46 for bronchospasm.

Recognition: stridor or silence with tracheal tug and a see-saw chest, a flat capnograph, then desaturation and bradycardia. Treatment is a sequence, and the mistake is to linger on any step while the saturation falls.

```
  1. Call for help. Stop the stimulus. 100% oxygen.
  2. Tight mask, firm jaw thrust with pressure behind the mandible,
     continuous positive pressure. Do not force gas into the stomach.
  3. Saturation still falling, intravenous access present:
        propofol 1 mg/kg IV
  4. No better, or saturation below about 85%, or pulse slowing:
        suxamethonium 1-2 mg/kg IV with atropine 20 microg/kg
        no access: suxamethonium 4 mg/kg intramuscular
  5. Ventilate, intubate if needed, empty the stomach, then look for
     aspiration and negative-pressure pulmonary oedema.
```

Prevention is half of it: an intravenous cannula before any airway instrumentation where possible, suction of the pharynx under deep anaesthesia, and removal of the airway either deep or fully awake and never in between.

## The child with a cold

A preschool child has six to eight colds a year, so the question arises on most lists. The airway is hyper-reactive during the illness and for a period after it. In the 1,078-child study children with a current or recent infection had more breath holding, more desaturation below 90% and more respiratory events overall, yet the authors concluded that most can have elective surgery without serious harm. The Perth cohort found the excess risk concentrated in children with current symptoms or symptoms in the previous two weeks.

| Proceed | Proceed with modifications | Postpone about two weeks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Clear runny nose, well, afebrile, playing, over a year old | Moist cough or green nasal discharge but well; history of asthma; short surgery not on the airway | Fever above 38 degrees, lethargy, wheeze or crackles, purulent sputum; under one year; airway surgery that is not urgent |

The modifications are the ones with evidence behind them: an experienced anaesthetist; intravenous induction with propofol where a cannula can be placed calmly; a mask or supraglottic airway in place of a tube where the operation allows, since a tube under five years was an independent risk factor; no desflurane; and inhaled salbutamol beforehand, which cut respiratory events from 47.9% to 27.8% in children having tonsillectomy. Postponement has a cost: the child having [adenotonsillectomy](../c/adult-and-paediatric-tonsillectomy-and-adenoidectomy.html) for obstruction or grommets for recurrent infection may never present without a runny nose.

## Premedication and induction

Anxiety peaks between one and five years, when the child is old enough to fear separation and too young to be reasoned with. The measures, in order of use: preparation and play, local anaesthetic cream on both hands, a calm parent, and a sedative for the child who needs one.

| Premedicant | Dose and route | Onset | Character |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Midazolam | 0.5 mg/kg orally, to 15-20 mg | 20-30 min | Reliable anxiolysis; bitter; occasional disinhibition |
| Clonidine | 4 microg/kg orally | 45-60 min | Sleepy and rousable; reduces emergence delirium; slow |
| Dexmedetomidine | 1-2 microg/kg intranasally | 30-45 min | Natural-looking sleep; painless to give |
| Ketamine | 3-5 mg/kg orally or intramuscularly | 10-20 min | For the combative or needle-phobic older child |

A meta-analysis of eleven trials found intranasal dexmedetomidine gave better sedation at separation and at induction than oral midazolam. Parental presence at induction is expected by families and is good practice, but trials do not show that it lowers the child's anxiety; an anxious parent raises it. The parent is briefed on what they will see (eyes rolling, limbs moving, noisy breathing) and is led out the moment the child is asleep.

Induction is intravenous when a cannula can be sited without a fight, because it is quicker and carries fewer respiratory events, and inhalational with sevoflurane when it cannot. The inhalational induction has one dangerous interval, between loss of consciousness and intravenous access, when laryngospasm has no drug treatment except the intramuscular route.

## The neonate and the former premature infant

A neonate is a different organism, and the [neonatal emergencies](../c/neonatal-emergencies.html) are done in specialist centres for that reason. The European NECTARINE audit of 5,609 infants in the first weeks of life recorded an event needing intervention in 35.2% of anaesthetics, mostly hypotension or desaturation. The physiology explains why.

- Cardiac output depends on heart rate, because the ventricle is stiff and stroke volume is nearly fixed. Bradycardia is a fall in output and is hypoxia until proved otherwise.
- Breathing control is immature. Periodic breathing is normal; apnoea after anaesthesia is the specific risk.
- Glycogen stores are small. Glucose is measured and a glucose-containing infusion continues.
- Heat is lost through a large surface and made only by brown fat, which volatile agents switch off.
- The kidney cannot concentrate or excrete a sodium load well, and drug clearance is a fraction of the adult's.
- The premature retina and lung are injured by oxygen, so the fraction is titrated to a saturation target and not left at 100%.

Postoperative apnoea is the question that reaches the general list, because former premature infants present for [inguinal hernia repair](../c/paediatric-inguinal-hernia-orchidopexy-and-circumcision.html). In the combined analysis of 255 such infants the risk fell with both gestational age at birth and post-conceptual age at surgery, and did not fall below 1% until 56 weeks post-conceptual age in a baby born at 32 weeks, or 54 weeks in one born at 35 weeks. Anaemia added risk. The usual policy that follows is admission with apnoea monitoring overnight for any former premature infant under about 60 weeks post-conceptual age, and for term babies in the first month or two. Spinal anaesthesia without sedation reduces early apnoea (1% against 3% in the first 30 minutes in the GAS trial) but not apnoea over the following 12 hours, and prematurity remained by far the strongest predictor, so the monitoring is the same whichever anaesthetic is given.

## Temperature

An infant has two to three times the adult's surface area for each kilogram and a head that accounts for a large share of it. Anaesthesia abolishes vasoconstriction and non-shivering thermogenesis, and a small abdomen opened under cold lights loses heat by evaporation as well. Hypothermia in the neonate means apnoea, acidosis, slow drug clearance and bleeding.

The routine: warm the theatre before the child arrives, keep the child covered through induction and line placement (which is when most heat is lost), forced-air warming under and around the body, warmed fluids and skin preparation, a hat, and a temperature probe in every case over half an hour. Small children under forced air also overheat, which is the other reason to measure.

## Caudal and other blocks

Blocks in children are placed after induction, and the paediatric registry of more than 100,000 blocks supports that: no permanent neurological injury, transient deficit in 2.4 per 10,000, and no added risk from the child being asleep. Severe local anaesthetic toxicity (0.76 per 10,000) clustered in infants, which is the reason for the lower ceilings.

| Ceiling, single dose | Under 6 months | Over 6 months |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bupivacaine or levobupivacaine | 2 mg/kg | 2.5 mg/kg |
| Ropivacaine | 2 mg/kg | 3 mg/kg |
| Lidocaine | 3 mg/kg | 3 mg/kg; 7 mg/kg with adrenaline |
| Infusion of bupivacaine or ropivacaine | 0.2 mg/kg/h | 0.4 mg/kg/h |

Work out the ceiling in millilitres of the chosen concentration, and include what the surgeon will infiltrate. In the registry's caudal series a quarter of children had received more than 2 mg/kg of bupivacaine equivalent.

| Block | Operations | Volume of 0.2% ropivacaine or 0.25% levobupivacaine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Caudal | Circumcision, hypospadias, orchidopexy, hernia, anorectal surgery, up to about 20-25 kg | 0.5 mL/kg sacral; 1 mL/kg to cover the groin; 1.25 mL/kg low thoracic (use 0.2%) |
| Ilioinguinal or transversus abdominis plane | Hernia, orchidopexy (groin incision) | 0.2-0.3 mL/kg per side |
| Rectus sheath | Umbilical hernia, pyloromyotomy, laparoscopic ports | 0.1-0.2 mL/kg per side |
| Dorsal penile | Circumcision | 0.1 mL/kg per side, plain solution, no adrenaline |
| Wound infiltration by the surgeon | Everything else | Within the ceiling |
| Awake spinal | Hernia repair in the former premature infant | Bupivacaine 1 mg/kg |

The caudal has forgiving anatomy under about six years and a good record: complications in 1.9% of 18,650 registry caudals, nearly all failed block or blood in the needle, with no lasting harm. The traps are intravascular injection (aspirate, inject slowly in portions, watch the electrocardiogram), the low dural sac of the infant, and the sacral dimple that suggests dysraphism. A single-shot caudal lasts four to six hours; clonidine 1 microg/kg, preservative-free, roughly doubles that and is avoided in young infants because of apnoea. For [circumcision](../c/scrotal-and-penile-surgery.html) the penile block and the caudal give equivalent analgesia, and the penile block spares the legs and the bladder.

## Emergence delirium

A preschool child who wakes from sevoflurane thrashing, staring through the parent and impossible to console has emergence delirium. It lasts 10 to 30 minutes and ends without treatment, but in that time the child can pull out a cannula, a drain or a fresh [cleft repair](../c/cleft-lip-and-palate-repair.html). The scale used to diagnose it scores eye contact, purposeful action, awareness of surroundings, restlessness and inconsolability. The first three distinguish it from pain: a child in pain looks at the parent and is comforted.

It is commonest between two and five years, after ear, nose, throat and eye surgery, and after sevoflurane or desflurane. A meta-analysis of 37 trials found propofol, alpha-2 agonists, ketamine, fentanyl and good analgesia each reduced it by roughly two-thirds or more; midazolam premedication did not. The practical prevention is certainty about analgesia (a block or opioid, so that pain is excluded), then one of: propofol maintenance, propofol 1 mg/kg IV at the end of a sevoflurane anaesthetic (4.8% against 26.8% in a trial of non-painful procedures), or clonidine or dexmedetomidine as premedication or during the case. Treatment is the same propofol dose, a quiet dim bay, and the parent warned beforehand.

## Anaesthesia and the developing brain

Anaesthetic drugs cause neuronal apoptosis and later learning deficits in young animals, at doses and durations comparable to clinical ones. Three human studies with different designs have tested whether this matters for a child having ordinary surgery.

| Study | Design | Exposure | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GAS | Randomised trial, 722 infants, hernia repair | Just under an hour of sevoflurane against awake regional | No difference in development at two years; IQ equivalent at five years |
| PANDA | 105 sibling pairs | One anaesthetic before 36 months | Mean IQ 111 in both exposed and unexposed siblings |
| MASK | Population cohort, 997 children | None, one, or several anaesthetics before three years | IQ no different; lower processing speed and fine motor scores after multiple exposures only |

The consensus reading is that a single short anaesthetic in infancy does not affect intelligence. Uncertainty remains about repeated or prolonged exposure and about behavioural outcomes, where associations are reported more often and confounding by the illness that required the surgery is hard to remove. What parents are told: surgery that is needed should not be delayed for this reason; there is good evidence that one brief anaesthetic is safe for development; and for a truly elective procedure in an infant it is reasonable to ask whether it can wait.

## Analgesia after surgery

Children's pain after general surgery is mostly incisional and inflammatory and responds to simple drugs given by the clock. The plan has four parts: a block or infiltration that covers the first hours; paracetamol and ibuprofen regularly, both, for two or three days and not "as needed"; a small supply of oral opioid for the few operations that need it; and dexamethasone for its analgesic and antiemetic effect, which PROSPECT includes for tonsillectomy alongside paracetamol and an NSAID. The parent leaves with times written down for the first 24 hours, because under-dosing at home is the usual failure.

Codeine has no place. It is a prodrug converted to morphine by CYP2D6: poor metabolisers get no analgesia and ultra-rapid metabolisers get an overdose. The reported deaths were in young children after adenotonsillectomy for sleep apnoea who carried gene duplications. Regulators have since restricted codeine to those aged 12 and over, and excluded it under 18 after tonsillectomy or adenoidectomy. Tramadol depends on the same enzyme for its active metabolite and carries equivalent restrictions; Australian children's hospital practice keeps it for those of 12 years and over.

When an opioid is needed, oxycodone or morphine at the doses in the table is used. The child with obstructive sleep apnoea having tonsillectomy is more sensitive to opioid, not less, because recurrent hypoxia up-regulates the receptors: the dose is halved, and the first night is spent monitored when the apnoea is severe.
