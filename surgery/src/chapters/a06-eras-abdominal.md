## The stress response is the thing being treated

A laparotomy injures tissue, and the body answers through two channels. Afferent nerves from the wound and the peritoneum drive the hypothalamus and the sympathetic system: cortisol, catecholamines, antidiuretic hormone, aldosterone and glucagon rise within minutes. Cytokines released from the wound and the handled bowel (interleukin-6 above all) drive the acute phase response over the following hours and days. The combined result is useful for an animal that has to survive an injury without help and unhelpful for a patient who has help:

```
 incision, bowel handling, blood loss, fasting, cold, pain
        |                                   |
  neural afferents                     cytokines (IL-6)
        |                                   |
  cortisol, catecholamines,          acute phase response,
  ADH, aldosterone, glucagon         fever, capillary leak
        \                                   /
   insulin resistance   --> hyperglycaemia, infection
   protein catabolism   --> weakness, poor healing
   salt and water kept  --> oedema of gut and lung
   sympathetic drive    --> ileus, tachycardia, ischaemia
```

Insulin resistance is the central metabolic lesion. Its size tracks the magnitude of the surgery, it lasts for days to weeks after an open operation, and it is the common pathway to hyperglycaemia, muscle loss and infection. Enhanced recovery is not a list of rituals. It is the observation that each input to that diagram can be made smaller (a laparoscope instead of a laparotomy, a fed patient instead of a starved one, a blocked afferent pathway instead of an opioid, a normal salt and water balance instead of four litres positive) and that the reductions add up. The Enhanced Recovery After Surgery Society guideline for colorectal surgery grades each element; this chapter covers the ones the anaesthetist owns. Compliance with the whole pathway matters more than any single item, and that is a statement about systems as much as about physiology.

## Before the incision: fed, not starved

The fasting rule is the easy part: clear fluids until two hours before induction and solids until six hours before, with patients encouraged to drink rather than permitted to. A patient who arrives after 14 hours without food or fluid is already catabolic and mildly dehydrated, and the first litre of your fluid plan goes on replacing an avoidable deficit.

Carbohydrate loading goes a step further. A clear maltodextrin drink the evening before and again two to three hours before surgery puts the patient into a fed, insulin-secreting state at the time of incision, which blunts postoperative insulin resistance. The physiology is measurable: the Cochrane review found improved insulin sensitivity on clamp studies. The clinical gain is small: across 27 trials, length of stay was 0.30 days shorter against fasting or placebo, the difference disappeared when the comparison was with a placebo drink alone, and complications were unchanged. No trial reported aspiration. The reasonable reading is that the benefit comes mostly from not starving, that the drink is safe, and that it is a low-cost element worth keeping. The exclusions are the patients whose stomach does not empty on schedule: gastric outlet or bowel obstruction, severe gastroparesis, and, by the author's judgement, those on a GLP-1 receptor agonist, in whom the standard clear fluid interval cannot be assumed.

The other preoperative elements belong to the clinic but change your day: anaemia found and treated weeks ahead, alcohol and smoking stopped, a prehabilitation programme for the deconditioned, and no routine sedative premedication, which delays the first drink and the first walk.

## Fluid: the curve has two sides

Too little fluid and too much fluid both cause complications, and the history of the subject is a swing between the two. The liberal era rested on two ideas that did not survive measurement: that fasting leaves a large deficit, and that an open abdomen loses large volumes into a third space. Blood volume after an ordinary fast is normal, evaporative loss from an open abdomen is small, and no fluid-consuming third space has been demonstrated. Crystalloid given in excess of need does not stay in the circulation; it loads the interstitium, and hypervolaemia itself strips the endothelial glycocalyx and increases the leak. The result of six or seven litres on the day of surgery is a patient several kilograms heavier with an oedematous anastomosis, a gut that will not move and wet lungs.

The correction went too far. "Zero balance" or restrictive regimens were adopted on the strength of small trials, and RELIEF tested the idea at scale: 2,983 patients at increased risk having major abdominal surgery in Australia, New Zealand and elsewhere, randomised to a restrictive or a liberal regimen for surgery and the first 24 hours. The restrictive group received a median of 3.7 L and the liberal group 6.1 L. Disability-free survival at one year was the same (81.9% against 82.3%). Acute kidney injury was more frequent with restriction (8.6% against 5.0%), and surgical site infection (16.5% against 13.6%) and renal replacement therapy (0.9% against 0.3%) pointed the same way.

```
 complications
   |\                                   /
   | \   hypovolaemia:          oedema: /
   |  \  AKI, hypoperfusion,   ileus,  /
   |   \ anastomotic ischaemia leak,  /
   |    \                    lungs   /
   |     \_________________________ /
   |          modestly positive
   +-------------------------------------> fluid given
       RELIEF restrictive   RELIEF liberal
```

What RELIEF supports is a moderately liberal approach for major open abdominal surgery: a balanced crystalloid at a maintenance rate that covers the operation, replacement of measured losses, and a net balance at the end of the day that is positive by a litre or two rather than at zero. It does not rehabilitate the six-litre anaesthetic; the liberal arm of the trial was moderate by historical standards. Use a balanced solution rather than 0.9% saline for volume, because large volumes of saline produce a hyperchloraemic acidosis. Stop intravenous fluid when the patient is drinking, which in an elective colorectal pathway is the evening of surgery or the next morning.

The laparoscopic case sits lower on the curve. The abdomen is closed, losses are small, and the head-down position for pelvic surgery turns excess fluid into facial and airway oedema ([laparoscopy chapter](a01-laparoscopy-pneumoperitoneum.html)).

## Goal-directed therapy: a sound idea that did not survive its trial

The logic is attractive. Hypovolaemia that is too subtle to change the blood pressure still reduces stroke volume and gut perfusion; a monitor that measures stroke volume lets you give a 250 mL bolus, see whether stroke volume rises by 10%, and stop when it no longer does, placing each patient at the top of the steep part of their own Starling curve. Add a low-dose inotrope and oxygen delivery rises further.

OPTIMISE tested this in 734 high-risk patients having major gastrointestinal surgery: complications or death at 30 days occurred in 36.6% with the algorithm and 43.4% with usual care, a relative risk of 0.84 whose confidence interval crossed one, and the accompanying meta-analysis of 38 trials suggested fewer complications. OPTIMISE II was the definitive trial: 2,498 patients aged 65 or over having major elective gastrointestinal surgery, randomised to cardiac output-guided fluid with low-dose dobutamine or to usual care. Postoperative infection was the same (23.2% against 22.7%). Acute cardiac events within 24 hours were more frequent with the intervention (3.0% against 1.7%), mostly tachyarrhythmias. The authors' conclusion is that routine use in unselected patients is not recommended.

Why the earlier signal disappeared is probably the comparator. Usual care in the 2020s is already a moderate fluid volume, a laparoscopic approach and early feeding, which leaves little occult hypovolaemia to find, and the inotrope has a price. What remains reasonable, as author's judgement:

- A stroke volume or pulse pressure variation monitor as a decision aid in the patient with major blood loss or fluid shifts, to answer the question of whether the next bolus will do anything, without a protocol that mandates boluses or an inotrope.
- Restraint in reading dynamic indices during laparoscopy, with an open chest, with spontaneous breathing, with arrhythmia or with low tidal volumes, all of which invalidate them.
- For emergency laparotomy the Enhanced Recovery guideline still advises considering goal-directed therapy in high-risk patients, on moderate evidence extrapolated largely from elective work. A large trial in that population, FLO-ELA, was not reported at the time of writing.

## Blood pressure and vasopressors

Most hypotension under anaesthesia for elective abdominal surgery is vasodilatation: the anaesthetic agents, the epidural or spinal, and the release of sympathetic tone. Treating a dilated circulation with volume moves the patient up the right-hand side of the curve above without addressing the cause. The correct drug is a vasopressor, and a low-dose noradrenaline or metaraminol infusion started at induction and titrated is cleaner than repeated boluses. The emergency laparotomy guideline notes that noradrenaline through a large peripheral vein is safe until central access is established.

The target is less settled than the lecture slides suggest. Large cohort analyses associate a mean pressure below 65 mmHg, or a fall of more than 20% from baseline, with kidney and myocardial injury in a dose and duration dependent way. INPRESS, in 292 high-risk patients mostly having abdominal surgery, found less postoperative organ dysfunction (38.1% against 51.7%) when systolic pressure was held within 10% of the patient's resting value with noradrenaline. The much larger POISE-3 comparison of a hypotension-avoidance with a hypertension-avoidance strategy found no difference in vascular complications (13.9% against 14.0%), although the separation in pressure between its groups was small. A defensible default is a mean pressure of at least 65 mmHg in everyone, and a systolic pressure within about 10-20% of the true resting value in the older hypertensive patient, the patient with chronic kidney disease, and anyone with a fixed coronary or carotid lesion.

An epidural deserves a specific plan. The sympathetic block outlasts the operation, the ward response to hypotension is a fluid bolus, and three boluses overnight undo the fluid strategy. Agree in advance that epidural hypotension on the ward is treated with a vasopressor in a monitored bed or by reducing the infusion, and that fluid is for the patient who is dry.

## Opioid-sparing analgesia

Opioids are an input to the diagram, not just a treatment for one. They slow the gut, cause nausea, sedate and delay mobilisation, so the pathway aims to block the afferent signal at the abdominal wall or the cord and to reserve opioid for rescue. The [truncal analgesia chapter](a02-truncal-blocks-epidural.html) covers technique selection; the points that matter for recovery are these.

- Open midline laparotomy has an [abdominal wall pain](../b/b01-why-operations-hurt.html) mechanism that is severe on coughing for days. Thoracic epidural analgesia gives the best dynamic pain relief and attenuates the neural limb of the stress response; its costs are hypotension, immobility if the block is too dense, and failure in a meaningful minority. Intrathecal morphine with rectus sheath or wound catheters is the common alternative.
- Laparoscopic resection does not justify an epidural. In a randomised comparison within an enhanced recovery programme, median stay was 3.7 days with an epidural against 2.7 days with spinal analgesia and 2.8 days with patient-controlled analgesia, and bowel function returned more slowly with the epidural. Intrathecal morphine or a fascial plane block with multimodal oral analgesia fits the short, [laparoscopic pain](../c/laparoscopic-and-open-colectomy.html) profile better.
- Paracetamol regularly, and an NSAID where the kidneys and the surgeon allow. Some colorectal surgeons avoid NSAIDs because of observational signals for anastomotic leak; surgeons vary, and the question is settled per unit rather than by trial.
- Intravenous lidocaine has lost its main argument. ALLEGRO randomised 557 patients having minimally invasive colonic resection to lidocaine infusion or placebo: return of gut function at 72 hours was 57.3% against 59.0%, with no difference in any secondary outcome. There is no case for routine use in laparoscopic colonic surgery; whether it helps the open case without a regional technique is not known from that trial.
- Ketamine by low-dose infusion (0.1-0.5 mg/kg/h in the emergency guideline's summary) is for the opioid-tolerant patient and the failed regional technique, at the cost of dysphoria in the elderly.
- Dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction reduces nausea and has an analgesic effect. PADDI settled the infection worry: surgical site infection was 8.1% with dexamethasone and 9.1% with placebo, noninferior, including in patients with diabetes.

## Nausea and vomiting

A patient who is vomiting is not drinking, eating or walking, so prophylaxis is a recovery intervention. Abdominal surgery, volatile anaesthesia, nitrous oxide and postoperative opioids all add risk to the patient factors (female sex, non-smoking status, previous nausea or motion sickness, younger age). The consensus guideline's approach is to reduce baseline risk and to give multimodal prophylaxis to anyone with risk factors: in practice dexamethasone at induction and a 5-HT3 antagonist at the end as the floor, with a third agent from a different class (droperidol, an NK1 antagonist, or propofol maintenance in place of volatile) for the high-risk patient. In PADDI, nausea and vomiting in the first 24 hours fell from 53.9% to 42.2% with dexamethasone alone, which shows both that it works and how much remains. Rescue is with a class that has not already been given. The [nausea chapter](a11-ponv-day-case.html) has the detail.

## The other intraoperative elements

- Temperature: active warming from the anaesthetic room and a core temperature of 36 degrees C or above at the end. Hypothermia increases bleeding, infection and the catecholamine response to shivering.
- Glucose: hyperglycaemia is the visible face of insulin resistance. The emergency guideline targets 7.7-10 mmol/L, by insulin infusion if needed, and that is a sensible elective range too.
- Ventilation: a lung-protective pattern with modest tidal volumes and PEEP. Upper abdominal incisions halve the functional residual capacity for days, and the patient who leaves theatre with atelectasis starts behind.
- Neuromuscular block: quantitative monitoring and a train-of-four ratio above 0.9 before extubation. Residual block is a respiratory complication and an aspiration risk in a patient with an ileus.
- Tubes: no routine nasogastric tube after elective surgery, drains only where the surgeon has a specific reason, and the urinary catheter out on day one or two unless there is an epidural or a low pelvic dissection.

## After the operation: use the gut and the legs

The gut recovers faster when it is used. Fluids on the evening of surgery and food the next day are the elective default; the fear that early feeding endangers an anastomosis has not been borne out, and the fed state reverses the catabolic signal. Mobilisation on the day of surgery protects the lungs and the calves and is only possible if the analgesia permits it and the patient is not attached to five lines. Ileus is minimised by the combination already described: laparoscopy, modest fluid, little opioid, early feeding and walking.

The anaesthetist's contribution to day one is a patient who is warm, pain-controlled on movement, not nauseated, not oedematous and free of unnecessary attachments at six o'clock on the evening of surgery.

## The emergency laparotomy: a different patient on the same table

The [emergency laparotomy](../c/emergency-laparotomy.html) patient has had none of the preparation above. They are starved, often septic, hypovolaemic from vomiting and sequestration, in pain, and older and frailer than the elective list. Mortality is an order of magnitude higher. Cohort studies a decade ago reported 30-day mortality of 14-18.5%, rising above 25% over the age of 80; in the United Kingdom's national audit it has since fallen from 11.8% to 9.3%. The audit's risk model was built on 38,830 patients with a 30-day mortality of 11.5%. In an analysis of the Australian and New Zealand audit, 12% of patients with complete records died, 42% went to intensive care and 29% did not return to their usual residence.

Enhanced recovery for this patient is a pathway built on speed and seniority.

| Stage | Element | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recognition | Early warning score, lactate, sepsis screen | Sepsis is the reversible killer; lactate marks risk even without sepsis |
| First hour | Antibiotics, fluid resuscitation started | Delay in antibiotics increases mortality in sepsis |
| Diagnosis | CT with contrast, reported at once | Defines the operation; must not delay a patient in extremis |
| Risk | Validated score, documented and discussed | Sets seniority, monitoring, destination and the conversation with the family |
| Timing | Source control within 3 h for septic shock, 6 h for sepsis | Mortality rises with delay |
| Theatre | Consultant surgeon and anaesthetist for high risk | Decisions on damage control, stoma, extubation |
| Intraoperative | Arterial line early, vasopressor, measured fluid | The patient is both dry and vasodilated |
| End of surgery | Rescore; extubation decided jointly | Risk changes with the findings |
| After | Critical care for high risk; delirium, nutrition and thrombosis plans | Most deaths follow a complication that was detected late |

Risk scoring is the hinge. A validated tool (the audit's own model has a C-statistic of 0.863; P-POSSUM and the surgical risk calculators are alternatives) is applied before surgery and again at the end, because the operative findings change the answer. The usual service standard is that a predicted mortality of 5% or more calls for consultant presence and consideration of critical care, and 10% or more for a critical care bed. No score should be applied to the individual as a prophecy; it is a prompt for resources and a number to anchor an honest conversation.

The evidence for the bundle is instructive. In four hospitals, a bundle of early senior assessment, prompt antibiotics, surgery within six hours of the decision, goal-directed resuscitation and postoperative critical care was followed by a fall in adjusted mortality from 15.6% to 9.6%. EPOCH then tested a national quality improvement programme to implement a similar pathway in 93 hospitals and 15,873 patients as a stepped-wedge trial, and found no difference in 90-day mortality (16% in both periods). The usual interpretation is that EPOCH tested the delivery of a programme, not the biology of the bundle: fidelity was poor, teams had neither time nor resources, and few process measures moved. The elements remain standard of care, and the lesson is that a pathway on paper does nothing.

For the anaesthetic itself:

- Resuscitate before induction where time allows, with an arterial line placed awake in the shocked patient. Induction is the most dangerous minute: a rapid sequence in a hypovolaemic, vasodilated patient with a full stomach. Reduce the induction dose, have the vasopressor running, and drain the nasogastric tube first.
- Run noradrenaline early and give balanced crystalloid in measured boluses against lactate, urine output and, where available, a stroke volume monitor. The guideline suggests a mean pressure of 60-65 mmHg and a cardiac index above 2.2 L/min/m2, individualised.
- Analgesia after assessment for sepsis and coagulopathy: an epidural or spinal is often unwise in the septic, coagulopathic patient, and rectus sheath or wound catheters with multimodal systemic analgesia are the usual answer.
- Decide extubation with the surgeon at the end. Ongoing vasopressor requirement, a lactate that is not clearing, hypothermia, an open abdomen or a planned relook argue for staying intubated.
- Hypoxaemia after extubation is treated with CPAP or non-invasive ventilation rather than more oxygen, where aspiration risk is low and the unit is skilled.
- Feed early. Tube feeding within 24 hours is advised where oral intake will be inadequate, and parenteral nutrition where the gut cannot be used.

## Frailty

Frailty is the loss of physiological reserve across systems, and it is a better predictor than age of what an emergency laparotomy will do to an older person. In 937 patients aged 65 or over, a fifth were frail, 90-day mortality was 19.5% overall, and after adjustment for age and sex the odds of death were 3.18 times higher at a Clinical Frailty Scale of 5 and 6.10 times higher at 6-7 than in the fittest group. Complications, intensive care stay and hospital stay rose in step.

The guideline asks for a frailty score and a simple cognitive test in everyone over 65. The score takes a minute and changes three things. It changes the consent conversation: survival is one outcome, and return to independent living is the one the patient usually cares about; in the Australasian data nearly a third did not go home. It changes the plan: a lower threshold for invasive monitoring and critical care, avoidance of deliriogenic drugs, depth of anaesthesia monitoring to avoid deep burst suppression, and early geriatric medicine involvement. And in the frailest patients with catastrophic pathology it opens the question of whether an operation is in the patient's interest, a decision for the senior surgeon, anaesthetist, intensivist, patient and family together, before induction and with a documented ceiling of care.

## Postoperative destination

The decision is made twice: provisionally at booking and finally at the end of the case.

| Destination | Elective | Emergency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ward enhanced recovery bed | Laparoscopic or uncomplicated open resection, fit patient | Predicted mortality under 5%, stable, no vasopressor |
| High dependency | Epidural needing a vasopressor, major open resection in a comorbid patient, significant blood loss | Predicted mortality 5-10%, frail, or abnormal lactate that is clearing |
| Intensive care | Unplanned: massive transfusion, ongoing vasopressor, failed extubation | Predicted mortality 10% or more, septic shock, vasopressor, ventilated, open abdomen |

Failure to rescue is the mechanism by which the wrong destination kills: the complication rate after major abdominal surgery varies less between hospitals than the death rate after a complication. A bed with the right nursing ratio and a doctor who will come is the treatment.

## The rule set

1. Think of each element as removing an input to the stress response; the gains are additive and depend on the whole pathway.
2. Clear fluids to two hours; carbohydrate drink unless the stomach will not empty.
3. Balanced crystalloid to a modestly positive balance for open surgery; less for laparoscopy; stop when drinking.
4. Treat anaesthetic and epidural vasodilatation with a vasopressor; keep the mean pressure at 65 mmHg or above and nearer baseline in the patient at risk.
5. No routine cardiac output-guided fluid and inotrope protocol in elective surgery; use the monitor as a decision aid in the bleeding or unstable patient.
6. Block the abdominal wall or the cord, give paracetamol and an NSAID where permitted, and keep opioid for rescue. No epidural for laparoscopic resection; no routine lidocaine infusion.
7. Dexamethasone and a second antiemetic for everyone; a third for high risk.
8. Warm, normoglycaemic, fully reversed, and free of unnecessary tubes at the end.
9. Emergency laparotomy: score the risk twice, antibiotics in the first hour, source control within three to six hours in sepsis, senior staff, arterial line, noradrenaline, and a critical care bed at 10% predicted mortality.
10. Measure frailty in everyone over 65 and let it shape the consent, the plan and the ceiling of care.
