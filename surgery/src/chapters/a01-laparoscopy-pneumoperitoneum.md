## Two insults: a pressure and a gas

The surgeon needs a room to work in. The abdominal wall is lifted off the viscera by gas delivered through a Veress needle or an open (Hasson) port, the insufflator holds a set pressure by topping up whatever leaks or is absorbed, and every instrument then works inside a pressurised dome. That gives two separate insults, and it helps to keep them apart in your head because they are fixed by different levers.

The first is mechanical. An intra-abdominal pressure of 12-15 mmHg, the conventional working range, is above normal central venous pressure and well above normal intra-abdominal pressure, so it is transmitted to everything compressible: the vena cava, the splanchnic and renal veins, the renal parenchyma, the diaphragm and, through the diaphragm, the chest. Its effects scale with the pressure set on the insufflator and are altered by tilt.

The second is chemical. Carbon dioxide is used because it is cheap, does not support combustion, and is so soluble in blood that a small intravascular bubble dissolves and is buffered and exhaled rather than lodging. The same solubility means that the peritoneum absorbs it continuously, so the patient acquires a second source of carbon dioxide on top of metabolism, and any gas that tracks into the wrong space (a vein, the subcutaneous tissue, the pleura, the mediastinum) is carbon dioxide and behaves differently from air in the same place: more of it is absorbed, and it leaves faster.

```
                 PNEUMOPERITONEUM
                        |
        +---------------+----------------+
        |                                |
     PRESSURE                          CO2
        |                                |
  veins squeezed  -> preload falls   absorbed -> more ventilation
  aorta, humoral  -> afterload up    acidosis -> PVR up, arrhythmia
  diaphragm up    -> FRC, compliance wrong space:
  renal vein      -> urine output      vein    -> embolism
  peritoneal      -> vagal brady       tissue  -> emphysema
  stretch                              pleura  -> capnothorax
```

## Venous return, afterload and the heart

The cleanest human data are still the pulmonary artery catheter study of 15 healthy patients having laparoscopic cholecystectomy at 14 mmHg in a 10 degree head-up tilt. Induction lowered arterial pressure and cardiac index; the tilt lowered cardiac index further; insufflation then raised mean arterial pressure by about 35%, lowered cardiac index by a further 20% or so, and raised systemic vascular resistance by about 65% and pulmonary vascular resistance by about 90%. The combined effect of anaesthesia, tilt and gas was a 50% fall in cardiac index from the awake value. The changes were largest in the first minutes after insufflation and partly recovered over the following half hour.

The causal chain behind those numbers:

1. Preload. The response of venous return to abdominal pressure depends on filling. In a well-filled patient at low pressure, the splanchnic reservoir is squeezed towards the heart and venous return is briefly maintained or raised. At working pressure, and at any pressure in a hypovolaemic patient, the vena cava is compressed, femoral venous flow slows, and venous return falls. Head-up tilt adds gravitational pooling in the legs to the same effect; head-down tilt offsets it.
2. Afterload. Systemic vascular resistance rises through direct compression of the aorta and splanchnic arterioles and through a humoral response (catecholamines, vasopressin, the renin-angiotensin system) that outlasts the first few minutes. Pulmonary vascular resistance rises with transmitted pressure, atelectasis and hypercarbia.
3. The result. Stroke volume falls because the ventricle is both under-filled and working against a higher resistance, while the arterial pressure on the monitor rises. The normal or high blood pressure is the product of a low output and a high resistance.

That last point is the practical one. A healthy heart tolerates a 20% fall in output without anyone noticing. A ventricle with an ejection fraction of 25%, severe aortic stenosis, or a failing right ventricle facing a near-doubling of pulmonary vascular resistance has no such reserve, and the standard monitor will not show the fall until it is advanced. The review literature is consistent that the haemodynamic effects are accentuated in heart failure, ischaemic and valvular heart disease, pulmonary hypertension and congenital heart disease. For these patients the plan is an arterial line before insufflation, a filled circulation before the head goes up, slow insufflation, the lowest workable pressure, the least tilt the surgeon can accept, and an agreed threshold for deflating. Laparoscopy is still usually the better operation for them, because the postoperative respiratory and analgesic cost of the open alternative is higher; the price is paid intraoperatively.

The other cardiovascular event belongs to the first minute. Rapid stretch of the peritoneum is a vagal stimulus, and bradycardia, nodal rhythm or asystole at first insufflation is well recognised, particularly in a young patient under light anaesthesia. Ask the surgeon to release the gas, give atropine or glycopyrronium IV, deepen the anaesthetic, and re-insufflate slowly. Bradycardia at insufflation that does not behave like this is gas embolism until shown otherwise.

## The lungs and the carbon dioxide load

The diaphragm is pushed towards the head. Functional residual capacity falls, the dependent lung collapses, and respiratory system compliance falls substantially at insufflation, further in a head-down tilt, and further still in an obese patient. With a fixed tidal volume the plateau pressure rises by the corresponding amount. Much of that rise is chest wall, not lung: the transpulmonary pressure has changed far less than the number on the ventilator, so a plateau pressure that would be alarming in an open abdomen is an expected finding with a pneumoperitoneum, and chasing it by cutting tidal volume produces atelectasis and hypercarbia.

The ventilatory approach that follows from the mechanism is to recruit after insufflation and positioning, apply enough PEEP to hold the dependent lung open against the raised abdominal pressure, and accept the higher plateau pressure that goes with it. How much PEEP is not settled. In the PROBESE trial of 1,976 obese patients, most having abdominal surgery, PEEP of 12 cmH2O with recruitment manoeuvres did not reduce postoperative pulmonary complications compared with 4 cmH2O (21.3% versus 23.6%), although it cut intraoperative hypoxaemia from 13.6% to 5.0%. PEEP during laparoscopy is therefore a tool for intraoperative oxygenation, titrated against its haemodynamic cost in a patient whose venous return is already impaired, and not a proven way to prevent postoperative lung problems.

The carina moves cephalad with the diaphragm while the tube stays fixed at the teeth. A tube that sat 2 cm above the carina in the supine position can be endobronchial after insufflation and head-down tilt. A rise in airway pressure with a fall in saturation in the minutes after positioning is endobronchial intubation first, bronchospasm second, and capnothorax third.

Carbon dioxide absorbed from the peritoneum has to be exhaled, and minute ventilation needs to rise to hold the arterial tension steady, most easily by rate. The end-tidal value rises over the first 15-30 min and then plateaus. Two patterns matter:

| Pattern | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Rise to a plateau over 15-30 min, controlled by more ventilation | Normal peritoneal absorption |
| Continued rise after 30 min despite more ventilation | Extraperitoneal gas (subcutaneous emphysema, retroperitoneal or mediastinal tracking), or capnothorax |
| Sudden fall with hypotension | Gas embolism, or any other cause of a sudden fall in cardiac output |

The arterial to end-tidal gradient widens with head-down tilt, obesity and lung disease, so in a long robotic case or a patient with chronic lung disease the end-tidal value under-reads, and an arterial gas is the measurement. Hypercarbia is not benign for everyone: it raises pulmonary vascular resistance, intracranial pressure and intraocular pressure, and it is arrhythmogenic.

## The kidney and the splanchnic bed

Oliguria during laparoscopy is expected and is mostly mechanical. Abdominal pressure compresses the renal vein and parenchyma, renal blood flow falls, and the humoral response (vasopressin, renin) completes the picture. In a systematic review, 17 of 20 studies found reduced renal blood flow and 20 of 25 found reduced renal function during pneumoperitoneum, with the size of the effect depending on baseline renal function, hydration, the pressure used, position and duration. Urine output recovers after deflation.

Two things follow. A low urine output during a pneumoperitoneum is a poor guide to volume status, and treating it with litres of crystalloid produces a wet patient with an oedematous bowel and, in a head-down case, a swollen face and airway. And the patient with chronic kidney disease, the donor in a [laparoscopic donor nephrectomy](../c/renal-transplantation-and-donor-nephrectomy.html) and the patient having a [partial nephrectomy](../c/nephrectomy-and-partial-nephrectomy.html) are the ones for whom the pressure setting is worth a conversation with the surgeon. Splanchnic and hepatic flow fall in proportion to pressure and duration by the same mechanism, which is of little consequence in the healthy and may matter in the patient with cirrhosis or mesenteric vascular disease.

## Tilt: head-up, head-down and the robot

Tilt is the surgeon's retractor: gravity moves the bowel out of the field. It is also the second variable in the haemodynamic equation.

Head-up (reverse Trendelenburg) for upper abdominal surgery such as [cholecystectomy](../c/laparoscopic-cholecystectomy-and-bile-duct-exploration.html), [fundoplication](../c/laparoscopic-fundoplication-and-hiatus-hernia-repair.html) and [bariatric surgery](../c/bariatric-surgery-sleeve-gastrectomy-and-gastric.html) helps the lungs and hurts the circulation. Venous pooling in the legs adds to caval compression, so hypotension at the moment the table tilts, a few minutes after induction and insufflation, is the predictable low point. Fill before the tilt, tilt in stages, and have the vasopressor running or drawn up.

Head-down (Trendelenburg) for pelvic surgery helps venous return and hurts almost everything else. For robotic pelvic surgery ([prostatectomy](../c/robotic-and-open-radical-prostatectomy.html), [hysterectomy](../c/laparoscopic-and-robotic-hysterectomy.html), [low rectal surgery](../c/anterior-resection-and-abdominoperineal-resection.html), [cystectomy](../c/radical-cystectomy-and-urinary-diversion.html)) the tilt is steep, 25 to 45 degrees, and lasts hours. In 31 patients monitored through robotic prostatectomy at 40 degrees, haemodynamic and respiratory variables stayed within clinically acceptable limits, cerebral perfusion pressure fell from 77 to 71 mmHg and regional cerebral oxygenation was preserved. Healthy patients tolerate it. The problems are slower and are the product of pressure multiplied by time:

1. Venous congestion of the head. Facial, conjunctival, laryngeal and tongue oedema develop over hours. Intraocular pressure climbs through the case: in 33 robotic prostatectomies it was on average 13 mmHg above the awake value by the end of the head-down period, and the predictors of the rise were duration and end-tidal carbon dioxide. The rare catastrophic expression is ischaemic optic neuropathy. Tape the eyes and protect them from the camera arm, restrict fluid until the patient is level, keep the end-tidal carbon dioxide controlled, and ask for a levelling break in a very long case. A patient with advanced glaucoma or raised intracranial pressure should not be put in this position without a specific discussion.
2. The airway at the end. A swollen face predicts a swollen larynx. Check for a leak around the deflated cuff before extubation, sit the patient up, and delay extubation if there is no leak after a long case.
3. The lungs. Compliance is at its worst with gas and steep head-down tilt together. Expect high plateau pressures, an endobronchial tube unless it was checked after positioning, and a wide arterial to end-tidal gradient.
4. Sliding and nerves. The patient slides towards the head unless held by a non-slip mattress. Shoulder braces hold the patient at the cost of the brachial plexus and are best avoided. Legs in stirrups for hours with the calves above the heart are at risk of compartment syndrome. The detail is in the [positioning chapter](a14-positioning.html).
5. Fluid. During prostatectomy and cystectomy the surgeon wants a dry field until the urinary tract is rejoined, and fluid given in the head-down phase goes to the face and airway. A restrictive strategy until the anastomosis, with replacement afterwards, suits both of you.

The robot itself changes the ergonomics. Once the arms are docked the table cannot be moved (unless the system has integrated table motion), the patient is under drapes and metal, and access to the airway and lines is poor. Everything is therefore placed, secured and tested before docking: a second cannula, extensions on every line, the arterial line if indicated, the tube position confirmed in the final tilt. Movement of a patient with instruments fixed inside the abdomen tears viscera, so neuromuscular block has to be reliable until undocking. The team should know how long an emergency undock takes and who calls it; for a cardiac arrest the answer is to deflate, undock and level while compressions start.

## When the gas goes where it should not

### Gas embolism

Small gas emboli are common and silent. With transoesophageal echocardiography, bubbles were seen in 11 of 16 laparoscopic cholecystectomies, arriving through the inferior vena cava at insufflation or during dissection of the gallbladder bed, and in every one of a series of laparoscopic hysterectomies, mostly as the round and broad ligaments were divided; none of those patients became unstable. Carbon dioxide dissolves quickly enough that small volumes do not matter.

A clinically significant embolus is rare and is a different event: a large volume delivered quickly, classically from a Veress needle or trocar placed in a vein or a solid organ, so that the insufflator pumps gas directly into the circulation at the very start of the case. It also occurs later when large veins are open under pressure, for example hepatic veins during laparoscopic [liver resection](../c/liver-resection.html). The gas lock in the right ventricle and pulmonary outflow obstructs output.

Recognition: sudden hypotension, tachycardia or bradycardia, a fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide (sometimes after a brief rise), desaturation, raised airway pressure, arrhythmia, and in a large embolus a mill-wheel murmur or arrest. A patent foramen ovale allows paradoxical embolism to the brain and coronaries.

Response, in order:

1. Tell the surgeon to stop insufflating and release the pneumoperitoneum. This removes the source and restores venous return.
2. Oxygen 100%, and stop nitrous oxide if it is in use.
3. Head down and left side down if the table allows, to move the gas away from the pulmonary outflow tract.
4. Support the circulation: fluid, vasopressor, adrenaline, and chest compressions early in a low-output state, which also break up the gas lock.
5. Aspirate from a central venous catheter if one is already in place. Do not delay the other steps to insert one.
6. Because the gas is carbon dioxide, recovery is usually quick once the source is removed. Persistent neurological signs after resuscitation suggest paradoxical embolism and are a reason for imaging and a hyperbaric opinion.

### Subcutaneous emphysema

Gas tracks into the abdominal wall from a port that has slipped into the extraperitoneal plane, from deliberate extraperitoneal insufflation (totally extraperitoneal hernia repair, retroperitoneal nephrectomy), or along tissue planes during a long dissection. The absorptive surface is large and the end-tidal carbon dioxide rises progressively and does not respond to ventilation as it should. In a series of 968 laparoscopies the incidence of subcutaneous emphysema was 2.3% and of pneumothorax or pneumomediastinum 1.9%; emphysema was predicted by an end-tidal carbon dioxide above 50 mmHg, an operating time over 200 min and the use of six or more ports.

Feel the chest wall and neck when the carbon dioxide will not come down. Crepitus confined to the abdominal wall and chest needs nothing beyond more ventilation and a word to the surgeon about port position and pressure. Crepitus in the neck and face means gas around the upper airway and probably in the mediastinum: look for a pneumothorax, expect a prolonged carbon dioxide load in recovery (the reservoir continues to empty into a patient who is now breathing spontaneously with opioid on board), and keep the patient intubated until the carbon dioxide is controllable and the airway is not at risk. The emphysema itself resolves in hours.

### Capnothorax, pneumomediastinum and pneumopericardium

Carbon dioxide reaches the pleura through a surgical breach (dissection at the oesophageal hiatus in fundoplication, hiatus hernia repair and [oesophagectomy](../c/oesophagectomy.html)), through congenital channels in the diaphragm, or by tracking from the mediastinum. Fundoplication is the operation where it is to be expected. The picture is a rise in airway pressure, a fall in compliance and saturation, a rising end-tidal carbon dioxide, and often a comment from the surgeon that the hemidiaphragm is bulging down into the field.

The management differs from that of an air pneumothorax, because the gas is soluble and the source can be switched off:

1. Confirm it is not an endobronchial tube. Listen, look at the diaphragm on the screen, and use ultrasound for lung sliding if there is doubt.
2. Stable patient: reduce the insufflation pressure, apply or raise PEEP to oppose the pressure gradient across the pleural hole, increase ventilation, stop nitrous oxide, and continue. The capnothorax resolves within about an hour of deflation without a drain.
3. Unstable patient (tension physiology): release the pneumoperitoneum, which usually decompresses the chest through the same hole; decompress the pleura if that does not work.
4. A drain is for a pneumothorax that persists or enlarges after the abdomen is deflated, which implies a lung injury and air, not carbon dioxide. A chest radiograph in recovery sorts the two.

## Deep or moderate block

The abdominal wall is the lid of the dome, and its compliance sets how much room a given pressure buys. Neuromuscular block relaxes the wall, and it stops the sudden movement (a cough, a hiccup, a diaphragmatic kick) that puts an instrument through something. The definitions: moderate block is a train-of-four count of 1-3; deep block is a train-of-four count of zero with a post-tetanic count of one or more. The diaphragm and abdominal wall are more resistant than the adductor pollicis, so a patient with one or two twitches at the thumb can still cough.

What deep block buys is modest and measurable. A meta-analysis of 12 studies found that it improved the surgeon's rating of the operating space by 0.65 points on a five-point scale, made low-pressure pneumoperitoneum more feasible, and lowered pain scores in recovery by about half a point. What it does not buy is a proven reduction in complications, and it has a ceiling: a critical review found that laparoscopic cholecystectomy attempted at 8 mmHg failed in about 40% of cases even at a post-tetanic count of one or less. Some abdomens are not compliant whatever the block.

The defensible position: deep block is worth having where space is the limiting factor (the obese patient, the low-pressure strategy, the long pelvic or retroperitoneal dissection, the robot, where movement is dangerous) and is unnecessary for a short operation with good exposure. It is only safe with quantitative monitoring and a reversal agent that works from that depth, which in practice means rocuronium with sugammadex dosed to the measured depth, and a train-of-four ratio above 0.9 confirmed before extubation. When the surgeon reports a tight abdomen, measure before giving more drug: with a post-tetanic count already at one or two, the answer is the insufflation pressure, the tilt, a full stomach or bladder, or the patient's anatomy.

## Low-pressure pneumoperitoneum

If the insults scale with pressure, the obvious experiment is to lower it, usually to 6-10 mmHg against a standard of 12-15 mmHg. The evidence is weaker than the logic.

For laparoscopic cholecystectomy, the Cochrane review (21 trials, 1,092 participants, all but one at high risk of bias) found that the operation could be completed at low pressure in about 90% of patients, at a cost of about 2 min of operating time, with no demonstrated difference in serious adverse events, conversion or length of stay, and concluded that there was no evidence to support routine use in low-risk patients. A broader systematic review found that low pressure reduces postoperative pain, has no clinical cardiopulmonary advantage in patients who are otherwise well managed, and shows signals for less hepatic and renal injury that have no clinical consequence in the healthy. The PROSPECT group, looking only at pain, recommends low-pressure pneumoperitoneum for laparoscopic cholecystectomy. In laparoscopic colorectal surgery, a randomised trial of 178 patients comparing 8 mmHg with deep block against 12 mmHg with moderate block found a better quality of recovery on day 1 (Quality of Recovery-40 score 167 versus 159).

The European endoscopic surgery guideline reached the sensible summary two decades ago: use the lowest pressure that gives adequate exposure, not a routine number. For the anaesthetist the request is most worth making for the patient with poor ventricular function, pulmonary hypertension, renal impairment or severe lung disease, where the physiological argument is strongest even though the trials did not study them, and for any patient in whom shoulder pain and early recovery matter, which is most day cases. The trade is exposure: a surgeon who cannot see is a worse outcome than 4 mmHg of extra pressure.

## Shoulder tip pain

Pain after laparoscopy has three components with different time courses, which is the reason the "laparoscopic" mechanism tag in the [pain model](../b/b01-why-operations-hurt.html) carries a day 0-1 peak: port-site pain (somatic, worst in the first hours, covered by infiltration), visceral pain from the operated organ, and referred shoulder tip pain. The last is the one patients were not expecting. Residual gas under the diaphragm, stretch of the diaphragm during the case, and peritoneal acidification by dissolved carbon dioxide irritate the phrenic afferents (C3-C5), and the pain is felt in the C4 dermatome over the shoulder. It typically appears as the patient sits up and mobilises, is worse on day 1 than in recovery, eases on lying flat, and responds better to NSAIDs than to opioids.

Prevention is surgical and cheap. PROSPECT recommends low-pressure pneumoperitoneum, saline lavage and active aspiration of the gas at the end of laparoscopic cholecystectomy. A pulmonary recruitment manoeuvre at the end of the operation, with the ports open and the patient head-down so that the inflating lungs push the gas out, reduced shoulder pain in a randomised trial of cholecystectomy patients; it costs a few sustained manual breaths and is reasonable to offer where the haemodynamics allow. Tell the patient beforehand that shoulder pain is expected, harmless and gone in two to three days, because unexplained shoulder pain at home generates telephone calls and readmissions.

## Patients and operations that change the plan

- Poor left ventricle, severe aortic stenosis, pulmonary hypertension or a failing right ventricle: invasive arterial pressure before insufflation, staged insufflation and tilt, low pressure, an agreed deflation threshold. Covered above.
- Raised intracranial pressure and ventriculoperitoneal shunts: abdominal pressure, hypercarbia and head-down tilt each raise intracranial pressure. Avoid head-down tilt, keep the carbon dioxide tightly controlled, and involve the neurosurgeons where a shunt lies in the field.
- Pregnancy: left lateral tilt from the second trimester, low pressure, tight control of maternal carbon dioxide because fetal carbon dioxide follows it. See [non-obstetric surgery in pregnancy](../c/cervical-cerclage-and-non-obstetric-surgery.html).
- Severe obstructive lung disease: bullae and high airway pressures are a poor combination, and the carbon dioxide load may be hard to clear. Low pressure, longer expiratory time, an arterial line, and readiness to convert.
- The hypovolaemic emergency ([appendicectomy](../c/laparoscopic-and-open-appendicectomy.html), ectopic pregnancy in [gynaecological laparoscopy](../c/laparoscopic-gynaecology.html)): a bleeding patient has no venous reserve to give up to caval compression. Resuscitate before insufflation or ask for a low pressure until the bleeding is controlled.
- Children and infants: lower pressures, a proportionately larger carbon dioxide load and a short trachea. See [paediatric laparoscopic and thoracoscopic surgery](../c/paediatric-laparoscopic-and-thoracoscopic-surgery.html).

## The rule set

1. Before insufflation: know the ventricle and the right heart; fill before a head-up tilt; arterial line for the patient with no cardiac reserve.
2. First insufflation is the moment for bradycardia and for massive embolism. Watch the monitor, not the screen, and have the surgeon ready to release the gas.
3. Hypotension with a normal heart: release some pressure, reduce the tilt, give volume and a vasopressor. Hypertension with a poor heart: the output has probably fallen; treat the afterload and the pressure setting, not the number.
4. Recheck the tube position after insufflation and final positioning. Recruit, then set PEEP for oxygenation; accept a higher plateau pressure.
5. End-tidal carbon dioxide that plateaus is normal; one that keeps climbing after 30 min is extraperitoneal gas or a capnothorax; one that falls suddenly with the blood pressure is an embolus.
6. Oliguria during the pneumoperitoneum is mechanical. Do not chase it with fluid.
7. Ask for the lowest pressure that gives the surgeon a view. Offer deep block, with quantitative monitoring and sugammadex, where space or stillness is the limiting factor.
8. Steep head-down tilt: non-slip mattress, no shoulder braces, eyes protected, fluid restricted until level, a cuff-leak test and a sitting patient before extubation.
9. Robot docked means no access and no table movement. Lines, tube and monitoring finished before docking; emergency undock rehearsed.
10. At the end: gas actively evacuated, a recruitment manoeuvre where tolerated, port sites infiltrated, and the patient warned about shoulder pain.
