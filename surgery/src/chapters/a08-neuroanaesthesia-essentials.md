## The closed box

The adult skull is rigid and its contents are almost incompressible: about 1,400 mL of brain, 150 mL of blood and 150 mL of cerebrospinal fluid. The Monro-Kellie doctrine is the arithmetic that follows. If one component grows, or a fourth appears (tumour, haematoma, oedema), another must shrink or the pressure rises.

The compensation has a fixed order and a small capacity. First, cerebrospinal fluid is displaced into the spinal subarachnoid space and its absorption increases. Second, venous blood is squeezed out of the compliant veins and sinuses. Together these absorb the first tens of millilitres of new volume with almost no change in pressure. When they are used up, there is nothing left to displace, and the pressure-volume relationship turns from flat to steep.

```
 ICP
(mmHg)
  60 |                                   * herniation
     |                                 *
  40 |                               *
     |                             *     steep limb: each mL adds
  22 |- - - - - - - - - - - - -  *       several mmHg
     |                      *  *
  10 |  *    *    *    *
     +----------------------------------------> added volume
        compensation     ^ reserve exhausted
        (CSF, venous blood)
```

Two practical consequences come from the shape of the curve. A patient on the flat part looks well and has a normal pressure of 7-15 mmHg, yet may be within a few millilitres of the bend; the history, the scan (midline shift, effaced sulci and cisterns) and the level of consciousness tell you where they are, not the number. And on the steep part, small volumes matter in both directions: the 10-20 mL of extra blood volume from a cough, a head-down tilt or a rise in carbon dioxide can precipitate herniation, and removing the same volume with hyperventilation or a few millilitres of ventricular drainage can rescue the patient.

Pressure matters because of what it does to flow. Cerebral perfusion pressure is mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure (or minus venous pressure if that is higher). A rising intracranial pressure lowers perfusion pressure, ischaemic brain swells, and the pressure rises further. At the end of that spiral, pressure gradients push brain through the tentorial notch or the foramen magnum. The Cushing response of hypertension and bradycardia is the brainstem's last attempt to perfuse itself and should be read as an instruction to lower intracranial pressure, not to treat the blood pressure.

## Cerebral blood flow and what sets it

The brain takes about 15% of the cardiac output, roughly 50 mL per 100 g per minute, to support an oxygen consumption of about 3-3.5 mL per 100 g per minute, and it has no store. Function fails when flow falls to around 20 mL per 100 g per minute, and membranes fail near 10. Four things set the flow, and each is a lever or a hazard.

```
 CBF
  |            PaCO2 (linear)          . PaO2 (only below
  |           /                       .   about 60 mmHg)
  |          /   ________________    .
  |         /   /  MAP plateau   \   .
  |        /   /   (autoregulation)\  '.....
  |       /   /                     \
  +---------------------------------------------
     20   40   60        MAP        150   mmHg
```

- Metabolism. Flow follows regional metabolic rate. Agents that reduce metabolism reduce flow and therefore blood volume, provided the coupling is intact; this is the whole basis of propofol as the neuroanaesthetic default. Seizures and fever raise metabolism and flow.
- Carbon dioxide. Between a PaCO2 of about 20 and 80 mmHg flow changes almost linearly, by 3-4% per mmHg. Hyperventilation is therefore the fastest way to reduce cerebral blood volume, and it works by vasoconstriction, which means it reduces flow to brain that may already be ischaemic. The effect also fades over hours as cerebrospinal fluid bicarbonate adjusts, after which returning to normocapnia causes rebound vasodilatation.
- Pressure. Autoregulation holds flow steady across a range of perfusion pressures, classically a mean arterial pressure of 60-150 mmHg in health, by adjusting arteriolar tone over seconds. The plateau is shifted to the right in chronic hypertension, is narrower than the textbook figure in many individuals, and is impaired or abolished around tumours, after trauma and subarachnoid haemorrhage, and by high-dose volatile agents. Where it is lost, flow follows pressure passively: hypotension causes ischaemia and hypertension causes hyperaemia, oedema and bleeding.
- Oxygen. Flow rises steeply only when PaO2 falls below about 60 mmHg, at which point hypoxia is adding blood volume to a tight head.

The fifth determinant is on the venous side. Cerebral venous outflow has no valves and is exquisitely sensitive to back pressure: head rotation or flexion that kinks a jugular vein, tube ties, a head-down table, high airway pressures and coughing all raise cerebral blood volume immediately.

## Producing a slack brain

The surgeon's request for a relaxed brain is a request to reduce intracranial volume so that retraction is gentle and the dura can be closed. The levers, in the order they are usually pulled:

| Lever | Action | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Venous drainage | Head up 15-30 degrees, neck neutral, two fingers between chin and sternum, no ties, abdomen free if prone | Head-up raises the risk of air entrainment and lowers arterial pressure at the head |
| Airway pressure | Full neuromuscular block, no coughing, modest PEEP | PEEP up to about 10 cmH2O is transmitted little if the head is up |
| Carbon dioxide | Normocapnia by default; PaCO2 30-35 mmHg briefly on request | Ischaemia; effect wanes; check against an arterial sample, not end-tidal alone |
| Agent | Propofol infusion in place of volatile; if volatile, below 1 MAC; no nitrous oxide | See below |
| Osmotherapy | Mannitol or hypertonic saline before the dura is opened | Needs an intact blood-brain barrier to work |
| Blood pressure | Avoid hypertension; treat hypotension | Depends on autoregulation |
| Cerebrospinal fluid | Lumbar or ventricular drainage by the surgeon | Not before the dura is open if there is a mass; risk of herniation |
| Steroid | Dexamethasone for vasogenic oedema around tumour | Works over hours; harmful in head injury |
| Last resort | Deepen to burst suppression, check for a new haematoma, decompress | |

When the brain is tight despite all of this, look for a simple cause before a pharmacological one: the head has been turned too far, the tube has migrated and the patient is straining against one lung, the carbon dioxide has risen, or the patient is light.

## Anaesthetic agents and the brain

| Agent | Metabolism | Flow and volume | Autoregulation and CO2 response | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Propofol | Reduced | Reduced in proportion | Preserved | Default maintenance where pressure is a concern and for evoked potentials |
| Sevoflurane, desflurane, isoflurane | Reduced | Direct vasodilatation offsets the fall; net increase above about 1 MAC | Impaired dose-dependently; CO2 response kept | Acceptable below 1 MAC with normal compliance; sevoflurane dilates least |
| Nitrous oxide | Raised | Raised | Kept | Avoid: raises volume, expands intracranial air and air emboli |
| Thiopentone | Reduced, to burst suppression | Reduced | Preserved | Rescue for refractory pressure |
| Ketamine | Regionally raised | Variable | Preserved | With controlled ventilation and a background hypnotic it does not appear to raise pressure; useful in the shocked head injury |
| Opioids | Little change | Little change if ventilation controlled | Preserved | Remifentanil for titratable control and fast wake-up |
| Dexmedetomidine | Reduced | Reduced | Preserved | Awake craniotomy, emergence, analgesic adjunct |
| Suxamethonium | Nil | Small transient rise in pressure | Nil | The rise is minor beside the cost of a failed or hypoxic intubation |

The clinical evidence for the first two rows is a randomised study in patients with tumours: under propofol-fentanyl, measured subdural pressure was lower, perfusion pressure higher and swelling after dural opening less than under isoflurane or sevoflurane. No trial shows a difference in neurological outcome, and for a patient with a small lesion and normal compliance a low-dose volatile is reasonable. For a tight brain, a large tumour with shift, or a head injury, total intravenous anaesthesia is the defensible default.

## Osmotherapy and fluids

The intact blood-brain barrier is impermeable to sodium and mannitol, so raising plasma osmolality draws water out of normal brain. That is a strength and a limit: osmotherapy shrinks the healthy brain to make room, and does little for the damaged region where the barrier is open.

Mannitol 0.25-1 g/kg IV (the range the head injury guideline restates) acts in two phases: an immediate fall in viscosity that improves flow and prompts autoregulatory vasoconstriction, and an osmotic effect that peaks after 20-30 minutes and lasts a few hours. It is a diuretic, so the hypovolaemia and potassium loss arrive an hour later, and the guideline cautions against giving it to a hypotensive patient. Hypertonic saline in an equiosmolar dose achieves the same shift without the diuresis and expands plasma volume, which suits the trauma patient; it raises sodium and chloride. Meta-analyses of small trials favour hypertonic saline for control of raised pressure and for brain relaxation at craniotomy (odds ratio 2.05 for good relaxation in nine trials), and a continuous prophylactic infusion after head injury did not improve functional outcome. Choice between the two is mostly about the patient's volume state and sodium. The agent is given early enough to be working when the dura is opened, and repeated doses are guided by measured sodium and osmolality.

For maintenance, use isotonic fluid and keep the patient euvolaemic. Hypotonic fluids (including, strictly, Ringer's lactate, which the European trauma guideline advises against in severe head injury) add free water to the brain. Albumin was associated with higher mortality than saline in head-injured patients in the follow-up of a large intensive care trial (33.2% against 20.4% at two years). Glucose-containing fluid is avoided unless treating hypoglycaemia, and both hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia harm injured brain.

## Blood pressure targets by disease

The same number can be right in one patient and dangerous in the next, because the question is always which is the greater risk: ischaemia from too little pressure, or bleeding and oedema from too much.

| Condition | Target | Reasoning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Severe head injury | Systolic at least 100 mmHg (age 50-69) or 110 mmHg (age 15-49 and over 70); perfusion pressure 60-70 mmHg once intracranial pressure is measured; mean at least 80 mmHg when bleeding elsewhere | Autoregulation is impaired and intracranial pressure is high, so hypotension is ischaemia. In the Traumatic Coma Data Bank, a single episode of systolic pressure below 90 mmHg was associated with a 150% increase in mortality |
| Subarachnoid haemorrhage, aneurysm unsecured | Systolic below about 160 mmHg, without hypotension or swings | The force on the aneurysm wall is the transmural pressure, arterial minus intracranial. A surge at laryngoscopy or pin placement, or a sudden fall in intracranial pressure from aggressive hyperventilation or drainage, both raise it |
| Temporary clip applied | Systolic raised by 10-20% | Collateral flow to the territory beyond the clip |
| Aneurysm secured; delayed ischaemia | Normotension to induced hypertension; euvolaemia, not hypervolaemia | Vasospastic territory is pressure-dependent |
| Ischaemic stroke, vessel occluded | Systolic 140-180 mmHg, diastolic below 105 mmHg | The penumbra is perfused through collaterals at maximal dilatation; any fall in pressure is a fall in flow |
| Ischaemic stroke, vessel reopened | Below 180/105 mmHg; do not drive below 120-130 mmHg systolic | Reperfused brain bleeds with high pressure, but intensive lowering worsened outcome |
| Elective tumour craniotomy | Within about 20% of baseline; strict avoidance of hypertension at emergence | Haematoma in the resection bed |

A few of these rows need their evidence stated.

Head injury. The thresholds come from the Brain Trauma Foundation guideline, which also sets the treatment threshold for intracranial pressure at 22 mmHg and advises against driving perfusion pressure above 70 mmHg with fluid and vasopressors because of lung injury. In the multiply injured patient this collides with [permissive hypotension](a05-major-haemorrhage.html), and the brain wins: the European trauma guideline asks for a mean of at least 80 mmHg. Steroids increase mortality after head injury (21.1% against 17.9% at two weeks in the CRASH trial). Decompressive craniectomy for refractory pressure nearly halved mortality at six months (26.9% against 48.9%) at the cost of more survivors with severe disability, which is the conversation behind the [head injury card](../c/traumatic-brain-injury.html). At induction, the priority is pressure: a reduced dose of induction agent, a vasopressor in hand, and an arterial line placed first if it takes under a minute.

Subarachnoid haemorrhage. The current American guideline emphasises early repair and avoidance of severe hypertension, hypotension and variability before the aneurysm is secured, and euvolaemia afterwards. Mild intraoperative hypothermia did not improve outcome in good-grade patients (66% against 63% good outcome), and ultra-early tranexamic acid did not improve outcome either. Nimodipine 60 mg enterally every four hours for 21 days is the one drug with an outcome claim, and it lowers blood pressure, so the pressure is supported rather than the nimodipine stopped. If the aneurysm ruptures during surgery or coiling, the manoeuvres are brief controlled hypotension or adenosine-induced flow arrest (0.3-0.5 mg/kg) to let the surgeon see, then restoration of pressure once a temporary clip is on, with blood ready. The [aneurysm card](../c/aneurysm-clipping-and-endovascular-coiling.html) has the sequence.

Thrombectomy. The intra-procedural window is expert consensus rather than trial evidence. A pooled analysis of three single-centre trials found less disability at three months with protocolised general anaesthesia than with sedation, despite more hypotension under general anaesthesia (80.8% against 53.1%); the reading is that either technique is acceptable if it does not delay the groin puncture and the pressure is held with a vasopressor from induction. After successful reperfusion, a systolic target of 100-129 mmHg did not reduce haemorrhage in one trial, and a target below 120 mmHg worsened functional outcome in another that was stopped early. See the [thrombectomy card](../c/endovascular-thrombectomy-for-stroke.html).

## Ventilation

Normocapnia (PaCO2 35-40 mmHg) and a PaO2 comfortably above 60 mmHg, confirmed on an arterial sample, are the defaults for every neurosurgical patient. The end-tidal to arterial gradient widens with head-up positioning, lung disease and low cardiac output, so a ventilator set to an end-tidal value of 30 mmHg may be producing an arterial value that is either adequate or dangerously low.

Hyperventilation is a bridge, not a setting. The head injury guideline advises against prolonged prophylactic hyperventilation to a PaCO2 of 25 mmHg or less, and the first day after injury, when flow is lowest, is the most hazardous time for it. A brief period at 30-35 mmHg is reasonable for an acutely tight brain on the table or a blown pupil on the way to the scanner, while the definitive treatment (osmotherapy, evacuation) is arranged. Come back to normocapnia gradually.

## Positioning and venous drainage

Neurosurgical positions are chosen for surgical access, and each has a venous cost. The [positioning chapter](a14-positioning.html) gives the detail; the principles are constant. Keep the head above the heart. Avoid extreme rotation and flexion: turn the shoulders with the head for a lateral approach, and keep two fingers' breadth between chin and sternum to protect both the jugular veins and the cervical cord. Tape the tube instead of tying it. In the prone position leave the abdomen free so that the vena cava and the epidural veins are not congested. In the park bench and sitting positions, check the neck again after the final adjustment.

Head fixation in a pin frame is a brief, intense stimulus. Without preparation it produces a surge in arterial pressure that is transmitted directly to a tight brain or an unsecured aneurysm. Pre-empt it with a remifentanil or propofol bolus, local infiltration at the pin sites or a scalp block, and tell the surgeon when you are ready, not the reverse. Once the head is pinned, any movement risks a scalp laceration or a cervical injury, which is the reason neuromuscular block or a deep remifentanil-based anaesthetic is maintained until the pins are out.

## Venous air embolism

Air enters a vein when the vein is open, held open, and at a pressure below atmospheric. The sitting position for [posterior fossa surgery](../c/posterior-fossa-surgery.html) provides all three: the wound is well above the heart, and the dural sinuses, diploic veins and emissary veins are held open by bone and dura and cannot collapse. The same physics applies at lower risk to any craniotomy with the head up, and to spinal surgery with the wound above the heart.

Small volumes entrained slowly are filtered by the pulmonary circulation and show only on a monitor. Larger volumes obstruct the pulmonary arterioles and provoke vasoconstriction: dead space rises, so end-tidal carbon dioxide falls, pulmonary artery pressure rises, and the right ventricle fails. A large bolus causes an airlock in the right ventricular outflow tract and cardiac arrest. If right atrial pressure exceeds left, air crosses a patent foramen ovale, present in about a quarter of adults, to the coronary and cerebral arteries; this is the reason for screening echocardiography before a planned sitting case.

Detection, in descending order of sensitivity: transoesophageal echocardiography, precordial Doppler (placed over the right heart, with its characteristic change in sound), then a fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide and a rise in pulmonary artery pressure, and lastly hypotension, arrhythmia and the mill-wheel murmur, which are signs of a large embolus. A sudden fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide with a stable ventilator in a head-up craniotomy is air until proved otherwise.

The response is a drill:

1. Tell the surgeon, who floods the field with saline, waxes the bone edges and looks for the open vein.
2. 100% oxygen; stop nitrous oxide if it is in use, since it expands the bubbles.
3. Stop further entry: lower the head if the table and pins allow, and compress the jugular veins briefly to raise venous pressure at the wound.
4. Aspirate from a central venous catheter if one was placed for the purpose, with the tip at the junction of the superior vena cava and right atrium.
5. Support the circulation: fluid, vasopressor, adrenaline for right ventricular failure, chest compressions for arrest (which also break up an airlock).
6. If the patient does not wake appropriately, consider paradoxical embolism and image.

Prevention is euvolaemia, the least head-up tilt the surgeon can work with, meticulous surgical haemostasis of bone and sinus, and avoiding nitrous oxide.

## Neuromonitoring and what the anaesthetic does to it

Monitoring functional pathways lets the surgeon change course before an injury is permanent, and it constrains the anaesthetic more than any other feature of the case. The rule that organises the pharmacology: the more synapses in the monitored pathway, the more anaesthetic agents depress the signal. Volatile agents and nitrous oxide act at synapses in cortex and anterior horn, so cortically generated and muscle-recorded responses suffer most.

| Modality | Pathway tested | Volatile agents | Propofol | Opioids | Neuromuscular block | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EEG, raw and processed | Cortex | Dose-dependent slowing to burst suppression | Same | Little | None | Depth, burst suppression, ischaemia at carotid clamping |
| Somatosensory evoked potentials | Dorsal columns to sensory cortex | Amplitude down, latency up; tolerable to about 0.5 MAC | Mild | Minimal | None; relaxant improves the signal | Spine, aneurysm, carotid |
| Motor evoked potentials (muscle) | Corticospinal tract, anterior horn, junction, muscle | Marked depression; often abolished | Mild to moderate, dose-dependent | Minimal | Abolishes the response | Spine deformity, intramedullary tumour, aneurysm, tumour near motor cortex |
| D-wave (epidural recording) | Corticospinal axons only | Resistant | Resistant | Resistant | Unaffected | Intramedullary cord tumour |
| Brainstem auditory evoked potentials | Cochlear nerve and brainstem | Resistant | Resistant | Resistant | None | Acoustic neuroma, microvascular decompression |
| Electromyography, free-running and triggered | Cranial or spinal nerve to muscle | Little | Little | Little | Abolishes the response | Facial nerve, pedicle screws |

For motor evoked potentials the position statement of the neurophysiological monitoring society is explicit that intravenous anaesthesia with propofol and an opioid is optimal. The working recipe is a propofol and remifentanil infusion at a steady state, a single intubating dose of relaxant that is allowed to wear off or is reversed before baseline recordings, no further relaxant, and a soft bite block between the molars, because transcranial stimulation makes the jaw clench hard enough to lacerate the tongue or bite through a tube. A patient with no neuromuscular block, a pinned head and an open wound must be kept still by depth of anaesthesia and remifentanil. Ketamine and dexmedetomidine in low dose are compatible adjuncts; a propofol bolus or a step change in infusion rate depresses the signal for minutes and should be announced.

Warning criteria depend on the modality and the operation. The conventional alarm for somatosensory potentials is a 50% fall in amplitude or a 10% increase in latency from baseline. For motor potentials, disappearance of a previously present response is always a major criterion; the D-wave thresholds are a fall of more than 50% for intramedullary surgery and more than 30-40% for surgery around the motor cortex.

When the neurophysiologist reports a change, the response is shared. Yours is to check and state aloud, within a minute: the mean arterial pressure (raise it; a cord or cortex at risk is pressure-dependent), the anaesthetic (any bolus, any change in rate, any volatile turned on), temperature, haematocrit, oxygenation and carbon dioxide, and whether a relaxant was given. A global, symmetrical change suggests anaesthesia or physiology; a focal change after a surgical manoeuvre suggests the surgery. The surgeon's is to pause, release retraction, remove the last clip or screw, and irrigate with warm saline.

## Emergence and the neurological assessment

The end of a craniotomy has three aims that pull against each other: a patient awake enough to be examined within minutes, no coughing or straining on the tube, and no hypertension. The examination is the monitor. A new deficit or a failure to wake found in theatre leads to an immediate scan; the same deficit found two hours later in a sedated patient is a missed haematoma.

Hypertension at emergence matters because the resection bed has just been made haemostatic at a normal pressure. In a case-control study within 11,214 craniotomies, postoperative haemorrhage occurred in 0.77%, and hypertension in the first 12 postoperative hours was found in 62% of those who bled against 25% of controls, an odds ratio of 4.6. The practical measures:

- Plan the wake-up from the start: short-acting agents, a scalp block or infiltration so that the wound is not the stimulus, paracetamol given, antiemetics given (vomiting raises intracranial pressure and obscures the examination).
- Keep the patient asleep and still until the head is out of pins and the dressing is on. Head bandaging is as stimulating as pinning.
- Run remifentanil at a low rate through extubation to suppress coughing, or use a small dose of lidocaine IV or dexmedetomidine; reverse the relaxant fully under quantitative monitoring.
- Have labetalol or esmolol drawn up and treat a systolic pressure above the agreed ceiling (commonly 140-160 mmHg) at once.
- Extubate awake enough to obey a command; then check pupils, speech, and power in all four limbs before leaving theatre.

If the patient does not wake within the expected time, work through the list in order: residual drug (opioid, relaxant, hypnotic), carbon dioxide, hypoxia, temperature, glucose, sodium, seizure (including non-convulsive), and then a surgical cause such as haematoma, oedema, pneumocephalus or infarction. The threshold for a scan is low and the time allowed is short, about 20-30 minutes. Plan to keep the tube in for the patient who was obtunded before surgery, has lower cranial nerve or brainstem involvement, or has had a long operation with major swelling.

## Scalp block

Craniotomy pain is somatic pain from scalp, pericranium and muscle; the brain itself is insensate. That makes it one of the few major operations in which a set of superficial nerve blocks covers nearly all of the nociceptive input, and it earns its pain score of 3 mainly from the temporalis muscle and the first 24 hours.

Six nerves on each side supply the scalp, from the trigeminal divisions anteriorly and the cervical plexus posteriorly: supraorbital and supratrochlear (V1) above the orbit; zygomaticotemporal (V2) through the temporalis fascia; auriculotemporal (V3) in front of the ear, beside the superficial temporal artery; and the greater and lesser occipital nerves (C2, C3) over the occiput. Blocks are matched to the incision and the pin sites. The scalp is vascular and absorption is rapid, so the total dose of local anaesthetic is calculated before starting, a long-acting agent with adrenaline is usual, and the auriculotemporal injection is kept superficial and above the tragus to avoid the facial nerve.

The block does three jobs. Before pinning, it blunts the haemodynamic response. For [awake craniotomy](../c/awake-craniotomy.html), it is the anaesthetic. And for postoperative analgesia, a meta-analysis of seven small trials found pain scores lower by 1.61 at one hour, with benefit lasting to 6-8 hours when the block was placed before surgery and to 12 hours when placed at the end, and less opioid over 24 hours. The procedure-specific guideline for craniotomy recommends paracetamol, an NSAID, a regional technique (scalp block or incision infiltration) and intraoperative dexmedetomidine, with opioid as rescue. Surgeons vary on NSAIDs in the first day because of the haematoma risk, and that is a conversation to have before the list. Opioid is titrated to keep the patient examinable; an alert patient in pain and a sedated patient with a rising carbon dioxide are both failures.

## The rule set

1. Know where the patient sits on the pressure-volume curve before induction; a normal number does not mean reserve.
2. Blood volume is your compartment: head up, neck neutral, no coughing, normocapnia, propofol in preference to volatile when the brain is tight.
3. Hyperventilate briefly and deliberately, against an arterial sample, as a bridge to something definitive.
4. Osmotherapy early enough to work before the dura opens; isotonic fluid, no albumin and no steroid in head injury.
5. Set the blood pressure by the disease: defend it in head injury and occluded stroke, damp it before an aneurysm is secured, do not over-lower after thrombectomy, and cap it at emergence from craniotomy.
6. A sudden fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide with the head up is air: tell the surgeon, flood the field, 100% oxygen, lower the head.
7. Motor evoked potentials mean propofol and remifentanil, no relaxant after intubation, a bite block, and an announced steady state.
8. When signals change, raise the pressure and report the anaesthetic variables within a minute.
9. Wake the patient for an examination, without a cough or a pressure surge, and scan early if the examination is wrong.
10. Block the scalp: for the pins, for the awake patient, and for the first night.
