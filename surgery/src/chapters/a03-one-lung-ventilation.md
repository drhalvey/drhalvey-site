## What the surgeon needs and what it costs

Lung surgery through a thoracoscope is impossible on an inflated, moving lung, and through a thoracotomy it is slow and bloody. The surgeon needs the operated lung collapsed and still. A second, older reason is protection: when one lung contains pus, blood or lavage fluid, or has a hole into the pleura that would steal the tidal volume ([bronchopleural fistula](../c/pleural-surgery-decortication-pleurodesis-and-pneumothorax.html)), the other has to be sealed off from it. The first is exposure, the second is isolation, and the distinction matters when choosing a device: a bronchial blocker gives exposure well and isolation less securely.

The cost is physiological. The collapsed lung is still perfused, so everything that flows through it returns to the left heart unoxygenated. The patient lies on the side, the ventilated lung is underneath, compressed by the mediastinum above and the abdominal contents pushing the diaphragm up, and that single compressed lung now does all the gas exchange and takes the whole tidal volume. Oxygenation during one-lung ventilation is the balance between how much blood still goes through the collapsed lung and how well the dependent lung is kept open.

## Lung isolation devices

### The choice

| | Left double-lumen tube | Right double-lumen tube | Bronchial blocker through a single-lumen tube |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Use | Default for almost all cases, either side | Surgery or disease involving the left main bronchus | Difficult airway, patient already intubated, tracheostomy, planned postoperative ventilation, small adults |
| Margin for error | Left main bronchus is long, so position is forgiving | Right upper lobe bronchus arises close to the carina; the ventilating slot must face it | Balloon sits in the main bronchus and migrates with surgical traction |
| Suction and CPAP to the collapsed lung | Good | Good | Poor: narrow lumen, slow collapse |
| Switching between lungs | Either lung, either time | Either lung, either time | Blocked lung only; repositioning needed to swap |
| End of case | Exchange needed if ventilation continues | Exchange needed | Withdraw the blocker; tube stays |
| Airway trauma | More sore throat, hoarseness and airway injury | Same | Less |

The randomised evidence supports that table. In a trial comparing three blocker designs with left double-lumen tubes for left-sided surgery, surgical exposure was equivalent, but the blockers took longer to position (203 s against 93 s) and needed repositioning far more often (35 episodes against two). A meta-analysis of 39 trials found double-lumen tubes quicker to place and less often malpositioned, and blockers associated with less sore throat, less hoarseness and fewer airway injuries. The reasonable default is a left double-lumen tube, with a blocker for the patient whose upper airway makes a large, stiff, curved tube a bad idea. In the difficult airway, the safe sequence is to secure the trachea with a single-lumen tube by whatever method the airway demands, and then add a blocker or exchange over a catheter under videolaryngoscopic vision; the alternatives are reviewed in the difficult airway lung isolation literature and the general principles are in the [shared and difficult airway chapter](a04-shared-difficult-airway.html).

A right-sided tube is needed when the left main bronchus cannot take a tube or will be operated on: left pneumonectomy with a proximal tumour, left sleeve resection, a left main bronchus distorted or compressed by tumour or a thoracic aortic aneurysm, and left lung transplantation. It is more often malpositioned and is a reason to have the bronchoscope in hand, not on the trolley.

### Sizing and depth

The aim is the largest tube whose bronchial limb passes easily into the main bronchus with a small air leak when the bronchial cuff is down: a tube that is too small needs a high cuff volume to seal, sits too deep, and has narrow lumens that make suction, bronchoscopy and passive deflation harder. Conventional starting sizes are 37 Fr for most women and 39 Fr for most men, one size down for small patients and one up for large, and the measured tracheal width on the chest radiograph or CT is a better guide than sex and height where the anatomy is unusual. A conventional starting depth is about 29 cm at the teeth for a patient 170 cm tall, adjusted by about 1 cm for every 10 cm of height. These are starting points for the bronchoscope to correct, not endpoints.

The bronchial cuff needs only 1-3 mL. A cuff that needs more than that to seal means a tube that is too small or not in the bronchus.

### Bronchoscopic checks

Auscultation is not good enough. In a prospective study of 200 patients, bronchoscopy found 79 of 172 tubes that had been judged correct by clinical signs to be malpositioned, 25 of them critically; after the patient was turned lateral, 93 tubes had moved, 48 critically. More than one third of tubes needed repositioning. Two looks are therefore routine: after intubation, and again after final positioning. A third look is the first step whenever saturation falls or airway pressure rises.

For a left double-lumen tube:

1. Down the tracheal lumen. The carina is in view, the bronchial limb enters the left main bronchus, and the blue bronchial cuff is just visible below the carina, not herniating across it. Look into the right main bronchus and identify the right upper lobe orifice with its three segmental openings, the only orifice in the bronchial tree that looks like that; this confirms which side is which.
2. Down the bronchial lumen. The tip is clear of the wall and the left upper and lower lobe orifices are both visible beyond it. A tip that has passed the secondary carina into the lower lobe will isolate the lung and leave the left upper lobe unventilated.

For a right double-lumen tube, add a look through the ventilating slot of the bronchial limb to confirm that it faces the right upper lobe orifice. For a blocker, the balloon is inflated under vision so that its upper surface sits just below the carina in the main bronchus of the operated side.

Tubes move in a predictable direction. Turning lateral and extending or flexing the neck shifts the tube, most often outwards, so that the bronchial cuff herniates over the carina and obstructs the trachea; surgical traction on the hilum does the same to a blocker. Fix the head in a neutral position before the final check.

## The lateral position and the shunt

In the awake, upright or lateral subject, the dependent lung receives more blood (gravity) and more ventilation (it sits on the steeper part of its compliance curve), so the two are matched. Anaesthesia, paralysis and an open chest break the match. Functional residual capacity falls, the dependent lung is squeezed by the mediastinum and the paralysed diaphragm is pushed up by the abdomen, so it drops to a stiff part of its curve, while the upper lung, freed by the open chest, is easy to inflate. Ventilation goes preferentially to the top and perfusion to the bottom.

Collapsing the upper lung removes the wasted ventilation and leaves the perfusion problem: all the blood still passing through it is shunt. Three things reduce that flow.

```
 Two lungs, lateral:     upper ~40% of flow     lower ~60%

 Upper lung collapsed:
   passive only          ~40% shunt   (untenable)
   + gravity             already counted in the 40%
   + HPV (halves it)     ~20-25% shunt
   + surgical retraction,
     ligation of vessels  less again as the resection proceeds

 Supine one-lung ventilation: no gravity help, ~50% to each lung,
 so the shunt and the hypoxaemia are worse.
```

The figures are the standard teaching approximations; the point is the direction. Gravity favours the dependent lung in the lateral position, which is why one-lung ventilation supine (for an [oesophagectomy](../c/oesophagectomy.html) abdominal phase done with the lung down, or anterior mediastinal and cardiac approaches) desaturates more. Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction diverts flow away from the unventilated lung. And the surgeon compresses the lung and, in a lobectomy or [pneumonectomy](../c/pneumonectomy.html), progressively ligates its vessels, so oxygenation often improves as the operation proceeds.

### Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction

Small pulmonary arteries constrict when the alveoli they serve are hypoxic. The reflex is biphasic: an early phase within minutes and a second, stronger phase that is active after about an hour. Two consequences are practical. Saturation typically reaches its lowest point 15-30 min after the lung goes down and then improves as the reflex matures, so the early dip is often worth riding out with oxygen alone. And once the second phase is established it takes hours to reverse, which matters for a second period of one-lung ventilation on the other side in a bilateral procedure: the lung that was down first is still vasoconstricted when it is asked to do all the work.

The reflex is blunted by anything that raises pulmonary artery pressure or mixed venous oxygen, by alkalosis and hypocapnia, by vasodilators, and by volatile agents in a dose-dependent way, the modern agents less than the older ones; intravenous agents have little effect. At clinical concentrations the difference does not show: reviews conclude that the choice of anaesthetic does not influence oxygenation during one-lung ventilation, and a multicentre randomised trial of desflurane against propofol in lung surgery found no difference in major complications. Choose the maintenance agent for other reasons.

### Predicting who will desaturate

The patients who desaturate are not the ones with the worst lungs. Risk is higher with right-sided surgery (the larger lung is the one collapsed), supine position, a high proportion of perfusion going to the operated lung on a preoperative scan, a poor arterial oxygen tension on two-lung ventilation in the lateral position, and normal preoperative spirometry. The last is counterintuitive: the patient with severe obstructive disease has air trapping in the dependent lung that acts as intrinsic PEEP and keeps it open, while the dependent lung of a patient with normal lungs, a high body mass index or restrictive disease collapses readily.

## The hypoxaemia ladder

Decide in advance what saturation will be tolerated; most would accept 90% or above in a patient without critical coronary or cerebrovascular disease, and act earlier on a falling trend. A sudden or severe fall is managed by telling the surgeon and reinflating the lung with oxygen, then working out the cause with two lungs ventilated. A gradual fall is managed in order, causes before symptoms:

1. Oxygen 100%, and look at the capnograph, the airway pressure and the blood pressure. A low cardiac output lowers mixed venous saturation and makes any shunt worse; treat hypotension and check the haemoglobin.
2. Bronchoscope. Confirm the tube position and suction the ventilated bronchi. A bronchial limb that has slipped into the lower lobe, a cuff herniated over the carina, or a plug of secretions or blood is the cause in a large share of cases and nothing else will work until it is fixed.
3. Open the dependent lung. A recruitment manoeuvre, then PEEP adjusted to the best compliance. In the patient with severe obstructive disease, check for breath stacking first and lengthen expiration; adding PEEP on top of intrinsic PEEP diverts blood to the collapsed lung and makes things worse.
4. Give oxygen to the operated lung without moving it. Insufflation of oxygen or CPAP at 2-5 cmH2O to the collapsed lung after a partial reinflation is the most reliable single manoeuvre in an open thoracotomy. During thoracoscopic surgery it partly reinflates the lung into the surgeon's view, so it has to be negotiated: low-flow oxygen insufflation through a suction catheter, or selective insufflation of a segment remote from the operation under bronchoscopic vision, are the compromises.
5. Intermittent two-lung ventilation, with the surgeon's agreement, at natural pauses.
6. Reduce flow to the operated lung. The surgeon can compress or clamp the pulmonary artery of the lung or lobe being resected, which removes the shunt at source; in a pneumonectomy this is the definitive fix.
7. If none of this holds, return to two-lung ventilation and reconsider the plan: an open approach, a staged procedure, or extracorporeal support in the rare patient for whom the operation is essential and one-lung ventilation is impossible.

## Protective one-lung ventilation

The dependent lung is at risk from the ventilator, and the operated lung from handling, ischaemia and re-expansion. Acute lung injury after resection is uncommon and lethal. In a cohort of 879 resections its incidence was 4.2%; the early form, appearing within three days with no other cause, carried a mortality of 26% and was independently associated with high intraoperative ventilatory pressures (odds ratio 3.5), excessive fluid (2.9), pneumonectomy (2.8) and preoperative alcohol abuse (1.9). Two of those four are the anaesthetist's.

The components of a protective strategy, with the strength of the evidence for each:

| Component | Target | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tidal volume | 4-6 mL/kg predicted body weight during one-lung ventilation | Physiological and observational; a small volume without PEEP is not protective, and in one large observational series lower volumes without adequate PEEP were associated with more complications |
| PEEP | 5 cmH2O as a starting point, adjusted to compliance | Randomised: higher PEEP with recruitment did not reduce complications and caused more hypotension |
| Driving pressure | Plateau minus PEEP as low as achievable | One randomised trial of 292 patients: PEEP titrated to the lowest driving pressure gave complications in 5.5% against 12.2% |
| Recruitment | Before and after one-lung ventilation, and for hypoxaemia | Physiological |
| Inspired oxygen | The lowest that holds the agreed saturation once stable | Consensus |
| Carbon dioxide | Permissive hypercapnia accepted, except with pulmonary hypertension or a failing right ventricle | Consensus |
| Re-expansion | Slow, under vision, with limited pressure | Consensus |

The large trial is PROTHOR: about 2,100 patients with a body mass index below 35 randomised to PEEP of 10 cmH2O with periodic recruitment manoeuvres or 5 cmH2O without, all at a tidal volume of 5 mL/kg predicted body weight during one-lung ventilation. Postoperative pulmonary complications were no different (53.6% against 56.4%). Intraoperative complications were more frequent with high PEEP (49.8% against 31.3%), driven by hypotension (37.3% against 14.3%) and new arrhythmias (9.9% against 3.9%), while rescue for hypoxaemia was needed less often (3.3% against 8.8%). The reading that fits both that trial and the smaller driving-pressure trial: a fixed high PEEP is not protective and has a haemodynamic price; PEEP is set for the individual lung, by compliance or driving pressure and by the oxygenation in front of you, and recruitment is used when there is a reason.

Pressure-controlled and volume-controlled modes give the same outcome at the same tidal volume; use the one whose alarms you read best. With a small tidal volume the rate goes up, and in obstructive disease the expiratory time is what limits it.

## Fluid

The lung that has been handled, collapsed and re-expanded has leaky capillaries, and after a resection the remaining lung takes the whole cardiac output through a reduced vascular bed. Fluid that would be harmless after abdominal surgery appears as pulmonary oedema. The association is consistent across four decades: the original description of post-pneumonectomy pulmonary oedema implicated right pneumonectomy and a large perioperative fluid load; the 879-patient cohort found excessive fluid to be an independent risk factor for early lung injury; and a later case-control study found greater perioperative fluid administration and a lower predicted postoperative diffusion capacity to be the predictors, with a mortality of 25% in those who developed lung injury against 2.6% in controls.

The enhanced recovery guideline for lung surgery recommends euvolaemia, which is a correction of the older habit of running these patients dry: hypovolaemia buys acute kidney injury and a hypotensive patient on an epidural. In practice that means a maintenance crystalloid rate of a few mL/kg/h, replacement of measured blood loss, no fluid loading for the epidural or for urine output, and a vasopressor for the vasodilatation of anaesthesia and neuraxial block. Pneumonectomy gets the strictest version, with a deliberately small positive balance over the first 24 h. A patient who needs repeated fluid boluses during a lung resection is bleeding, and the place to look is the suction canister and the drain.

## Preoperative assessment: the thresholds

The question is how much lung the patient can lose. Three legs are assessed: respiratory mechanics (FEV1), parenchymal function (DLCO) and cardiopulmonary reserve (exercise capacity). FEV1 and DLCO measure different things and are poorly correlated, so both are needed, and the predicted postoperative (ppo) value of each is the working number.

The ppo value is estimated by segment counting. There are 19 segments (right upper 3, middle 2, right lower 5; left upper 3, lingula 2, left lower 4):

```
 ppo value = preoperative value x (1 - segments resected / 19)

 Example: FEV1 60% predicted, right lower lobectomy (5 segments)
          ppoFEV1 = 60 x (1 - 5/19) = 44% predicted
```

Segments that are already obstructed or unperfused are subtracted from both the numerator and the denominator, and a perfusion scan replaces counting before pneumonectomy or where the disease is unevenly distributed.

The American College of Chest Physicians algorithm then sorts patients:

| Finding | Meaning | Next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ppoFEV1 and ppoDLCO both above 60% | Low risk | No further testing |
| Either between 30% and 60% | Indeterminate | Stair climb or shuttle walk: low risk if above 22 m of ascent or above 400 m |
| Either below 30%, or a failed stair or shuttle test | Higher risk | Formal cardiopulmonary exercise test |
| Peak oxygen consumption above 20 mL/kg/min or 75% predicted | Low risk | Proceed |
| Peak oxygen consumption 10-20 mL/kg/min or 35-75% predicted | Moderate risk | Individual decision; consider a lesser resection |
| Peak oxygen consumption below 10 mL/kg/min or 35% predicted | High risk of death and long-term disability after major anatomical resection | Sublobar resection or non-surgical treatment |

The British guideline frames the same data as a shared decision about the risk of postoperative breathlessness and does not treat any single value as an absolute bar. A cardiac assessment precedes the lung function in both.

For the anaesthetist the numbers translate into a plan. A patient with ppo values above 40% and no other concern is extubated in theatre and goes to the ward or a high-dependency bed. Between 30% and 40%, extubation in theatre is still the aim and depends on analgesia, temperature, residual block and how the operation went. Below 30% the patient has been selected despite the numbers; expect a slow wean, arrange the bed in advance, and make sure regional analgesia is working before emergence. Thoracoscopic resection is tolerated at lower values than thoracotomy, because the chest wall is less disturbed, and resection of emphysematous lung can leave function better than predicted.

## The rest of the thoracic list

- Pneumonectomy: the right side is worse. Fluid restriction is strictest, the right ventricle faces a sudden rise in afterload when the pulmonary artery is clamped (ask for a trial clamp before division if the patient is marginal), and the drain is never put on suction. See the [pneumonectomy card](../c/pneumonectomy.html).
- Anterior mediastinal mass: the airway and the great vessels can be compressed when muscle tone is lost. This is a separate problem with its own plan; see [mediastinoscopy and anterior mediastinal mass](../c/mediastinoscopy-and-anterior-mediastinal-mass.html).
- Central airway surgery, stents and rigid bronchoscopy share the airway with the operator; see [rigid bronchoscopy, stents and tracheal resection](../c/rigid-bronchoscopy-stents-and-tracheal-resection.html).
- Oesophagectomy, [thymectomy](../c/thymectomy-and-myasthenia-gravis.html), [pectus repair](../c/pectus-repair.html), thoracic spine and [thoracic aortic surgery](../c/surgery-of-the-thoracic-aorta-and.html) use one-lung ventilation for exposure in patients whose lungs are usually normal, which are the lungs that collapse and desaturate most readily.
- Infants and small children cannot take a double-lumen tube; the options are in [paediatric laparoscopic and thoracoscopic surgery](../c/paediatric-laparoscopic-and-thoracoscopic-surgery.html).
- Emergence: recruit both lungs under vision before the chest is closed, confirm that the drain bubbles and swings, reverse the block completely, sit the patient up, and extubate early. A cough against a fresh bronchial stump is undesirable but a reintubation is worse.

## The link to analgesia

A thoracotomy that hurts produces a patient who will not cough, and retained secretions in a patient with a ppoFEV1 of 35% are the route to reintubation. The regional technique is therefore part of the ventilatory plan and is chosen before induction. PROSPECT recommends thoracic epidural or paravertebral block as first-line for open thoracotomy, and for video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery recommends paravertebral or erector spinae plane block, with serratus anterior plane block as second choice and no epidural, all on a base of paracetamol and an NSAID with opioid for rescue. A paravertebral catheter gives less hypotension than an epidural, which fits the need to avoid fluid. The reasoning, the dose ceilings and the anticoagulation timing are in the [truncal analgesia chapter](a02-truncal-blocks-epidural.html); the operation-specific plans are on the [lobectomy card](../c/video-assisted-and-open-lobectomy-and.html).

## The rule set

1. Left double-lumen tube by default; right for left main bronchus surgery or distortion; blocker for the difficult airway, the tracheostomy and the patient staying ventilated.
2. Bronchoscope after intubation, after turning, and first whenever saturation falls or pressure rises.
3. Expect the lowest saturation 15-30 min after lung collapse, and expect the patient with normal lungs, right-sided surgery or a supine position to be the one who desaturates.
4. The ladder: oxygen and cardiac output, bronchoscope, recruit and adjust PEEP on the dependent lung, oxygen or CPAP to the operated lung, intermittent two-lung ventilation, pulmonary artery clamp.
5. Tidal volume 4-6 mL/kg predicted body weight with PEEP, the lowest achievable driving pressure, and no routine high PEEP.
6. Euvolaemia. Vasopressor for vasodilatation, blood for bleeding, and no fluid for urine output or the epidural. Strictest after pneumonectomy.
7. Read the ppoFEV1, ppoDLCO and exercise capacity before the day; below 40% plan the extubation, below 30% plan the bed.
8. Regional analgesia decided and placed as part of the respiratory plan: epidural or paravertebral for thoracotomy, paravertebral or erector spinae block for thoracoscopic surgery.
