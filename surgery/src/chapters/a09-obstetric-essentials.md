## Two patients on one circulation

Every obstetric decision is a decision about oxygen delivery to two people through one heart. The fetus has no reserve of its own: it lives on a low oxygen tension made tolerable by fetal haemoglobin and a high cardiac output, and it depends on a placental bed that has lost the ability to regulate its own flow. The mother, meanwhile, has rebuilt her physiology around the pregnancy in ways that make her harder to oxygenate, easier to block and quicker to bleed.

The related cards are [elective caesarean section](../c/elective-caesarean-section.html), [emergency caesarean section by category](../c/emergency-caesarean-section-by-category.html), [labour epidural analgesia](../c/labour-epidural-analgesia-and-its-complications.html), [general anaesthesia and the failed intubation](../c/general-anaesthesia-for-caesarean-section-and.html), [major obstetric haemorrhage](../c/major-obstetric-haemorrhage-and-placenta-accreta.html) and [pre-eclampsia](../c/pre-eclampsia-eclampsia-and-the-parturient.html). This chapter holds the physiology they share.

## What pregnancy changes, and what each change costs you

| System | Change by term | Consequence for the anaesthetic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Oxygen | Consumption up by about a fifth, functional residual capacity down by about a fifth | Apnoea desaturates in half the usual time; preoxygenation is not optional |
| Ventilation | Minute ventilation up by about half; arterial carbon dioxide near 30 mmHg | A "normal" carbon dioxide of 40 mmHg is hypoventilation; ventilate to the pregnant value |
| Circulation | Cardiac output up 40-50%, resistance down, plasma volume up more than red cell mass | Dilutional anaemia; she tolerates 1 L of blood loss with few signs, then decompensates |
| Vena cava | Compressed by the uterus when supine from about 20 weeks | Preload falls with spinal block; see below |
| Coagulation | Fibrinogen rises to 4-6 g/L; platelets fall slightly | A fibrinogen of 2 g/L is already low for pregnancy and predicts worsening haemorrhage |
| Gut | Lower oesophageal sphincter tone reduced; gastric emptying slowed by labour and opioids | Aspiration risk from mid-pregnancy; antacid prophylaxis before any section |
| Airway | Mucosal oedema and vascularity, larger breasts, worse in labour and pre-eclampsia | Smaller tube, short handle or videolaryngoscope, no nasal instrumentation |
| Nervous system | Volatile requirement and neuraxial local anaesthetic requirement both reduced by about a quarter to a third | Lower spinal and epidural doses; do not let that lead to awareness under general anaesthesia |
| Kidney | Filtration up by half, creatinine falls | A creatinine in the non-pregnant normal range is renal impairment |
| Plasma proteins | Albumin and alpha-1 acid glycoprotein diluted | Higher free fraction of local anaesthetic |

The first row is the one that hurts people. A small tank that is emptied quickly gives a term woman about half the safe apnoea time of a non-pregnant adult, and less if she is obese or in labour. That single fact is why the obstetric airway guidance (below) was rewritten around oxygenation in place of intubation.

## Aortocaval compression

Supine, the term uterus rests on the inferior vena cava. Magnetic resonance imaging puts numbers on it: caval volume was 3.2 mL in supine women at term against 17.5 mL in non-pregnant controls, which is near-complete occlusion. The aorta was not compressed. A left tilt of 15 degrees did not change the caval volume; 30 degrees partly restored it. Venous return when supine depends on collaterals (the azygos and paravertebral veins) and on sympathetic tone in the legs, which is why most awake women tolerate lying flat and why a spinal, which removes that tone, unmasks the problem within minutes.

```
        supine                        15 degrees left tilt       30 degrees
   _______________                 _______________
  |    uterus     |               |    uterus     |          uterus rolls off
  |_______________|                \_______________\         the cava: volume
     IVC   aorta                      IVC   aorta            partly restored;
    (flat) (round)                   (still flat)            not achievable on
   ====================           ====================       an operating table
         spine                          spine
```

The clinical trial that matters randomised 100 healthy women having elective sections to supine or 15 degrees of tilt, with a crystalloid coload and phenylephrine titrated to baseline pressure. Neonatal acid-base status was the same. The supine group needed more phenylephrine and had a lower cardiac output for the first 15 minutes. Read together, the two studies say that 15 degrees of table tilt is a weak intervention, that a vasopressor infusion covers most of what tilt was meant to achieve in a healthy woman, and that the cost of lying flat is paid in cardiac output.

Practical position: tilt or wedge remains the default because it is free and the consensus statement on spinal hypotension still includes uterine displacement. It becomes critical in three settings where the vasopressor cannot compensate: hypotension not responding to treatment, a compromised fetus, and cardiac arrest, where manual displacement of the uterus to the left with the woman flat for compressions replaces tilt and delivery within minutes is part of the resuscitation of the mother.

## Uteroplacental flow

At term the uterus takes roughly a tenth of the cardiac output. The spiral arteries have been remodelled into wide, passive channels that are close to maximally dilated and do not autoregulate, so flow is a simple pressure-over-resistance problem.

```
                      uterine artery pressure - uterine venous pressure
  uterine blood flow = -------------------------------------------------
                               uterine vascular resistance

  falls when:   numerator down                  denominator up
                maternal hypotension            contractions, tetany,
                caval compression                 oxytocin overdose
                (raises venous pressure           high-dose vasoconstrictor
                 as well as cutting output)      maternal catecholamines
                haemorrhage                       (pain, fear), hypocapnia
```

Three consequences. Hypotension after a spinal is a fetal problem as well as a maternal one, and nausea is its earliest symptom because the brainstem notices first. A contracting uterus stops its own inflow, so a hypertonic uterus (after too much oxytocin in labour, or with abruption) produces fetal distress that is treated by relaxing the uterus, not by raising pressure. And overventilation under general anaesthesia harms the fetus twice, by vasoconstriction and by shifting the maternal oxygen dissociation curve to the left.

## Neuraxial dosing and block height

Less drug does more in pregnancy. Engorged epidural veins reduce the volume of the lumbar cerebrospinal fluid, the lordosis and the wide pelvis change how a hyperbaric solution runs, and progesterone increases neural sensitivity. The dose is still not small: in a dose-ranging trial with intrathecal fentanyl 10 microg and morphine 200 microg, the dose of hyperbaric bupivacaine that provided a block lasting the whole operation in 95% of women was 11.2 mg, and in half of women 7.6 mg. A "low-dose" spinal of 7-8 mg is therefore a block that fails in every second woman unless an epidural catheter is there to rescue it, which is the logic of the combined spinal-epidural for the short, the obese and the patient in whom hypotension must be avoided.

| Technique | Typical drugs | Onset to surgery | Use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Single-shot spinal | Hyperbaric bupivacaine 0.5% 11-12.5 mg, fentanyl 15 microg, morphine 100-150 microg | 5-10 min | Default for elective and most urgent sections |
| Combined spinal-epidural | Bupivacaine 7.5-10 mg with the same opioids; epidural lidocaine 2% in 5 mL increments if needed | 10-15 min | Long surgery (placenta accreta, repeat section with adhesions), cardiac disease, extremes of height or weight |
| Epidural top-up | Lidocaine 2% with adrenaline 15-20 mL, fentanyl 50-100 microg | 10-15 min | Working labour epidural in place |
| Rapid sequence spinal | Bupivacaine 12.5 mg, fentanyl 25 microg, no-touch, one attempt | 5 min | Category 1 with no epidural and a reason to avoid general anaesthesia |

Height within the usual adult range does not justify a change of dose; the outliers are better served by a catheter than by guessing. For the opioid, a meta-analysis of eleven trials found that morphine above 100 microg bought 4.5 hours more before the first analgesic request, with no difference in pain scores or opioid use at 24 hours and with more nausea and itch. With regular paracetamol and an NSAID on top, as PROSPECT recommends, 100 microg is the defensible default and 150 microg is reasonable for the woman with chronic pain or an opioid history.

The target is a block to T4 to cold and T5 to light touch, bilateral, with dense sacral block, tested and written down before the knife. This matters legally as well as clinically: pain during caesarean section under neuraxial anaesthesia has overtaken awareness as the commonest successful claim against obstetric anaesthetists. When a woman says she has pain, the sequence is to believe her, ask the surgeon to pause, treat (epidural top-up if there is a catheter, fentanyl 25-50 microg IV, nitrous oxide, ketamine 10-20 mg IV), and offer general anaesthesia early. Pain at uterine exteriorisation and peritoneal traction is visceral and vagal and can break through a T4 block; say so before it happens.

## Hypotension and phenylephrine

A spinal to T4 removes sympathetic tone from the legs and splanchnic bed in a woman whose venous return is already compromised. Untreated, most women become hypotensive. The international consensus statement set the modern standard: keep systolic pressure at or above 90% of an accurately measured baseline, avoid any fall below 80%, and do it with a prophylactic infusion started at the moment of intrathecal injection, not with boluses after the pressure has fallen.

```
  spinal injected ----> start phenylephrine 25-50 microg/min
        |               crystalloid coload (rapid, as the block develops)
        |               left uterine displacement
        v
  measure pressure every minute until delivery
        |
        +-- systolic < 90% baseline ......... bolus 50-100 microg, raise rate
        +-- systolic > 120% baseline ........ halve or stop the infusion
        +-- heart rate falling, pressure ok . reduce the rate: reflex slowing
        |                                     means cardiac output is falling
        +-- slow AND hypotensive ............ ephedrine 6-9 mg or
                                              glycopyrronium 200 microg IV
```

Phenylephrine replaced ephedrine for a mechanistic reason. Ephedrine crosses the placenta and stimulates fetal metabolism, so the baby is delivered with a lower pH even when maternal pressure is identical. Phenylephrine does not, but as a pure alpha agonist it slows the heart by baroreflex and lowers cardiac output in a dose-dependent way. Heart rate is therefore the bedside surrogate for output: a pressure held at baseline with a heart rate of 50 is overdosing. Noradrenaline by dilute peripheral infusion is an alternative with less slowing. In pre-eclampsia hypotension after a spinal is less frequent and less severe than in healthy women, so the infusion starts low or is replaced by small boluses.

## The airway

The obstetric airway is difficult for stacked reasons, none of them exotic: oedematous mucosa that bleeds, breasts in the way of the handle, cricoid pressure applied by someone who does it twice a year, a stomach assumed full, half the usual apnoea time, and a team under time pressure at night. The incidence of failed intubation has not moved in 40 years: 2.6 per 1,000 obstetric general anaesthetics, or 1 in 390, with one maternal death for every 90 failures. Death came from aspiration or from hypoxaemia after airway obstruction or oesophageal intubation, not from the failure itself.

The Obstetric Anaesthetists' Association and Difficult Airway Society guidelines turn that into a drill:

1. Plan before induction. Head-up position with the ear level with the sternal notch, antacid prophylaxis, tight-fitting mask preoxygenation to an end-tidal oxygen of 0.9, and nasal oxygen left on through the attempt. The team agrees, aloud, whether a failed intubation in this patient means wake or proceed.
2. After induction, gentle mask ventilation at low pressure is permitted and recommended, because desaturation kills faster than regurgitation.
3. Two attempts at intubation. The second uses a different device or operator and cricoid pressure is released early if the view is poor.
4. Declare failure. Insert a second-generation supraglottic airway or ventilate by mask. Oxygenation, not the tube, is the goal.
5. If neither works, it is "cannot intubate, cannot oxygenate": scalpel front-of-neck access, and delivery of the baby as part of maternal resuscitation.

The wake-or-proceed table in the guideline weighs maternal condition, fetal condition, the anaesthetist's experience, obesity, surgical factors, aspiration risk, the feasibility of an alternative anaesthetic, and how well the rescue airway is working. In practice, since the late 1990s most failed intubations at caesarean section have continued under general anaesthesia on a supraglottic airway. The cases that should wake are those with no maternal or fetal emergency and an airway that is only just adequate.

Drug choices follow from the physiology. Propofol 2-2.5 mg/kg IV is the usual induction agent. Rocuronium 1-1.2 mg/kg IV with sugammadex 16 mg/kg immediately available is an alternative to suxamethonium 1-1.5 mg/kg IV. A short-acting opioid at induction (alfentanil 10-20 microg/kg or remifentanil 1 microg/kg IV) is given when the pressor response matters more than neonatal respiratory depression, which means pre-eclampsia and cardiac disease, and the paediatrician is told. Volatile is run at an adequate end-tidal concentration from the start: pregnancy lowers the requirement, but the commonest error is too little agent in the minutes before delivery. Extubation is awake and head-up, because aspiration deaths have moved from induction to emergence.

## Uterotonics

After delivery the uterus must contract to close the spiral arteries, which have no muscle of their own; the myometrium is the tourniquet. The drugs that make it contract all have systemic vascular effects, and those effects set the dose and the route.

| Drug | Dose and route | What it does to the mother | Avoid or modify |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Oxytocin | Slow IV bolus 1 IU (elective) to 3 IU (intrapartum), then infusion | Vasodilation, tachycardia, hypotension, ST depression, flushing; water retention with large doses | Fixed cardiac output, hypovolaemia: smaller bolus, slower |
| Carbetocin | 100 microg IV slowly, once | As oxytocin, lasting about an hour; no infusion needed | As oxytocin |
| Ergometrine | 250-500 microg IM, or 250 microg slow IV | Vasoconstriction, hypertension, coronary spasm, vomiting | Pre-eclampsia, hypertension, cardiac disease |
| Carboprost | 250 microg IM, repeated at 15 min to 2 mg | Bronchospasm, shunt and desaturation, diarrhoea, fever | Asthma; never intravenous |
| Misoprostol | 600-800 microg sublingual or rectal | Shivering, fever | Slow onset; an adjunct |
| Tranexamic acid | 1 g IV | Not a uterotonic; antifibrinolytic | Given with, not instead of, the drugs above |

The international consensus statement on uterotonics makes three points worth keeping. First, traditional oxytocin boluses of 5-10 IU are several times what is needed, and the cardiovascular effects are dose-dependent, so it recommends a small initial bolus followed by a titrated infusion. Second, the labouring uterus that has been exposed to oxytocin for hours has desensitised receptors: intrapartum sections need more oxytocin and should move to a second-line agent early if tone is poor. Third, women with cardiac disease can be very sensitive to all of these drugs, and their doses are individualised. The observational data in severe pre-eclampsia show the oxytocin effect cleanly: resistance falls and heart rate and output rise within a minute of the bolus.

The trap is the bolus given quickly to a woman who is already hypovolaemic and vasodilated by a spinal. Oxytocin 5 IU as a push in a bleeding woman can stop the circulation. Give it slowly, into a running phenylephrine infusion, and tell the surgeon it has gone in.

## Pre-eclampsia

Pre-eclampsia is an endothelial disease driven by the placenta. The circulation is vasoconstricted, volume-contracted and leaky at the same time, which explains each anaesthetic rule.

- The killer is intracerebral haemorrhage from systolic hypertension. A systolic pressure of 160 mmHg or more is treated urgently (labetalol, hydralazine or oral nifedipine), and the pressor response to laryngoscopy is obtunded with an opioid, with magnesium, or with both.
- Magnesium prevents seizures. In the Magpie trial of 10,141 women it cut eclampsia from 1.9% to 0.8%. The regimen is 4 g IV over 15-20 minutes, then 1 g/h continued for 24 hours after delivery or the last seizure, and it continues through the section. It potentiates non-depolarising relaxants, blunts the response to vasopressors a little, and is cleared by the kidney, so oliguria means measuring levels. Loss of tendon reflexes precedes respiratory depression; the antidote is calcium gluconate 10% 10 mL IV.
- The leak means fluid is restricted. Pulmonary oedema is a leading cause of death, so no preload, a cautious coload, and a running total.
- The airway is more oedematous than in normal pregnancy. Stridor or voice change predicts a smaller tube.
- Platelets fall, and fall fast in HELLP syndrome. The interdisciplinary consensus on thrombocytopenia concludes that the risk of spinal haematoma is likely to be low at a count of 70 x 10^9/L or above when there is no other coagulopathy. The trend matters as much as the number: a count taken within six hours is the author's rule in severe disease, and the epidural catheter comes out only after a repeat count.
- Spinal anaesthesia is the technique of choice for caesarean section in severe pre-eclampsia without a catheter in place. Cardiac output is stable and hypotension is less than in healthy women.
- Ergometrine is avoided, and NSAIDs wait until renal function and platelets are known.

## Categories of urgency

The four-grade classification is the common language between the obstetrician and the anaesthetist, and it describes the threat, not the technique.

| Category | Definition | Usual anaesthetic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Immediate threat to the life of the woman or fetus | Top-up of a working epidural started at once, rapid sequence spinal, or general anaesthesia |
| 2 | Maternal or fetal compromise that is not immediately life-threatening | Epidural top-up or spinal |
| 3 | Needs early delivery, no compromise | Spinal |
| 4 | At a time to suit the woman and the team | Spinal |

Audit standards attach decision-to-delivery intervals to the first two categories (30 minutes for category 1 is the familiar one), but the category is a clinical statement and the target is a tool. Two habits keep a category 1 section safe. Ask the obstetrician the reason, in a phrase, because cord prolapse with a hand in the vagina, a bradycardia that has recovered, and an abruption are three different anaesthetics. And start intrauterine resuscitation while the theatre is prepared: left lateral position, oxytocin infusion off, a fluid bolus, treat hypotension, and a tocolytic (terbutaline 250 microg subcutaneously) for hyperstimulation. The fetus that recovers can be re-categorised, and a category 2 section under a spinal is safer for the mother than a category 1 under general anaesthesia.

## Converting a labour epidural

The decision has two parts: whether this epidural deserves a top-up, and what to put down it.

An epidural that has given bilateral, reliable analgesia with few clinician top-ups will convert. One that has needed repeated rescue boluses, has been one-sided, or was resited is likely to fail in theatre, and it is better removed and replaced by a spinal at the outset than discovered inadequate at skin incision. A spinal after a recent large-volume top-up is the classic route to a high block, because the epidural volume compresses the dural sac and drives the intrathecal dose cephalad; if the top-up has already been given and has failed, the choices are a reduced-dose spinal with the woman sitting, a combined technique, or general anaesthesia.

For the solution, a meta-analysis of eleven trials (779 women) found lidocaine 2% with adrenaline, with or without fentanyl, reached a surgical block a mean 4.5 minutes faster than the alternatives; bupivacaine or levobupivacaine 0.5% doubled the need for intra-operative supplementation and was the least effective choice; ropivacaine 0.75% gave the best quality when time allowed. The working recipe is lidocaine 2% with adrenaline 1 in 200,000, 15-20 mL in two or three increments, with fentanyl 50-100 microg, aiming for T4 to cold. Twenty millilitres of 2% lidocaine is 400 mg, which is within the 7 mg/kg ceiling with adrenaline for most women and above the plain ceiling for nearly all of them, so the adrenaline is not optional.

Starting the top-up in the delivery room saves minutes and costs monitoring. The defensible position is to give the first 5 mL as a test in the room for a category 1 section and the remainder under full monitoring in theatre, with the anaesthetist staying with the woman from the first dose.

## High block

High neuraxial block was the most frequent serious complication in a repository of more than 257,000 obstetric anaesthetics. It arises from a spinal on top of an epidural, an unrecognised intrathecal or subdural catheter, or an ordinary dose in a short woman or one with a twin pregnancy.

```
  level reached     what she says or you see              what to do
  -------------     ----------------------------------    ---------------------
  T1-T4             slow pulse, falling pressure          vasopressor, atropine
  C6-C8             tingling fingers, weak grip           head-up, reassure, O2
  C3-C5             cannot cough, whispering, "I can't    assist ventilation;
                    breathe", falling saturation          prepare to intubate
  brainstem         unresponsive, apnoeic, fixed pupils   intubate, ventilate,
                                                          support circulation
```

The grip is the test: ask her to squeeze your fingers every minute once the block passes T2. Nausea and a slow pulse come before breathlessness. If she can no longer speak in sentences, intubate with an induction agent (she is awake and frightened) and a relaxant, keep the uterus displaced, support the pressure with phenylephrine and adrenaline if needed, and deliver the baby if she is undelivered. The block recedes over one to two hours and she is ventilated and sedated until it has. A subdural block behaves differently: slow onset over 20 minutes, patchy, unexpectedly high with sparing of the sacral roots and little hypotension.

## Local anaesthetic toxicity

Pregnancy raises the risk on each side of the equation: epidural veins are distended and easily cannulated, protein binding is reduced, cardiac output carries drug to the heart and brain quickly, and the total dose is often large (an epidural infusion for hours, a top-up of 400 mg of lidocaine, then bilateral abdominal wall blocks). The practice advisory on systemic toxicity notes that presentation is now often delayed, which fits this pattern of accumulation.

Prevention is fractionation, aspiration and arithmetic: every epidural dose is a test dose, and the dose for any fascial plane block after a section is calculated against what the epidural has already delivered. Treatment is the standard sequence with obstetric additions. Stop the injection, call for help and the lipid, give 100% oxygen and control the airway, stop seizures with a benzodiazepine, and give lipid emulsion 20% as a bolus of 1.5 mL/kg over about a minute followed by an infusion, repeating the bolus if the circulation has not recovered. Adrenaline is used in small doses. The uterus is displaced throughout, and in cardiac arrest the baby is delivered to make the mother resuscitable.

## Breastfeeding

The Association of Anaesthetists guideline removes most of the folklore. A woman can breastfeed as soon as she is alert and able to hold the baby after general anaesthesia, sedation or regional anaesthesia, and there is no need to express and discard milk. The anaesthetic drugs in ordinary use (propofol, volatile agents, relaxants and reversal, fentanyl, local anaesthetics, ondansetron, dexamethasone, paracetamol and NSAIDs) reach milk in amounts too small to affect a term infant.

The exceptions are the opioids taken repeatedly on the ward. Codeine is not used, because a mother who is an ultra-rapid metaboliser delivers morphine to her infant. Any regular opioid is given at the lowest dose for the shortest time, with the woman told to watch the baby for unusual sleepiness or poor feeding, and more caution with a premature or unwell infant. This is one more reason the analgesic plan after caesarean section is built on intrathecal morphine, paracetamol and an NSAID (see [why operations hurt](../b/b01-why-operations-hurt.html)), with oral opioid as rescue.
