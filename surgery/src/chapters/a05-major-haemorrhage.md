## Why bleeding kills: two failures, not one

A bleeding patient dies of one of two things, and usually of both. The first is a failure of oxygen delivery. Delivery is cardiac output multiplied by arterial oxygen content, and haemorrhage attacks both terms: venous return falls, so stroke volume falls, and each litre that is replaced with crystalloid carries almost no oxygen. The second is a failure of haemostasis. The hole would seal if a clot could form on it, but the blood arriving at the hole is progressively less able to clot. Everything in this chapter is an attempt to interrupt one of those two failures without making the other worse.

The compensation is what makes haemorrhage dangerous under anaesthesia. An awake young adult holds a normal systolic pressure through the loss of roughly a third of the blood volume by vasoconstriction and tachycardia, then falls off a cliff. Anaesthesia, a neuraxial block, a beta blocker or old age remove the vasoconstriction, so the pressure falls earlier and more smoothly, which is a kindness. The trap is the reverse case: the fit patient or the parturient whose numbers look acceptable while the suction canister says otherwise. Narrowing pulse pressure, a rising heart rate against a static blood pressure (the shock index, heart rate divided by systolic pressure, which the European trauma guideline recommends for grading shock), a falling end-tidal carbon dioxide at fixed ventilation, a rising lactate and a widening base deficit all move before the systolic pressure does. A haemoglobin taken early is reassuring and wrong, because whole blood has been lost and nothing has yet diluted what remains.

## The lethal triad, and the fourth corner

```
          tissue hypoperfusion
           /               \
     lactic acidosis     less heat production
           |                 |   (plus cold fluid, open cavity)
     enzymes slow         HYPOTHERMIA
     at low pH               |
           \                /
            COAGULOPATHY  <---- dilution, consumption,
                 |              fibrinolysis, low fibrinogen
            more bleeding  <--- HYPOCALCAEMIA (citrate)
                 |
          more hypoperfusion  (back to the top)
```

Coagulation is a set of enzyme reactions on a phospholipid surface, and enzymes have a preferred temperature, a preferred pH and a calcium requirement. Shock takes away all three. Hypoperfused tissue makes lactate, and thrombin generation slows as pH falls. Hypoperfused tissue also makes less heat, and the patient is then given cold fluid with a cavity open to theatre air; the European guideline puts the cost at roughly a 10% loss of coagulation factor function for each degree, with platelet function impaired as well. A cold, acidotic patient bleeds, and bleeding makes the patient colder and more acidotic. That loop is the lethal triad, and the reason that warming and perfusion are haemostatic treatments rather than housekeeping.

Calcium is the proposed fourth corner. Stored blood is anticoagulated with citrate, which binds ionised calcium; a healthy liver clears citrate quickly, a shocked and cold liver does not. Calcium is factor IV, and it is also what the myocardium and vascular smooth muscle contract with, so hypocalcaemia produces a patient who both bleeds and fails to respond to vasopressors. In 156 trauma patients given a massive transfusion, 97% became hypocalcaemic and 71% had an ionised calcium below 0.90 mmol/L; mortality in that severe group was 49% against 24%. That is an association in sicker patients, not proof that calcium saves lives, but the physiological case for replacing it is strong and the guidelines agree.

Trauma adds a coagulopathy of its own. About a quarter of severely injured patients arrive already coagulopathic before any fluid has been given (24.4% in the original series, with a mortality of 46.0% against 10.9%), driven by tissue injury and hypoperfusion acting on the endothelium, with early fibrinolysis and a fall in fibrinogen. Surgical bleeding in theatre starts from normal coagulation and becomes coagulopathic by dilution and consumption; the end state looks similar, but the surgical patient gives you a window in which to prevent it.

## Declaring it and the first ten minutes

The textbook definitions (one blood volume in 24 hours, half a blood volume in three hours, 150 mL/min) are retrospective and of no use at the table. Declare major haemorrhage when bleeding is fast, not controlled, and the patient is showing it, or when the surgeon says a named vessel is open. The Australian National Blood Authority guideline, updated in 2023, makes a major haemorrhage protocol the standard of care in every institution that manages critical bleeding, because the protocol's real function is logistic: one phone call that mobilises the blood bank, a porter, a second anaesthetist and the laboratory.

The first ten minutes, in order:

1. Say it aloud and ask the surgeon for pressure, packing or a clamp. Tamponade buys time that no product can.
2. Call for help and activate the protocol. One person runs the rapid infuser and nothing else.
3. Large-bore access above the diaphragm for abdominal and pelvic bleeding; an arterial line as soon as there are hands for it.
4. Red cells first, emergency group O if needed, then group-specific as soon as the laboratory can issue it. Everything through a warmer.
5. Baseline bloods at activation: full blood count, coagulation screen with a Clauss fibrinogen, blood gas with ionised calcium and lactate, and a viscoelastic test if the hospital has one. The Australian guideline asks for these early and frequently, with a full blood count repeated after every four units of red cells.
6. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV if the bleeding is traumatic or obstetric and under three hours old.
7. Forced-air warming, a warm theatre, and calcium drawn up.

## Ratios: what the fixed-ratio phase is for

Red cells in additive solution contain almost no plasma and no functioning platelets. Replacing whole blood with red cells and crystalloid therefore manufactures a dilutional coagulopathy at a predictable rate, and the fixed-ratio pack exists to prevent that during the minutes when no laboratory result is available.

PROPPR is the trial behind the numbers. It randomised 680 severely injured patients predicted to need massive transfusion to plasma, platelets and red cells at 1:1:1 or 1:1:2. Mortality did not differ at 24 hours (12.7% against 17.0%) or 30 days (22.4% against 26.1%). Death from exsanguination in the first 24 hours was lower with 1:1:1 (9.2% against 14.6%) and more patients achieved haemostasis (86% against 78%), with no safety penalty from the extra plasma and platelets. The honest reading is that a higher ratio probably helps the patient who is bleeding to death and has not been shown to change survival overall.

The guidelines reflect that. The Australian guideline finds insufficient evidence to prefer 1:1:1 over 2:1:1 and sets the floor: a ratio of red cells to plasma to platelets no lower than 2:1:1, which in component terms is at least one unit of fresh frozen plasma for every two units of red cells and at least one adult dose of platelets for every eight units of red cells, achieved as soon as possible and held until bleeding is controlled. The European trauma guideline offers two acceptable opening strategies: plasma to red cells of at least 1:2, or red cells with fibrinogen concentrate or cryoprecipitate and factor concentrates guided by testing. Both then move to goal-directed treatment.

| Measure | Value that signals critical derangement (National Blood Authority) | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Temperature | below 35 degrees C | Warm everything; target at least 35, ideally 36-37 |
| pH | below 7.2 | Restore perfusion; bicarbonate does not fix the cause |
| Base excess | below -6 mmol/L | As above |
| Lactate | above 4 mmol/L | As above; trend it |
| Ionised calcium | below 1.0 mmol/L | Calcium chloride IV; recheck |
| PT, INR or APTT | above 1.5 times normal | Plasma, or PCC where indicated |
| Fibrinogen | below 2.0 g/L | Fibrinogen concentrate or cryoprecipitate |

For red cells, the European guideline recommends a haemoglobin of 70-90 g/L once transfusion is being titrated. During uncontrolled bleeding the haemoglobin is a lagging number; transfuse to the rate of loss, and use the target when the rate slows. For platelets, the same guideline suggests a count above 50 x 10^9/L during ongoing bleeding and above 100 x 10^9/L with traumatic brain injury. Recombinant factor VIIa is not recommended as a routine measure by either guideline.

## Tranexamic acid: the three-hour rule and where it stops

Tranexamic acid blocks the lysine binding sites on plasminogen, so plasmin cannot form on the clot and dissolve it. Early after injury or delivery, fibrinolysis is switched on; later it is switched off, and an antifibrinolytic given into a shut-down system has nothing to offer and may do harm. That biology is exactly what the trials show.

CRASH-2 randomised 20,211 bleeding trauma patients to 1 g over ten minutes followed by 1 g over eight hours, or placebo. All-cause mortality fell from 16.0% to 14.5% and death from bleeding from 5.7% to 4.9%, with no excess of vascular occlusion. The timing analysis is the part to remember: the relative risk of death from bleeding was 0.68 when treatment began within an hour of injury, 0.79 between one and three hours, and 1.44 after three hours. WOMAN repeated the pattern in 20,060 women with postpartum haemorrhage: 1 g IV, repeated once if bleeding continued after 30 minutes or restarted within 24 hours, reduced death from bleeding from 1.9% to 1.5%, and from 1.7% to 1.2% when given within three hours of birth. Hysterectomy was not reduced, largely because that decision had often been made by the time of randomisation.

The limits are equally clear. In acute gastrointestinal bleeding (HALT-IT, 12,009 patients, a high-dose 24-hour infusion) tranexamic acid did not reduce death from bleeding and doubled venous thromboembolism from 0.4% to 0.8%; it has no routine place there. In isolated head injury (CRASH-3) the benefit was confined to mild and moderate injury treated early. In an advanced prehospital system (PATCH-Trauma, run largely in Australia and New Zealand) it did not improve functional survival at six months, although 28-day mortality was lower at 17.3% against 21.8%. For elective and urgent non-cardiac surgery, POISE-3 found less major bleeding (9.1% against 11.7%) with prophylactic tranexamic acid, and could not formally exclude a small increase in cardiovascular events. In cardiac surgery the dose-related risk is seizures, covered in the [bypass chapter](a07-cardiopulmonary-bypass.html).

The Australian guideline accordingly supports tranexamic acid within three hours of injury in trauma and within three hours of onset in obstetric haemorrhage, and makes no recommendation for gastrointestinal bleeding. For unexpected surgical bleeding in theatre there is no trial of rescue dosing; giving 1 g IV early in a major surgical bleed is the author's practice by extrapolation, and the three-hour clock starts when the bleeding does.

## Fibrinogen: the first factor to run out

Fibrinogen is the substrate of the clot, present in grams rather than milligrams, and it reaches a critically low level before any other factor during dilution because nothing in a standard resuscitation replaces it in quantity. Plasma contains fibrinogen at about the concentration of the patient's own blood, so plasma can maintain a level but cannot raise a low one without a very large volume. Raising it needs a concentrated source.

The trigger in the European trauma guideline is a Clauss fibrinogen of 1.5 g/L or below, or viscoelastic evidence of a functional deficit, treated with an initial 3-4 g. The Australian guideline lists a fibrinogen below 2.0 g/L as a critical derangement and gives the same dose with its local equivalents: 3-4 g of fibrinogen concentrate, or 10 units of whole blood cryoprecipitate, or 4 units of apheresis cryoprecipitate. In cardiac surgical bleeding, concentrate was noninferior to cryoprecipitate (FIBRES), so the choice is about speed, storage and supply rather than efficacy.

Measured replacement works; blind replacement has not been shown to. CRYOSTAT-2 gave an empirical 6 g fibrinogen equivalent of cryoprecipitate within 90 minutes to bleeding trauma patients on top of standard care, and 28-day mortality was unchanged (25.3% against 26.1%). The practical position is to measure fibrinogen at activation, send it again with every round of bloods, and treat the number.

## Calcium

Give calcium early and by the number. Both guidelines recommend monitoring ionised calcium throughout a massive transfusion and correcting it, the European guideline specifying calcium chloride, which delivers about three times the elemental calcium of the same volume of calcium gluconate. The author's practice is calcium chloride 10% 10 mL IV through a reliable line for an ionised calcium below 1.0 mmol/L, or empirically after about every four units of red cells or plasma when products are going in faster than gases can be run, with a target above 1.1 mmol/L. An unexplained fall in blood pressure during rapid plasma or platelet infusion is citrate until proved otherwise.

Potassium is the mirror problem. Stored red cells leak potassium into their supernatant, and rapid infusion through a central line into a cold, acidotic patient can produce an arrhythmia. Calcium treats that too. Check potassium on every gas.

## Viscoelastic testing: what the trace does and does not see

Thromboelastography and rotational thromboelastometry measure the mechanical strength of a clot forming in whole blood over time, at the bedside, with a usable answer in about ten minutes. The value is speed and the separation of problems that a conventional screen lumps together.

| What the trace shows | Mechanism | Treatment it points to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slow initiation (long clotting time or R time) | Low factor activity, or heparin | Plasma or PCC; protamine if a heparinase channel corrects it |
| Weak clot in the fibrinogen channel (FIBTEM, functional fibrinogen) | Low fibrinogen | Fibrinogen concentrate or cryoprecipitate |
| Weak overall clot with a normal fibrinogen channel | Low platelet count or contribution | Platelets |
| Clot that forms then dissolves | Hyperfibrinolysis | Tranexamic acid |

The blind spots matter as much. The sample is warmed to 37 degrees C and recalcified, so the trace does not show the effect of the patient's hypothermia or hypocalcaemia. Standard assays do not detect aspirin or P2Y12 inhibitors, von Willebrand deficiency, or reliably the direct oral anticoagulants. A normal trace in a patient who is still oozing means look for a surgical cause, a cold patient or an antiplatelet drug.

The outcome evidence is strongest where bleeding is predictable. A meta-analysis of 17 trials, most in cardiac surgery, found fewer patients transfused red cells, plasma and platelets with viscoelastic guidance, and a mortality signal that the authors themselves graded as low quality. In trauma, ITACTIC found no difference between viscoelastic and conventional guidance layered on an empirical protocol (67% against 64% alive and free of massive transfusion at 24 hours). In postpartum haemorrhage, OBS2 showed that fibrinogen need not be replaced when the Fibtem A5 is above 12 mm. The Australian guideline says viscoelastic testing may be beneficial and stops short of a recommendation. Use it if the hospital has it and staff who run it daily; do not delay products waiting for it.

## Cell salvage and its limits

A cell saver aspirates blood from the field through a double-lumen sucker that mixes it with anticoagulant at the tip, filters it into a reservoir, then centrifuges and washes it with saline. What returns to the patient is a concentrated suspension of the patient's own red cells in saline. Plasma, clotting factors, platelets, free haemoglobin, anticoagulant and most contaminants go to the waste bag. Two consequences follow. Salvaged blood is fresh, warm, immediately available and avoids donor exposure. It is also red cells only, so a patient given three litres of salvaged blood has been given three litres of red cells towards the ratio and needs plasma, fibrinogen and platelets accordingly.

The Association of Anaesthetists guideline recommends considering collection whenever blood loss may exceed 500 mL, and setting up in "collect only" mode (suction and reservoir, with the processing set opened only if enough is collected) when the loss is uncertain. That makes it cheap to start for a [liver resection](../c/liver-resection.html), an [open aortic repair](../c/open-abdominal-aortic-aneurysm-repair.html), a [ruptured aneurysm](../c/ruptured-abdominal-aortic-aneurysm.html) or a placenta accreta. The Australian guideline says salvage may be considered within a major haemorrhage protocol and finds the evidence insufficient for a recommendation.

The limits are about what is in the suction.

- Obstetrics. The historical fear was that salvaged blood would carry amniotic fluid into the circulation. Washing removes the plasma-phase components of amniotic fluid in laboratory studies, and SALVO, in 3,028 women at caesarean section, reported no such harm. It also showed that routine use is not worthwhile: donor transfusion was 2.5% with salvage and 3.5% without (adjusted odds ratio 0.65, confidence interval 0.42 to 1.01), and blood was returned in only half of those in whom the machine was set up, a mean of about 260 mL. The guideline position is not to use it routinely at caesarean section, and to collect when the woman is anaemic, at high risk of haemorrhage or bleeding unexpectedly. Fetal red cells are not removed by washing, so a rhesus D negative mother needs a Kleihauer test and anti-D dosed to it. A leucocyte depletion filter may be considered, at the cost of a slower reinfusion rate and occasional bradykinin-mediated hypotension; if the pressure falls during reinfusion through a filter, stop the infusion and take the filter out before blaming the bleeding. The working party did not recommend routine double suction.
- Cancer. Tumour cells are aspirated and some survive washing. The guideline finds no conclusive evidence that salvage induces metastases or changes prognosis, notes that a leucocyte depletion filter reduces the malignant cell count, and asks for specific consent. In a patient exsanguinating during cancer surgery the comparison is with donor blood, which carries its own immunological effects, or with no blood.
- Contamination. Bowel contents and an infected field are relative contraindications, not absolute ones. Washing and a leucocyte depletion filter remove most bacteria, the patient is on antibiotics, and the risk is weighed against need. In a trauma laparotomy with a hollow viscus injury and massive loss, salvage is reasonable; in a contained elective case with a little faecal spill, it is not worth it.
- Things that must not be aspirated. Anything that should not be given intravenously or that lyses red cells: antibiotic irrigation, iodine, topical haemostatic agents, and bone cement while it is being applied. The surgeon uses the wall sucker for those and returns to the salvage sucker after saline irrigation.

## Permissive hypotension, and when not to use it

Before the hole is closed, pressure is what drives blood out of it, and crystalloid given to restore a normal pressure dilutes clotting factors, cools the patient and can dislodge a soft clot. In penetrating torso trauma, withholding fluid until theatre improved survival to discharge from 62% to 70%. In theatre, a target minimum mean pressure of 50 mmHg rather than 65 mmHg reduced product use and postoperative coagulopathy in a preliminary trial. The European guideline turns this into a target: systolic 80-90 mmHg (mean 50-60 mmHg) with restricted volume until major bleeding has been stopped, using noradrenaline if restricted fluid cannot hold even that.

The strategy trades organ perfusion for time, so it is only for patients whose organs can afford the trade and only until the leak is controlled. Do not use it in:

- Traumatic brain injury. A swollen brain needs a perfusion pressure, and hypotension is the most damaging secondary insult. The European guideline asks for a mean pressure of at least 80 mmHg in severe head injury; the [neuroanaesthesia chapter](a08-neuroanaesthesia-essentials.html) and the [head injury card](../c/traumatic-brain-injury.html) give the detail. Spinal cord injury follows the same logic.
- Pregnancy before delivery. Uteroplacental flow is not autoregulated; maternal hypotension is fetal hypoxia.
- The older patient with chronic hypertension, a tight carotid or coronary stenosis, or chronic kidney disease, whose autoregulatory range sits higher. Author's judgement: accept a systolic nearer 100-110 mmHg and move faster on surgical control.
- Controlled bleeding. Once a clamp is on or the pack is holding, the argument has gone; restore volume and pressure.
- Prolonged application. The trials tested minutes to a few hours. A patient held at a mean of 50 mmHg for an afternoon acquires an ischaemic gut and kidneys.

Under general anaesthesia the patient is hypotensive partly from vasodilatation rather than vasoconstriction, so a low pressure does not carry the same guarantee of redistributed flow that it does in the awake trauma patient. Lighten the anaesthetic to what is needed for amnesia, use a vasopressor to offset the drugs, and give blood products for the volume.

## Reversing anticoagulants

| Drug | Reversal | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Warfarin | Four-factor PCC 25-50 IU/kg IV plus vitamin K 5-10 mg IV | PCC corrected the INR to 1.3 or below within 30 min in 62.2% against 9.6% with plasma; vitamin K sustains the effect after the concentrate has gone |
| Dabigatran | Idarucizumab 5 g IV | Complete reversal within minutes in the cohort study; the thrombotic events at 90 days reflect the underlying indication |
| Apixaban, rivaroxaban | PCC, or andexanet alfa where available | Andexanet controlled haematoma expansion better than usual care (67.0% against 53.1%) and doubled thrombotic events (10.3% against 5.6%); PCC data are uncontrolled, with pooled haemostatic effectiveness about 0.85 |
| Unfractionated heparin | Protamine, about 1 mg IV per 100 units of heparin still active | Give slowly; see the bypass chapter for reactions |
| Low molecular weight heparin | Protamine, partial effect | Reverses the anti-IIa activity more than the anti-Xa activity |
| Aspirin, P2Y12 inhibitors | No specific reversal | The European guideline recommends against routine platelet transfusion; tranexamic acid and surgical haemostasis first, platelets for life-threatening bleeding or a closed space |

For any of these, the time since the last dose and the renal function decide how much drug is left. A thrombin time within the normal range excludes a meaningful dabigatran effect, and a drug-calibrated anti-Xa level measures the factor Xa inhibitors; a normal INR excludes neither. The [vascular patient chapter](a10-vascular-patient.html) covers the elective interruption rules.

## Obstetric haemorrhage is a different disease

The [obstetric haemorrhage card](../c/major-obstetric-haemorrhage-and-placenta-accreta.html) covers the operation; four differences belong here.

First, the reserve hides the loss. Blood volume at term is expanded by close to half, so a healthy woman loses a litre and a half with little change in her observations, then decompensates quickly. Concealed loss in the uterus, the drapes and the floor is routinely underestimated; weigh swabs and count out loud.

Second, the bed bleeds at a rate no other organ approaches, because the placental site receives several hundred millilitres a minute at term and its haemostasis is mechanical: the myometrium contracts around the spiral arteries. Atony is therefore treated with uterotonics and compression first, and products second.

Third, fibrinogen is the sentinel. Normal fibrinogen at term is roughly double the non-pregnant level, so a result of 2 g/L, unremarkable elsewhere, is already a substantial fall. In women with established postpartum haemorrhage, each 1 g/L lower fibrinogen at presentation multiplied the risk of progression to severe haemorrhage by 2.63, and a level above 4 g/L was reassuring. OBS2 supplies the treatment threshold: replace below 2 g/L or a Fibtem A5 of 12 mm, not above.

Fourth, the coagulopathy depends on the cause. Atony and genital tract trauma bleed with normal clotting until late, and formulaic early plasma over-treats many of these women. Abruption, amniotic fluid embolism and a retained dead fetus consume fibrinogen from the first minute and need concentrate or cryoprecipitate before the first laboratory result returns. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV goes in at diagnosis in both.

## Upper gastrointestinal bleeding

The variceal or ulcer bleed that comes to theatre or endoscopy is the one setting in which giving less blood has been shown to be better. A restrictive strategy with a haemoglobin threshold of 70 g/L improved six-week survival (95% against 91%) and reduced rebleeding, and portal pressure rose in the liberally transfused group: in portal hypertension, volume is pressure at the bleeding point. Resuscitate the exsanguinating patient, but stop at adequate. Tranexamic acid is not part of this pathway. The airway is the anaesthetic problem, and it is covered in the [endoscopy card](../c/gastroscopy-and-colonoscopy.html).

## When the bleeding stops

Stopping is a decision, and the Australian guideline asks for it explicitly: stand the protocol down, return unused products, and move to targeted correction. Then:

- Recheck the full blood count, coagulation, fibrinogen, calcium, potassium, lactate and temperature, and correct what is abnormal.
- Expect the complications of the treatment: circulatory overload once vasoconstriction relaxes, lung injury, hypothermia and a late hypercoagulable state.
- Restart thromboprophylaxis. The European guideline recommends mechanical prophylaxis while the bleeding risk persists and adding pharmacological prophylaxis within 24 hours of bleeding control.
- Send the patient to a bed that can detect rebleeding: an arterial line, hourly drain outputs and a named person to call.

## The rule set

1. Call it early; the protocol is a logistics tool, and a false alarm costs little.
2. Ask the surgeon for pressure or a clamp before asking the blood bank for anything.
3. Red cells, plasma and platelets at no worse than 2:1:1 until results arrive, then treat the results.
4. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV within three hours for trauma and obstetric bleeding; not for gastrointestinal bleeding.
5. Measure fibrinogen at activation and replace it with 3-4 g when it is low: below 1.5-2.0 g/L in general, below 2 g/L in obstetrics.
6. Keep ionised calcium above 1.0 mmol/L, temperature above 35 degrees C and pH above 7.2; these are haemostatic targets.
7. Salvaged blood is red cells only. Start collection when loss may pass 500 mL, and keep irrigation, iodine, haemostatic agents and cement out of the sucker.
8. Accept a systolic pressure of 80-90 mmHg only until the leak is controlled, and never in head injury, spinal cord injury or the undelivered parturient.
9. Reverse the anticoagulant with its specific agent and give vitamin K with PCC.
10. Stand the protocol down deliberately and plan for overload, rebleeding and thromboprophylaxis.
