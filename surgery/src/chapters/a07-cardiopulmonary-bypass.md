## The circuit from first principles

To operate inside a heart, or on its surface without it moving, the surgeon needs it empty and still. Something else must then do what the heart and lungs do: collect systemic venous blood, add oxygen, remove carbon dioxide, and return it to the aorta at a pressure and flow that keeps the organs alive. The bypass circuit is that something, and it is easiest to understand as a line of components, each solving one problem and each creating one hazard.

```
   RA or venae cavae
        |  venous line: gravity siphon (height of table over reservoir)
        v
   [ venous reservoir ] <--- cardiotomy suckers, LV vent (filtered)
        |
   [ pump ]  roller or centrifugal
        |
   [ heat exchanger + membrane oxygenator ] <--- sweep gas (CO2), FiO2 (O2)
        |
   [ arterial line filter / bubble trap ]
        |
        v
   ascending aorta (distal to the cross-clamp)

   separate small circuit: cardioplegia pump --> aortic root or coronary sinus
```

Venous drainage is passive. The venous line is a siphon: blood runs downhill from the right atrium to a reservoir set below the patient, and the flow depends on the height difference, the bore of the cannula and line, and the venous pressure. The siphon fails if it fills with air (an airlock), if the cannula is malpositioned or the heart is lifted so that the atrial wall occludes it, or if the table is too low. Poor drainage shows as a falling reservoir level and a heart that fills when it should be empty. Vacuum can be applied to the reservoir to assist drainage through small cannulae, at the price of entraining air.

The reservoir is the circuit's compliance and its safety margin. It buffers the difference between drainage and pump output, lets air rise out of the blood, and gives the perfusionist a few seconds of volume if drainage stops. An empty reservoir with a running pump sends air to the aorta, which is why level alarms and the perfusionist's undivided attention exist.

The pump is one of two kinds. A roller pump squeezes tubing against a raceway and delivers a fixed volume per revolution regardless of resistance: reliable, but it will pump against a kinked line until something bursts, and it will pump air. A centrifugal pump spins blood in a cone and generates flow that depends on the resistance ahead of it: it will not over-pressurise an obstructed line and handles gross air poorly, which is protective, but if it stops with the lines unclamped the aorta drains backwards through it. Flow is non-pulsatile in routine use.

The oxygenator is a bundle of hollow fibres with gas inside and blood outside. Oxygenation is set by the oxygen fraction of the gas; carbon dioxide removal is set by the total gas flow, the sweep. The two are independent, which is the opposite of the lung under your ventilator and the reason the perfusionist can fix a high carbon dioxide without touching oxygenation. A heat exchanger, integrated with the oxygenator, cools and rewarms the blood against water from a heater-cooler unit. An arterial line filter and bubble trap is the last barrier before the patient.

Suckers return shed blood from the pericardium to the reservoir, and a vent in the left ventricle or aortic root keeps the arrested heart from distending with blood returning through bronchial and thebesian veins. Suction blood has touched pericardium and air, and it is the dirtiest blood in the circuit: activated, haemolysed and laden with fat.

Priming the circuit with crystalloid adds over a litre to the circulation at the moment bypass begins. The haematocrit falls by a quarter to a third, viscosity falls, and so does the oxygen content. That is tolerated because cooling and anaesthesia reduce demand, but it is the first step of the coagulopathy you manage two hours later, and it sets a floor on the patient you can safely put on bypass without blood in the prime.

## What adequate perfusion means

Pump flow replaces cardiac output. The conventional figure at normothermia is about 2.2-2.4 L/min/m2, reduced as the patient is cooled, with a mean arterial pressure in the range of 50-80 mmHg maintained by adjusting resistance with a vasopressor or vasodilator rather than by changing flow. Flow, not pressure, is the primary variable: a pressure of 70 mmHg produced by phenylephrine at a flow of 1.6 L/min/m2 is not adequate perfusion.

What the tissues need is oxygen delivery, which is flow multiplied by content, and haemodilution attacks the content. In coronary surgery the lowest oxygen delivery on bypass predicted acute renal failure better than the lowest haematocrit did, with a threshold near 272 mL/min/m2, and a randomised trial of perfusion targeted to oxygen delivery reduced stage 1 acute kidney injury without changing more severe injury. The practical point is that a low haematocrit on bypass can be offset by more flow before it has to be offset by transfusion.

For pressure, the brain is the organ of concern, and the evidence does not support chasing a high number: a randomised comparison of a higher with a lower pressure target on bypass found new lesions on diffusion-weighted imaging in over half of patients in both groups and no difference in volume. Most perioperative strokes are embolic. The author's practice is a mean of 50-70 mmHg for most patients and 70-80 mmHg for those with known carotid disease, chronic hypertension or chronic kidney disease. The monitors that matter on bypass are mixed venous saturation at the reservoir, lactate, urine output, temperature and, increasingly, cerebral oximetry as a trend.

## Cannulation and going on

The arterial cannula goes in first so that volume can be given through it if the venous cannulation bleeds or provokes arrhythmia. The standard site is the distal ascending aorta. The surgeon wants a systolic pressure near 100 mmHg while the purse-strings and cannula go in, because a tense aorta tears and dissects; you provide it and then let the pressure come back. The hazards at this step are aortic dissection (a sudden rise in line pressure with a fall in the patient's arterial pressure once on bypass), malposition into a head vessel, and atheroma dislodged from a diseased aortic wall. Epiaortic or transoesophageal imaging of the aorta before cannulation exists to find the last of these. Femoral or axillary arterial cannulation is used for redo sternotomy, aortic surgery and minimally invasive approaches, each with its own malperfusion pattern.

Venous cannulation is a single two-stage cannula through the right atrial appendage for operations that do not open the right heart, or two caval cannulae with snares for mitral, tricuspid and septal surgery, where the right atrium must be dry. Manipulating the atrium causes arrhythmia and hypotension; that is expected and short.

Before the perfusionist is told to go on: heparin given and the clotting time confirmed, arterial line pressure trace pulsatile and consistent with an intraluminal cannula, anaesthesia and neuromuscular block adequate for the period when the volatile is no longer delivered by the lungs, and cerebral monitoring in place. Once full flow is achieved and the heart is empty, ventilation stops. Anaesthesia continues by volatile into the oxygenator sweep gas or by infusion, and awareness on bypass is a recognised risk when neither is running, particularly during rewarming.

## Heparin and the activated clotting time

Blood clots on plastic. Unfractionated heparin is used because it is immediate, measurable at the bedside and reversible. It works by binding antithrombin and accelerating its inhibition of thrombin and factor Xa many times over, so it is only as effective as the antithrombin available.

The usual dose is 300-400 units/kg IV into a central line, with the activated clotting time (ACT) checked about three minutes later. The joint American guideline makes a functional clotting test before and at intervals during bypass a class I recommendation, and regards an ACT above 480 s as a reasonable target, noting that the threshold is approximate and device-dependent and that values above 400 s are commonly accepted with maximal-activation or microcuvette instruments. The ACT is repeated every 30 minutes or so, because heparin is consumed and the patient's response is individual: the initial dose predicts the first ACT poorly. Hypothermia and haemodilution prolong the ACT without any more heparin on board, so an ACT that looks comfortable during cold bypass can fall on rewarming.

Heparin resistance is failure to reach the target ACT after an adequate dose. The common settings are preoperative heparin infusion, which depletes antithrombin, and inflammatory states. The causes are several, and the ACT is not a pure measure of heparin effect, but the practical sequence is the same: confirm the dose went into the patient, give more heparin, and if the ACT still does not respond, supply antithrombin as concentrate or as fresh frozen plasma.

For a patient with acute or recent heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, the guideline's first advice is to delay elective surgery until the antibody is negative, when a single intraoperative exposure to heparin is acceptable. For urgent surgery, bivalirudin is the reasonable alternative; it has no reversal agent, is cleared by plasma proteolysis and the kidney, and clots in any blood left stagnant in the circuit or the pericardium.

Tranexamic acid is standard. In 4,631 patients having coronary surgery it halved reoperation for bleeding or tamponade (1.4% against 2.8%) and nearly halved blood product use, without more death or thrombosis. Seizures were more frequent (0.7% against 0.1%), a dose-related effect that is the argument for the lower end of the dose range, especially in open-chamber surgery and renal impairment.

## Cardioplegia and myocardial protection

With the aorta clamped between the arterial cannula and the coronary ostia, the heart has no blood supply. Unprotected, a warm, beating or fibrillating heart exhausts its high-energy phosphate within minutes. Protection rests on reducing demand to near zero and then, in most techniques, on restoring some supply intermittently.

Electromechanical work is most of the heart's oxygen consumption, so the first and largest step is arrest. A potassium-rich solution delivered into the coronary circulation depolarises the membrane and holds it there, with sodium channels inactivated: diastolic arrest. Cooling the myocardium removes a further share of the remaining basal demand. The solution also carries buffer and, in blood cardioplegia, oxygen and substrate.

| Choice | Options | What it means for you |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Route | Antegrade into the aortic root; directly into the ostia; retrograde through the coronary sinus | Antegrade needs a competent aortic valve, otherwise the solution fills and distends the left ventricle. Severe coronary stenoses limit distribution. Retrograde reaches beyond stenoses but protects the right ventricle poorly, and the catheter position is confirmed by pressure and by echocardiography |
| Vehicle | Blood or crystalloid | Blood carries oxygen and buffers; crystalloid adds to haemodilution |
| Temperature | Cold, tepid or warm | Warm techniques need near-continuous delivery |
| Schedule | Repeated every 15-20 min, or a single long-acting dose | Del Nido solution (with lidocaine) is given once for up to about 90 min. In a meta-analysis of randomised trials in adults it shortened cross-clamp time by a mean of about nine minutes and reduced the need for defibrillation (15% against 38%) |

The signs that protection is failing are visible from the head of the table: electrical activity returning on the electrocardiogram before the next dose is due, a left ventricle that distends on echocardiography (the vent is not working, or the aortic valve is leaking cardioplegia into the ventricle), and a rising potassium from repeated doses. The potassium load matters at the end: a level above about 5.5 mmol/L impairs conduction and is treated before weaning with insulin and glucose, calcium, diuresis or haemofiltration on the circuit.

The right ventricle is the chamber most often under-protected: it lies anteriorly under the theatre lights and rewarms, retrograde cardioplegia does not reach it well, and air entering the anteriorly placed right coronary ostium finds it first. Right ventricular failure after a long cross-clamp is a protection problem until proved otherwise.

## Temperature and acid-base

Cooling lowers metabolic rate, by roughly 6-7% per degree, and so widens the margin of safety for low flow or for an interruption of flow. Most adult valve and coronary surgery is now done at 32-35 degrees C or at normothermia, because deeper cooling prolongs bypass and worsens coagulopathy. Deep hypothermia (18-20 degrees C) with circulatory arrest, or moderate hypothermia with selective cerebral perfusion, is reserved for [arch surgery](../c/surgery-of-the-thoracic-aorta-and.html).

Rewarming is where the harm occurs. Gas is less soluble in warm blood, so warming cold blood quickly brings gas out of solution as bubbles, and a brain perfused with blood warmer than 37 degrees C is a brain with a fever at the moment it is receiving emboli. The joint temperature guideline is specific: use the oxygenator arterial outlet temperature as the surrogate for cerebral temperature and assume that it underestimates it; keep the arterial outlet below 37 degrees C; keep the gradient between arterial outlet and venous inflow at 10 degrees C or less during cooling and rewarming; and once the arterial outlet is at 30 degrees C or above, rewarm with a gradient of 4 degrees C or less at no more than 0.5 degrees C per minute. Nasopharyngeal or pulmonary artery temperature is the weaning measure. Bladder temperature lags, and the patient who is 37 degrees C in the nasopharynx with a cold periphery will drift down after bypass.

Gas solubility also changes pH. As blood cools, carbon dioxide becomes more soluble, its partial pressure falls and pH rises. Alpha-stat management accepts this: the sample is read at 37 degrees C and kept normal there, which preserves intracellular enzyme charge state and cerebral autoregulation. pH-stat corrects the values to the patient's actual temperature by adding carbon dioxide, which dilates cerebral vessels: more uniform brain cooling, more flow, and more emboli delivered. Adult practice at moderate hypothermia is alpha-stat. For deep hypothermic arrest the evidence in adults is mixed, and pH-stat during cooling is mainly a paediatric technique.

## The inflammatory response

Bypass provokes a whole-body inflammatory response by four routes: blood contacting the foreign surface of the circuit activates the contact system, complement, neutrophils and platelets; shed blood returned by the suckers is already activated; the heart and lungs are ischaemic and then reperfused; and reduced splanchnic flow lets endotoxin cross the gut. The consequences are capillary leak, vasodilatation, myocardial stunning, lung and kidney injury, and consumption of clotting factors and platelets. The size of the response tracks the duration of bypass, which is the simplest reason that a long pump run is followed by vasoplegia and bleeding.

Suppressing the response pharmacologically has been disappointing. In 7,507 patients, methylprednisolone did not reduce death or major morbidity, and in 4,494 patients high-dose dexamethasone did not significantly reduce major adverse events (7.0% against 8.5%), although it shortened ventilation and stay. Routine steroid for bypass is not supported. What does help is mechanical: shorter bypass, coated and smaller circuits, less cardiotomy suction, and washing shed blood.

## Weaning: the checklist

Separation is the transfer of the circulation back to a heart that has been ischaemic, cold and empty. It goes wrong when a precondition has been missed, so run the list aloud with the surgeon and perfusionist.

| Item | Target before coming off |
| --- | --- |
| Temperature | Nasopharyngeal about 36.5-37 degrees C; arterial outlet never above 37 |
| Ventilation | Lungs re-expanded under vision, ventilator on, alarms on, end-tidal trace present |
| Rhythm and rate | Sinus if possible, 80-90 per min; epicardial pacing attached and tested; defibrillate ventricular fibrillation |
| Chemistry | Potassium 4.5-5.5 mmol/L; ionised calcium normal; pH and base excess corrected; haematocrit acceptable |
| Air | De-airing completed for open-chamber cases, confirmed on echocardiography; head down while the root is vented |
| Contractility | Inotrope chosen and running if function was poor before, the clamp time was long or the ventricle looks sluggish |
| Resistance | Vasopressor ready; expect vasodilatation after rewarming |
| Monitors | Transducers re-zeroed; echocardiography probe in a view that shows both ventricles |
| Coagulation | Protamine drawn up, products ordered if the case predicts bleeding |

The perfusionist then partially occludes the venous line so the heart fills, reduces pump flow in steps, and stops. Watch the heart directly and on echocardiography as it fills: a ventricle that distends without ejecting needs to be emptied again and supported. Fill to the lowest pressure that gives an adequate output.

If the pressure is low, the question is which of four things is wrong, and echocardiography answers it in seconds: an empty, vigorous ventricle (volume); a full, poorly contracting left ventricle, global or regional (stunning, air in the right coronary, a kinked or occluded graft); a dilated right ventricle with an underfilled left (right ventricular failure, protamine reaction, pulmonary hypertension); or a full, vigorous heart with a low pressure (vasoplegia). A new structural problem (a paravalvular leak, systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve after repair or in a small hypertrophied ventricle) belongs on the same list. Failure after two reasonable attempts is a reason to go back on, rest the heart at full flow, and plan mechanical support, not to keep escalating adrenaline.

## Protamine

Protamine is a strongly basic protein that binds acidic heparin in a stable, inactive complex. It is given once the surgeon is satisfied with separation and before the arterial cannula comes out or as it does; tell the perfusionist when it starts, because pump suckers must stop or the circuit clots.

Dose is the first issue. Protamine in excess of heparin is itself an anticoagulant, impairing platelet function and factor activation. The narrative evidence is that the ratio should not exceed 1 mg per 100 units of heparin, that ratios above this are associated with more bleeding, and that the optimum may lie between 0.6 and 1.0 of the initial heparin dose; the American guideline sets an outer limit of 2.6 mg per 100 units and favours titrating protamine to the heparin actually present. A further dose for a prolonged ACT is reasonable once; a second and third are how protamine excess happens. Heparin rebound, the reappearance of heparin from tissue stores an hour or more later, is a cause of delayed ooze that a standard ACT does not reliably detect.

Reactions are the second issue, and they fall into three patterns. Hypotension from rapid injection is vasodilatation, is common and is prevented by giving the dose over at least five to ten minutes. Anaphylaxis is immune-mediated and more likely after previous exposure, including protamine-containing insulin. The third is rare and catastrophic: acute pulmonary vasoconstriction with right ventricular failure, a distended right heart and an empty left, within minutes of starting. The guideline's class I advice for that event is to stop the protamine and resuscitate, including re-heparinising and returning to bypass. Treat it as acute right heart failure: adrenaline, a pulmonary vasodilator, and the pump.

## Vasoplegia

Vasoplegic syndrome is low systemic vascular resistance and hypotension with a normal or high cardiac output after bypass. The mechanisms are induced nitric oxide synthesis, hyperpolarisation of vascular smooth muscle through potassium channels, and a relative deficiency of vasopressin after prolonged stress. It is more likely after a long bypass run, with preoperative ACE inhibitor use, poor ventricular function, endocarditis and mechanical support.

Confirm the diagnosis before treating it: the echocardiogram shows a full, vigorous ventricle. Then:

1. Noradrenaline is first line.
2. Add vasopressin early, because the deficiency is specific and it works when catecholamine receptors are saturated. In the one randomised trial in vasoplegic shock after cardiac surgery, vasopressin as the first agent reduced the composite of death or severe complications (32% against 49%) and atrial fibrillation (63.8% against 82.1%) compared with noradrenaline; it was a single-centre trial.
3. For refractory cases, methylene blue (which inhibits nitric oxide signalling, interferes with pulse oximetry and risks serotonin toxicity with serotonergic drugs) or hydroxocobalamin (a nitric oxide scavenger that discolours urine and interferes with some laboratory assays and dialysis sensors) are used on mechanism and case series; their effect on outcome is not established.

## Bleeding after bypass

Every patient leaves bypass with some coagulopathy: platelets are reduced in number and function, clotting factors and fibrinogen are diluted and consumed, fibrinolysis has been activated, and the patient may be cold. Residual or rebound heparin and protamine excess are added by us. The first question is surgical. Bright blood from one place, or loss that does not respond to products, is a stitch, and no algorithm fixes it.

For diffuse ooze, a test-guided algorithm outperforms clinician judgement. In a stepped-wedge trial across 12 hospitals and 7,402 patients, point-of-care viscoelastic and platelet function testing within a transfusion algorithm reduced red cell transfusion, platelet transfusion and major bleeding. The algorithm follows the mechanism: correct temperature, calcium and pH; check for heparin with a heparinase channel; platelets for a weak clot with normal fibrinogen contribution, or empirically after a long run or recent antiplatelet drugs; fibrinogen concentrate or cryoprecipitate for a low fibrinogen (the two were equivalent in FIBRES); plasma or prothrombin complex concentrate for delayed clot initiation. The [haemorrhage chapter](a05-major-haemorrhage.html) covers the general principles.

For red cells, TRICS III showed that a restrictive haemoglobin threshold was noninferior to a liberal one for death, infarction, stroke or dialysis, with a fifth fewer patients transfused. Cell salvage of shed blood after heparin reversal and washing of the residual pump volume both return red cells; salvage in place of the cardiotomy suckers during bypass strips factors and platelets and is not recommended.

## Lesion-specific haemodynamic goals

Between induction and bypass, and again afterwards if the lesion is not fully corrected, the anatomy dictates the targets. The logic for each row is what the abnormal chamber needs to keep ejecting or filling.

| Lesion | Preload | Heart rate and rhythm | Contractility | Afterload (SVR) | The error to avoid |

## Transoesophageal echocardiography: what to look for and when

The comprehensive examination is defined by the joint echocardiography and cardiovascular anaesthesia guideline; the essentials are the questions that change the operation.

Before bypass: confirm the lesion and its mechanism, since a repair plan depends on it; grade both ventricles and note regional wall motion as the baseline; look for aortic regurgitation, which decides how cardioplegia is given and whether the ventricle needs a vent; look for a patent foramen ovale, left atrial appendage thrombus and atheroma in the ascending aorta and arch; and guide placement of a coronary sinus catheter or femoral venous cannula.

Coming off: air, which collects at the anterior, highest points (the right upper pulmonary vein, the left ventricular apex, the aortic root) and should be cleared before the vent comes out; ventricular filling and function in real time; new regional wall motion abnormality in a grafted or right coronary territory; the valve result, including paravalvular leak, residual regurgitation after repair, leaflet motion of a prosthesis and the gradient across it; systolic anterior motion; and the aorta at the cannulation site for dissection.

Relative contraindications are oesophageal pathology (stricture, varices, recent surgery), and the probe is the cause of rare but serious oesophageal injury; insert it gently in the anticoagulated patient and do not force it.

## Mechanical support in outline

When the heart cannot carry the circulation off bypass despite reasonable drug support, the options form a ladder. The [resternotomy and support card](../c/resternotomy-tamponade-and-mechanical-circulatory-support.html) has the practical detail.

- Intra-aortic balloon pump. A balloon in the descending aorta inflates at the dicrotic notch and deflates just before systole. Inflation raises diastolic pressure and coronary perfusion; deflation lowers the pressure the ventricle ejects against. It adds perhaps half a litre a minute and needs a ventricle that ejects. It is contraindicated in significant aortic regurgitation and aortic dissection. In cardiogenic shock after infarction it did not reduce 30-day mortality (39.7% against 41.3%), but it remains in use after cardiotomy because it is quick and familiar.
- Microaxial flow pumps sit across the aortic valve and move blood from the left ventricle to the aorta, unloading the ventricle directly. They cannot be used through a mechanical aortic valve or with left ventricular thrombus.
- Venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation is a bypass circuit without a reservoir, usually femoral. It supports both ventricles and the lungs, but the retrograde aortic flow raises left ventricular afterload: a ventricle that cannot open the aortic valve distends, stagnates and clots, and needs venting. Where native ejection of poorly oxygenated blood meets retrograde oxygenated flow, the coronaries and right arm may receive the former, so the right radial line and right-hand saturation are the monitors of choice.
- Ventricular assist devices are the durable step, as a bridge to recovery, transplant or destination.

## The rule set

1. Think of the circuit as siphon, reservoir, pump, lung, filter: when something is wrong on bypass, ask which part.
2. No bypass without a confirmed ACT; above 480 s (or the local device threshold), repeated every 30 minutes and after rewarming.
3. On bypass, flow first, then pressure; watch venous saturation and lactate; keep anaesthesia running.
4. Watch for the signs of failed protection: electrical activity, a distending ventricle, a rising potassium.
5. Rewarm slowly, arterial outlet below 37 degrees C.
6. Wean by checklist, with echocardiography on the screen, and classify hypotension as volume, pump, right heart or resistance before treating it.
7. Protamine slowly, at no more than 1 mg per 100 units of heparin; for right heart collapse stop it and be ready to go back on.
8. Vasoplegia: noradrenaline, early vasopressin, then methylene blue or hydroxocobalamin.
9. Bleeding: surgical cause first, then a test-guided algorithm; do not chase the ACT with repeated protamine.
10. Know the lesion's goals before induction; for a stenotic valve, treat hypotension with a vasoconstrictor within seconds.
