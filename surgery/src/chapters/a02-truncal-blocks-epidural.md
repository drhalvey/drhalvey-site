title: "Analgesia for the trunk: epidural, spinal morphine, paravertebral and fascial plane blocks"
summary: "Truncal surgery hurts through two wiring systems: the body wall, supplied segmentally by the intercostal and thoracolumbar nerves, and the viscera, supplied by sympathetic, vagal and pelvic afferents that no abdominal wall block reaches. Thoracic epidural analgesia covers both and remains the reference for thoracotomy and open upper abdominal surgery, at the cost of hypotension, a meaningful failure rate and a rare catastrophic complication; it slows recovery after laparoscopic surgery and should not be used there. Intrathecal morphine gives about a day of visceral and wall analgesia from one injection and is the workhorse for laparoscopic and lower abdominal surgery at doses that stay below 300 micrograms. Paravertebral block matches epidural analgesia for unilateral chest surgery with less hypotension, and the fascial plane blocks and wound catheters are wall-only techniques whose effect is modest and which add nothing once a long-acting spinal opioid is on board. Choose by incision and by whether the viscera need covering, add up the local anaesthetic dose across every route, and time the needle around the anticoagulant."

## Two wiring systems

Pain after surgery on the trunk arrives by two routes, and the whole chapter is an exercise in matching a technique to the route that matters for a given operation.

The body wall (skin, muscle, fascia, ribs and parietal pleura or peritoneum) is supplied segmentally by the ventral rami of T1 to L1. Each thoracic spinal nerve leaves its foramen into the paravertebral space, gives off a dorsal ramus to the back, and runs forward as an intercostal nerve between the innermost and internal intercostal muscles. At about the mid-axillary line it gives off a lateral cutaneous branch that pierces the muscles to supply the skin of the lateral trunk. The main nerve continues forward; T7 to T11 leave the costal margin, run in the plane between transversus abdominis and internal oblique, enter the rectus sheath and end as anterior cutaneous branches near the midline. T12 and L1 (subcostal, iliohypogastric, ilioinguinal) supply the groin and suprapubic skin. Useful landmarks: xiphoid T6-T7, umbilicus T10, pubis T12-L1. The breast and axilla add nerves that are not intercostal: the supraclavicular nerves from the cervical plexus over the upper breast, the medial and lateral pectoral nerves from the brachial plexus to the pectoral muscles, the long thoracic and thoracodorsal nerves, and the intercostobrachial nerve (T2) to the axilla.

The viscera are wired separately. Afferents from the abdominal organs travel with the sympathetic nerves through the coeliac and mesenteric plexuses and the splanchnic nerves to spinal segments T5-L2; pelvic organs use the pelvic splanchnic nerves to S2-S4 as well; the vagus carries a further set that enters above the reach of any spinal technique; and the diaphragm refers through the phrenic nerve to the shoulder. Visceral pain is dull, poorly localised, nauseating and, after most laparoscopic and many open operations, the dominant pain for the first day.

The consequence is simple. A technique placed at the cord or the root (epidural, spinal opioid, and to a degree paravertebral block through the sympathetic chain) covers wall and viscera. A technique placed in the wall (TAP, rectus sheath, serratus, Pecs, wound catheter) covers the wall only, and only the part of the wall whose nerves it reaches. The [pain model](../b/b01-why-operations-hurt.html) tags "abdominal-wall", "chest-wall" and "visceral" map directly onto this.

## Thoracic epidural

### What it does

Local anaesthetic at the roots blocks somatic afferents, visceral afferents and sympathetic efferents over a band of segments centred on the catheter tip; an added lipophilic opioid improves the quality of the block at a given local anaesthetic dose. It is the only technique that reliably covers movement-evoked pain from a long upper abdominal or thoracic incision for three days, and the sympathetic block of the gut shortens ileus.

Whether that changes outcome has been argued for 30 years. In the MASTER trial of 888 high-risk patients having major abdominal surgery, epidural analgesia did not reduce the composite of death or major morbidity (57.1% versus 60.7%), but respiratory failure was less frequent (23% versus 30%) and pain scores were lower for three days. A later meta-analysis of randomised trials found lower mortality (3.1% versus 4.9%) and fewer arrhythmias, pneumonias and episodes of ileus, at the cost of more hypotension, pruritus, urinary retention and motor block, with technical failure in 6.1%. Most of those trials predate laparoscopic surgery and enhanced recovery, which have removed much of the respiratory and ileus burden that the epidural was treating.

That shift is the reason for the modern position. In laparoscopic colorectal surgery two randomised trials found that an epidural made things worse: a median stay of 3.7 days against 2.7 days with spinal analgesia and 2.8 days with patient-controlled analgesia in one, and slower recovery with more vasopressor use and more complications in the other. For open surgery the procedure-specific guidelines still recommend it: PROSPECT recommends epidural analgesia for open colorectal surgery, thoracic epidural or bilateral oblique subcostal TAP blocks for open liver resection, and thoracic epidural or paravertebral block for thoracotomy.

The indications that survive: open upper abdominal and thoraco-abdominal surgery ([oesophagectomy](../c/oesophagectomy.html), open [gastrectomy](../c/gastrectomy.html), [pancreaticoduodenectomy](../c/pancreaticoduodenectomy-and-distal-pancreatectomy.html), open [aortic surgery](../c/open-abdominal-aortic-aneurysm-repair.html), large midline laparotomies in patients with poor respiratory reserve, [cytoreductive surgery](../c/cytoreductive-surgery-with-hipec-and-pelvic.html)), thoracotomy, and the opioid-tolerant patient having any of these.

### Placement and dosing in principle

Site the catheter at the dermatomal middle of the incision: about T5-T7 for thoracotomy, T7-T9 for upper abdominal incisions, T9-T11 for lower abdominal incisions. A lumbar catheter for an abdominal incision blocks the legs and bladder and misses the wound. During an infusion, the dose of local anaesthetic per hour is the main determinant of block quality, with volume and concentration mattering less; an opioid or adrenaline adjuvant raises the success rate; and patient-controlled epidural boluses on a background infusion perform best.

### Hypotension

The block removes sympathetic tone from the splanchnic capacitance vessels, which hold a large fraction of the blood volume, and above T4 from the cardiac accelerator fibres. The result is venodilatation with a heart that cannot compensate by rate: relative hypovolaemia without any fluid having been lost. The reflex to treat it with fluid is the error. Litres of crystalloid given to fill a dilated venous bed end up in the bowel wall and the lungs, which is one of the mechanisms behind the worse outcomes in the laparoscopic trials. The treatment that matches the mechanism is a vasopressor (a noradrenaline or metaraminol infusion intraoperatively, and a low-dose vasopressor infusion on the ward where the unit can support it), a reduced local anaesthetic concentration, and fluid only for the patient who is hypovolaemic by other evidence. Surgeons worry about vasopressors and anastomotic perfusion; an under-perfused anastomosis in a hypotensive patient is the larger risk. Hypotension that appears on day 1 or 2 in a previously stable epidural patient is bleeding or sepsis until shown otherwise, and the epidural is the last explanation to accept.

### Failure

An epidural that is not working is common enough that a plan for it belongs in the original decision. The causes sort into three: the catheter was never in the epidural space, it has migrated out (often by day 2, with dressings and movement), or the dose is inadequate for the incision. The bedside sequence:

1. Test the block to cold on both sides and compare it with the incision. No block at all means the catheter is out. A unilateral block means a catheter too far in or lateral. A block that is present but too low or too narrow is a dosing problem.
2. Look at the catheter marking at the skin against the insertion record.
3. For a narrow or patchy block, give a bolus of the infusion solution and reassess in 20-30 min. For a unilateral block, withdraw the catheter 1-2 cm and bolus.
4. If one bolus has not produced a usable block, stop. Re-site early if the patient has days of need ahead and the anticoagulation timing allows, or convert to the fallback: opioid patient-controlled analgesia with ketamine or IV lidocaine, or wound or rectus sheath catheters placed by the surgeon or under ultrasound.

A failing epidural with a new motor block is not a failure to be boluses through: a dense or progressive motor block on a thoracic epidural infusion is an epidural haematoma or abscess until imaging says otherwise.

### The rare disaster

The national audit of 707,455 central neuraxial blocks put permanent injury at 2.0 to 4.2 per 100,000 and paraplegia or death at 0.7 to 1.8 per 100,000, depending on how pessimistically the cases were read. Those are averages across obstetric, spinal and perioperative blocks; the perioperative epidural in an elderly, anticoagulated surgical patient sits at the high end of that range. It is a risk worth taking for a thoracotomy or an oesophagectomy and hard to justify for an operation where a simpler technique gives nearly the same pain relief.

## Intrathecal morphine

A single injection of preservative-free morphine into the cerebrospinal fluid spreads rostrally over hours and binds opioid receptors in the dorsal horn at every level it reaches. It is slow (onset 1-2 h, so it is a postoperative analgesic and not an intraoperative one), long (about 18-24 h), and it treats visceral and somatic pain together. It suits operations where the worst pain lasts about a day, where an epidural is unjustified, and where a wall block alone would miss the viscera: laparoscopic and open colorectal resection, [hysterectomy](../c/abdominal-hysterectomy-and-myomectomy.html), [prostatectomy](../c/robotic-and-open-radical-prostatectomy.html), [nephrectomy](../c/nephrectomy-and-partial-nephrectomy.html), liver resection in units that avoid epidurals because of postoperative coagulopathy, and [caesarean section](../c/elective-caesarean-section.html).

The evidence in outline: across major surgery, intrathecal morphine without local anaesthetic lowered pain on movement by about 2 cm on a 10 cm scale at 12 and 24 h and spared 24 mg of morphine in the first day after abdominal surgery (less, about 10 mg, after cardiothoracic surgery), with more pruritus and more respiratory depression than control and no linear dose-response. In laparoscopic colonic resection within an enhanced recovery programme, it brought fitness for discharge forward by a day. In laparoscopic colorectal surgery, spinal analgesia gave a shorter stay than epidural.

### Dose

The side effects are dose-related and the analgesia has a ceiling, so the dose question is how little will do.

| Setting | Dose logic |
| --- | --- |
| Caesarean section | Low dose. In a meta-analysis, higher doses bought about 4.5 h more analgesia than lower doses with no difference in pain scores or 24 h opioid use, and more nausea, vomiting and pruritus. Around 100 micrograms is the common choice. |
| Laparoscopic abdominal and pelvic surgery, hysterectomy, prostatectomy | 100-200 micrograms is the usual range. |
| Open abdominal surgery in a robust adult | Up to 300 micrograms, accepting more pruritus and nausea. |
| Elderly, frail, obstructive sleep apnoea, opioid-naive with renal impairment | Lower end of the range or an alternative technique. |

The ceiling of 300 micrograms has a basis: in a meta-analysis of spinal morphine added to spinal anaesthesia, doses below 0.3 mg produced no more respiratory depression than placebo with systemic opioids, whereas respiratory depression was more frequent at higher doses (7 of 80 patients against 2 of 247). The ranges in the table are consensus practice and the author's judgement within that ceiling, not trial-derived optima.

### Monitoring and the ward

Respiratory depression after intrathecal morphine is late, because the drug takes hours to reach the brainstem; the risk window runs to about 24 h, with the peak in the first 12 h. Sedation precedes a falling respiratory rate, so a sedation score is the monitor that matters, with respiratory rate and oximetry. The obstetric consensus statement makes the modern argument: use a low dose, and match the intensity, frequency and duration of monitoring to the dose and to the patient's risk (obesity, sleep apnoea, concurrent sedatives, magnesium), instead of applying one schedule to everyone. The rules that prevent most events are organisational: no long-acting or background systemic opioid in the first 24 h unless prescribed by the anaesthetic or pain team, no sedatives, immediate-release oral opioid only for breakthrough, and a ward that knows the patient has had spinal morphine.

Pruritus is common and dose-related; nausea is frequent enough to justify two-agent prophylaxis; urinary retention is the reason most patients keep a catheter overnight. In the anticoagulated patient the spinal has an advantage over an epidural: one fine-needle pass and no catheter to remove later.

## Paravertebral block

Local anaesthetic in the thoracic paravertebral space bathes the spinal nerve as it leaves the foramen, with its dorsal ramus and the sympathetic chain. The block is unilateral, dense, covers several segments from one or two injections or a catheter, and takes in the lateral cutaneous branches that anterior wall blocks miss. For thoracotomy the Cochrane review found it as effective as thoracic epidural for acute pain with fewer minor complications, hypotension in particular (relative risk 0.16), and no difference in mortality, major complications or length of stay. PROSPECT now ranks thoracic epidural and paravertebral block as equal first-line options for thoracotomy, and for video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery recommends paravertebral or erector spinae plane block and advises against an epidural. The surgeon can place a paravertebral catheter under direct vision before closing the chest, which removes the pleural puncture risk. The lung operations are covered in the [one-lung ventilation chapter](a03-one-lung-ventilation.html).

For major breast surgery ([mastectomy and axillary clearance](../c/mastectomy-axillary-clearance-and-implant-reconstruction.html)) PROSPECT recommends a paravertebral block or a pectoral nerves block, with or without wound infiltration. Bilateral paravertebral blocks for abdominal surgery are possible but double the dose and the risk, and are rarely the best answer. The space is deep and non-compressible, so the anticoagulation rules for neuraxial block apply.

## The fascial plane blocks

All of these work by placing a large volume of dilute local anaesthetic in a plane that nerves run through or pierce. They share four properties: they depend on spread, so the extent is variable; they are wall-only (claims of visceral cover for the posterior approaches rest on spread towards the paravertebral space that is inconsistent); they last 8-16 h as single injections; and a substantial part of their effect may come from systemic absorption of a large local anaesthetic dose.

| Block | Where the injectate sits | What it reaches | Best fit |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Erector spinae plane | Deep to erector spinae on the transverse process | Dorsal rami reliably; ventral rami and lateral branches variably | Thoracoscopic surgery, rib fractures, a catheter alternative when paravertebral or epidural is unavailable |
| Serratus anterior plane | Superficial or deep to serratus at the mid-axillary line | Lateral cutaneous branches T2-T9, long thoracic, thoracodorsal | Lateral chest wall, axilla, thoracoscopic ports and drain sites |
| Pecs I and II (interpectoral and pectoserratus) | Between the pectoral muscles; between pectoralis minor and serratus | Pectoral nerves; intercostobrachial, lateral branches T2-T6 | Breast surgery, axillary surgery, subpectoral implants and expanders |
| Lateral TAP | Between internal oblique and transversus at the mid-axillary line | T10-L1 anterior wall | Lower abdominal incisions, hernia repair, laparoscopic ports below the umbilicus |
| Subcostal (oblique) TAP | Same plane along the costal margin | T6-T9 anterior wall | Upper abdominal incisions and ports |
| Rectus sheath | Between rectus muscle and posterior sheath | Anterior cutaneous branches near the midline | Midline incisions, umbilical ports; catheters for laparotomy |
| Quadratus lumborum | Lateral, posterior or anterior to quadratus lumborum | T7-L1 with lateral cutaneous branches; wider than TAP | Caesarean without spinal opioid, nephrectomy, hip and iliac crest |
| Parasternal (superficial or deep) | Beside the sternum, superficial or deep to the internal intercostals | Anterior cutaneous branches T2-T6 | [Sternotomy](../c/coronary-artery-bypass-grafting-on-and.html), medial breast |

How much they deliver is the honest question. For the best-studied, the TAP block, a meta-analysis found a reduction in IV morphine of about 6 mg at 6 h, described by its authors as marginal, across laparotomy, laparoscopy and caesarean delivery, and no additional effect in patients who had received a long-acting spinal opioid. For the pectoral blocks the evidence is stronger: Pecs II reduced pain and opioid use over 24 h compared with systemic analgesia alone and was non-inferior to paravertebral block for breast cancer surgery. PROSPECT recommends TAP or quadratus lumborum block after caesarean section only when intrathecal morphine has not been given, bilateral TAP for open colorectal surgery only when an epidural is not feasible, and erector spinae block as a first-choice option for thoracoscopic surgery.

The practical reading: a fascial plane block is a good wall analgesic for an operation whose pain is mostly wall and lasts under a day (breast surgery, [open inguinal hernia](../c/inguinal-and-femoral-hernia-repair-open.html), [umbilical and incisional hernia](../c/umbilical-epigastric-and-incisional-hernia-repair.html), laparoscopic ports), a useful opioid-sparing addition when the better technique is contraindicated, and redundant on top of a working spinal opioid or epidural. It is not a substitute for covering the viscera after a bowel resection.

## Wound catheters and surgical infiltration

The surgeon can put local anaesthetic exactly where the injury is. Port-site and wound infiltration is recommended in almost every procedure-specific guideline and costs nothing. Continuous wound infusion through multi-holed catheters laid in the preperitoneal plane or the rectus sheath at closure extends that for two to three days: in the POP-UP trial, continuous wound infiltration was non-inferior to epidural analgesia after hepato-pancreato-biliary surgery within an enhanced recovery pathway, and PROSPECT lists continuous preperitoneal infusion as an alternative to epidural for open colorectal surgery. Rectus sheath catheters (placed under ultrasound or by the surgeon from inside the abdomen) do the same job for a midline laparotomy and are the standard fallback for the [emergency laparotomy](../c/emergency-laparotomy.html) patient who is septic, coagulopathic or too unstable for an epidural. They are wall-only; pair them with a systemic strategy for the visceral component, or with intrathecal morphine given before induction for the first day.

## Local anaesthetic dose ceilings

Truncal blocks are volume blocks, often bilateral, in vascular planes, frequently combined with surgical infiltration, and sometimes followed by infusions. That is the setting for systemic toxicity. After bilateral TAP blocks with ropivacaine 3 mg/kg, the mean peak total plasma concentration was 2.54 micrograms/mL at 30 min and the highest was 4.00 micrograms/mL, in the range associated with neurological symptoms. The peak is at 30 min, not at injection, and the anaesthetised patient shows nothing until the arrhythmia.

Working ceilings for a single dose, as conventionally quoted:

| Drug | Conventional maximum single dose |
| --- | --- |
| Ropivacaine | 3 mg/kg |
| Levobupivacaine, bupivacaine | 2 mg/kg |
| Lidocaine | 3 mg/kg plain, 7 mg/kg with adrenaline |

These numbers are conventions, not thresholds, and the maximum dose is properly a multifactorial judgement: lower in the elderly, the frail, pregnancy, low body weight, and renal, hepatic or cardiac disease, and lower again for repeated or continuous dosing because clearance is what fails in those patients. The rules that follow:

1. Calculate on lean or ideal body weight, in milligrams, before drawing up. For bilateral blocks, dilute to get volume; do not add dose.
2. One budget for all routes. Agree with the surgeon who is using the local anaesthetic: a full-dose block followed by generous wound infiltration is the usual way the limit is exceeded.
3. IV lidocaine and a local anaesthetic block or infusion do not run together. PROSPECT states the principle directly: local anaesthetic should not be given by two routes at the same time.
4. Catheter infusions and programmed boluses count per hour and per day, and bilateral catheters count twice.
5. Know where the lipid emulsion is and follow the current checklist when toxicity is suspected.

## Timing around anticoagulants

The catastrophic complication of neuraxial and deep truncal techniques is bleeding into a space that cannot be compressed. The European guideline sets minimum intervals between the last dose and a neuraxial or deep block (paravertebral, and by most readings the deeper quadratus lumborum approaches), and applies the same intervals to catheter removal:

| Drug | Minimum interval from last dose to needle or catheter removal |
| --- | --- |
| LMWH, low (prophylactic) dose | 12 h (24 h if creatinine clearance below 30 mL/min) |
| LMWH, high (treatment) dose | 24 h |
| Unfractionated heparin, low dose SC | 4 h |
| Rivaroxaban, edoxaban, low dose | 24 h (30 h if creatinine clearance below 30 mL/min) |
| Apixaban, low dose | 36 h |
| Dabigatran, low dose | 48 h |
| Any direct oral anticoagulant, high dose | 72 h, or a normal specific assay |
| Warfarin | INR back in the normal range |
| Aspirin, low dose | No interval required |
| Clopidogrel | 5-7 days |
| Ticagrelor | 5 days |
| Prasugrel | 7 days |

After the procedure, prophylactic doses resume on the usual postoperative schedule (about 6 h), and high-dose anticoagulation is delayed for 48-72 h after a procedure with high bleeding risk. Superficial, compressible blocks (TAP, rectus sheath, Pecs, serratus, erector spinae by most readings) may be performed in anticoagulated patients; that is one of their real advantages. The North American guideline, now in its fifth edition, differs in some intervals, and the local hospital policy decides which is followed.

Three practical consequences. The epidural commits you to a catheter removal that has to be timed around every subsequent dose of prophylaxis, and the patient who will need treatment-dose anticoagulation or a P2Y12 inhibitor postoperatively (see the [vascular patient](a10-vascular-patient.html)) is a poor candidate. Liver resection produces a coagulopathy on days 1-3 that can strand a catheter. And the single-shot spinal with morphine, done before the first dose of anything, sidesteps the removal problem.

## The rule set

1. Ask first whether the pain is wall, viscera or both, and for how long. That decides between a wall block, a spinal opioid and an epidural before any other consideration.
2. Thoracic epidural for thoracotomy and open upper abdominal surgery in patients who will benefit; not for laparoscopic surgery.
3. Site the epidural at the middle of the incision, treat its hypotension with a vasopressor, test it properly before theatre ends, and give a failing one a single bolus before moving to the fallback.
4. New motor block on a thoracic epidural is a haematoma until imaged.
5. Intrathecal morphine at the lowest dose that will do, never above 300 micrograms, with no other long-acting opioid and a ward that knows.
6. Paravertebral block for unilateral chest and breast surgery when a dense block is wanted without the bilateral sympathectomy.
7. Fascial plane blocks for wall pain of under a day, or as the fallback; nothing added by stacking them on a spinal opioid.
8. One local anaesthetic budget in milligrams per kilogram of lean weight across the anaesthetist, the surgeon and any infusion; no IV lidocaine alongside.
9. Time the needle and the catheter removal around the anticoagulant, and choose the technique with the postoperative anticoagulation plan already known.
