title: "Nausea, vomiting and day-case anaesthesia"
summary: >
  Postoperative nausea and vomiting is predictable from four patient factors, and each antiemetic class or
  anaesthetic change removes about a quarter of whatever risk remains, independently of the others. The
  plan is therefore arithmetic: count the Apfel factors, lower the baseline (propofol maintenance, less
  opioid, fluid), add one drug class per level of risk, and rescue with a class not yet used. The same
  logic runs day surgery. Patients are selected on physiology and home circumstances, not on age or body
  mass index alone; the anaesthetic is built to leave no residue (short-acting drugs, local anaesthetic,
  paracetamol and an NSAID); and discharge is by scored criteria, not by the clock. Obstructive sleep
  apnoea is a day-case problem only when the operation needs opioids afterwards, and a short-acting spinal
  is a day-case anaesthetic when the agent is chosen for the length of the operation.

## Why it matters more than it seems

Patients rank vomiting as worse than pain. For the surgeon it is a mechanical event: retching raises venous and intra-abdominal pressure, which is what opens a [thyroid](../c/thyroidectomy-and-parathyroidectomy.html) bed, a middle ear graft, a fundoplication wrap or a hernia repair. For the day unit it is the commonest anaesthetic reason, with pain, for a patient who should have gone home staying the night. And it is one of the few postoperative complications that can be predicted from the pre-anaesthetic history and prevented by arithmetic.

## Mechanism and receptors

Vomiting is a brainstem reflex coordinated in the nucleus tractus solitarius and adjacent medulla, and it has four inputs. Each input uses different transmitters, which is why drugs of different classes add to one another and two drugs of the same class do not.

```
   BLOOD and CSF                     GUT                       INNER EAR
   opioids, volatile agents,         distension, blood,        movement, trolley,
   nitrous oxide, toxins             bowel handling,           middle ear surgery
        |                            serotonin release              |
        v                                 |                         v
   area postrema                     vagal afferents           vestibular nuclei
   (outside the blood-brain          5-HT3                     H1, muscarinic
   barrier)  D2, 5-HT3, NK1, mu           |                         |
        |                                 v                         |
        +--------------->  nucleus tractus solitarius  <------------+
                           and pattern generator (NK1)
                                     ^
                    cortex: anxiety, anticipation, pain, smell
                                     |
                                  VOMITING
```

| Class | Receptor and site | Drug, adult dose and timing | Main cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5-HT3 antagonist | Vagal afferents and area postrema | Ondansetron 4 mg IV at the end; granisetron 1 mg IV; palonosetron 0.075 mg IV | Headache, constipation, QT prolongation |
| Corticosteroid | Not known; anti-inflammatory and central | Dexamethasone 4-8 mg IV after induction | Glucose rise for 24 h; perineal burning if given awake |
| D2 antagonist | Area postrema | Droperidol 0.625-1.25 mg IV at the end; amisulpride 5 mg IV | Sedation, restlessness, QT prolongation; avoid in Parkinson disease |
| NK1 antagonist | Pattern generator | Aprepitant 40 mg orally before induction | Cost; interaction with hormonal contraception |
| Antihistamine | Vestibular nuclei | Cyclizine 50 mg IV slowly | Sedation, tachycardia, pain on injection |
| Anticholinergic | Vestibular and brainstem | Hyoscine patch the evening before or two hours before | Dry mouth, blurred vision, confusion in the old |
| Propofol | Not known | Maintenance by infusion; 20 mg IV as rescue in recovery | Short-lived as rescue |

Metoclopramide 10 mg is no better than placebo for prophylaxis at that dose and is not on the list. The timing column follows the kinetics: slow-onset, long-acting drugs (dexamethasone, aprepitant, hyoscine) are given early; short-acting receptor antagonists are given at the end so that they cover recovery and the trip home.

## Risk: the Apfel score

The simplified score counts four things: female sex, a history of postoperative nausea and vomiting or motion sickness, non-smoking, and the expected use of postoperative opioids. With none, one, two, three or four present the incidence in the original cohorts was 10%, 21%, 39%, 61% and 79%. A later analysis of 95,154 patients confirmed the ranking (female sex was the strongest patient factor, with an odds ratio of 2.57) and added the anaesthetic contributors: volatile agents (odds ratio 1.82), duration (1.46 per hour), postoperative opioids and nitrous oxide. Younger age raises risk. The evidence that the type of surgery matters independently is conflicting; cholecystectomy, laparoscopy and gynaecological surgery carry more nausea largely because of who has them and for how long.

The score discriminates only moderately, which is one reason the fourth consensus guideline moved away from "treat only the high-risk" towards giving every adult with any risk factor at least two interventions.

## Prophylaxis: the arithmetic

The IMPACT factorial trial is the foundation. Ondansetron, dexamethasone and droperidol each reduced nausea and vomiting by about 26%; propofol in place of a volatile agent reduced it by 19% and air in place of nitrous oxide by 12%, so total intravenous anaesthesia was worth about one antiemetic. Every intervention worked independently of the others and of the baseline risk. Relative reductions therefore multiply, and absolute benefit depends on where the patient starts.

```
  baseline risk    one drug     two        three
  (Apfel)          (x 0.74)     (x 0.74)   (x 0.74)
  -----------      --------     -------    -------
   1 factor  21%     16%          11%         9%
   2 factors 39%     29%          21%        16%
   3 factors 61%     45%          33%        25%
   4 factors 79%     58%          43%        32%
```

The table makes two points. A patient with four factors is still at one in three after three drugs, so the baseline must be lowered as well. And the third drug in a low-risk patient buys two percentage points at the price of its side effects.

The guideline's approach, in the order it is applied:

1. Lower the baseline for everyone at risk: regional anaesthesia where it suits, propofol maintenance, no nitrous oxide in long cases, the least opioid that controls pain, sugammadex in place of neostigmine where available, and adequate intravenous fluid.
2. One or two risk factors: two agents, in practice dexamethasone at induction and ondansetron at the end.
3. Three or four risk factors: three or four interventions. Add a dopamine antagonist or aprepitant and use propofol maintenance.
4. Previous severe vomiting despite prophylaxis, or surgery where retching is dangerous: everything, including aprepitant before induction and a total intravenous technique with no long-acting opioid.

Two large trials have removed the usual objections to dexamethasone. In PADDI (8,725 patients, 13% with diabetes) a single 8 mg dose did not increase surgical-site infection (8.1% against 9.1% with placebo). In DREAMS (1,350 patients having bowel surgery) adding 8 mg at induction to standard care cut vomiting within 24 hours from 33.0% to 25.5% without more complications. Glucose rises for a day in diabetic patients and is measured; that is a reason to monitor, not to withhold. The Cochrane network meta-analysis of 585 trials found that the recommended and higher doses of dexamethasone, ondansetron, granisetron and droperidol outperform low doses, which supports 8 mg when dexamethasone is also doing analgesic work.

## Total intravenous anaesthesia and opioid sparing

Propofol maintenance reduces nausea and vomiting by about 40% against inhalational maintenance in pooled trials (relative risk 0.61), with the effect concentrated in the first six hours, when volatile agent is still being exhaled. It does nothing for the opioid given in recovery or the tablets taken at home.

That later component belongs to the opioid. A meta-analysis of 23 trials found that leaving opioid out of the anaesthetic altogether gave the same pain scores at two hours and less nausea and vomiting (relative risk 0.77). The practical reading is not that every anaesthetic should be opioid-free but that intra-operative opioid given by habit, in a case where local anaesthetic and simple analgesics cover the pain, is paid for in recovery. Remifentanil is the exception that needs a plan: it leaves no nausea and no analgesia.

## Rescue

A patient who vomits in recovery after prophylaxis has shown that the classes already given were not enough. The rule is a drug from a class not yet used, and no repeat of the same class within six hours, because a second dose of ondansetron soon after the first performs no better than placebo.

- Before the drug, look for a cause: hypotension (the commonest after a spinal), hypovolaemia, pain, swallowed blood after nasal or tonsil surgery, a distended stomach, or opioid that can be reduced.
- If dexamethasone and ondansetron were given: droperidol 0.625 mg IV, or cyclizine 50 mg IV slowly, or amisulpride 10 mg IV, which gave a complete response in 41.7% against 28.5% with placebo in patients who had failed prophylaxis.
- Propofol 20 mg IV works for a few minutes and is useful while the next drug acts.
- If nothing was given, ondansetron 4 mg IV is the first rescue.

Post-discharge nausea is the larger and less visible problem: it occurred in 37% of ambulatory patients in a multicentre cohort, most of whom had not been sick in hospital. The predictors were female sex, age under 50, a history, opioid in recovery and nausea in recovery, with an incidence of 7% with none and 89% with all five. For the patient with three or more, a long-acting agent (dexamethasone, palonosetron, aprepitant or a hyoscine patch) and a non-opioid discharge regimen do more than a second dose of ondansetron at the door.

## Three settings where the cause is not the anaesthetic

Antiemetics treat the reflex. In three common settings the stimulus is something else and the drug is the second step.

Under a spinal for [caesarean section](../c/elective-caesarean-section.html), nausea in the first ten minutes is hypotension until proved otherwise: the area postrema is underperfused, and the treatment is the vasopressor, not ondansetron. Nausea later in the same operation is visceral traction (exteriorising the uterus, swabbing the paracolic gutters) or the uterotonic, and nausea on the ward that evening is the intrathecal morphine. Each has a different answer, which is why the prophylaxis there is a phenylephrine infusion, a slow oxytocin bolus, and dexamethasone with ondansetron after delivery.

After [laparoscopy](../c/laparoscopic-gynaecology.html) the patient is usually a young, non-smoking woman who will be offered opioid, which is three Apfel factors before the operation is considered. Residual gas and peritoneal stretch add vagal input. This is the population in which the guideline's multimodal prophylaxis for everyone makes most sense.

After nasal, dental and [tonsil](../c/bleeding-tonsil.html) surgery, swallowed blood is a potent emetic that no receptor antagonist blocks. A throat pack, suction of the stomach before extubation where blood has been swallowed, and an awake extubation on the side do more than a third drug, and repeated vomiting of fresh blood is bleeding, not nausea.

## Children

Vomiting is uncommon under three years and peaks in the school-age child. The paediatric score counts surgery of 30 minutes or longer, age three years or over, strabismus surgery, and a history of postoperative vomiting in the child or of nausea and vomiting in a parent or sibling: the incidence was 9%, 10%, 30%, 55% and 70% with none to four. [Strabismus surgery](../c/strabismus-surgery.html), [tonsillectomy](../c/adult-and-paediatric-tonsillectomy-and-adenoidectomy.html) and [middle ear surgery](../c/middle-ear-surgery-tympanoplasty-mastoidectomy-cochlear.html) are the operations that reach two factors on their own.

The guideline recommends ondansetron 0.1 mg/kg IV (to 4 mg) with dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV for the child at moderate or high risk, propofol maintenance for the highest risk, and droperidol only as rescue. Doses and the rest of the paediatric plan are in [the child](a13-the-child.html).

## Day surgery: who is suitable

Day surgery is the default for any operation after which the patient can drink, walk and control pain with tablets. Selection has three parts, and the British guideline is explicit that fitness is judged on function and on the stability of chronic disease, not on arbitrary limits of age, body mass index or ASA class.

| Part | Suitable | Not suitable as a day case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Operation | Blood loss small; pain manageable with oral analgesia and local anaesthetic; no need for drains, parenteral drugs or overnight observation | Expected need for intravenous opioid, prolonged immobility, a real risk of airway swelling or bleeding into a closed space overnight |
| Patient | Chronic disease stable and optimised; able to follow instructions | Unstable angina, decompensated heart failure, poorly controlled diabetes or lung disease until treated |
| Home | Responsible adult to take the patient home and stay overnight; telephone; reasonable travel time to help | Lives alone with no carer after a general anaesthetic or a procedure with risk of bleeding |

Age alone is not a limit; the older patient has less delirium at home than in hospital. The ex-premature infant under about 60 weeks post-conceptional age is the exception and stays for apnoea monitoring ([the child](a13-the-child.html)). The decision is made at pre-assessment, not on the morning, so that an optimisable problem is treated and the list runs.

The anaesthetic follows from the aim of leaving no residue: short-acting agents, a supraglottic airway where appropriate, local anaesthetic by the surgeon or a block for every wound, paracetamol and an NSAID given before the end, dexamethasone, and opioid only as needed. Long-acting sedative premedication, gabapentinoids and intrathecal morphine each trade a small analgesic gain for a patient who cannot go home.

## Discharge criteria

Recovery runs in two scored stages, and a score replaces a fixed time.

| Stage | Tool | Items | Threshold |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Recovery room to day ward | Modified Aldrete score | Activity, breathing, circulation, consciousness, oxygen saturation; each 0-2 | 9 or more |
| Day ward to home | Post-Anesthetic Discharge Scoring System | Vital signs, ambulation, nausea and vomiting, pain, surgical bleeding; each 0-2 | 9 or more, on two readings |

In the validation study the discharge score, reassessed every 30 minutes, agreed closely with clinical judgement. Two traditional requirements have been dropped for low-risk patients: drinking before discharge, which provokes vomiting, and passing urine. Voiding is required only when retention is likely: after spinal anaesthesia, after [anorectal](../c/haemorrhoidectomy-fissure-fistula-and-pilonidal-surgery.html), [hernia](../c/inguinal-and-femoral-hernia-repair-open.html) and urological surgery, and in men with prostatic symptoms. Reported retention rates run from 5% to 70% depending on the operation and the anaesthetic, and a bladder scan settles the question in the patient who has not voided: a large volume is drained once and the patient still goes home.

Every patient leaves with written instructions, analgesia already dispensed, a number to ring, an escort, and the advice not to drive, sign or supervise for 24 hours. A limb with a working block goes home in a sling or on crutches with a warning about the hour the block will wear off and what to take before it does; the reasoning is in [why operations hurt](../b/b01-why-operations-hurt.html).

## Obstructive sleep apnoea and obesity

Sleep apnoea is common, mostly undiagnosed, and dangerous in one specific circumstance: an opioid given to a sleeping patient with nobody watching. The STOP-Bang questionnaire is the screen. In 746 surgical patients the probability of moderate or severe disease rose from 0.36 at a score of 3 to 0.60 at 7 or 8, and a score of 5 or more is where the odds of severe disease rise steeply.

The ambulatory anaesthesia society's consensus statement turns that into three rules:

- Diagnosed sleep apnoea, comorbidities optimised, and able to use positive airway pressure after discharge: suitable for day surgery. The machine comes with the patient.
- Presumed sleep apnoea on screening, comorbidities optimised: suitable if postoperative pain can be managed predominantly without opioids.
- Either group with poorly controlled comorbidity (hypertension, heart failure, arrhythmia, obesity hypoventilation): not suitable until treated.

The operation therefore decides. A knee arthroscopy, a cataract or a [colonoscopy](../c/gastroscopy-and-colonoscopy.html) in a patient with a STOP-Bang of 6 is a day case under local, regional or light propofol. An airway operation, or one that will need strong opioid for two nights, is not. The technique is the day-case technique carried further: regional or local where possible, short-acting drugs, full reversal confirmed by monitoring, extubation awake and sitting up, no sedating adjuncts, and a non-opioid discharge regimen.

Obesity alone is not an exclusion, and guidelines no longer set a ceiling on body mass index. What matters is the company it keeps (sleep apnoea, hypoventilation, diabetes, reflux), the mechanics (positioning, airway, access) and the staff's familiarity. The obese day-case patient is positioned ramped, preoxygenated head-up, dosed on lean weight for induction agents, and mobilised early.

## Spinal anaesthesia for day cases

A spinal avoids the airway, the volatile agent and most of the nausea, and leaves a patient who can eat in recovery. It fails as a day-case anaesthetic for one reason: a block that lasts three hours longer than the operation. The solution is to match the agent to the surgical time.

| Agent | Typical dose | Surgical block | Comment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2-Chloroprocaine 1% | 40-50 mg | about 40 min | Fastest recovery; for short perineal, urological and lower limb procedures |
| Hyperbaric prilocaine 2% | 40-60 mg | 60-90 min | The general-purpose day-case spinal; lower doses for saddle block |
| Hyperbaric bupivacaine 0.5% | 5-7.5 mg with fentanyl 10-25 microg, patient lateral for a unilateral block | 60-90 min, variable | Recovery slower and less predictable; fentanyl adds itch |
| Lidocaine | not used | | Transient neurological symptoms |

The Cochrane network meta-analysis explains the last row and qualifies the first: transient neurological symptoms (buttock and leg pain for a few days) are less likely with bupivacaine, levobupivacaine, prilocaine and ropivacaine than with lidocaine, while 2-chloroprocaine and mepivacaine could not be shown to differ from lidocaine on low-quality evidence. Patients having a chloroprocaine or prilocaine spinal are told that a few days of aching buttocks is possible and harmless.

Three practical rules complete the technique. Use a 25 or 27 gauge pencil-point needle, since the day-case population is young and a headache at home is a readmission. Add no intrathecal morphine. And manage the bladder deliberately: restrict intra-operative fluid to what is needed, use the short-acting agents (retention tracks block duration), and scan before discharge in the higher-risk groups listed above. The low-risk patient who has not voided goes home with instructions to return if unable to pass urine by a stated time.

The case for a day-case spinal is strongest in the patient the general anaesthetic suits least: the obese patient with sleep apnoea, the patient with previous severe vomiting, and the patient with reactive airways having a 30-minute operation below the umbilicus.
