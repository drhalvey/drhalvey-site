summary: >
  Anaesthesia away from the theatre suite injures patients in one dominant way: sedation deepens, breathing
  fails, and nobody notices in time. The claims data show it, and the answer is to carry the theatre
  standard to the endoscopy room, the scanner and the angiography suite: a trained assistant, capnography,
  a second oxygen source and a plan for the airway made before the first dose. Sedation is a continuum and
  propofol moves along it within seconds, so what an anaesthetist gives for endoscopy is usually general
  anaesthesia without an airway device and is monitored as such. Target-controlled infusion makes the dose
  smoother, high-flow nasal oxygen buys time, and neither replaces watching ventilation. Fasting needs a
  second look in patients on GLP-1 receptor agonists. MRI, radiation and contrast each add a short list of
  site-specific rules.

## Why remote is riskier

The same anaesthetist giving the same drugs is less safe in the endoscopy room than in theatre, and the closed claims analyses show where the harm comes from. Claims arising outside the operating room involved older and sicker patients and a far higher proportion of sedation cases (58% against 6% in theatre). The damaging event was inadequate oxygenation or ventilation in a third of them, against 2% in theatre, and death was the outcome in 54% of remote claims against 29%. The gastrointestinal suite was the commonest location. The reviewers judged most of these events preventable by better monitoring.

The drugs are not the cause. The environment removes the layers that catch a problem early:

| What is different | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Room built for a machine, not for anaesthesia | Anaesthetist at the feet or behind a screen; airway out of reach under a C-arm, in a bore, or with the patient prone |
| Dim lighting, noise | Cyanosis and chest movement not seen; alarms not heard |
| Staff trained for the procedure | No one who can pass a bougie, find the suxamethonium or apply cricoid pressure |
| Distance | Help and the difficult airway trolley are minutes away |
| Equipment | Older machine or none, cylinder oxygen, no scavenging, unfamiliar monitor |
| Case selection | "Too sick for theatre" patients sent for the "small procedure" |
| List pressure | Twelve colonoscopies before lunch; no time for the pre-assessment |

The answer is a fixed set-up check done before the first patient, wherever the room is.

```
  S  suction        working, on, reaches the head
  O  oxygen         wall supply AND a full cylinder; self-inflating bag
  A  airway         masks, oral and nasal airways, supraglottic, laryngoscope,
                    tube, bougie; where the difficult airway trolley is
  P  pharmacy       induction agent, suxamethonium, atropine, vasopressor,
                    adrenaline; reversal agents if benzodiazepine or opioid
  M  monitors       oximetry, capnography, ECG, blood pressure; alarms audible
  E  escape         tilting trolley, defibrillator location, who to call and
                    how, the route to theatre or intensive care
```

## The sedation continuum

Sedation is not a set of boxes. It is a slope, and a patient moves down it with the next dose, the end of a painful stimulus, or the passage of two minutes.

| Level | Response | Airway | Breathing | Circulation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Minimal (anxiolysis) | Normal to voice | Unaffected | Unaffected | Unaffected |
| Moderate ("conscious") | Purposeful to voice or light touch | No intervention needed | Adequate | Usually maintained |
| Deep | Purposeful only to repeated or painful stimulus | May need support | May be inadequate | Usually maintained |
| General anaesthesia | None, even to pain | Often needs support | Often inadequate | May be impaired |

Two rules follow. Whoever gives sedation must be able to rescue the patient from one level deeper than intended. And the label on the booking form does not describe what is given: in a survey of Australian anaesthetists propofol was used in 99-100% of endoscopy cases and the majority aimed for a patient unresponsive to painful stimulation for everything except elective gastroscopy. That is general anaesthesia with a natural airway, and it needs the monitoring, the assistant and the recovery of a general anaesthetic.

The Australian and New Zealand college guideline on procedural sedation draws the same line. It confines "procedural sedation" to minimal and moderate levels, which any registered practitioner with the listed competencies may give, and it treats anything deeper as anaesthesia, to be given by an anaesthetist or another suitably credentialled specialist. Its other requirements that can be stated with confidence:

- Waveform capnography is strongly recommended for moderate sedation in adults and children, and for minimal intravenous sedation unless verbal contact can be kept.
- Risk rises with ASA class and becomes clinically significant from class 3. High-risk patients, those with severely limiting comorbidity or who need intubation to protect against aspiration, need an anaesthetist (or equivalent) and an assistant who is exclusively available to them.
- If the proceduralist is also the sedationist and propofol is used, two additional staff are essential, one of them an assisting practitioner with airway and resuscitation skills.
- Fasting is matched to the aspiration risk of the procedure and the depth intended; it may not be needed for minimal sedation.

For the anaesthetist the practical minimum in any remote site is the theatre standard: pulse oximetry, capnography, electrocardiogram and non-invasive pressure at intervals of five minutes or less, oxygen from the start, intravenous access, a trained assistant present for the whole case, and a staffed recovery area.

## Propofol and target-controlled infusion

Propofol suits procedural work because of how it leaves, not how it arrives. Redistribution and a high clearance give a context-sensitive half-time that stays short after hours of infusion, so the patient wakes clear-headed and without nausea. The cost is a steep dose-response curve for airway tone and breathing, strong synergy with opioids and benzodiazepines, and no antagonist.

The delay between plasma and brain is the source of most overshoot. After a bolus the plasma concentration peaks at once; the effect peaks about 90 seconds to two minutes later. An anaesthetist who gives a second bolus at 45 seconds because the patient is still moving has stacked two doses.

```
  conc.
    |  P
    | P P            P = plasma: peaks immediately, falls fast
    |P   P  e e e    e = effect site: lags, peaks later and lower
    |   e P e    e e
    | e     P  P       e e
    |e          P   P      e  e
    +-----------------------------> time
      ^ bolus   ^ peak effect: judge the dose HERE, not before
```

A target-controlled infusion pump solves the arithmetic. It holds a three-compartment model of the patient and delivers the bolus and decreasing infusion needed to reach and hold a chosen concentration. These systems are approved in at least 96 countries. The model matters:

| Model | Built from | Inputs | Behaviour and traps |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Marsh | Adult data, scaled by weight | Weight | Large central compartment; in plasma-targeting mode gives a generous initial bolus; ignores age, so overdoses the old |
| Schnider | 24 adult volunteers | Age, weight, height, sex (lean body mass) | Small fixed central compartment; designed for effect-site targeting; smaller boluses in the elderly; the lean mass equation fails in severe obesity |
| Eleveld | 1,033 individuals, premature neonates to 88 years, 0.68-160 kg | Age, weight, height, sex, concurrent opioid | One model for children, adults, the old and the obese; newer pumps only |

The number on the screen is a prediction with a wide error in any one patient, so the target is titrated to the patient and not the reverse. As working figures for an effect-site target with a small dose of opioid: 0.5-1.5 microg/mL gives conscious sedation, and 2-3.5 microg/mL covers endoscope insertion in a fit adult. The frail patient of 85 needs about half. Raising the target in small steps of 0.5 and waiting for equilibration is the technique; chasing movement with boluses defeats the purpose of the pump.

Without a pump the equivalent is a bolus of 0.5-1 mg/kg IV given slowly, then 10-20 mg increments no closer than a minute apart.

Adjuncts change the shape. Fentanyl 25-50 microg or alfentanil 250-500 microg IV before a stimulating step (scope through the pharynx, sphincterotomy, biopsy needle) cuts the propofol dose and the coughing, and adds apnoea. Midazolam 1-2 mg IV adds amnesia and slows recovery. Ketamine 10-20 mg IV supports breathing and pressure in the frail. Dexmedetomidine preserves ventilation and is slow to start and slow to wear off, which suits [MRI](../c/anaesthesia-for-mri-and-ct.html) and awake procedures and does not suit a fast endoscopy list.

## Airway and oxygenation

The sequence that kills is obstruction or hypoventilation, then hypoxia, then bradycardia and arrest. Every technique below either delays the second step or reveals the first.

Position comes first. The left lateral position used for endoscopy holds the tongue forward and lets secretions drain; the supine patient with a bite block obstructs. A jaw thrust and a nasopharyngeal airway solve most obstruction.

Capnography detects the first step. Oxygen saturation in a pre-oxygenated patient on supplemental oxygen stays normal for minutes after breathing has stopped, so the oximeter reports the problem late. In the ColoCap trial of 760 patients having colonoscopy under propofol, adding capnography cut desaturation from 53.2% to 38.9% and saturation below 90% from 19.8% to 12.5%, because apnoea was acted on before the saturation fell. A meta-analysis of thirteen trials found the same (severe desaturation relative risk 0.59). The waveform matters more than the number: a sampled trace during open-mouth breathing or high-flow oxygen is diluted, but a trace that disappears is apnoea or obstruction.

High-flow nasal oxygen delays the second step. Heated, humidified oxygen at 30-70 L/min fills the pharynx, washes out dead space and continues to oxygenate during short apnoeas. In 1,994 fit outpatients having gastroscopy it reduced hypoxia compared with 2 L/min. In the ODEPHI trial of 379 patients at risk of hypoxaemia under deep sedation, saturation fell to 92% or below in 9.4% with high flow against 33.5% with standard oxygen, and airway manoeuvres were needed a third as often. The trap is the one it creates: by preventing desaturation it hides hypoventilation, so carbon dioxide rises unseen in a long case. High flow extends the safe time; it does not ventilate.

The decision to secure the airway is made before the case, not during it. The Australian survey shows where practice has settled: a natural airway for almost all elective endoscopy, and a tube as routine for about half of emergency gastroscopies and two-thirds of emergency [ERCPs](../c/ercp-and-advanced-endoscopy.html). The defensible triggers for intubation are upper gastrointestinal bleeding, gastric outlet or bowel obstruction, achalasia and food bolus, a prone or semi-prone patient who is obese or has a difficult airway, a long therapeutic procedure, and any patient in whom losing the airway mid-procedure could not be retrieved in that position.

## Aspiration and fasting

Aspiration under sedation for elective endoscopy is uncommon but not negligible: in a population analysis of 165,527 colonoscopies it was recorded after 0.14% of those with anaesthesia services and 0.10% of those without, the difference plausibly reflecting deeper sedation. The standard adult fast is six hours for solids and two hours for clear fluids, and bowel preparation taken as a split dose finishing a few hours before the procedure is clear fluid.

GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide) and the dual agonist tirzepatide have changed the assumption that a fasted patient has an empty stomach. They slow gastric emptying as part of how they work. In a gastric ultrasound study of 124 fasted patients, 56% of those taking one had increased residual gastric content against 19% of controls, and how long the drug had been withheld made no difference. Stopping a weekly drug for one dose does not empty the stomach and does disturb glucose control and weight management.

The Australian multi-society recommendation (the anaesthesia college with the diabetes, gastroenterology and obesity societies, updated in April 2025) therefore reads:

1. Continue the drug.
2. Clear-fluid diet for 24 hours before the standard fast, starting on waking the day before.
3. Then the six-hour fast, with water only from six hours and nothing for the final two hours. Unrestricted sipping until called is not recommended in this group.
4. Erythromycin as a prokinetic is an option where the stomach is thought to be full.
5. Patients on insulin are referred for advice, because a day of clear fluids alters their dosing.

The British consensus statement reaches the same position on continuing the drug and adds individual risk assessment and aspiration-mitigating technique. On the day, the anaesthetist asks about nausea, vomiting, bloating and reflux, since symptoms predict a full stomach, and treats a patient who did not follow the diet as unfasted for solids. The options are then a gastric ultrasound where the skill exists, deferral, or a rapid sequence induction and a tube. Gastroscopy has one advantage: the endoscopist sees the stomach first. When a gastroscopy and [colonoscopy](../c/gastroscopy-and-colonoscopy.html) are booked together the gastroscopy goes first, head-up, with light sedation until the stomach is known to be empty.

## MRI

The scanner adds hazards that have nothing to do with pharmacology, and the Association of Anaesthetists guideline exists because for many anaesthetists it is an unfamiliar site visited rarely.

- The magnet is always on. Ferromagnetic objects become projectiles at the bore: cylinders, laryngoscope batteries, scissors, a stethoscope, the trolley. Everything that enters is labelled MR Safe, MR Conditional (safe under stated conditions of field strength and position) or MR Unsafe, and the anaesthetist is screened like the patient.
- Induced currents heat conductors. Cables are run straight, never looped, and kept off skin. Only MR-conditional electrodes and oximeter probes are used. Burns are the commonest injury.
- Implants are checked individually: pacemakers and defibrillators, cochlear implants, aneurysm clips, neurostimulators, programmable shunts.
- Noise exceeds safe limits; the anaesthetised patient gets ear protection.
- The patient is out of reach and out of sight in the bore for 30-60 minutes. The airway is chosen for that: a supraglottic airway or tube for general anaesthesia, and for sedation a technique that is stable without hands. Infusion pumps stay outside the room with long lines, or are MR-conditional and beyond their marked distance.
- Monitoring is degraded: the electrocardiogram is distorted by the field and useless for ST analysis, and the capnograph has a long sampling line and a delay.
- In an emergency the patient is brought out of the scanner room to the resuscitation equipment. The arrest team does not enter. A quench (venting of the helium) is for a person pinned to the magnet, and it can displace oxygen from the room.

Gadolinium contrast carries a specific risk in severe renal failure: nephrogenic systemic fibrosis, described after the older linear (group I) agents in patients on dialysis or with very low filtration. The newer macrocyclic agents carry a far lower risk, and the choice of agent is the radiologist's.

## Radiation and contrast

In the angiography suite and at [ERCP](../c/ercp-and-advanced-endoscopy.html) the source of the anaesthetist's dose is scatter from the patient, and three physical facts govern it. Dose falls with the square of distance, so two steps back from the table quarters it. Scatter is greatest on the tube side of a lateral or oblique beam, so the anaesthetic machine and the anaesthetist belong on the detector side. And dose is proportional to screening time, which the operator controls and the anaesthetist can shorten by keeping the patient still.

Protection is a wrap-around lead apron, a thyroid collar, a mobile screen between the patient and the anaesthetist, a personal dosimeter and, for regular work, lead glasses. The lens of the eye is the tissue at risk: in the neurointerventional suite the dose at the anaesthetist's face averaged 6.5 microSv per case, and the authors concluded that an anaesthetist working there regularly can approach the radiologist's ocular exposure. Long extension lines and a monitor that can be read from behind the screen make distance possible. A pregnant staff member declares it and is monitored at the abdomen under the apron.

Iodinated contrast causes two problems. Hypersensitivity reactions are treated as anaphylaxis, and previous reactions are asked about. Kidney injury from contrast has been overstated; the current consensus limits saline prophylaxis to patients with acute kidney injury or an eGFR below 30 who are not on dialysis. The evidence and the perioperative rules are in [the vascular patient](a10-vascular-patient.html).

## Recovery and discharge

The procedure ends before the risk does. Stimulation stops when the scope comes out and the propofol given for the last painful step is still acting, so the deepest sedation of the case often occurs in the corridor. The patient is therefore recovered where the procedure was done, or moved with oxygen, an oximeter and someone able to manage the airway.

Recovery staff, monitoring and discharge scoring are the same as for day surgery ([nausea, vomiting and day-case anaesthesia](a11-ponv-day-case.html)): an Aldrete-type score to leave first-stage recovery, a home-readiness score to leave the unit, an escort, written instructions, and no driving or signing until the next day. Three procedure-specific warnings are given before discharge. Abdominal pain with shoulder tip pain after colonoscopy is perforation until shown otherwise, although gas pain is far commoner. Pain after ERCP is pancreatitis until the lipase says not. And a sore throat with fever or breathlessness after a difficult gastroscopy or a vomit during the case is aspiration, and the patient is told to return.
