29 Revision total knee arthroplasty

Aseptic revision for loosening, instability, stiffness, wear and malalignment; one-stage and second-stage revision for infection are on the infection card

5Severe and prolonged

Dominant mechanism: Joint re-tensioned. A scarred, stiff joint is re-exposed through a quadriceps snip or a tibial tubercle osteotomy, old components and cement are levered and burred out of bone that has already been cut, metaphyseal cones and stems are impacted into both canals, and a constrained implant re-tensions whatever ligament is left; then the whole construct is loaded by flexion from day 1, often with a healing osteotomy under the patellar tendon

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Block
Spinal (or general anaesthesia for the long case) with an adductor canal catheter, surgeon-delivered periarticular infiltration where the tissue allows it, and IV dexamethasone
Catheter: routine Continuous adductor canal block is the default: the case runs two to four hours, the pain outlasts a single shot by days, movement is re-provoked by day 1 physiotherapy and most patients arrive opioid-exposed; a single shot is reasonable only for the isolated polyethylene exchange
Alternatives: Epidural for the stiff knee that will be manipulated from day 1, accepting the mobilisation cost; IPACK added to the adductor canal catheter for the posterior capsule when infiltration is not possible; General anaesthesia with the catheter and a long-acting spinal is the pragmatic answer when the case will outrun a single spinal dose
Position
Supine, thigh in a holder, with the leg hanging free; the surgeon will want hyperflexion to deliver the old femoral component and the position changes through the case
Tourniquet
Sometimes. Used by many for the exposure and cement removal and then let down, because inflation times run past two hours; some surgeons operate without one and accept the blood loss
Cement
Yes. Hybrid fixation is the norm: cemented joint-surface components and metaphyseal regions, press-fit or cemented diaphyseal stems; cement removal from the old bed is a major part of the case and the new cement load is large
Blood loss
1,000-2,000 mL measured plus hidden loss; transfusion in a quarter to a half of patients without tranexamic acid and a blood management protocol, around 5% with one
Duration
120-240 min skin to skin; the exposure, cement removal and a hinge each add time, and the second stage of a two-stage infection revision is the longest
Length of stay
3-7 nights. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV within 60 min of incision, 3 g over 120 kg, repeated at 3-4 h and after 1.5 L of loss; for a suspected infection the surgeon may ask for the dose to be held until deep tissue samples are taken
Tranexamic acid
Tranexamic acid 1 g IV before incision and a second dose at 3 h or at closure; topical TXA into the joint by some surgeons; the one intervention with a large measured effect on transfusion in this operation
VTE
Chemoprophylaxis is routine; the long case, the stems in both canals and the slow mobilisation put this patient above the primary knee risk
Antiemetic
Dexamethasone 8-10 mg IV at induction and ondansetron 4 mg IV; a long case under spinal with sedation still earns both
Tell the patient
This is a bigger operation than the first replacement and it will hurt more and for longer: a local anaesthetic pump through a thin tube in the thigh runs for two or three days, bending starts straight away but under protection, and the strong painkillers are for the nights of the first week.
The trap
Planning this as a long primary: the exposure needed to get a stiff old knee open (a quadriceps snip or a tubercle osteotomy), the cement removal and the stems in both canals turn a 90-minute spinal into a three-hour case with litres of blood loss, and the single-shot block that works for a primary leaves this patient in trouble on night one

What the surgeon is doing and why

A knee replacement fails in one of a handful of ways, and the reason it has failed is what the surgeon is about to undo. Aseptic loosening is a component that has worked free of its bone or cement bed, usually the tibial tray, with bone lost around it to the osteolysis that polyethylene wear debris provokes. Instability is a joint whose collateral balance was never right or has stretched. Stiffness is a fibrosed joint, or one whose components were oversized or malrotated, that will not bend past 70 or 80 degrees. Malalignment and malrotation produce patellar maltracking and pain. Infection has its own card (periprosthetic joint infection); the second stage of a two-stage infection revision is this operation performed through a spacer and scar, and this card covers the mechanics of it.

The surgeon’s problem is a joint that was already cut once. The native landmarks are gone, the bone that is left is sclerotic where cement sat and absent where osteolysis ate it, the soft-tissue envelope is scarred, the quadriceps is short and adherent, and the collaterals may be attenuated or gone. The sequence is fixed:

  1. Get in through the old scar. If the knee flexes to 90 degrees with the patella mobile, a medial parapatellar arthrotomy with a synovectomy and release of the suprapatellar pouch and gutters will do. If it does not, the surgeon extends the exposure: a quadriceps snip (an oblique cut across the proximal quadriceps tendon that lets the extensor mechanism swing without tearing the patellar tendon off the tubercle), or a tibial tubercle osteotomy (a 6-8 cm shingle of anterior tibial cortex with the patellar tendon attached, hinged laterally and fixed back with wires or screws). The randomised comparison in two-stage infection revisions found better function and range with the osteotomy at long follow-up and the same reinfection rate; it adds a healing bone shingle under the patellar tendon and six weeks of protected extension.
  2. Get the old implants out: the femoral component freed at its cement interface with thin osteotomes and saws, the tibial tray lifted, and cement burred, chiselled or ultrasonically removed from the metaphysis and canals. Well-fixed components take a long time to remove without fracturing the condyles.
  3. Grade the bone loss (the AORI classification: contained defects, uncontained ones, loss of a whole condyle or plateau) and rebuild the platform: diaphyseal stems reamed into both canals to carry load past the damaged metaphysis, porous metal cones or sleeves impacted into the metaphyseal defects, metal augments for uncontained loss.
  4. Restore the joint line and balance. The femoral component is positioned to put the joint line back near its native level (a raised joint line gives a patella baja and a knee that cannot bend), the gaps are balanced with augments and insert thickness, and constraint is added in proportion to what the ligaments can no longer do: a posterior-stabilised insert, a condylar-constrained insert whose tall post substitutes for a collateral, or a rotating hinge when no collateral is left.
  5. Cement the joint-surface components and the metaphyseal region, with the stems cemented, press-fit or hybrid by preference; close over a drain, fix the osteotomy, repair the snip, infiltrate what tissue will hold it.

The pain logic follows. The dominant mechanism is retension, as in the primary knee, but of a worse kind: scarred tissue is released and a constrained implant sets the remaining ligaments and the capsule to a tension that the fibrosed envelope resists, and then the knee is bent from day 1 to stop the scar re-forming. Underneath it is more bone than any primary: reamed canals in femur and tibia, impacted cones, burred cement beds, augment cuts, and sometimes an osteotomy that the patellar tendon pulls on with every quadriceps contraction. Periosteal stripping, a long tourniquet, and a quadriceps cut across or lifted on a bone shingle finish the picture. The curve is what earns the 5: the first-night peak is no higher than a primary with the catheter running, but the pain is re-provoked by movement for weeks and the patient usually arrives opioid-exposed from months of a failing knee. In the one direct comparison under continuous adductor canal blocks, revision patients used the same opioid as primaries over 48 hours, but day 1 pain trended higher and rose with the number of previous operations on the knee; the catheter is what made the courses look alike.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Pre-operative assessment: the failed knee and the patient who owns it

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs infection excluded (aspiration, inflammatory markers) before an aseptic revision, old operation notes to know what implants and cement are in, imaging to grade the bone loss, and a plan for exposure, stems and constraint. The case length and blood loss are decided by that plan.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This patient is older than at the primary and has usually been on opioids for the months it took the knee to fail; a pre-operative opioid history and a plan with the pain service belong in the assessment, not the recovery room. Crossmatch for a stemmed revision; a haemoglobin that was acceptable for the primary may not survive a 1.5 L loss. Look for the things that make a cement load dangerous (pulmonary hypertension, severe aortic stenosis, frailty) because this operation reams and cements both canals. Chronic kidney disease matters twice: for the NSAID and for antibiotic-loaded cement. The exposure plan is yours to know: a tubercle osteotomy means six weeks of protected extension and a different conversation with the patient about day 1.

  2. 2 Anaesthetic choice for a case that may run four hours

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants a still, relaxed leg for an exposure that will be forced, a dry field for cement removal, and a knee that bends on day 1.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A spinal with sedation remains the defensible default and covers the tourniquet and the first cement step, but it is dosed for the planned length with a plan for outrunning it: a general anaesthetic from the start for the case booked beyond three hours, or a combined spinal-epidural where the unit runs the epidural for the operation and removes it before mobilisation. The adductor canal catheter goes in before the spinal, with 15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% as the loading dose; this is the author’s practice rather than a guideline, because PROSPECT’s knee guideline does not cover revision surgery. Intravenous dexamethasone 10 mg at induction, tranexamic acid 1 g IV before incision with a second dose planned, an arterial line for a hinge or a two-canal reconstruction, and a warming plan for four hours of open joint.

  3. 3 Positioning, tourniquet decision and antibiotic

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Supine with the thigh in a holder and the leg free; the surgeon will hyperflex the knee to deliver the old femoral component. A tourniquet is common for the exposure and the cement removal, when the field bleeds most and the view matters most, and is usually let down once the new components are in or when the inflation time reaches the surgeon’s limit.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cefazolin 2 g IV before the cuff; if infection has not been excluded the surgeon may ask you to hold it until deep samples are taken, a conversation for before the patient is asleep. The tourniquet time is the issue: revision inflation times run to two hours in the published cohorts, well past the primary knee trials in which a tourniquet already raised serious adverse events and day 1 pain, and the reperfusion at release (hypotension, a rise in carbon dioxide and lactate, the infiltrated local anaesthetic arriving in the circulation) is proportional. Agree a deflation time with the surgeon before the start. In the spinal patient the cuff pain begins at 60-90 minutes and is not a failing block; sedation, a small dose of ketamine and a conversation about deflation are the answers.

  4. 4 Exposure: arthrotomy, synovectomy, quadriceps snip or tibial tubercle osteotomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The old scar is reopened, the arthrotomy repeated, a thick synovectomy done to find the planes, and the gutters and suprapatellar pouch released. If the patella will not translate laterally and the knee will not flex, the surgeon makes a quadriceps snip, or lifts a tubercle osteotomy with the patellar tendon on it. The danger the extensile exposure prevents is the patellar tendon avulsing from the tubercle under the retractor, which is close to unsalvageable.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the slow, bloody half hour that sets the case length. The quadriceps snip is a cut across a working tendon and the osteotomy is a bone cut under the patellar tendon’s pull: both will hurt with every quadriceps contraction for six weeks, both restrict what the physiotherapist may ask on day 1, and both are the reason the catheter runs beyond the second night. Note which the surgeon did; it changes the brace and the quadriceps instructions.

  5. 5 Component and cement removal

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The femoral component is separated from its cement with thin osteotomes and saws passed along the interface and levered off; the tibial tray is cut free and lifted; cement is removed from the metaphysis and canals with burrs, chisels and ultrasonic tools. The aim is to lose as little bone as possible, and a well-fixed component in osteoporotic bone is where the condyle fractures.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The loss is continuous, hidden under the tourniquet or steady without it, and this is the step in which the first litre goes. A condylar or plateau fracture here converts the plan to a more constrained implant and a longer case; adjust the spinal expectation and the blood plan when it happens. Watch the clock against the agreed tourniquet deflation.

  6. 6 Canal reaming, cones, sleeves and augments

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The femoral and tibial canals are reamed to accept diaphyseal stems that bypass the damaged metaphyseal bone; porous metal cones are impacted into prepared metaphyseal defects, or sleeves broached in, to give the joint-surface components a fixed platform; metal augments make up distal femoral and tibial plateau loss. This is where the registry data show the survival benefit of stems.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Two reamed canals are the marrow embolisation of a bilateral femoral nailing in one knee: the first reamer pass down each canal produces the fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide and saturation that a long bone does, and in a patient with poor right ventricular reserve it is the dangerous moment of the case. Cone impaction is heavy hammering through the metaphysis with a fracture risk of its own. The bone work here is the second mechanism of the pain, and it is more bone than any primary.

  7. 7 Trials, joint line, balancing and the choice of constraint

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Trial stems, cones, augments and components go in; the surgeon sets the joint line to its native level using the fibular head, the epicondyles or the old films, balances the gaps with augments and insert thickness, checks patellar tracking, and chooses the constraint the ligaments demand. A condylar-constrained insert substitutes for an incompetent collateral with a tall post; a rotating hinge takes over when no collateral is reliable, at the cost of transferring every rotational load to the stems.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The retension mechanism is set here and it is harsher than a primary’s: scar tissue that was released will tighten again, and a constrained post or a hinge holds the joint at a tension the capsule resists, so the deep stiff ache that follows is the pain the catheter is for. A hinge means long stems in both canals and a bigger cement step to come. Repeated trial reductions in a spinal patient rock the pelvis and dip the pressure.

  8. 8 Cementing stems, cones and components

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Pulsed lavage, then cement into the metaphyseal regions and onto the joint surfaces, with the stems cemented (full length, with a restrictor and pressurisation), press-fit, or hybrid (cemented metaphysis, press-fit stem). Tibia first, then femur, then the insert, with the knee held in extension until the cement cures.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the cement step the primary knee does not have: a pressurised cemented stem in a freshly reamed femoral canal is the full bone cement implantation syndrome mechanism of a hip, and a cemented tibial stem adds a second. The preparations in the cement chapter apply in full: pressure supported before the cement goes in, a vasopressor running rather than drawn up, 100% oxygen, and a plan if the end-tidal carbon dioxide falls. The cure time of two components in sequence is 20-25 minutes, when the second tranexamic acid dose goes in and the haemoglobin is checked.

  9. 9 Osteotomy fixation, snip repair, infiltration, drain and closure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A tubercle osteotomy is fixed back with cerclage wires or screws and protected afterwards; a quadriceps snip is repaired side to side and needs less protection. The surgeon infiltrates the posterior capsule before the final components where it can be reached and the capsular edges at closure; scarred tissue takes the volume unevenly. A drain is usual, the arthrotomy is closed in layers, and a brace may go on for an osteotomy.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Tourniquet release, if the cuff is still up, is the reperfusion of two hours of ischaemic leg: hypotension, a carbon dioxide rise, a potassium and lactate wash, and the infiltrated local anaesthetic arriving in the circulation on top of the catheter loading dose; the ropivacaine arithmetic across block, infiltration and infusion needs doing before the surgeon starts injecting. The bleeding into the joint after release is the second litre in a bad case, and the drain tells you about it overnight. Confirm the brace goes on before the patient lightens, and start the catheter infusion in theatre rather than in recovery.

  10. 10 Recovery, the first week and the quadriceps question

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants early flexion to prevent the scar re-forming, with the limits set by the exposure: free flexion after a standard arthrotomy, protected active extension for six weeks after a tubercle osteotomy, something between after a snip. Full weight-bearing is usual with a stemmed cemented construct.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The trajectory on this card has pain at 3 on the first evening with the catheter running, 6 on days 1 and 2 with physiotherapy, 5 on day 3 and still 4 at a week, which is the shape that makes this a 5. The adductor canal infusion runs for 48-72 hours, which keeps the quadriceps working for the physiotherapy and covers the medial side; the posterior capsule relies on the infiltration and then on the oral regimen. Regular paracetamol and an NSAID (if the kidneys allow it) start in recovery; the opioid is charted for the nights and for physiotherapy sessions, and in an opioid-exposed patient the baseline dose continues with the rescue on top. Ketamine has no guideline support for a primary knee and a reasonable claim here in the opioid-tolerant patient, as author’s judgement. The transfusion decision is usually made on day 1 when the hidden loss declares itself. Stay is three to seven nights, longer with an osteotomy or a complication.

Pain plan

The mechanism is retension of a scarred, constrained joint plus more bone work than any primary, a cut or lifted extensor mechanism, and a long tourniquet, all loaded by physiotherapy from day 1 in a patient who is usually opioid-exposed. The plan has to run for three days, keep the quadriceps working, and not depend on a 2 am oral dose being given. PROSPECT’s primary knee guideline stops at the primary; its skeleton (paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, adductor canal block, local infiltration, intravenous dexamethasone 10 mg or more, no femoral or sciatic block, no epidural) is the starting point, and the card’s departures from it are the author’s judgement for an operation it does not cover.

The departure that matters is the catheter. A continuous adductor canal block is the default for every revision beyond an isolated polyethylene exchange, because the case runs two to four hours, the pain outlasts a single shot by days, and the only direct comparison showed that with a continuous block in both arms, revision patients consumed the same opioid as primaries over 48 hours while their day 1 pain trended higher and rose with each previous operation on the knee; the catheter is what levelled the courses. The adductor canal catheter keeps the quadriceps working, which matters more after a snip or an osteotomy, not less. An epidural is the alternative for the stiff knee that will be manipulated hard from day 1 and for the patient in whom the catheter cannot be placed; it costs a bladder catheter, hypotension, delayed mobilisation and a conversation about anticoagulant timing, which is why it is second. A femoral catheter gives no more analgesia than the adductor canal and takes the quadriceps away. IPACK is reasonable when the posterior capsule could not be infiltrated.

Rebound pain is the problem of the night the infusion stops rather than the first night; stepping the infusion down rather than off, with the oral regimen running for a day before the catheter comes out, is the practical answer. Escalation: pain above 7 with the infusion running and a quadriceps that fires is a catheter that has migrated (check the site, re-bolus, replace it) before it is a prescription problem; a tense, hugely swollen knee on day 1 is a haemarthrosis and the surgeon’s; a tense calf or numb toes under a block that should only touch the medial leg is a compartment syndrome or a vascular injury until proven otherwise, and the revision knee, with its long tourniquet and its posterior cement removal, is the knee where that happens. A patient who was on long-acting opioids before surgery gets them back on day 0.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Isolated polyethylene exchange. A short case through the old arthrotomy with no stems and no cement: a single-shot adductor canal block, infiltration and a primary-knee plan. Confirm it is really only the insert before the spinal is dosed.
  • Tibial tubercle osteotomy. Better function in the randomised infection-revision comparison, but a healing bone shingle under the patellar tendon: protected extension for six weeks, a brace, a catheter that runs longer, and a tubercle fracture or non-union as the late complication.
  • Quadriceps snip. A quicker exposure with less protection afterwards; the quadriceps is weaker for weeks and the physiotherapy slower.
  • Rotating hinge. Long stems in both canals, the largest cement load, the longest case, and every rotational load on the stems; this is the patient for the arterial line and the full cement precautions.
  • Press-fit versus cemented stems. A press-fit stem means no pressurised cement in the canal but heavy impaction and a fracture risk; end-of-stem pain for months afterwards is a known late problem.
  • Second stage of a two-stage infection revision. The exposure is through a spacer and more scar, antibiotic-loaded cement in large volumes is used, and the pain course starts from a knee already operated on twice; see the infection card.
  • Periprosthetic fracture as the reason for revision. A distal femoral fracture around a loose component is fixed or revised with a distal femoral replacement; the case is longer again and the patient frailer; see the periprosthetic fracture card.
  • The opioid-tolerant patient. Most revisions are; baseline opioid continued, the catheter as routine, ketamine and a pain service plan as reasonable additions, and a week of significant pain stated plainly before surgery.
  • Chronic kidney disease. The NSAID comes out of a regimen that needs it; the catheter and paracetamol carry more, and antibiotic-loaded cement in large volumes is a nephrotoxin.
  • Blood loss. A litre is usual and two is not rare; two doses of tranexamic acid and a blood management protocol took transfusion from 58% to 5% in one revision cohort. Crossmatch, cell salvage where no infection is suspected, and a first-morning haemoglobin.
  • Tourniquet time. Two hours is common and the primary-knee trials already show harm at shorter exposures; agree a deflation point before the start.

Key sources

There is no revision-specific analgesia guideline; PROSPECT’s primary knee guideline is extrapolated and the catheter-as-routine recommendation is the author’s judgement, supported by a single retrospective comparison in which continuous adductor canal blocks levelled the courses of primary and revision patients. The blood loss figures come from a retrospective revision cohort with a historical control. The exposure evidence is a single randomised comparison in infection revisions; the reconstruction evidence is systematic reviews of cones and sleeves and registry data. The pain score and trajectory are calibrated against the primary knee card and are author’s judgement.
