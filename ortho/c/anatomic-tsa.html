1 Anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty

Stemmed, stemless, hemiarthroplasty and humeral head resurfacing

3Moderate

Dominant mechanism: Bone. Humeral head osteotomy and glenoid reaming; subscapularis takedown and repair; cuff and capsule left at native length and tension, and the pre-operative pain generator (bone on bone) is removed

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: moderate.

What the surgeon is doing and why

Glenohumeral osteoarthritis is a joint that has worn through its cartilage with the rotator cuff still working. That last clause is the whole operation. The humeral head flattens and grows a collar of inferior osteophytes, the glenoid wears posteriorly and retroverts, the anterior capsule contracts so the arm cannot externally rotate, and the patient’s pain is bone on bone at the centre of the joint. The cuff, if it is intact, still centres the head. An anatomic replacement resurfaces both sides and restores the original geometry so that the intact cuff can go back to doing its job. If the cuff is gone, the same implant will rock the glenoid loose within a few years, which is why a cuff-deficient shoulder gets a reverse instead (see reverse TSA).

The surgeon has four aims, in order:

  1. Get in. The only clean way into the front of the joint is through the subscapularis, so the tendon is taken down (tenotomy, a “peel” off the lesser tuberosity, or a lesser tuberosity osteotomy) and repaired at the end. The three methods heal about as well as each other over two years; the choice is the surgeon’s habit.
  2. Take the head off at the anatomical neck, in the patient’s own retroversion, and measure it. The replacement head is sized to what was removed so that the cuff and capsule end up at their original length. This is the sentence that explains the pain profile: nothing is stretched, lengthened or re-tensioned.
  3. Ream the glenoid flat and correct its version, then implant a polyethylene glenoid, usually cemented, sometimes with a posterior augment if the wear is severe. This is the step with the worst exposure and the hardest retraction.
  4. Seat the humeral component (stemless on the cut surface of the metaphysis, or a short or standard stem down the canal, press-fit in good bone), reduce, check that the head translates about half its width in each direction and that the subscapularis will reach, then repair the subscapularis and close.

A hemiarthroplasty skips step 3 and leaves the native glenoid. Humeral head resurfacing skips the osteotomy as well and caps the head. Both are shorter, bleed less, and leave the glenoid-side pain generator in place, so the post-operative pain pattern is the same shape but the long-term pain relief is less reliable.

The pain logic follows from the anatomy. The dominant mechanism is bone: an osteotomy, reaming, and either impaction or broaching of the metaphysis, all of which hurt in the first 48 hours and then settle because nothing is being loaded through them. The only repaired tendon is the subscapularis, protected by a sling and by the fact that nobody uses it hard for six weeks, so the tendon-tension mechanism that dominates rotator cuff repair is minor here. There is no raw subacromial bone surface. And the pre-operative pain generator has been removed, so by the end of the first week most patients are less sore than they were before surgery. Compare the reverse, where the arm is deliberately lengthened and the deltoid tensioned, and the pain is deeper and lasts longer.

One honest correction to the folk wisdom that an anatomic replacement is the least painful shoulder operation: in the only prospective comparison, 102 patients having either arthroscopic cuff repair or short-stay arthroplasty under liposomal bupivacaine interscalene blocks had the same early pain, peaking on day 2 at a mean of 4.4 for cuff repair and 5.1 for arthroplasty, with no difference over 14 days and only slightly more opioid use after cuff repair at a week. The difference between the two operations is not the height of the peak. It is the shape of the curve afterwards: bone pain falls away, tendon pain is re-provoked by every movement for weeks.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Block, induction and airway before the chair goes up

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Everything the surgeon needs (an immobile head, a free arm, a quiet field) is set up now. Once the drapes are on, the head is out of your reach.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Interscalene block (or superior trunk block if the patient’s lungs cannot afford a phrenic palsy) is placed awake or lightly sedated, before induction, with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg given at the same time; it both prolongs the block and reduces rebound pain. Secure the airway for a case where the head is away from you behind a screen: a tracheal tube is the author’s default, a second-generation supraglottic airway is reasonable in a slim patient with a short case, and the decision is made now, not at the first episode of airway obstruction in the chair. Tape the eyes, protect the contralateral ulnar nerve, put the blood pressure cuff on the non-operative arm, and record a true awake baseline pressure before any drug is given. That baseline is the target for the next two hours.

  2. 2 Beach chair positioning and head fixation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Sitting the patient up drops the scapula and the deltoid away from the joint and gives the surgeon a vertical humerus to work down, which is the geometry every instrument in the set assumes.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Venous pooling in the legs, sympathetic block from the interscalene spread, and the anaesthetic together produce a predictable fall in pressure as the chair goes up. The hazard is not the number on the cuff but where the brain is: the external auditory meatus sits 20-40 cm above the cuff, so subtract about 1 mmHg for every 1.25 cm of that height when you read it. A cuff mean of 65 mmHg is a brainstem mean in the 40s. Aim to keep cuff pressure near the awake baseline with a vasopressor infusion started early rather than chased with boluses, treat bradycardia-hypotension episodes promptly (they are common in the sitting position with an interscalene block and a vagal reflex is the usual cause), and do not accept a request for deliberate hypotension to dry the field. Check that the head is neutral in the holder and strapped, not rotated or flexed onto the chest, that the endotracheal tube and circuit are not pulling on it, and that the eyes are free of pressure. Then look once more after the surgeon has positioned the arm, because the arm support moves the shoulder girdle and the neck with it.

  3. 3 Skin preparation, antibiotic and tranexamic acid

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Cutibacterium acnes lives in the dermal sebaceous glands of the shoulder, is not reliably cleared by chlorhexidine, and is the organism behind the slow, culture-negative-looking infections that ruin shoulder arthroplasties. Surgeons prepare the skin with benzoyl peroxide for days beforehand and are fastidious about not touching the dermal edge.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cefazolin 2 g IV finished before the knife, 3 g over 120 kg. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV at the same time: in the pooled trials it cuts the haemoglobin fall by 0.64 g/dL and drain output by about 117 mL, and makes transfusion rare. Nothing else is needed for C. acnes from your side; it is a surgical skin measure.

  4. 4 Deltopectoral approach

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The interval between deltoid and pectoralis major, marked by the cephalic vein, is an internervous plane (axillary nerve laterally, medial and lateral pectoral nerves medially) and gives a direct route to the front of the joint without cutting muscle. The conjoint tendon (short head of biceps and coracobrachialis) is retracted medially with the musculocutaneous nerve a finger’s breadth behind it.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The cephalic vein bleeds if it is torn and is usually preserved or ligated; this is the first blood loss. Retractors on the conjoint tendon are the first traction on the plexus, which is why the arm is kept adducted at this point. If the patient is in a light plane the shoulder girdle will tense and the surgeon will say so; deepen rather than relax, because the subscapularis repair later will be easier to judge with the muscle working.

  5. 5 Subscapularis takedown and anterior capsular release

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The tendon is the door. It is taken down by one of three methods and the anterior capsule beneath it is released from the humeral neck so that the stiff, internally rotated shoulder can be externally rotated enough to deliver the head. The long head of biceps is usually tenodesed or cut here because it is a pain generator and in the way.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    External rotation of the arm to expose the inferior capsule puts the axillary nerve on stretch where it runs under the joint; the surgeon will want the arm held exactly where they put it. There is nothing to do pharmacologically. The capsular release is the moment of maximal nociception before the osteotomy, and in a patient without a working block the pressure and heart rate will tell you so.

  6. 6 Humeral head dislocation and osteotomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The head is delivered out of the wound by extension, adduction and external rotation, osteophytes are cleared so the true anatomical neck can be seen, and the head is cut off at that neck in the patient’s native retroversion. The removed head is measured in diameter and height and the replacement is chosen to match.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The largest single bleed of the operation comes from cancellous bone at the osteotomy and from the circumflex vessels at the inferior neck. Forcible extension and external rotation of a stiff arm in an osteoporotic patient is the point at which a humeral shaft fracture happens; a wince from the surgeon here means the case just doubled in length. For a stemless implant the surgeon will test the cut surface for bone quality (a thumb test or a density check) and convert to a stem if it is soft; have no opinion on this except that a cemented stem brings the one cement step that matters.

  7. 7 Glenoid exposure and reaming

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The glenoid is a small, deep target behind the humerus. Exposure needs the humerus retracted posteriorly with a lever, the labrum excised and a circumferential capsular release. The glenoid is reamed to a flat, bleeding surface in corrected version, then drilled or slotted for the pegs or keel.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the most physiologically demanding step and the one you should be watching rather than charting. The posterior humeral retractor levers against the plexus and the axillary nerve; prolonged or forcible retraction is the usual mechanism of the post-operative neuropraxias. The surgeon needs the arm in exactly the position that opens the glenoid (usually abduction and external rotation) and will ask for it to be held still, so a brief deepening of anaesthesia is often what they mean by “can you give me something”. Reaming throws bone and blood and raises the surgeon’s heart rate more than the patient’s.

  8. 8 Glenoid implantation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A polyethylene glenoid is cemented into the pegs or keel; some are hybrid with a central press-fit post and cemented peripheral pegs, and a metal-backed or augmented component is used for severe posterior wear.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A small volume of cement, hand-pressurised into pegs with no medullary canal behind them: bone cement implantation syndrome at this step is a theoretical risk rather than a practical one, and this is not the moment for the cement precautions that a hip demands. Note the time; the glenoid cement curing is dead time in which the surgeon prepares the humerus.

  9. 9 Humeral preparation, trial and definitive component

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Stemless: the metaphysis is impacted and a cage or anchor seated on the cut surface. Stemmed: the canal is entered at the cut surface and reamed and broached to size, press-fit in good bone, cemented in poor bone. A trial head goes on, the joint is reduced, and the surgeon checks tension (the head should translate about half its width posteriorly and sit centred), range, and that the subscapularis will reach its repair site.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Press-fit impaction and broaching are hammering, with the transient drops in pressure and the fat and marrow venting that any canal work produces, far less than a femur because the humeral canal is small and not pressurised. If a stem is cemented, treat it as a true cement step: full canal preparation, pressurisation, and a patient whose pressure is already being supported; this is where a frail patient with a fracture hemiarthroplasty gets into trouble, and that case has its own card (proximal humerus fracture). Trial reduction and dislocation are repeated plexus traction. Ask for a 10-second pause if the pressure dips with each reduction; it will.

  10. 10 Subscapularis repair, closure and sling

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The tendon or tuberosity fragment is repaired back to the lesser tuberosity with heavy sutures, often through the stem or through bone tunnels, with the arm in neutral to slight internal rotation so the repair sits without tension. Layered closure of the deltopectoral interval, which has not been cut, and skin. Drains are uncommon. A sling with the arm in neutral protects the subscapularis for six weeks.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The surgeon will want the arm held precisely and motionless for the repair, and this is the point to be reducing anaesthetic depth rather than deepening it; there is no need for neuromuscular block. Give the antiemetic. The sling goes on before the drapes come off and before the patient lightens, because an awake patient with a blocked, flail arm will try to move it with the other hand. Emergence is unremarkable; the arm is insensate and the wound is small.

  11. 11 Recovery, the first night and discharge

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The implant is stable from the moment it is reduced; what the surgeon wants is a protected subscapularis, an early check radiograph, and the deltoid working.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Document deltoid and sensation over the regimental badge area before the block if you can, because afterwards you cannot test the axillary nerve until the block recedes, and a dense interscalene block reads as a plexus injury to anyone who did not know it was there. The block lasts 12 to 24 hours with dexamethasone; the first dose of oral paracetamol and NSAID goes in before the patient leaves recovery, not when the block wears off. Tell the patient and the ward in words that the arm will come back to life overnight with pain that rises over a few hours and that the oral regimen started on time is the treatment, not the trigger for a rescue call. Day 1 physiotherapy is pendular and passive; it does not need the block to still be working. Length of stay is one to two nights; same-day discharge is reasonable in a younger, fitter patient with home support, where the pooled data show no excess readmission or revision, but the cohorts that did well were selected to do well.

Pain plan

The mechanism is bone-dominant and short, so the plan is a single-shot block that covers the operation and the first night, with the oral regimen carrying the patient from day 1. The defensible default is an interscalene block with 15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% (or a superior trunk block with the same volume when the patient cannot afford a hemidiaphragm, which gives the same analgesia at 24 h with complete phrenic paresis in 5% rather than 73%), intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg at induction, regular paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started before the block recedes, and oral opioid as rescue for the first two or three days. PROSPECT’s rotator cuff repair guideline is the nearest procedure-specific evidence and it recommends exactly this skeleton, with intravenous dexamethasone preferred over perineural.

The catheter question is the one the trigger case asked, and the honest answer is that for an anatomic replacement a catheter is a reasonable choice rather than a necessary one. In the one randomised comparison that added a continuous ropivacaine infusion to day 2 on top of dexamethasone and full multimodal analgesia, in a mixed arthroplasty and cuff repair population, morphine use at 24 h was 10 mg with the infusion and 14 mg without, not different, pain scores were the same, and four of the catheters fell out. The catheter earns its place when the patient, rather than the operation, predicts a bad course: an opioid-tolerant or chronic pain patient, a revision or a previous arthroplasty, someone who has had a catastrophic night after a previous single-shot block, or a unit that cannot deliver a reliable oral regimen at 2 am. Rebound pain is the thing to plan against: in 972 ambulatory block patients it occurred in half, and the odds were higher with bone surgery (OR 1.82), younger age and female sex, and lower when intravenous dexamethasone was given (its absence carried an OR of 1.78). Everything on this card that involves bone qualifies.

Escalation: pain above 7 after the block has receded, with the arm neurovascularly intact and the sling correctly on, is treated with the oral opioid that should already be charted; pain that is out of proportion, with a tense swollen deltopectoral wound, is a haematoma and needs the surgeon, not more morphine. A rescue block the next morning is rarely needed and, if the first block was interscalene with a phrenic palsy, a second on the same side within 24 h is not the right reflex.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Stemless versus stemmed. Stemless implants mean no canal work, less bleeding, a shorter case, and an easier future revision; they need good metaphyseal bone, so the surgeon may convert to a stem after the osteotomy. A cemented stem is rare in elective anatomic replacement and common in fracture surgery.
  • Hemiarthroplasty and resurfacing. Shorter, less bleeding, no glenoid cement, same block, same first night. The glenoid is left native, so some of the original pain stays.
  • Conversion to reverse. If the cuff is found to be irreparable or the glenoid cannot hold an anatomic component, the surgeon converts on the table. The case lengthens by an hour, the arm will be lengthened, and the pain plan moves to the reverse card.
  • Beach chair physiology in the patient with cerebrovascular disease, carotid stenosis or uncontrolled hypertension. The margin between cuff pressure and brainstem perfusion is the whole problem. An arterial line transduced at the ear is the honest way to know, and lateral decubitus is an alternative some surgeons will accept.
  • Phrenic palsy. Near-universal after interscalene block, mostly unnoticed, occasionally not. For the patient with COPD, a single lung, contralateral diaphragm palsy or severe obesity, use a superior trunk block or a suprascapular plus axillary combination and expect slightly less analgesia from the latter.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis. Cervical spine instability for the head holder, a cuff that may be thinner than the imaging suggested, steroid cover, and a tendency to bleed more.
  • Post-traumatic arthritis and failed hemiarthroplasty. Scar, retained metal, a long case, more blood loss, more plexus traction: budget for a catheter and for transfusion samples.
  • Diabetes. Dexamethasone 8 mg raises glucose for a day; it is still worth giving for the block, and the ward needs to know.
  • The dense block that looks like a nerve injury. The axillary nerve is the one at risk from retraction; document deltoid function before the block or accept that you cannot assess it until the next day.

Key sources

The evidence on pain after anatomic replacement is thinner than the operation’s frequency deserves. The strongest procedure-specific guidance is PROSPECT’s rotator cuff repair guideline, which the shoulder arthroplasty literature leans on by extrapolation. The two studies that change practice are the 2025 prospective comparison showing early pain after short-stay arthroplasty equal to arthroscopic cuff repair, and the 2024 randomised trial showing no benefit from a continuous infusion once dexamethasone and multimodal analgesia are in place. The beach chair blood pressure advice rests on physiology and case reports, not trials, and will stay that way.
