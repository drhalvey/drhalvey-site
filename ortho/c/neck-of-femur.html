39 Neck of femur fracture: hemiarthroplasty, THA, DHS and nail

Intracapsular fractures (cemented hemiarthroplasty, total hip arthroplasty for the fitter patient, cannulated screws) and extracapsular fractures (dynamic hip screw, cephalomedullary nail on a traction table)

3Moderate

Dominant mechanism: Bone. A mobile fracture before surgery; then a neck osteotomy with canal preparation and cement (hemiarthroplasty), or a reamed lag screw track through the fracture with a lateral approach through vastus lateralis (DHS) or a nail entry through gluteus medius at the trochanteric tip (nail). Fixing the fracture removes most of the pre-operative pain generator

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: moderate.

Block
Fascia iliaca block (supra-inguinal) or femoral nerve block in the emergency department, repeated before positioning for the spinal if more than 6 h have passed; PENG block is a reasonable alternative with equivalent dynamic pain relief in the one direct comparison
Catheter: rarely PROSPECT and the UK guideline both advise against routine catheters; a catheter is reasonable only when surgery will be delayed beyond a day and a repeated single shot cannot be organised
Alternatives: PENG block (motor sparing, covers the anterior capsule, weaker for the lateral incision); Low-dose spinal with hyperbaric bupivacaine and no intrathecal opioid; General anaesthesia with the block as the analgesic component; Surgeon's periarticular infiltration for a posterior approach as a supplement
Position
Lateral for hemiarthroplasty and total hip arthroplasty (posterior or anterolateral approach; supine for some surgeons' direct lateral approach); supine on a traction table with a perineal post and the image intensifier in the field for DHS and nail
Tourniquet
No
Cement
Sometimes. Hemiarthroplasty stems are cemented in most units because cemented fixation gives better function, fewer periprosthetic fractures and no excess mortality; this is the cement step with the highest incidence of bone cement implantation syndrome in orthopaedics. DHS and nail use no cement; THA for fracture is cemented or hybrid according to the surgeon
Blood loss
Hemiarthroplasty 200-500 mL in the wound; DHS and nail bleed less into the wound but the extracapsular fracture has already bled into the thigh, so the day 1-2 haemoglobin falls further than the measured loss predicts and transfusion is common
Duration
Cannulated screws 30-45 min; DHS 45-75 min; cephalomedullary nail 45-90 min; hemiarthroplasty 60-90 min; total hip arthroplasty for fracture 90-150 min
Length of stay
Acute stay of a week or more, then rehabilitation; about a quarter of patients are dead within a year in registry and trial cohorts. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV within 60 min of incision (3 g over 120 kg), after the spinal is in; an implant goes into every one of these operations
Tranexamic acid
Tranexamic acid 1 g IV at induction is common practice and reasonable; the hip fracture trial base is smaller than for elective arthroplasty. Draw it up only after the spinal is sited and never on the same tray as the spinal drugs
VTE
Low molecular weight heparin from admission, timed around the neuraxial block (last dose at least 12 h before the spinal, next dose at least 4 h after), continued for 28-35 days; mechanical prophylaxis from admission
Antiemetic
Ondansetron 4 mg IV; dexamethasone 4-8 mg IV is reasonable for block prolongation and antiemesis, with a day of hyperglycaemia in the diabetic patient
Tell the patient
The operation takes away most of the pain of the broken bone; the hip will be sore for a few days when you move, and getting up with the physiotherapists the day after surgery is part of the treatment, not something to wait for.
The trap
The cemented hemiarthroplasty is the one cement step in orthopaedics that kills: have the pressure supported and a vasopressor running before the surgeon tells you the canal is being cemented, because the collapse at pressurisation is a right heart failing against an embolic load, and the time to treat it is before it happens

What the surgeon is doing and why

A hip fracture is two different injuries that share a ward, and the capsule is the line between them. An intracapsular fracture tears the retinacular vessels that run up the neck to the head, so a displaced one in an old patient has a head that will die if it is fixed, and the surgeon replaces it. An extracapsular fracture breaks through cancellous metaphysis outside the capsule, leaves the head alive and bleeds into the thigh rather than the joint; the surgeon fixes it.

                     hip fracture
                          |
          +---------------+----------------+
     intracapsular                     extracapsular
   (head blood supply torn)         (head alive, bleeds into thigh)
          |                                |
   undisplaced  displaced           stable 2-part   unstable, reverse
       |            |               intertroch.     oblique, subtroch.
  cannulated   fit, independent,        |                 |
   screws      cognitively intact:     DHS       cephalomedullary nail
               THA                                  (traction table)
               everyone else:
               cemented hemiarthroplasty

The displaced intracapsular fracture gets a hemiarthroplasty in most patients: a stemmed femoral component with a large head on the native acetabulum, cemented in most units. WHiTE 5 settled the cement argument in 1,225 patients: the cemented stem gave better quality of life at four months, a quarter of the periprosthetic fracture rate (0.5% against 2.1%) and 12-month mortality of 23.9% against 27.8%, so the cement that frightens the anaesthetist does not cost lives overall, and the uncemented stem fractures the osteoporotic femur it is hammered into. The fit, independent, cognitively intact patient gets a total hip arthroplasty, because the native acetabulum wears under a metal head; the price is a longer operation, more blood loss and a higher dislocation rate. The undisplaced fracture gets cannulated screws through a small lateral incision.

For the extracapsular fracture the device depends on whether the lateral femoral wall will hold a plate. A stable two-part intertrochanteric fracture gets a dynamic hip screw: a lag screw up the neck into the head, sliding in a barrel on a lateral plate so the fracture compresses as the patient weight-bears. An unstable, reverse oblique or subtrochanteric fracture gets a cephalomedullary nail from the trochanteric tip down the canal, with a lag screw or blade through it into the head. Both are done on a traction table under image intensification.

The frail patient is the operation. The mean age in the big trials is about 80, a fifth become delirious, and about a quarter are dead within a year, mostly from what they already had. Surgery is the treatment for the pain and for the immobility that causes the pneumonia and the pressure injuries, which is why the Australian clinical care standard and the UK guideline both set a 36-h limit from presentation and treat delay as a harm. HIP ATTACK tested 6 h against 24 h in 2,970 patients: 90-day mortality (9% against 10%) and major complications (22% in both arms) did not change, but delirium, urinary infection, moderate-to-severe pain and length of stay all fell. Hours inside the window buy comfort.

The pain logic is bone throughout. Before surgery the generator is a mobile fracture that hurts on movement far more than at rest, which is why positioning for a spinal without a block is cruel. After surgery it is the osteotomy and cement interface (hemiarthroplasty), or a reamed track through the fracture plus a lateral incision through vastus lateralis (DHS) or a nail entry through gluteus medius (nail). Nothing is under tension, so the pain falls over two or three days as bone pain does, and the operation has removed most of what hurt before. The score is 3. The patient often cannot tell you, so the plan is written for someone who will not ask.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Emergency department: block, clock and assessment

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the patient on a trauma list within 36 h with the implant decision made, which needs the radiographs, the pre-fracture walking and cognition, and the anticoagulant history.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The block goes in here. The Australian standard and PROSPECT both want a fascia iliaca or femoral nerve block from presentation, and the Australian standard measures it before surgery and before any inter-hospital transfer. Supra-inguinal fascia iliaca is the PROSPECT preference; a PENG block matched it for dynamic pain in the one head-to-head trial, with no difference in resting pain or motor block. Paracetamol starts now; an NSAID waits for a renal function result. The admission bundle that matters to you: a 4AT, a frailty score, an ECG, haemoglobin, electrolytes, renal function, a group and hold, the time and dose of the last anticoagulant, and intravenous fluid, because almost every one of these patients is dry.

  2. 2 Optimisation and the reasons to delay

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A delayed operation is a longer time in pain and in bed; the surgeon pushes for the next list, the orthogeriatrician for a patient fit for it, and both are right.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The UK guideline lists the reasons that justify delay and insists they are corrected inside the 36 h rather than used to cancel: haemoglobin under 80 g/L, sodium under 120 or over 150 mmol/L, potassium under 2.8 or over 6.0 mmol/L, uncontrolled diabetes, acute left ventricular failure, a rate over 120, chest sepsis and a reversible coagulopathy. A murmur without symptoms is not on the list; an echocardiogram that costs a day is a worse trade than an arterial line and the assumption that the valve is tight. Anticoagulants: warfarin is reversed with vitamin K 5 mg IV on admission, with surgery at an INR of 1.8 or below and a spinal at 1.5 or below; a factor Xa inhibitor is stopped and the operation planned 24 h after the last dose (48 h if the eGFR is under 60), or sooner if an anti-Xa level is under 50 ng/mL; dabigatran is planned for the next afternoon with a normal thrombin time, or reversed with idarucizumab if surgery cannot wait. A single antiplatelet agent, including clopidogrel, is not a bar to a spinal in the UK guidance. Transfuse to above 90 g/L, or 100 g/L with ischaemic heart disease, before the table rather than during cementing.

  3. 3 Spinal or general: what the decision rests on

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon has no preference beyond a still patient and no delay.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Two large trials took the belief that spinal anaesthesia protects the brain and failed to confirm it. REGAIN randomised 1,600 patients with a mean age of 78: death or inability to walk at 60 days in 18.5% with spinal and 18.0% with general anaesthesia, delirium 20.5% and 19.7%, and 15% of the spinal group converted on the day. RAGA randomised 950 patients over 65 to unsedated regional or general anaesthesia: delirium within a week 6.2% and 5.1%. So the decision is individual. The defensible default is a spinal for the patient who can lie still on a blocked hip for the duration, with a low dose of hyperbaric bupivacaine (the UK specimen protocol uses under 2 mL of 0.5%) and no intrathecal opioid when a block is working, because the hypotension of a full-dose spinal in a dry 85-year-old is the preventable harm of the technique. A general anaesthetic is the right answer for dementia that will not tolerate the position, dual antiplatelet therapy, or a nail that will take 90 min on a traction table; a fixed cardiac output lesion takes either, with an arterial line and a vasopressor infusion from the start. Sedation is propofol in small doses, not midazolam. Target a mean arterial pressure above 70 mmHg.

  4. 4 The second block, the spinal and the drugs on the tray

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The spinal has to be done sitting or lateral, and either position moves the fracture.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    If the emergency department block is more than 6 h old, repeat it before the patient is moved; the UK guideline asks for a block in the department and a block at surgery, and the second is what makes the spinal civilised. Cefazolin 2 g IV goes in once the spinal is sited. Tranexamic acid is drawn up only after the spinal is in and never on the same tray; intrathecal tranexamic acid is a lethal drug error that the hip fracture list has produced more than once.

  5. 5 Positioning: lateral for arthroplasty, traction table for fixation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Hemiarthroplasty and THA need a lateral position for the posterior or anterolateral approach. DHS and nail need the patient supine on a traction table with the fractured leg in a boot, the pelvis against a padded perineal post, the other leg abducted or scissored out of the way of the image intensifier, and the operative-side arm across the chest.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    On the traction table the hazards are the post and the pull. The perineal post presses on the pudendal nerve and the genitalia; a pudendal neuropraxia and perineal pressure injury follow a long pull against poor padding, so check the padding and ask for traction to be released whenever the surgeon is not reducing. The pull stretches the sciatic nerve and, in a tight boot, the peroneal nerve. The well leg, abducted and elevated, can develop a calf compartment syndrome in a long subtrochanteric nailing. A spinal patient on their side under a drape for 80 min has a face you cannot see; the capnography line on the mask is the sedation monitor. Forced-air warming from the start: the traction table exposes the patient and the spinal has removed thermoregulation below the waist.

  6. 6 Hemiarthroplasty: approach, capsulotomy and removal of the head

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Through a posterior approach (short external rotators divided, capsule opened at the back) or an anterolateral approach (anterior gluteus medius lifted off the trochanter), the surgeon opens the capsule, removes the fractured head with a corkscrew, measures it and cuts the neck. Posterior is faster and spares the abductors at the cost of a higher dislocation rate.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Removing the head is where the blood starts, from the neck and from the haematoma in the capsule; the 2015 safety guideline tells you to watch for cardiorespiratory compromise from this moment, not from the cement. It is also where you should hear the words “we will be cementing” and acknowledge them; the guideline makes that exchange a formal requirement.

  7. 7 Hemiarthroplasty: canal preparation and cement

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The canal is broached, washed with pressurised lavage, dried, plugged distally and filled retrogradely from a cement gun; the stem is inserted and held while the cement cures. The washing, drying and plugging are the surgical half of the cement precautions, because cement pressurised into a canal full of fat and marrow drives both into the femoral veins.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the step the 2015 AAGBI, BOA and BGS safety guideline was written for. Bone cement implantation syndrome is graded 1 (saturation below 94% or a systolic fall over 20%, in about 20% of cemented hemiarthroplasties), 2 (saturation below 88%, a systolic fall over 40% or loss of consciousness, about 3%) and 3 (cardiac arrest, about 1%); the risk factors are age, male sex, cardiopulmonary disease and diuretic use, which describes most of the list. The mechanism is embolic: fat, marrow and air into the right heart, pulmonary vasoconstriction and acute right ventricular failure. Under a spinal the first sign is confusion or restlessness; under a general anaesthetic it is an abrupt fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide before the pressure moves. The precautions are all before the event: fluid before induction and again before cementing, a vasopressor infusion already running, systolic within 20% of the pre-induction value, stat-cycling non-invasive pressure or an arterial line in the higher-risk patient, adrenaline diluted and drawn up, and the surgeon told not to pressurise with vigour. When it happens: 100% oxygen, fluid, adrenaline in small boluses for the right heart, and the surgeon stops and waits. See bone cement and BCIS.

  8. 8 Hemiarthroplasty and THA: trial, reduction and closure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The head size matches the one removed, the hip is reduced, stability is tested, and the capsule and rotators or the abductors are repaired. A THA adds acetabular reaming and a cup: 30-60 min more and a further bleed from the acetabular bed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Reduction needs a relaxed patient; under a general anaesthetic a bolus of relaxant is what the surgeon means when the hip will not go in. Each trial reduction is a tug on the femoral vein and a transient fall in pressure in a patient just cemented. The THA patient follows the elective card for the approach (posterior or anterior and lateral) with the fracture’s blood loss added.

  9. 9 Extracapsular fractures: closed reduction on the traction table

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Before any incision the surgeon pulls on the boot, internally rotates the leg to bring the neck into profile and checks the reduction on two views: neck-shaft angle restored, medial cortex in contact, no varus. A reduction that fails closed means a clamp or an open reduction and a longer case.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Traction on an unblocked fracture is the most painful moment of the day and the whole case for the second block. Under a spinal the patient feels the pull as pressure in the groin; under a general anaesthetic the heart rate tells you the traction has gone on. The C-arm sweeps across the chest and your lines, so secure both before the drapes go on.

  10. 10 Dynamic hip screw: lateral approach, reaming and plate

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A lateral incision from the trochanter down the shaft, through fascia lata with vastus lateralis lifted off the bone, exposes the lateral cortex. A guidewire goes up the neck into the centre of the head under imaging, the track is reamed over it, the lag screw is inserted, the barrel plate slides over it and is screwed to the shaft, and the traction is released so the fracture compresses along the screw.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The bleeding comes from vastus lateralis stripped off bone and from the reamed cancellous track, which is why this 45-min operation has a bigger haemoglobin fall than its length suggests, on top of the unit or more already lost into the thigh. The vastus split and periosteal stripping are the muscle and periosteal components of the pain, and the reason a fascia iliaca block covers a DHS better than a PENG block.

  11. 11 Cephalomedullary nail: entry, reaming, lag screw and locking

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A short incision proximal to the trochanter, through gluteus medius at its tip, admits a guidewire and an entry reamer; the canal is reamed (little for a short nail, to the isthmus for a long one), the nail is driven in, the lag screw or helical blade goes up the neck through it, and the nail is locked distally under imaging.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Reaming the canal through a fresh fracture in an elderly patient is a marrow embolisation step, smaller than a shaft nailing but the same physiology: a fall in saturation and end-tidal carbon dioxide as the reamer passes, more with a long nail; see fat embolism. The entry through gluteus medius is the pain the patient complains of on the stairs for months and the muscle mechanism in this card. Freehand distal locking of a long nail adds 15 min of fluoroscopy at the end.

  12. 12 Recovery, the first night and day 1

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants a check radiograph and full weight-bearing from the day after surgery; every implant on this card is designed to be loaded from day 1, which is the Australian standard’s mobilisation statement.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The haemoglobin next morning is lower than the one in recovery, because the thigh keeps oozing and the fluids redistribute; transfuse to the thresholds above rather than waiting for symptoms the patient cannot report. Delirium touched a fifth of REGAIN patients whichever anaesthetic they had, and its drivers in the first 24 h are yours: unassessed pain, hypoxia, hypotension, a full bladder, anticholinergic or benzodiazepine drugs, and a night without sleep. The things that stop a patient standing on day 1 are also yours: a dense block that has not worn off (the reason both guidelines discourage catheters), postural hypotension in a patient still dry, a haemoglobin under 90 g/L, delirium, and pain not treated overnight.

Pain plan

The mechanism is bone with a muscle component in a patient who often cannot report pain, so the plan is a single-shot block early, a neuraxial or general anaesthetic for the operation, and a regular oral regimen that does not depend on the patient asking. PROSPECT’s hip fracture recommendations are the skeleton: a single-shot femoral or fascia iliaca block (supra-inguinal preferred) before surgery, paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started pre-operatively and continued where the kidney and the gut permit, spinal or general anaesthesia, and opioid as rescue only. PROSPECT lists the PENG block as not recommended for lack of evidence rather than evidence against it. The author’s view: fascia iliaca covers the lateral incision of a DHS better; PENG preserves quadriceps function and suits the patient who will stand next morning after a nail or a hemiarthroplasty.

The catheter decision is close to settled: PROSPECT advises against catheters except for an expected surgical delay, and the UK guideline notes that continuous blocks may delay remobilisation, the outcome the whole pathway protects. A catheter is reasonable for the patient whose surgery is delayed beyond a day and who cannot be given a repeated single shot.

The oral regimen shape: regular paracetamol from the emergency department, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor for the first few days where renal function, cardiac failure and gastrointestinal risk allow (a minority here, so the NSAID is a decision rather than a default), and a low-dose short-acting opioid charted regularly for the first 48 h in the patient who cannot ask, with a behavioural pain score as the trigger. Dexamethasone 4-8 mg IV at induction prolongs the block and reduces nausea.

Escalation and rebound: pain rising as the block recedes on the first night is moderate, because the fracture that hurt on movement is fixed, and it answers the regular regimen started on time. Severe pain with a tense thigh is a haematoma in an anticoagulated patient and needs a haemoglobin and the surgeon. Pain in a hemiarthroplasty with a leg that is shortened and rotated is a dislocation. New confusion on night one is delirium until proved otherwise: was the analgesia given, are the saturation and pressure adequate, is the bladder full.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Uncemented hemiarthroplasty. Chosen by some surgeons for severe cardiopulmonary disease to avoid the cement step; WHiTE 5 shows the trade is a four-fold periprosthetic fracture rate and no survival gain, so the honest discussion is a cemented stem with the full precautions.
  • The dual antiplatelet patient. A general anaesthetic with the block as the analgesic is the common answer; the UK guideline does not forbid a spinal but asks for a good reason to avoid the general anaesthetic. A recent stent is a cardiology conversation inside the 36 h.
  • Dementia and a spinal. Lying still on one side under a drape for 80 min is beyond many of these patients, and a spinal that becomes a struggle with deep sedation and an unprotected airway is the worst of both techniques. Decide at the door.
  • The subtrochanteric fracture. The hardest reduction, often open, a long nail reamed to the isthmus, the biggest blood loss and the longest case; plan it as a femoral shaft nailing with an old patient attached.
  • The periprosthetic fracture that presents as a hip fracture is a different operation (see periprosthetic fractures): longer and bloodier.
  • Pathological fracture. A lytic lesion means a long nail or a long-stem replacement, more bleeding and a bigger cement step.
  • The patient who is not for resuscitation. The operation is still the treatment for pain and is still done; the ceiling-of-care conversation is documented before the cement goes in rather than during the arrest. See the orthopaedic patient.

Key sources

The anaesthetic evidence for hip fracture is better than for any other trauma operation. REGAIN and RAGA close the question of whether spinal anaesthesia protects the brain or improves survival: it does not. HIP ATTACK shows that speed inside the 36-h window buys comfort rather than survival. WHiTE 5 shows the cemented stem is the better implant despite the cement step, which puts the burden on the anaesthetist to make the step safe, and the 2015 safety guideline says how. PROSPECT and the two national standards agree on the block, the timing and the oral regimen. The PENG versus fascia iliaca choice rests on one 80-patient trial and is the author’s judgement where the card says so.
