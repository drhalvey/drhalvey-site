90 Anterior and thoracoscopic thoracic spine surgery

Transthoracic discectomy, corpectomy and reconstruction, anterior release and instrumentation through a thoracotomy, mini-thoracotomy, thoracoscopy or thoracoabdominal approach

4Severe

Dominant mechanism: Nerve. Ribs spread or a rib resected, with the intercostal nerve above and below crushed against the retractor; latissimus and serratus divided, parietal pleura stripped off the vertebral bodies and a chest drain left rubbing on it for two or three days; every breath and cough loads the wound, and the intercostal nerve injury is what makes a proportion of these chronic. Thoracoscopy swaps the spread for three or four port sites and is one point lower

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Block
Thoracic paravertebral catheter placed by the surgeon under direct vision before closure, sited above the stripped pleura, running ropivacaine 0.2% for two to three days, with regular paracetamol, an NSAID once the surgeon agrees, and opioid patient-controlled analgesia as rescue
Catheter: routine After a thoracotomy or thoracoabdominal approach a catheter is the default, because the pain is re-provoked by every breath for days and a single shot wears off on the first night with the drain still in; after a purely thoracoscopic case a single-shot paravertebral or erector spinae plane block is enough
Alternatives: Thoracic epidural: equivalent analgesia, more hypotension and urinary retention, and it blurs the leg examination the surgeon wants hourly; Erector spinae plane catheter placed by ultrasound before positioning or at the end, when the paravertebral space has been opened by the surgery; Surgeon intercostal blocks under vision at closure, two levels above and below, as the minimum; Intrathecal morphine 0.15-0.2 mg where a posterior stage follows under the same anaesthetic
Position
Lateral decubitus with the operative side up (right side up for the upper and mid thoracic spine, left side up for the thoracolumbar junction), axillary roll, table flexed at the operative level to open the rib spaces, upper arm on a gutter support, pelvis and chest taped so the lateral fluoroscopy image is true; a posterior stage, if planned, follows prone
Tourniquet
No
Cement
Sometimes. Polymethylmethacrylate is occasionally used to fill a corpectomy defect in a patient with limited prognosis, or to augment vertebral body screws in poor bone; small volumes, with leak into the canal or the segmental veins as the hazard
Blood loss
200-500 mL for a thoracoscopic discectomy or release; 500-1,500 mL for a corpectomy and cage; beyond 2 L for a vascular tumour or infected vertebral body, most of it from cancellous bone and epidural veins that cannot be tamponaded
Duration
150-240 min for a single-level discectomy; 240-360 min for a corpectomy and reconstruction; longer when a posterior stage is added
Length of stay
5-8 nights after thoracotomy; 3-5 after a thoracoscopic procedure; a night in a high-dependency bed is usual. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV within 60 min of incision (3 g over 120 kg), repeated at 4 h; an implant and a long case
Tranexamic acid
Tranexamic acid 1 g IV before incision with a further 1 g at 3 h or an infusion for a corpectomy; the spine meta-analyses support it and the bleeding surface here is bone
VTE
Mechanical from induction; low molecular weight heparin from day 1 or 2 by agreement with the surgeon, since the canal has been opened from the front and a paravertebral or epidural catheter has its own timing rules
Antiemetic
Dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction and ondansetron 4 mg IV before emergence; retching against a thoracotomy is to be avoided
Tell the patient
The spine is reached through the side of the chest, so you will wake with a drain between the ribs and a fine tube giving local anaesthetic beside it; breathing deeply and coughing will hurt for the first few days and are the most useful things you can do.
The trap
Two teams want opposite things from the same anaesthetic: one-lung ventilation wants a still, relaxed chest, and motor evoked potentials want no relaxant and a mean pressure of 80 mmHg or more while the cord's segmental supply is being ligated; agree the sequence before the knife, and never let the pressure drift during the corpectomy to dry the field

What the surgeon is doing and why

The thoracic cord fills its canal, has a watershed blood supply and does not tolerate being moved. A problem in front of it (a calcified central disc, a burst or collapsed vertebral body, a tumour or abscess in the body, a rigid kyphosis that needs its anterior tether released) cannot be reached safely from behind without retracting the cord, so the surgeon goes through the chest and arrives at the front of the spine with the cord on the far side of the pathology. That is the whole reason for this approach, and the price is a chest operation added to a spine operation.

The route is chosen by level. For T4 to T10 the approach is usually from the right, because the heart and the aortic arch are on the left. For the thoracolumbar junction (T11 to L2) it is from the left, because the aorta tolerates retraction better than the vena cava and the liver is out of the way; the diaphragm is then detached from its costal margin or split, which makes it a thoracoabdominal approach. The access options run from a full posterolateral thoracotomy with a rib resected (which also supplies the bone graft), through a mini-thoracotomy with a tubular or table-mounted retractor, to a purely thoracoscopic operation through three or four ports.

Once in, the sequence is the same. The lung is collapsed and retracted forward. The parietal pleura is incised over the spine and stripped off the vertebral bodies and rib heads. The segmental vessels, which run across the waist of each body, are ligated and divided at mid-body so that collateral flow at the foramen is preserved. The rib head is removed to find the pedicle, the pedicle leads to the canal, and the surgeon then removes disc or body from front to back, drilling a cavity and collapsing the last shell of bone or disc forward into it, away from the cord. A corpectomy defect is filled with an expandable cage or strut graft and often spanned by a lateral plate or screw-rod construct.

Three consequences drive the anaesthetic. The operated lung has to be down for hours. The cord’s segmental arteries are being sacrificed over several levels in the zone where the largest radiculomedullary artery usually enters (T8 to L1, more often from the left), so cord perfusion depends on collaterals and on the pressure you provide. And the patient leaves with a thoracotomy’s pain and pulmonary cost: in a systematic review of 1,036 operations for thoracic disc herniation overall morbidity was 29%, mostly medical complications, and the anterior and lateral routes carried more of them than the posterolateral route.

The pain logic: a thoracotomy hurts because ribs are levered apart against intercostal nerves, large muscles are cut, the pleura is inflamed by the stripping and the drain, and none of it can be rested because the patient has to breathe. The mechanism tag is nerve because the intercostal nerve injury governs both the severity and the chronic pain that follows a share of thoracotomies. Thoracoscopy removes the spreading and most of the muscle division, and leaves port-site intercostal neuralgia and the drain.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Assessment and the plan agreed with two surgeons

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A spinal surgeon and often a thoracic access surgeon share the case. The pathology decides how much lung function the patient can spare: a myelopathic patient with a giant disc is usually fit; a patient with a metastasis or tuberculous collapse is not.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Assess as for a lung resection without the resection: exercise tolerance, spirometry if there is any doubt, and whether the dependent lung is healthy enough to carry the patient alone for four hours. Open thoracotomy for spine costs lung function permanently (a fall of 11-13% in percent-predicted FEV1 and FVC at two years in adolescents, against 4-5% after thoracoscopy). Agree four things at the brief: side, whether motor evoked potentials are being monitored, the pressure target, and whether a posterior stage follows. Arterial line always; central access for a corpectomy, a tumour or poor veins; crossmatch.

  2. 2 Lung isolation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon is working on the posterior mediastinal surface at the bottom of a deep cavity; a ventilated lung fills the field.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A left-sided double-lumen tube is the default and is checked with a bronchoscope after the lateral turn. A bronchial blocker through a single-lumen tube is the better choice when a prone posterior stage follows, when the patient will stay intubated, or when the airway is difficult, because it avoids a tube exchange at the end of a long case with a swollen airway. Upper thoracic approaches from the right need good right upper lobe collapse, which a blocker delivers less reliably. For thoracoscopy some surgeons add low-pressure carbon dioxide insufflation; it reduces venous return and behaves like a small tension pneumothorax if the pressure creeps up.

  3. 3 Lateral positioning

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The table is flexed at the operative level to open the rib spaces and the spine is positioned truly lateral so that screws and cages can be placed on fluoroscopy without the body being rotated.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Axillary roll, dependent brachial plexus and common peroneal nerve padded, neck neutral, upper arm supported without abduction past 90 degrees. Recheck the tube position after the table is broken. If motor evoked potentials are in use, baselines are recorded after positioning and before incision, and the relaxant given for intubation and positioning must be wearing off by then. A lateral position for five hours on a flexed table is a pressure injury risk at the dependent hip and shoulder.

  4. 4 Thoracotomy or port placement and lung collapse

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The rib overlying the target (usually one or two above the vertebral level, because the ribs slope down) is resected or the space is spread; for thoracoscopy the ports are triangulated on the level after fluoroscopic marking.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Start one-lung ventilation before the pleura is opened so the lung is down when the surgeon looks. Protective settings (tidal volume 4-6 mL/kg, PEEP to the dependent lung, the lowest oxygen fraction that holds saturation) for a case that will run for hours. Hypoxia during one-lung ventilation in the lateral position is handled by the usual ladder: check the tube, recruit the dependent lung, oxygen insufflation or CPAP to the operative lung with the surgeon’s agreement. The spine surgeon, unlike a lung surgeon, can often tolerate intermittent two-lung ventilation during the bony work, and it is worth asking.

  5. 5 Pleural stripping and segmental vessel ligation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The vessels crossing each vertebral body to be exposed are tied and cut so the great vessels can be swept forward and the lateral surface of the body is bare.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Each ligated segmental artery removes one input to the cord’s collateral network. Hold a mean arterial pressure of 80 mmHg or more from this point until the reconstruction is finished, with noradrenaline, not with volume alone. Where the anatomy is uncertain or several levels are being taken on the left at the thoracolumbar junction, the surgeon can place a temporary clip and wait for the motor evoked potentials to be repeated before dividing the vessel; that test only means something if the anaesthetic and the pressure are steady while it is done. A segmental vessel that retracts under the aorta bleeds briskly and is hard to recover.

  6. 6 Rib head removal, discectomy or corpectomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The pedicle is drilled away to show the lateral dura, a cavity is made in the disc or body in front of the canal, and the compressing material is delivered forward into it. A calcified disc is adherent to, or through, the dura in a proportion of cases.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The highest-risk period for the cord and the main blood loss. Propofol and remifentanil with no relaxant if motor potentials are monitored, which means the diaphragm and chest wall can move: keep the anaesthetic deep enough and the remifentanil generous enough that the patient does not cough with a drill beside the dura. A fall in potentials is worked through the standard checklist (pressure, haemoglobin, temperature, anaesthetic depth, then the surgical manoeuvre), as set out in spine essentials. Cancellous bone and epidural veins bleed steadily; cell salvage is useful except where the pathology is infection, and its use in tumour is a unit policy. A dural tear here opens the subarachnoid space into the pleural cavity.

  7. 7 Reconstruction and instrumentation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    An expandable cage or strut graft is seated between the endplates and distracted to restore height; a lateral plate or dual-rod construct locks it. The table flexion is taken out first so the cage is not placed into a spine bent away from it.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Unbreaking the table and expanding the cage both change cord tension, and potentials are repeated after each. Screws into vertebral bodies from the side point at the aorta or the opposite pleura; a sudden pressure fall with no visible bleeding in the field is the far side until proven otherwise. If cement is used to fill a defect it is hand-packed and low-volume, and the concern is heat and leak near the dura, not a cement reaction of the hip type (see bone cement).

  8. 8 Chest drain, regional catheter and closure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    One drain, sometimes two, is left for blood and air. The pleura over the spine is closed if it will come together. The ribs are approximated and the muscle layers repaired.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is when the analgesic plan is delivered. The paravertebral space at the operated levels has been opened by the pleural stripping, so a catheter threaded there leaks into the chest; ask the surgeon to tunnel it under intact pleura one or two spaces above the incision, or place an erector spinae plane catheter yourself. Re-expand the lung under vision and check for an air leak. If the dura was breached, the drain goes on water seal without suction, because negative pleural pressure maintains a cerebrospinal fluid fistula.

  9. 9 Extubation or a second stage

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The front of the spine is now stable in compression; the surgeon may add posterior screws the same day or a few days later.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    If the case stops here, most patients are extubated in theatre, sitting up, warm, with the block working and after a recruitment manoeuvre. Keep the tube in for facial or airway oedema after a long lateral case, a transfusion beyond half a blood volume, or marginal gas exchange on two lungs. If a prone stage follows, change a double-lumen tube for a single-lumen tube over an exchange catheter under vision, or withdraw the blocker, before the turn.

  10. 10 The first 48 hours

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants hourly leg power, the drain output charted, and the patient sitting out.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A high-dependency bed. The problems arrive in a predictable order: atelectasis and sputum retention on day 1-2 if analgesia is inadequate for coughing; drain output that is high and clear (cerebrospinal fluid) or milky after feeding (chyle from a thoracic duct injury); and new leg weakness, which is haematoma, cage displacement or cord ischaemia and goes back to the scanner. Whichever regional technique is running must allow that leg examination, which is the practical argument for a unilateral block over a dense epidural.

Pain plan

The mechanism is chest wall under continuous movement, so the plan is a continuous unilateral block with multimodal analgesia underneath it. For thoracotomy the Cochrane comparison of paravertebral block with thoracic epidural (14 trials, 698 patients) found the same analgesia at rest and on coughing, with far less hypotension, nausea, pruritus and urinary retention on the paravertebral side. For this operation there are two further reasons to prefer it, both author’s judgement: the surgeon needs an unclouded motor examination of the legs, and hypotension is the one thing the cord cannot afford. The defensible default is therefore a paravertebral or erector spinae plane catheter running ropivacaine 0.2% at 8-12 mL/h for 48-72 hours, in practice until the drain is out, with regular paracetamol, opioid patient-controlled analgesia, and a low-dose ketamine infusion for the opioid-tolerant.

For a thoracoscopic case the PROSPECT guideline for video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery is the nearest procedure-specific evidence: paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, a paravertebral or erector spinae plane block as first choice, opioid as rescue. A single shot is enough. NSAIDs need a conversation where a fusion is intended, and most surgeons accept a few days. Gabapentinoids have not been shown to prevent chronic post-thoracotomy pain and are not part of the default.

Rebound is high for a single shot after an open approach: the block recedes at 2 am with the drain in and the patient unable to cough, which is the reason the catheter is routine. Escalation: a catheter that is not working on day 1 is replaced or supplemented with an erector spinae plane block on the ward before the opioid dose climbs. Shoulder-tip pain is diaphragmatic irritation from the drain and responds to an NSAID, not to the block. Burning, band-like pain along a rib that persists beyond two weeks is intercostal neuralgia and wants a neuropathic agent and early referral.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Thoracoabdominal approach. The diaphragm is detached peripherally and repaired; it works poorly for days, atelectasis at the left base is the rule, and the retroperitoneal dissection adds ileus. The same catheter plan applies, sited at T8-T10.
  • Upper thoracic levels (T1-T4). Reached by a high thoracotomy with the scapula mobilised or by a sternal split; lung isolation is harder, and the great vessels and recurrent laryngeal nerve are in the field.
  • Monitoring and relaxant. If the surgeon does not monitor motor potentials (some do not for a simple anterior release) the anaesthetic is a conventional thoracic one with relaxant; if they do, it is a total intravenous anaesthetic and the lung isolation has to be reliable without paralysis.
  • The myelopathic cord. A patient with established myelopathy has a cord that is already ischaemic; induction hypotension counts, so the arterial line goes in awake.
  • Tumour and infection. Vascular metastases (renal, thyroid) bleed heavily from the body and may be embolised beforehand; a tuberculous or pyogenic body is friable and the patient catabolic. See spinal metastasis decompression and spinal infection.
  • Re-expansion pulmonary oedema after a long collapse is uncommon and presents in recovery as frothy sputum and falling saturation on the operated side.
  • Chylothorax and cerebrospinal fluid-pleural fistula. Both present as a drain that will not dry up; neither is an analgesic problem, and both keep the drain, and therefore the catheter, in for longer.
  • Same-day anterior and posterior surgery. Blood loss, fluid shifts and airway oedema add up; decide before the turn whether the patient will be extubated at the end, not after.

Key sources

The complication figures come from a network meta-analysis of observational series of thoracic disc surgery, and the lung function data from adolescents with scoliosis, so both are extrapolated to the adult corpectomy. The regional evidence is from lung surgery: the paravertebral against epidural comparison is a Cochrane review of thoracotomy for pulmonary resection, and PROSPECT’s guideline is for thoracoscopic lung surgery. No trial has compared regional techniques in transthoracic spine surgery, and the preference for a unilateral technique on the grounds of the leg examination and cord perfusion is the author’s reasoning. The mean pressure target of 80 mmHg is conventional and untested.
