21 Primary total hip arthroplasty: posterior approach

Posterolateral approach in lateral decubitus; cemented, uncemented and hybrid fixation

2Mild to moderate

Dominant mechanism: Bone. Acetabular reaming and femoral broaching; a capsulotomy and short external rotator release repaired without tension; gluteus maximus split along its fibres rather than cut; the bone-on-bone pain generator is removed and nothing is left under load

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: low.

Spinal anaesthesia with surgeon-delivered periarticular infiltration; single-shot PENG or supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block when infiltration is not on offer or the patient predicts a bad course

Alternatives: Supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block, about 40 mL, single shot (PROSPECT first line); PENG block, single shot, motor sparing and equivalent to supra-inguinal fascia iliaca in the pooled trials; General anaesthesia with a block when the spinal is refused or impossible
Position
Lateral decubitus, operative side up, pelvis held by clamps at the pubis and sacrum, dependent leg flexed and padded, operative leg draped free so it can be flexed, adducted and internally rotated to dislocate

No

What the surgeon is doing and why

Osteoarthritis of the hip is a ball and socket that have worn through their cartilage and started to deform each other. The femoral head flattens, grows osteophytes and migrates superiorly; the acetabulum goes sclerotic and cystic beneath it; the capsule thickens and shortens, so the leg sits short, adducted and externally rotated, and the pain is bone grinding on bone with every step and every turn in bed. The joint bears weight, so unlike the shoulder there is no option of leaving the socket alone: both surfaces are replaced and the implant takes full body weight from the first afternoon. That fact sets the whole plan. The surgeon needs a cup that is immediately stable in the pelvis, a stem that is immediately stable in the femur, a head that will not come out when the patient sits on a toilet, and a leg the same length as the other one.

The posterior approach is the commonest way in worldwide because it is extensile, spares the abductors entirely and gives the best view of the femur. The surgeon:

  1. Splits gluteus maximus along its fibres (no muscle is cut) and finds the short external rotators (piriformis, the gemelli, obturator internus and the upper part of quadratus femoris) lying over the back of the joint, with the sciatic nerve a centimetre or two behind them.
  2. Takes the short external rotators and the posterior capsule off the femur as a flap, tagged with sutures so they can be put back. This is the step that gives the posterior approach its reputation for dislocation, and the step whose repair has largely removed it.
  3. Dislocates the hip posteriorly by flexion, adduction and internal rotation, cuts the femoral neck, and removes the head.
  4. Reams the acetabulum to a bleeding hemisphere, corrects the version and inclination, and press-fits a cup one or two millimetres larger than the last reamer; a few surgeons still cement a polyethylene cup in very old bone.
  5. Opens the femoral canal, broaches it to size, and either press-fits a stem that will osseointegrate over weeks or cements a polished or collared stem in a column of pressurised cement.
  6. Trials the head and neck length until the hip is stable through a range of positions and the leg length and offset match the other side, then seats the definitive head and reduces.
  7. Repairs the capsule and the short external rotators back to the greater trochanter and closes the gluteus maximus split.

The anaesthetic reading of that sequence: the two bone steps, reaming and broaching, are the pain generators, and they are moderate and short. No muscle is cut, so there is no muscle mechanism of consequence. The only repaired structures are a thin capsule and small rotators, sutured without tension and not loaded by walking, so the tendon-tension mechanism that dominates the shoulder cards is absent. And the pre-operative pain generator is gone: a patient who has had bone-on-bone pain for two years is, by the end of the first week, better than before surgery. That is why primary hip arthroplasty sits low in the pain ranking despite being a large operation; in the 179-procedure cohort it is not among the 40 highest-scoring operations, in a table led by open calcaneal reduction, spinal fusion and foot arthrodesis. With a spinal and infiltration in place it is a 2 on the Part B scale: regular paracetamol and an NSAID, a few opioid doses on day 0 and 1, and a patient who copes without a catheter.

Two of the surgeon’s decisions are mostly about you. The stem decision (cemented or press-fit) is made on bone quality and age, and it decides whether the case has a bone cement implantation syndrome step. And the leg length and stability trialling decides how many times the hip is dislocated and reduced with the pelvis clamped on its side, which is the moment the operation moves the patient most.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Spinal, sedation and the decision about general anaesthesia

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs a motionless, relaxed patient for 60-120 minutes, a lax hip for the dislocation and the trial reductions, and a patient who can stand that afternoon.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Neuraxial anaesthesia is the ICAROS group’s strong recommendation for hip arthroplasty, on 94 mostly observational studies in which spinal or epidural anaesthesia carried lower odds of death (OR 0.67), pulmonary complications, acute kidney injury, deep vein thrombosis, infection and transfusion than general anaesthesia, with more urinary retention. The evidence is moderate to low quality and no large randomised trial settles it, so a spinal is the defensible default rather than a proven life-saver. A plain or short-acting spinal sized to the case with propofol sedation is the usual technique; intrathecal morphine up to 0.1 mg is a PROSPECT option for inpatients and is left out on a day-case pathway because nausea and pruritus delay discharge more than the analgesia advances it. General anaesthesia with a tracheal tube is the answer when the spinal is refused, the spine is fused or infected, or the patient is anticoagulated; it brings end-tidal carbon dioxide monitoring, the best early warning of a cement event. Decide the airway now: turning a patient lateral on a supraglottic device and then needing a tube is a bad position to be in.

  2. 2 Lateral decubitus positioning

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Side-lying with the pelvis clamped gives the surgeon a stable pelvis to ream against and lets the leg be flexed across the table to dislocate the hip. The clamps must hold the pelvis square, because the cup is placed by eye against the floor and the table, and a pelvis that has rolled forward puts it in the wrong version.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Dependent arm on a board with the axilla free, upper arm supported at shoulder height, head on a pillow that keeps the neck neutral, dependent eye, ear and peroneal nerve checked, legs separated by a pillow. The anterior pelvic clamp sits on the pubis and the posterior on the sacrum; in a slim patient the anterior clamp can press on the femoral vessels and nerve, and in any patient it compresses the abdomen enough to raise the diaphragm. Under sedation with a spinal this is tolerable; under general anaesthesia the dependent lung is underventilated and overperfused, so expect a lower saturation than supine. A hyperbaric spinal followed by an immediate turn onto the non-operative side fixes on the wrong side, so give it a few minutes to set before the turn. The pressure falls as the patient goes over and the clamps come on; have the vasopressor infusion running before that rather than reaching for the metaraminol afterwards.

  3. 3 Antibiotic, tranexamic acid and the baseline

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A joint replacement is a large implant in a dead space; infection is the complication the surgeon fears most and the one that produces the revision card. Blood in the field and in the thigh afterwards is what tranexamic acid prevents.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cefazolin 2 g IV finished before the knife (3 g over 120 kg); antibiotic-loaded cement in a cemented stem is an addition, not a substitute. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV before incision: in the 34-trial network meta-analysis intravenous, topical and oral routes all cut blood loss and transfusion, no route is better, and a second dose, a larger dose or a different time add nothing measurable. Record the pre-induction pressure, because the AAGBI cement target is a systolic within 20% of it.

  4. 4 Gluteus maximus split, short external rotator release and capsulotomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A curved incision over the posterior trochanter, fascia lata opened, gluteus maximus split bluntly along its fibres: no muscle is divided and the abductors are never touched, which is why this approach does not limp. Piriformis and the conjoint tendon are then taken off the posterior femoral neck as one flap with the capsule beneath them, tagged for repair; repairing that layer at the end is the intervention that cut the posterior approach’s dislocation rate from about 4.5% to under 1% in the pooled series. Quadratus femoris is left if it can be, because the medial femoral circumflex artery runs under it and bleeds hard when cut.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The first bleeding is diffuse, from the gluteal split, and from here the loss runs at a steady rate rather than in events. The sciatic nerve lies a centimetre or two behind the rotators and the surgeon works beside it for the next hour; the leg is moved by the surgical team and nobody else. The capsulotomy is the first moment of real nociception: nothing happens under a working spinal, and the heart rate climbs under a general anaesthetic without a block. A sudden pool of dark blood and a call for more suction means the surgeon has gone through quadratus femoris into the circumflex vessels; the loss is a few hundred millilitres over a minute, not a crisis.

  5. 5 Dislocation and femoral neck osteotomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    With the leg flexed, adducted and internally rotated the head is levered out of the socket backwards. The neck is cut at the level planned on the template, which is what sets the leg length and offset later, and the head is removed and measured.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The leg is forced across the table against a clamped pelvis; a frail osteoporotic femur fractures here if the hip is stiff and the surgeon impatient, and an ankylosed or protrusio hip may need the neck cut in situ before it will come out. The pressure often dips at the moment of dislocation as the patient’s mass shifts against the clamps. The femoral vein is kinked from now until reduction, which is the mechanical half of the venous thromboembolism story; the spinal’s vasodilatation is half of the protection.

  6. 6 Acetabular exposure and reaming

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The labrum and remaining capsule are excised, osteophytes cleared to find the true floor and rim, and hemispherical reamers of increasing size are run until the whole socket is bleeding cancellous bone at the planned inclination (about 40 degrees) and anteversion (15-20 degrees). The cup goes in one or two millimetres larger than the last reamer, driven home with a mallet, with screws if the fit is doubtful.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Reaming a hemisphere of cancellous bone is the largest single bleed of the case and it continues until the cup seals it; it is also the first of the two bone pain mechanisms. Cup impaction transmits through the pelvis to the clamps and the table, so the whole patient moves, and the head and dependent arm are checked afterwards. A drill or screw through the medial wall can injure the external iliac or obturator vessels: a pressure that falls and keeps falling after the cup is in, with no bleeding in the field, is that until proven otherwise. Hypotension during reaming under a spinal is more often the spinal and the position than blood; a haemoglobin on a gas tells you which.

  7. 7 Femoral preparation: broaching, and the cemented or uncemented decision

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The femoral canal is opened at the piriformis fossa and broaches of increasing size are hammered down it until one is rotationally stable. For an uncemented stem the last broach is the size of the implant and the stem is press-fit into cortical contact. For a cemented stem the canal is broached a size larger, washed with pulsed lavage, dried, plugged distally and filled retrograde from a gun before the stem is pushed in.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Each blow of the broach vents fat, marrow and air into the femoral veins and produces the transient dips in saturation and pressure that pass unnoticed in a well patient. In an uncemented hip this is the only canal event and it is over in ten minutes. In a cemented hip the dangerous step is next, and this is the moment to prepare for it, not when the gun is loaded.

  8. 8 Cementing the stem: bone cement implantation syndrome

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Polished and collared cemented stems rely on a pressurised cement mantle for immediate fixation, so the surgeon pressurises the cement in the canal deliberately and then drives the stem in against it. That pressurisation is what pushes marrow fat and debris into the venous sinusoids and, from there, into the right heart and lungs.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The Donaldson classification is the common language: grade 1 is a saturation below 94% or a systolic fall of more than 20%; grade 2 is a saturation below 88%, a systolic fall of more than 40% or unexpected loss of consciousness; grade 3 is cardiovascular collapse needing resuscitation. In elective cemented hips the pooled intra-operative mortality is about 1 in 1,000, far below the hip fracture figures, but the mechanism is the same and the elderly patient with cardiopulmonary disease on a diuretic is in the risk group the AAGBI hip fracture guidance identifies. That guidance was written for hemiarthroplasty and applies unchanged here: the surgeon tells you before the cement is mixed, washes and dries the canal, vents it, uses a gun retrograde on a plug and avoids heavy pressurisation in the high-risk patient; you confirm that you know cement is coming, have the pressure within 20% of the pre-induction baseline already, have metaraminol or adrenaline drawn up, run the cuff on its fastest cycle or an arterial line in the high-risk patient, and watch the end-tidal carbon dioxide if the patient is ventilated, because a sudden fall is the right ventricle failing against an embolised pulmonary bed. Treat it as acute right heart failure: oxygen, vasopressor to keep the right ventricle perfused, fluid with judgement, adrenaline early if the pressure does not respond, resuscitation for grade 3. The worst of it is in the minutes after the stem goes in and again at reduction, and the vigilance runs through both.

  9. 9 Trial reduction, leg length and stability

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A trial head goes on, the hip is reduced and tested: flexion with adduction and internal rotation (the toilet-seat position), extension with external rotation, and a shuck test for soft tissue tension. Leg length is checked against a mark on the pelvis or by palpating the knees and heels together. A different neck length, offset or a lipped liner is chosen and the trial repeated until the hip is stable and the legs match.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Each reduction is a hard pull on the leg against the clamps; the pressure dips with it and recovers. A patient under a light general anaesthetic or a thin spinal tenses the abductors, the surgeon reads the hip as tight when it is not, and the wrong neck length goes in. A request for relaxation here means neuromuscular block or depth under general anaesthesia, and more sedation and time under a spinal, because the block cannot be deepened. The surgeon is trading stability against length, and in a stiff or dysplastic hip the two conflict; a leg lengthened by more than a couple of centimetres puts the sciatic nerve on stretch, and that is the foot drop that presents to you first.

  10. 10 Definitive head, reduction, capsular and rotator repair

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The definitive ceramic or metal head is impacted onto the trunnion and the hip reduced for the last time. The posterior capsule and short external rotators are sutured back to the trochanter through drill holes or to the tendon stump: the repair that makes the posterior approach safe. Gluteus maximus and fascia lata are closed; most surgeons have given up drains.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The final reduction is the second embolic moment in a cemented hip, as the femoral vein unkinks and releases what was dammed in it. The rotator repair needs the leg held in slight external rotation and the patient still. Give the antiemetic before closure. From here the day is decided by the spinal receding on time and the first oral doses going in before it does.

  11. 11 Recovery, mobilisation and the day-case pathway

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The implant is stable from the moment of reduction; the surgeon wants the patient standing the same day, walking the next morning, and the posterior repair protected from deep flexion with adduction and internal rotation for a few weeks. Many surgeons with a confident repair have dropped formal precautions altogether.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Standing on the afternoon of surgery needs a spinal that has gone, a leg that bears weight, a pressure that survives standing and a bladder that has emptied; that is the argument against intrathecal morphine, heavy spinals, lumbar plexus and femoral nerve blocks here, and for PENG or supra-inguinal fascia iliaca if a block is used at all. Same-day discharge is reasonable under 80 years, ASA 1-2, Timed Up and Go of 10 seconds or less, BMI under 35 and someone at home; in the randomised and matched cohorts that pathway shows no excess reoperation, readmission or emergency visits, and it fails on hypotension, pain and nausea in the first six hours. The inpatient stays one to three nights. A foot drop in recovery is a sciatic nerve stretched by lengthening or a retractor and the surgeon sees it now; a tense swollen thigh with a falling haemoglobin on day 1 is bleeding into the compartment, which a drain does not reliably reveal.

Pain plan

The mechanism is two bone surfaces and a repaired capsule under no tension, in a patient whose pre-operative pain is gone. The plan does not need to carry anyone through a week; it needs to cover the first two nights and start the oral regimen before the spinal recedes. PROSPECT’s basic regimen for everyone is paracetamol with an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started before or during surgery and a single intravenous dexamethasone dose up to 10 mg; opioids are for rescue.

The block question has an evidence answer and a practical answer, and they differ. The 2026 PROSPECT update recommends a single-shot supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block (about 40 mL) or a PENG block before surgery, finds the two comparable, and places local infiltration below them: it may be considered when a block is not feasible, its benefit is transient and modest, and combining it with a block is not supported. The author’s default for the posterior approach under a spinal differs: surgeon-delivered periarticular infiltration with a dilute long-acting local anaesthetic, no block, and PENG or supra-inguinal fascia iliaca reserved for the patient whose surgeon does not infiltrate, who is having a general anaesthetic, or who predicts a bad course (chronic pain, opioid tolerance, a previous bad arthroplasty night). The reasoning: the spinal already covers the operation and the first hours; in the one randomised comparison, adding a PENG block to infiltration changed neither day 1 opioid use nor pain scores; and on a day-case pathway the only thing a block can add is a reason the leg will not hold the patient at four o’clock. That is a judgement, not a guideline, and a unit whose surgeons do not infiltrate should follow PROSPECT and block. Lumbar plexus, femoral nerve, quadratus lumborum and erector spinae blocks are not recommended or unsupported in the update, and none fits the mechanism.

No catheter. An epidural or a continuous lumbar plexus technique brings motor block, hypotension, urinary retention and a delayed stand to an operation whose pain is a 2; they belong on the revision card, where the mechanism earns them.

Rebound is low because nothing dense wears off suddenly: the spinal recedes over an afternoon with the infiltration behind it, and the first paracetamol and NSAID doses go in before that. Escalation triggers: day 1 pain out of proportion, in the thigh rather than the buttock, with a tense swollen thigh, is a haematoma; new sharp pain on standing with a short externally rotated leg is a dislocation or a periprosthetic fracture and needs a radiograph before any analgesic decision; buttock pain with numbness down the leg and a foot that will not dorsiflex is the sciatic nerve. None of these is treated with more opioid.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Cemented versus uncemented. The cemented stem is the safer choice in old osteoporotic bone and the only BCIS step; the uncemented stem has no cement event but more intra-operative periprosthetic fractures in the same bone. Surgeons vary by age cut-off and training; find out which you have before the femur is opened, not when the gun appears.
  • Hybrid fixation. An uncemented cup with a cemented stem; the cement precautions apply in full.
  • The frail elderly patient. The ICAROS mortality signal for neuraxial anaesthesia is strongest here, the BCIS risk factors (age, cardiopulmonary disease, diuretics, male sex) cluster here, and an arterial line before the cement is the AAGBI’s explicit advice for the higher-risk patient.
  • Dysplastic and protrusio hips. The head may be cut in situ, the cup may need a graft or a high centre, the leg may be lengthened beyond what the sciatic nerve tolerates, and the case runs long; the periacetabular osteotomy and revision cards cover the longer dissection.
  • Ankylosing spondylitis and the fused spine. A spinal may be impossible and the airway difficult; plan the general anaesthetic and the block in advance, not after a failed needle.
  • Anticoagulation. The timing rules are the unit’s; the alternative is a general anaesthetic with a block, not a spinal placed in a hurry.
  • Obesity. The lateral position, the clamps and the depth of the wound cost time, blood and exposure; the cup is harder to orient; the sedated patient breathing spontaneously on their side desaturates.
  • Dislocation precautions. With a repaired posterior capsule and a large head many surgeons impose none; others still ask for no flexion past 90 degrees with adduction and internal rotation for six weeks. Ask what the surgeon wants on the ward instructions, because the physiotherapist will.
  • The direct anterior and direct lateral approaches change the position, the nerve at risk and the muscle pain: see anterior and lateral approaches.
  • The hip fracture patient having a total hip shares every cement and frailty hazard with the elective patient and adds the fracture pathway’s timing: see neck of femur.

Key sources

Primary hip arthroplasty is one of the best-evidenced operations in this book: a PROSPECT guideline updated in 2026, a neuraxial-versus-general consensus built on 94 studies, a network meta-analysis of tranexamic acid, the Donaldson classification and the AAGBI guidance that operationalised it. The weak points are that the neuraxial evidence is observational and the block-versus-infiltration trials are small; the card’s preference for infiltration under a spinal over a routine block is the author’s reconciliation of the PROSPECT recommendation with one negative randomised trial and the demands of same-day mobilisation, and it is labelled as such.
