41 Tibial shaft nailing and tibial plateau fixation

Infrapatellar and suprapatellar intramedullary nailing of the tibial shaft; open reduction and internal fixation of tibial plateau fractures, staged with a spanning external fixator for high-energy patterns

4Severe

Dominant mechanism: Bone. A reamed tibial canal with an entry through the patellar tendon or the knee (nail), or a crushed articular surface elevated, grafted and plated under periosteal stripping on one or both sides of the proximal tibia (plateau), in a limb whose soft-tissue envelope is thin, bruised and swollen; plus the tourniquet and the raw bone defect that a bone graft fills. The compartment syndrome risk shapes the block, not the pain

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Block
Single-shot popliteal sciatic and adductor canal (or femoral) blocks with dilute local anaesthetic and no adjuncts, placed after a documented compartment examination and with a surveillance plan the surgeon has agreed; dense long-acting blocks and catheters avoided in the fresh high-energy fracture
Catheter: selective Avoided in the fresh high-energy shaft fracture and the fracture-dislocation plateau in the first 48 h, where the compartment syndrome risk is highest; reasonable for the delayed definitive plateau fixation at 7-14 days with a fixator already on, when the swelling has settled and the window for compartment syndrome has closed
Alternatives: Spinal anaesthesia for the isolated shaft nail in a stable fasted patient, with a short-acting agent so the leg can be examined by the evening; General anaesthesia with multimodal analgesia and ketamine, no block, for the fracture-dislocation plateau in the first 48 h; Surgeon's local infiltration at the entry point and plate incisions
Position
Supine on a radiolucent table; knee flexed over a triangle or bolster for infrapatellar nailing, semi-extended over a small bump for suprapatellar nailing; supine with a bump under the hip and the knee flexed for lateral plateau plating; the leg often brought over the side or a posteromedial approach done with the hip externally rotated
Tourniquet
Sometimes. Not used for nailing (reaming needs the bleeding bone to be seen and a thigh tourniquet obstructs the entry point); commonly used for plateau ORIF at 250-300 mmHg for up to 120 min, deflated before closure in a limb whose circulation is already in question
Cement
No
Blood loss
Shaft nailing 200-400 mL; plateau ORIF 300-600 mL under tourniquet and more once it is released, with a bicondylar dual-incision case bleeding across a long operation. The fracture itself has lost 500 mL or more into the leg
Duration
Shaft nailing 60-120 min; lateral plateau ORIF 90-150 min; bicondylar plateau with dual incisions and bone graft 180-300 min
Length of stay
2-5 nights for a shaft nail; the staged plateau has two admissions or one long one, with the definitive fixation at 7-14 days and a stay of 3-7 nights after it. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV within 60 min of incision (3 g over 120 kg); open fractures were due their antibiotic within an hour of injury and follow the open fracture plan
Tranexamic acid
Tranexamic acid 1 g IV at induction for plateau ORIF; reasonable for nailing
VTE
Low molecular weight heparin from the night of surgery, continued through the non-weight-bearing period for a plateau fracture (6-12 weeks); mechanical prophylaxis on the other leg
Antiemetic
Dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction (it also prolongs whatever block is used, so it is withheld when a short, examinable block is the aim) and ondansetron 4 mg IV
Tell the patient
The first two days after a tibial operation are the hard ones, because the leg swells inside a tight skin; the pain then settles over a week, and the knee stays sore at the front for a long time after a nail, which is normal and not a sign of anything wrong.
The trap
Compartment syndrome is the complication of this bone, it declares itself in the first 48 h, and the thing that hides it is a dense long-acting block that nobody planned a surveillance for: the current UK guidance is that dilute single-shot blocks without adjuncts do not delay the diagnosis when the observation is competent, and that a block placed without that observation does

What the surgeon is doing and why

The tibia is the bone with the worst soft-tissue envelope in the body: a subcutaneous anteromedial border, a thin skin over it with a poor blood supply, and four muscle compartments bound by unyielding fascia behind and lateral to it. Everything that is difficult about tibial fracture surgery follows from that. A shaft fracture bleeds into compartments that cannot expand, so compartment syndrome is the complication of this bone and not of the femur. A plateau fracture crushes the articular surface of the knee and swells the skin over it until the surgeon cannot close an incision, so high-energy plateau fractures are fixed in two stages a fortnight apart. The anaesthetic decisions on this card (the block, the tourniquet, the timing) are all soft-tissue and compartment decisions before they are pain decisions.

Shaft nailing. The nail is a reamed, locked intramedullary splint, as for the femur, inserted from the top of the tibia. The infrapatellar approach flexes the knee to 90 degrees over a bolster and splits or retracts the patellar tendon to reach the entry point; the fracture is reduced by hand against the flexed knee, which is awkward for proximal fractures. The suprapatellar approach nails the tibia semi-extended through a small incision above the patella and a cannula through the patellofemoral joint, which holds the fracture reduced by gravity and gives better alignment (4% against 26% malalignment in the systematic review) at the cost of instruments through the articular cartilage. Anterior knee pain is the signature complaint after tibial nailing whichever route is used, 29% after suprapatellar and 39% after infrapatellar in the pooled studies, not significantly different; it is attributed to the tendon split, the fat pad, the nail tip and the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve, and it lasts for months.

Plateau fixation. The Schatzker classification sorts plateau fractures by energy and side: I-III are lateral split, split-depression and pure depression fractures from a valgus force; IV is the medial plateau, a high-energy injury that is really a fracture-dislocation of the knee with a popliteal artery and a peroneal nerve at risk; V and VI are bicondylar, with VI dissociating the metaphysis from the shaft. The low-energy lateral fracture is fixed in one sitting through a lateral incision: the depressed joint surface is elevated through a cortical window, the void beneath it filled with bone graft or substitute, and a lateral plate buttresses the split. The high-energy fracture arrives with a leg too swollen to open: the surgeon spans the knee with an external fixator on day 0, does a fasciotomy if the compartments are tight, and returns at 7-14 days when the skin wrinkles again to plate both columns through lateral and posteromedial incisions. In the series of 67 high-energy plateaus spanned within 48 h, compartment syndrome occurred in 27%, and in 53% of medial fracture-dislocations; ten cases declared after the frame was on, and three were diagnosed late. Wound breakdown and infection after extensive stripping run at around 20% in the older series, which is the reason for the staging.

The pain logic is bone with two amplifiers. The reamed canal and the entry point (nail), or the elevated, grafted and plated metaphysis (plateau), hurt as bone does for two or three days, then fall. The amplifiers are the tourniquet for a long plateau fixation and the soft-tissue envelope: the leg swells inside skin that cannot stretch, and movement-evoked pain limits the early knee range a plateau fixation needs. The score is 4: the first 48 h need a robust plan, rebound after a single-shot block is high because the bone and the swelling are both at their worst when it wears off, and the plateau patient has a knee that must bend while non-weight-bearing for six to twelve weeks. A catheter would be the obvious answer and is exactly what the compartment syndrome risk argues against in the first two days, which is the tension this card is about.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Emergency department: splint, compartments, block decision

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the fracture splinted, the skin and compartments examined and documented, a CT for any plateau fracture, an ankle-brachial index or CT angiogram for a medial plateau or a knee that looks dislocated, and the staging decision made.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The compartment examination before any block is the baseline against which the next 48 h are read: pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch of the toes, a tense compartment, and later paraesthesia; the pressure measurement (a differential of diastolic minus compartment pressure below 30 mmHg is McQueen’s threshold for fasciotomy) belongs to the surgeon. The block decision follows the Association of Anaesthetists’ 2021 guideline: a single-shot peripheral block with a lower concentration of local anaesthetic and no adjuncts is not associated with delayed diagnosis provided surveillance is competent; a dense, long-acting block or a neuraxial technique that lasts well beyond the operation is to be avoided; the patient is told and consents; and neither surgeon nor anaesthetist can veto the other. The retrospective series of 791 tibial fixations found compartment syndrome in 1.6% of blocked and 4.5% of unblocked patients and no excess of missed cases with blocks, with the caveat that all four missed cases in the blocked group had catheters.

  2. 2 Induction and the unfasted, swollen leg

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A closed shaft fracture is nailed within 24 h in a stable patient; a high-energy plateau is spanned the same day and fixed a fortnight later; a low-energy plateau waits for the swelling to settle if the skin is in doubt.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The acute case is a rapid sequence induction counted from the time of injury. A spinal is reasonable for the isolated shaft nail in a stable, fasted patient if a short-acting agent is used so the leg can be examined that evening; it is the wrong technique for a fracture-dislocation plateau in the first 48 h. The delayed definitive plateau fixation with a frame on is an elective anaesthetic with a block or a catheter that the compartment window no longer argues against. Cefazolin 2 g IV; dexamethasone withheld when the aim is a short, examinable block.

  3. 3 Positioning and the tourniquet

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Nailing: supine, knee flexed over a triangle (infrapatellar) or semi-extended over a bump (suprapatellar), the C-arm from the opposite side, no tourniquet because the reamed bone must bleed and a thigh cuff obstructs the entry. Plateau: supine with a bump under the ipsilateral hip and the knee flexed for the lateral approach; the posteromedial approach needs the hip externally rotated and the leg figure-of-four; a thigh tourniquet at 250-300 mmHg is common.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The tourniquet on a plateau fixation runs long, often to its 120-min limit before the surgeon asks for a deflate-and-reinflate, and it is applied to a limb whose circulation is already in doubt; see the tourniquet. A plateau fracture-dislocation with a vascular injury does not get a tourniquet. The peroneal nerve at the fibular neck is the nerve at risk from positioning and from the lateral plate, and a foot drop after surgery is attributed to the block until someone checks the timeline; document the foot before the block.

  4. 4 Nailing: entry point and reaming

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Infrapatellar: a 3-cm incision over the patellar tendon, the tendon split or retracted, an awl or guidewire into the anterior plateau just medial to the lateral tibial spine, and the canal opened. Suprapatellar: a 3-cm incision above the patella, a protective cannula through the patellofemoral joint, and the same entry point reached with the knee at 20 degrees. Flexible reamers are passed over a guidewire across the reduced fracture to 1-1.5 mm over the nail diameter.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Reaming the tibia embolises marrow as the femur does, with a smaller load. The thing that matters at this step is the compartment pressure: reaming raises it, the fracture has already bled into the deep posterior compartment, and a leg that is tense at the end of a nailing is one the surgeon should feel before the drapes come off. The tendon split and the entry through the fat pad are the anterior knee pain that follows.

  5. 5 Nailing: nail insertion, locking and the compartment check

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The nail is driven over the guidewire, locked proximally through a jig and distally freehand under imaging, the fracture compressed by backslapping, and alignment and rotation checked against the other leg.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Freehand distal locking is 10-15 min of fluoroscopy with a still leg. At the end of the case, before the patient wakes, the surgeon examines the compartments, and some will measure pressures in a high-energy fracture; this is the moment the first 48 h of surveillance starts from. A patient waking with a working dilute block will not report pain out of proportion that evening, so the ward observations have to be the palpation, the passive stretch of the toes and the pressure, on a chart, by someone trained to read it.

  6. 6 Plateau: the spanning external fixator

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    For the high-energy fracture, two pins in the femur and two in the tibial shaft, connected by bars across the knee, restore length and alignment by ligamentotaxis and hold the leg still while the swelling settles. A fasciotomy is done at the same sitting if the compartments are tight, and in the medial fracture-dislocation many surgeons release them prophylactically.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A short general anaesthetic, 30-45 min, often at night, in a patient who may have other injuries. No block that lasts beyond the operation: compartment syndrome in this group declares itself in the next 48 h in up to half of medial fracture-dislocations and a fifth of bicondylar fractures, and the frame holding the foot in neutral makes the examination harder, which is how the late diagnoses in the series happened. Analgesia for the first two days is opioid-based and multimodal, with ketamine, and the pain score that matters is the one that rises despite it.

  7. 7 Plateau: the wait and the wrinkle sign

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon waits 7-14 days until the skin over the proximal tibia wrinkles when pinched, the fracture blisters have epithelialised and the swelling has gone; opening earlier costs a wound breakdown and a deep infection in a joint with a plate in it.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The patient is on the ward in a frame, immobile, on opioids; this is the window for VTE, for pin-site infection and for the delirium of an older patient. The definitive operation is then elective in all but name, and the compartment window has closed: a catheter is reasonable for the definitive fixation and the author’s preference for a bicondylar case.

  8. 8 Plateau: approach, elevation of the joint surface and bone graft

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Through an anterolateral incision the surgeon lifts the anterior compartment off the lateral plateau, opens the submeniscal joint, elevates the depressed articular fragments from below through a cortical window with a bone tamp under fluoroscopy, and packs the void with autograft from the iliac crest, allograft or a calcium phosphate substitute. A bicondylar fracture adds a posteromedial incision between the pes anserinus and the medial gastrocnemius to buttress the medial column.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the long step under tourniquet. An iliac crest graft adds a second painful wound that outlasts the knee, and the surgeon should say in advance whether one is planned, because it changes the block (a fascia iliaca block or infiltration for the crest) and the first week’s pain. The periosteal stripping on two sides of the tibia is the periosteum mechanism, raw and incisional, and the reason the dual-incision plateau sits at the top of this card’s range.

  9. 9 Plateau: plating and tourniquet release

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A lateral locking plate buttresses the lateral column with rafting screws under the elevated joint surface; a medial or posteromedial plate holds the medial column; the construct is checked under imaging and the knee taken through a range to confirm stability.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Tourniquet release after 90-120 min is the reperfusion moment: the fall in pressure, the rise in end-tidal carbon dioxide and potassium, the fall in temperature, and bleeding from bone that was dry a minute ago; the haemoglobin after a bicondylar fixation is lower than the field suggested. Reinflation for closure is a request to refuse in a leg with a compromised envelope.

  10. 10 Closure, splint and the first 48 h

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Layered closure over the plate without tension, sometimes a drain, a bulky dressing and a hinged brace or a splint; the surgeon wants early passive knee range from day 1 and no weight-bearing for 6-12 weeks.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    For the nail, the compartment surveillance runs for 48 h on a chart; the regular regimen starts in recovery and the opioid is given regularly rather than on request. For the plateau, the first two nights are the worst of this card: the leg is swollen in a dressing, the bone defect under the graft aches, and the brace has to move. Rebound after a dilute single-shot block is high and arrives in the small hours; the plan for it is the opioid already charted and a nurse who knows the difference between rebound pain and pain out of proportion. Low molecular weight heparin from the night of surgery.

  11. 11 Day 1 onwards

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Physiotherapy starts the knee moving on day 1 for a plateau (the joint surface is held by the rafting screws and stiffness is the enemy) and the patient walking non-weight-bearing on crutches; the nail patient weight-bears as tolerated from day 1.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Movement-evoked pain limits the physiotherapy on a plateau and is the argument for a catheter once the compartment window has closed; a rescue block on day 2 is reasonable in a patient whose compartments have been examined and recorded as soft. Anterior knee pain after a nail is a weeks-to-months complaint and not an acute problem. The leg that is more painful on day 2 than day 1, with a tense calf, has a compartment syndrome until someone measures the pressure.

Pain plan

The mechanism is bone with a swollen, inelastic envelope and a tourniquet, so the plan is multimodal from the trauma bay with a regional technique chosen for the compartment risk rather than for the pain alone. There is no PROSPECT guideline for tibial fracture surgery; the structure is the author’s, built on the Association of Anaesthetists’ 2021 guideline for lower leg trauma and the regional anaesthesia literature for knee and ankle surgery.

The block: for the shaft nail and the low-energy plateau, a single-shot popliteal sciatic block and an adductor canal block (or a femoral block for the plateau, where the anterolateral incision crosses the femoral territory) with a dilute local anaesthetic and no perineural or intravenous adjunct, placed after a documented compartment examination and with a surveillance chart the surgeon has agreed. The retrospective evidence is that blocks placed this way do not hide compartment syndrome and reduce opioid use; the case reports that frightened a generation of surgeons involved dense blocks and no surveillance. For the high-energy shaft fracture with a tense leg at presentation and the medial plateau fracture-dislocation in the first 48 h, no block that outlasts the operation, and a multimodal general anaesthetic with ketamine as the analgesic backbone; this is the author’s judgement and the surgeon’s, agreed before the case, because the guideline says neither can veto the other and the only way to honour that is to decide together.

The catheter: no, in the first 48 h of a fresh high-energy fracture, because every one of the missed compartment syndromes in the blocked group of the large series had a catheter. Yes, selectively, for the delayed definitive plateau fixation at 7-14 days when the window has closed and the operation is long and movement-evoked pain will limit the knee; a popliteal sciatic catheter with a dilute infusion and the compartments still examined daily.

The oral regimen shape: regular paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor for the first week unless the surgeon withholds it for union, a regular opioid for the first 48-72 h and then as needed, ketamine at low dose for the unblocked patient, and the night plan written for the rebound. Rebound is high: when a dilute block recedes on the first night the bone and the swelling are both at their peak, and the pain is severe for an hour or two until the regular regimen catches up. Escalation: pain that is out of proportion to the operation, pain on passive stretch of the toes, a tense compartment or rising opioid requirement in the first 48 h is a compartment syndrome until the pressure says otherwise; the response is the surgeon and a pressure measurement, not more opioid and not a rescue block.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The medial plateau fracture-dislocation. A knee dislocation that reduced itself: a popliteal artery at risk, a peroneal nerve palsy, compartment syndrome in half, and a surgeon who spans and fasciotomises on day 0. No block, an arterial line, and a vascular surgeon’s number.
  • The open tibial fracture. The commonest open long bone fracture, because the bone is subcutaneous; antibiotics within an hour, debridement, a nail for most Gustilo I-IIIA fractures or a fixator for IIIB and vascular injuries, and the open fracture card.
  • Suprapatellar nailing. Semi-extended, better alignment, instruments through the patellofemoral joint; the same anterior knee pain in the pooled data. A bump under the knee rather than a triangle, and the C-arm comes in lower.
  • The proximal third shaft fracture. Hard to align with an infrapatellar nail (the fragment flexes); many surgeons plate it or use the suprapatellar route. A plated proximal tibia is a bigger soft-tissue dissection and a tourniquet.
  • The distal third and the pilon. A fracture that extends into the ankle joint is a pilon and belongs on the ankle and pilon card: staged, soft-tissue driven, and worse.
  • The fasciotomised leg. If the compartments were released on day 0, the leg comes back for debridement and closure every 48-72 h and a block is now reasonable; see compartment syndrome and fasciotomy.
  • Iliac crest bone graft. A second wound that hurts for longer than the knee and is often forgotten in the plan; infiltration or a fascia iliaca block for the crest, and the patient warned.
  • Intravenous dexamethasone. It prolongs the block by several hours and reduces rebound, which is the wrong trade when the aim is a block that has worn off by the evening examination; withhold it in the fresh fracture and use it for the delayed fixation.
  • The spinal that will not wear off. A plain bupivacaine spinal lasts into the evening and a leg that cannot be examined for 4 h after a high-energy nailing is the neuraxial version of the dense block the guideline warns against; a short-acting agent or a general anaesthetic instead.

Key sources

The regional anaesthesia and compartment syndrome evidence is the point of this card and it has changed: the 2021 Association of Anaesthetists guideline moved the profession from “no blocks in tibial fractures” to “dilute single-shot blocks with surveillance”, and the 2025 series of 791 patients is the largest cohort to find no excess of missed compartment syndrome with blocks, with the catheter caveat. McQueen’s differential pressure threshold is the diagnostic standard. The surgical evidence is observational. There is no procedure-specific analgesia guideline, and the catheter timing advice is the author’s reading of the compartment window rather than a trial result.
