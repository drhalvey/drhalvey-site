66 Calcaneal fracture fixation

Displaced intra-articular calcaneal fractures: extensile lateral approach, sinus tarsi approach and percutaneous fixation, timed to the soft tissues

5Severe and prolonged

Dominant mechanism: Bone. A crushed cancellous bone is levered back out to length, its depressed joint surface is elevated and the whole is plated, through a full-thickness flap lifted off the lateral wall (periosteum) of a heel that was already swollen to its limit by the injury. Bone and periosteum in a closed, dependent envelope; the swelling and the throbbing on dependency last for weeks, which, with its rank as the most painful operation in the 179-procedure cohort, is what puts it above the fusions. The week of injury pain and the opioid the patient arrives on raise the plan, not the score

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

The trap
The calendar is set by the skin, not the fracture: an extensile approach through a swollen heel breaks down, so the surgeon waits 7-14 days for the wrinkle sign, and the anaesthetist's risks sit at the two ends of that wait. On day 0 the patient who fell far enough to burst a calcaneus has a spine and a second heel to examine and a foot that can develop compartment syndrome; on the day of surgery the patient gets the most painful operation in the cohort, and a single shot that fades at 2 am is not a plan

What the surgeon is doing and why

The calcaneus breaks when the body lands on it. A fall from a ladder or a roof, or a foot braced on a pedal in a collision, drives the wedge-shaped talus down into the heel bone and splits it. The posterior facet of the subtalar joint is punched into the cancellous body of the bone, the heel shortens, widens and tilts into varus, and the lateral wall blows out under the fibula and against the peroneal tendons. The CT classifies the damage by the number of fragments of the posterior facet (Sanders types 2 to 4). The bone is a shell of thin cortex around sponge, inside a tight envelope of thin lateral skin and a specialised heel pad, and it bleeds into that envelope until the foot is tense and blistered.

Whether to operate at all is a fair question, and the largest trial answers it cautiously. In the UK Heel Fracture Trial, patients with typical closed displaced intra-articular fractures did no better at two years after open reduction and internal fixation than after non-operative care, and complications and reoperations were far more common after surgery. Surgeons now operate selectively: on younger patients with a reconstructible joint and a deformed heel, and increasingly through smaller approaches. When they do operate, the goals are the same whatever the incision:

  1. Restore the shape of the heel: pull the tuberosity down and out of varus (usually with a threaded pin in the tuberosity used as a handle), restoring height and narrowing the width.
  2. Rebuild the posterior facet: lift the depressed fragment and fix it to the intact medial (sustentacular) fragment with screws just under the joint surface.
  3. Hold it: a lateral plate, screws alone, or both. The void left under the elevated joint is sometimes filled with graft or bone substitute.

The approach is where the soft tissues come in. The extensile lateral approach lifts an L-shaped, full-thickness flap of skin, peroneal tendons and periosteum off the whole lateral wall and holds it up with wires, giving an excellent view through the worst skin in the foot. The sinus tarsi approach is a 4 cm incision over the joint with the plate slid in under the skin. Percutaneous fixation uses stab incisions, the pin as a joystick and screws placed on fluoroscopy. In the pooled randomised trials, wound complications were several times more frequent with the extensile approach than with the smaller ones, with equivalent reduction.

The pain logic is a crushed cancellous bone manipulated and plated inside a closed, swollen compartment, with periosteum stripped from its lateral wall. In the 179-procedure cohort, open reduction of the calcaneus was the single most painful operation on the first day, ahead of every spinal, thoracic and abdominal procedure, in patients who mostly had no block. With a popliteal catheter the first two days are controlled and the peak moves to the day the catheter comes out. What justifies a score of 5 and not 4 is the operation, not the patient: it stands first in that ranking, and the plated bone and stripped lateral wall sit in a closed envelope that fills again every time the leg hangs, so the pain persists well beyond 72 h and is re-provoked by dependency for weeks. The heel bears no weight for 8-12 weeks, so this is not a bone that keeps being loaded in the usual sense; dependency does to it what loading does elsewhere. That this patient arrives a week into severe injury pain, often on regular opioids, is a patient factor: it raises the plan (the established opioid continued, ketamine available, the catheter for as long as the service can run it) and not the score.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Day 0: the fall, and what else it broke

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The energy that bursts a calcaneus travels up the axial skeleton. The surgeon looks for the other heel, a tibial plateau, a pilon and above all a thoracolumbar burst fracture.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    You inherit that survey. A patient booked for a heel who has back pain, or who has not had the spine examined and imaged where indicated, is not ready for a lateral position or a spinal. Bilateral fractures mean a prone operation or two operations, and a patient with no good leg. The mechanism also selects the patient: falls at work, falls while intoxicated and deliberate falls each bring their own history, and smoking is over-represented and matters to the wound.

  2. 2 The wait: swelling, blisters and the compartment question

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The foot is elevated, sometimes in a compression pump or a backslab, while the swelling peaks at 48-72 h and then recedes. One pattern cannot wait: a tongue-type fracture in which the Achilles pulls the posterior fragment up against the heel skin, which blanches and dies within hours unless the fragment is reduced and pinned or screwed percutaneously.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Pain in the first days is severe and is also the symptom of foot compartment syndrome. Fasciotomy rates after an isolated calcaneal fracture are low (1% in a national database), but a follow-up series found clawed toes and plantar numbness from a missed compartment syndrome in 10%, all with comminuted joint fractures. A dense, long-acting block while the foot is still swelling removes the symptom the surgeons are watching, so acute analgesia is multimodal with opioid PCA, and any block in that period is low in concentration (ropivacaine 0.2% or less), free of adjuncts, single-shot or a catheter that can be turned down, agreed with the surgeon and accompanied by structured checks. The swelling peaks at 48-72 h and no source fixes the day on which the risk ends, so the period is judged from the foot and not from the calendar. The source for the block rules is the 2021 Association of Anaesthetists guideline, which states that single-shot or continuous peripheral nerve blocks using lower concentrations of local anaesthetic without adjuncts are not associated with delayed diagnosis provided surveillance is appropriate. That guideline addresses lower leg (tibial) trauma; applying it to the foot is extrapolation, set out in nerve injury and compartment syndrome. The urgent tongue-type reduction is a 30-min prone or lateral procedure inside that period: general anaesthesia, surgeon’s infiltration, and the block decision deferred to the definitive operation.

  3. 3 Timing and approach: the wrinkle sign

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    An incision through tense, oedematous skin does not heal. The surgeon pinches the lateral heel with the ankle dorsiflexed and everted; when fine skin creases return, the oedema has gone and an extensile flap will survive. That takes 7-14 days, and beyond three weeks the fracture is too sticky to reduce. Sinus tarsi and percutaneous fixation depend less on the skin and are done earlier, often within the first week.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The date is set in clinic and the patient comes in fasted on an elective trauma list. For an extensile approach at 7-14 days the swelling has receded and the skin wrinkles, and in the author’s judgement the period of compartment syndrome risk from the injury has passed; no source defines its end. Sinus tarsi or percutaneous fixation within the first week is done in a foot that may still be swelling, and it takes the low-concentration block and the structured surveillance of step 2. Ask what the patient has been taking: a week or two of regular oxycodone is common and makes this an opioid-tolerant patient for the purposes of the opioid-tolerant chapter. Low molecular weight heparin timing matters if a spinal is considered: the needle goes in at least 12 h after a prophylactic dose of enoxaparin and the next dose is given at least 12 h after the needle (venous thromboembolism), so an evening dose before a morning list is compatible and a dose on the morning of surgery is not. Smokers and diabetics are steered towards the smaller approaches or away from surgery, so the patient who reaches an extensile approach has usually been chosen.

  4. 4 Anaesthetic and catheter

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs a still limb, lateral, with a bloodless field for up to 2 h.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    General anaesthesia with a tracheal tube or a secure supraglottic airway for a lateral position of this length. Place the popliteal sciatic catheter before induction, awake or lightly sedated, so that the patient can report pain or paraesthesia on injection, as for the fusion and the ankle replacement: prone, or lateral with the injured side up, which presents the popliteal fossa and lets the catheter be dressed on the lateral thigh. Placing it after induction with the patient already turned is convenient and is not the default in an adult; it is kept for the patient whose heel makes awake positioning intolerable, with the reason recorded. Bolus 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%. The calcaneus, the subtalar joint and the lateral skin are tibial and sural territory, so a saphenous block is not needed for a lateral approach. Dexamethasone 8 mg IV; cefazolin 2 g IV before the cuff. A spinal performed in the lateral position is a reasonable alternative for a patient with a cleared spine and timed anticoagulation, with the same catheter for afterwards.

  5. 5 Lateral position and tourniquet

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Injured side up on a beanbag, the operative leg supported on a foam platform so the image intensifier can obtain lateral, axial and Broden views without moving the foot, the lower leg flexed forward.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Axillary roll, lower-leg padding at the fibular head and lateral malleolus, neutral neck, and the upper arm supported. Fix the airway before the drapes go on, because the head is at the far end from where you will be standing during imaging. The thigh cuff is set from the limb occlusion pressure (the tourniquet); an extensile fixation commonly needs all of 120 min, so call 60 and 90.

  6. 6 Raising the flap

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    In the extensile approach the L-shaped incision is taken straight to bone, and skin, sural nerve, peroneal tendons and periosteum are lifted as one layer off the lateral wall, without forceps on the corner and without undermining. Wires bent into the fibula, talus and cuboid hold it up. In the sinus tarsi approach the incision runs from the fibular tip towards the fourth metatarsal base, above the tendons.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The periosteal stripping is the second pain mechanism of the operation. The corner of the flap is the tissue that fails in the wound complications, and it tolerates neither traction nor hypoperfusion, so keep the blood pressure in the patient’s normal range and the patient warm. Nothing else is required of you here.

  7. 7 Reduction: the tuberosity and the joint surface

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The blown-out lateral wall is hinged open, the depressed posterior facet fragment is levered up and matched to the sustentacular fragment under direct vision and fluoroscopy, the tuberosity is pulled down, medialised out of varus and brought to length with the pin, and wires hold everything while the views are checked.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The longest step and one that cannot be hurried; the joint is reduced to within a millimetre or it is not worth the wound. Fluoroscopy is frequent, with the C-arm swinging through positions near your end of the table. Bleeding cancellous bone is hidden by the cuff until release.

  8. 8 Fixation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Screws under the joint surface into the sustentaculum, then a low-profile lateral plate contoured to the wall, or long screws from the tuberosity into the anterior process and the sustentaculum. A void under the joint may be filled with graft or injectable calcium phosphate.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The sustentacular screws aim at the medial side where the tibial neurovascular bundle and flexor hallucis longus run; the surgeon watches for the great toe moving with the drill. Bone substitute sets by crystallisation, without the monomer or heat of acrylic cement, and has no circulatory effect.

  9. 9 Smaller approaches

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Sinus tarsi and percutaneous fixation do the same reduction through a limited window with more reliance on the joystick pin and fluoroscopy. They are used for simpler fracture patterns, for patients whose skin or comorbidity rules out a flap, and by an increasing number of surgeons for most fractures.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Shorter, earlier after injury, less periosteal stripping and fewer wound problems. The pain mechanism is the same bone with less soft-tissue insult, a point lower on the scale, and this is the version for which a single-shot popliteal block with dexamethasone is a reasonable plan: in the one randomised trial a single block lowered pain scores for about 16 h after calcaneal fixation. If the operation is done in the first week, while the foot may still be swelling, the compartment considerations in step 2 apply: ropivacaine 0.2% or less, no perineural adjunct, intravenous dexamethasone omitted if its only purpose is to prolong the block, and scheduled surveillance for 24-48 h afterwards.

  10. 10 Cuff down, closure and splint

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The tourniquet is released, the flap is checked for bleeding at its edge, a drain is often placed under it, and the corner is closed without tension using sutures that evert the skin. A padded backslab holds the ankle at neutral.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Reperfusion after 2 h: a fall in pressure and a carbon dioxide and potassium load, managed with ventilation and a vasopressor at hand. Avoid a hypertensive, coughing emergence that fills the space under the flap with haematoma. The slab must not press on the lateral heel, and the lateral malleolus and fibular head of a numb leg need padding.

  11. 11 The first 72 h and the removal day

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the foot above the heart, the wound undisturbed and inspected at 48 h, and no weight on the heel for 8-12 weeks.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Start ropivacaine 0.2% at 5-8 mL/h with or without a patient bolus. Continue the patient’s pre-operative opioid at its established dose from day 0 so that the catheter is not also covering withdrawal, with regular paracetamol and an NSAID if the surgeon allows it. Give the breakthrough opioid before the catheter is removed and review afterwards. The surgeon’s wound check at 48 h needs no sensation, so the infusion can continue through it. After removal the pain is positional: tolerable with the leg up, throbbing within a minute of lowering it, which the patient should be told is expected and the ward should be told is not a reason to stop elevating.

Pain plan

The mechanism is bone and periosteum in a closed, swollen, dependent compartment, in a patient with a week of injury pain behind them, and the territory is entirely tibial and sural. That is the strongest case for a popliteal catheter in foot surgery, and the block menu lists calcaneal fixation as a catheter operation. Two features make it stronger than for an elective fusion: the patient is frequently opioid-tolerant by the day of surgery, and the cohort ranking (first of 179) describes what happens without regional analgesia.

The direct evidence is thin. One randomised trial of 120 calcaneal fixations showed that a single popliteal block lowered pain scores and PCA use for about 16 h and made no difference by 24 h, which is the expected behaviour of a single shot and the argument for something longer. The pooled trials of continuous against single-injection popliteal blocks in foot and ankle surgery found lower pain at 24 and 48 h, no infections or nerve injuries and leakage in 13.9%; none was confined to the calcaneus and none used dexamethasone. The routine catheter is therefore the author’s judgement from mechanism and ranking.

The defensible default: popliteal catheter for 48-72 h, intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, regular paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor where the surgeon permits (many do for a fracture, some do not), the patient’s established opioid continued, and an immediate-release breakthrough opioid. Low-dose ketamine is a reasonable adjunct in the opioid-tolerant patient or where the catheter fails. For percutaneous and sinus tarsi fixation, a single-shot block with dexamethasone and an overnight stay is acceptable.

Before definitive surgery, and for early fixation, the rule is different. While the foot is still swelling it is at risk of compartment syndrome; analgesia is systemic, and any block is low in concentration, without adjuncts and with surveillance in place. No source gives the day on which that risk ends: the swelling peaks at 48-72 h, and the 7-14 day wait for an extensile approach is, in the author’s judgement, beyond it.

Escalation triggers: severe pain under a running catheter means a displaced catheter, a tight splint or a haematoma under the flap, and the splint is opened and the wound looked at before the infusion is adjusted. Burning pain and numbness along the lateral border of the foot is the sural nerve, which lies in the flap. Pain that climbs again from day 5-10 with a discharging corner is the wound. Pain out of proportion with tense swelling and pain on passive toe movement while the foot is still swelling, or in the 24-48 h after an early fixation, is compartment syndrome until the surgeon has measured or decompressed.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The spine. A lateral or prone position and a spinal anaesthetic both assume the thoracolumbar spine has been cleared.
  • Bilateral fractures. Prone, both legs, twice the tourniquet considerations, bilateral catheters within a shared local anaesthetic dose, and a patient who will be in a wheelchair.
  • The tongue-type skin emergency. Hours matter; it is the one calcaneal operation done at night.
  • Open fractures. A medial wound from the sustentacular spike: debridement and a spanning frame first, as in open fractures and damage control.
  • Smokers, diabetics, vasculopaths. The wound complication rate is why many are treated non-operatively or percutaneously; where they are operated on, perfusion and temperature at closure are worth your attention.
  • Primary subtalar fusion. For an unreconstructible joint the surgeon may reduce the shape and fuse the subtalar joint at the same operation: add the joint preparation and the plan from ankle, subtalar and triple fusion.
  • Catheter and the neurological check. The surgeon may want sural and tibial function seen before the infusion starts; bolus after the examination in recovery.
  • Later operations. Peroneal tendon problems, removal of a prominent plate and subtalar fusion for post-traumatic arthritis are common in the following years; the patient often returns with firm views about their last anaesthetic.
  • The pilon in the same leg. The same mechanism produces both; see ankle and pilon fracture fixation.

Key sources

The pain ranking is from the Gerbershagen cohort and describes calcaneal surgery largely without regional analgesia. The decision to operate rests on a randomised trial that found no benefit from open fixation of typical fractures, which is why surgical practice has moved to selected cases and smaller approaches; the wound complication comparisons come from two meta-analyses that agree in direction and differ in size. Foot compartment syndrome data are a registry study of fasciotomy rates and a small follow-up series of missed cases, which measure different things. The analgesic evidence specific to the calcaneus is a single trial of a single-shot block against no block; the catheter recommendation is extrapolated and is the author’s judgement.
