91 Spinal infection: discitis, epidural abscess and debridement

Emergency decompression of a spinal epidural abscess; debridement, biopsy and stabilisation for pyogenic or tuberculous vertebral osteomyelitis and discitis

4Severe

Dominant mechanism: Muscle. Paraspinal muscle stripped for a laminectomy over two to five levels, often with pedicle screws, on top of an infected, inflamed disc and endplates that were producing severe pain before the operation and keep producing it for weeks until the antibiotics and the stabilisation take effect; in a patient who is frequently opioid tolerant

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: low.

Block
No regional technique: general anaesthesia with a low-dose ketamine infusion, the patient's baseline opioid or opioid substitution therapy continued, regular paracetamol, surgeon infiltration of the wound, and opioid patient-controlled analgesia
Catheter: no Nothing is placed in or near an infected epidural space in a bacteraemic patient; the continuous element of the plan is ketamine and the baseline opioid
Alternatives: Bilateral erector spinae plane blocks, single shot, at a level clear of the infection and through clean skin, for a posterior debridement in a patient who is no longer bacteraemic (author's judgement); Lidocaine infusion as a second systemic adjunct where ketamine is not tolerated; Clonidine or dexmedetomidine as an opioid-sparing and withdrawal-modifying adjunct in the patient who injects drugs
Position
Prone on a radiolucent frame or bolsters with the abdomen free for the posterior decompression and stabilisation that most patients get; supine with the neck extended for an anterior cervical drainage; lateral for a transthoracic or retroperitoneal debridement of a destroyed vertebral body
Tourniquet
No
Cement
No
Blood loss
200-500 mL for a laminectomy and washout; 500-1,500 mL for a multilevel debridement with instrumentation or an anterior corpectomy, more than the same operation in clean tissue because inflamed bone and granulation tissue ooze and the septic patient is often coagulopathic
Duration
60-120 min for an emergency decompression; 180-300 min for debridement with instrumented stabilisation or an anterior reconstruction
Length of stay
2-6 weeks, set by the intravenous antibiotic course, the neurological deficit and rehabilitation, not by the wound. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Not cefazolin by reflex. The septic patient or the patient with a neurological deficit is already on empirical treatment (commonly flucloxacillin or vancomycin, with Gram-negative cover by local protocol) and the next dose is timed to cover the incision. In the stable patient with no deficit and no organism, antibiotics are held until the surgeon has taken deep specimens, then given at once
Tranexamic acid
Tranexamic acid 1 g IV before incision for an instrumented or multilevel case; the spine trials support it in clean surgery, and in sepsis with a prothrombotic state the decision is individual
VTE
High risk: sepsis, paresis, immobility and often intravenous drug use. Mechanical prophylaxis from induction; low molecular weight heparin from day 1 or 2 when the surgeon is content the epidural space is dry
Antiemetic
Ondansetron 4 mg IV before emergence; dexamethasone is not given: it has no role in infective cord compression and blunts the inflammatory markers the physicians are following
Tell the patient
The operation takes the pressure off the nerves and removes infected tissue so the antibiotics can work; the back will be sore from the surgery, the deeper pain from the infection eases over weeks, and the antibiotics continue for at least six weeks.
The trap
A spinal epidural abscess with a progressing deficit is operated on now, not in the morning: the cord is being injured by compression and by septic thrombosis of its veins, the outcome is set by the neurology at the moment of decompression, and fasting status, an echocardiogram and a tidy antibiotic plan do not justify delay

What the surgeon is doing and why

Bacteria reach the spine through the blood. In adults they lodge in the vascular endplate, destroy the disc between two vertebrae (discitis, vertebral osteomyelitis) and may then spread backwards into the epidural space, forwards into the psoas or the mediastinum, or both. An epidural abscess can also arrive directly, from a spinal procedure or an epidural catheter. Staphylococcus aureus accounts for about two thirds of epidural abscesses. The patients are the ones bacteria find easily: people with diabetes, people who inject drugs, the immunosuppressed, the alcohol dependent, those on dialysis, and those with a distant focus such as endocarditis or an infected line.

There are two different operations on this card and they are separated by urgency.

  1. The emergency: decompression of an epidural abscess. Pus and granulation tissue in a closed canal compress the cord or cauda equina and thrombose its veins. The deficit, once it starts, progresses over hours and does not reliably recover. The surgeon performs a laminectomy over the collection, evacuates pus, takes specimens, washes out and closes over a drain. In the systematic review of 1,843 patients, three of the four studies that examined timing found large effects on neurological outcome in favour of early surgery, and medical treatment alone failed and needed surgery in 10-50% of those who started on it. This is a 60-120 minute operation on a septic patient at whatever hour the deficit is recognised.
  2. The planned operation: debridement and stabilisation of vertebral osteomyelitis. Most discitis is treated with six weeks of antibiotics after a biopsy. Surgery is for the patient whose infection has destroyed enough bone to produce deformity or instability, who has a deficit, who has failed antibiotics, or in whom no organism can be obtained any other way. The surgeon removes infected disc and dead bone, decompresses, and holds the segment with pedicle screws above and below; some add an anterior corpectomy and cage through the chest or retroperitoneum (anterior thoracic spine). Metalwork in an infected field is accepted practice because stability is part of what clears the infection.

What the surgeon wants from both is the same three things: tissue for the laboratory before antibiotics obscure it, a cord that is perfused throughout, and a patient who survives the sepsis.

The pain logic: the surgical component is the muscle mechanism of any posterior spinal operation, scaled by the number of levels. Under it is the infection, which hurts more than the surgery in many patients: an inflamed disc and endplates loaded by every movement, which settle over weeks, not days. And the patient is often opioid tolerant before the first incision, from weeks of undiagnosed back pain or from long-term drug use. The score of 4 matches posterior lumbar fusion: a multilevel stripped back, often instrumented, that needs a robust multimodal plan with opioid to day 3 and movement-evoked pain that limits mobilisation. An emergency laminectomy and washout without screws in an opioid-naive patient behaves like a 3, as on the tumour decompression card, because the operation removes part of the pain generator. In both the tail is longer than after clean surgery, because the infection keeps hurting until it is treated.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 The decision to operate and how fast

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A patient with back pain, fever and a normal neurological examination can be imaged, cultured and discussed. A patient with weakness, a sensory level or retention is losing cord by the hour.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    For the emergency, the pre-operative work is what can be done in the time it takes to open theatre: blood cultures (two sets, if not already taken), lactate, haemoglobin, clotting, glucose, creatinine, a group and screen, and a 12-lead electrocardiogram. An echocardiogram for endocarditis matters and is done afterwards unless there is a murmur with heart failure. A full stomach is managed with a rapid sequence induction, not with a delay. Document the neurology yourself, in a line, before induction: it is the baseline for everything that follows.

  2. 2 Antibiotic timing against cultures

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The whole antibiotic course, six weeks or more, is chosen from what grows. The surgeon wants several deep specimens (pus, disc, bone) from an untreated field.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the one spinal operation where the antibiotic is deliberately not given before the knife in some patients, and the rule needs to be explicit at the brief. The Infectious Diseases Society of America guideline separates two groups. The septic patient, and the patient with neurological compromise, gets empirical treatment immediately after blood cultures and is operated on anyway; do not stop or delay it. The stable patient with normal neurology and no organism has antibiotics withheld until tissue is obtained, and in that patient you hold the dose, hand it up ready, and give it the moment the surgeon says the specimens are out. The guideline addresses empirical therapy; applying it to the pre-incision dose is the author’s reading. The evidence for withholding is specific to the spine: in a meta-analysis, prior antibiotics did not clearly reduce bone culture yield overall but did in the vertebral osteomyelitis studies (odds ratio 2.95). This differs from the prosthetic joint, where prophylaxis is not withheld (see antibiotics and infection).

  3. 3 Induction in a septic patient with a threatened cord

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The cord under an abscess is ischaemic from compression and venous thrombosis; it has no reserve for a fall in pressure.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Arterial line before induction where time allows. Vasodilated, hypovolaemic and often with a septic cardiomyopathy, the patient will drop their pressure with any induction agent: reduce the dose, have noradrenaline connected and running, and aim for a mean of 80 mmHg or above from induction to the end of decompression. Where there is an established deficit the cord is acutely injured and the target is the 85-90 mmHg used for acute cord injury in spine essentials, as far as the septic circulation allows. A cervical abscess adds an airway that may be narrowed by a retropharyngeal collection and a neck that may be unstable from bone destruction: videolaryngoscopy with the neck held neutral, or an awake technique if mouth opening and swallowing suggest a prevertebral mass. Suxamethonium is avoided in the patient with an established deficit of more than two or three days.

  4. 4 Vascular access in the patient who injects drugs

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The patient needs reliable access now and for six weeks.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Peripheral veins are frequently unusable. Use ultrasound early; a central line placed under anaesthesia through clean skin is reasonable for theatre, but long-term access is a decision for the infection team, since a line in a patient who injects is its own risk. Assume blood-borne virus exposure for the team’s protection without assuming it of the patient. Ask what and how much is used daily, and whether the patient is on methadone or buprenorphine; continue either on the day. A patient in withdrawal at induction is tachycardic, hypertensive and vomiting, which confuses the picture of sepsis and endangers the airway.

  5. 5 Turning prone

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The posterior approach suits most collections, which lie dorsal to the cord in the thoracic and lumbar spine.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The turn is done with the spine held as unstable when the vertebral body is destroyed. Prone positioning lowers cardiac output further in a septic patient (see spine essentials); have the vasopressor running before the turn and recheck the arterial trace after it. Emaciated patients have no padding: pressure areas at the iliac crests, knees and face need more attention than usual, and the skin may already be broken from injecting.

  6. 6 Laminectomy, evacuation and specimens

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The lamina is removed over the collection, pus is released, granulation tissue is peeled off the dura where it comes away easily, and specimens go to microbiology and histology. Chronic abscesses are solid granulation and cannot simply be washed out.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Release of pus under pressure sometimes produces a transient bacteraemic shower: a pressure fall and a temperature rise in the next 30 minutes are expected and treated with vasopressor and fluid. This is the moment for the held antibiotic. Where motor evoked potentials are monitored, the anaesthetic is propofol and remifentanil without relaxant, but in an emergency case at night monitoring is usually not available and the surgeon relies on speed and pressure.

  7. 7 Debridement and instrumentation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    In vertebral osteomyelitis the infected disc is curetted from behind or through the foramen, dead bone is removed, and pedicle screws span the destroyed segment. Bone graft or a cage fills the gap.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is where the blood is lost: inflamed cancellous bone and epidural veins ooze continuously and do not respond to diathermy. Check haemoglobin, clotting and fibrinogen at the hour; sepsis consumes platelets and factors, and the patient with liver disease from hepatitis C or alcohol started short. Cell salvage is not used in an infected field. Keep the patient warm; the washout uses litres of room-temperature saline.

  8. 8 Closure, drain and emergence

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The wound is closed in layers over a drain; some surgeons leave antibiotic powder or beads.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Surgeon infiltration of the muscle and skin with long-acting local anaesthetic. Extubate if the patient was breathing adequately before surgery, the vasopressor requirement is low and the lactate is falling; otherwise ventilate in intensive care. The patient with a high cervical or upper thoracic deficit has weak intercostals and a poor cough, and the decision is made on that, not on the surgery.

  9. 9 The days after

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants a neurological examination every hour or two at first, the drain output, and the cultures.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    High-dependency care for the septic or the neurologically impaired. Worsening neurology is re-accumulation or haematoma and returns to the scanner. The analgesic plan has to survive weeks of admission: baseline opioid, a rescue agent, and a written plan for reduction. Renal function is watched daily, because sepsis, contrast, vancomycin or an aminoglycoside, and a hypotensive anaesthetic all converge on the kidney, which is the reason NSAIDs are left out at first.

Pain plan

The mechanism is a stripped back over an infected spine in a patient who may be opioid tolerant, so the plan is systemic and built on the baseline. There is no regional default: neuraxial techniques are contraindicated by the infection itself, and a fascial plane block through or near infected tissue in a bacteraemic patient is not worth its small benefit. The defensible default is the patient’s usual opioid or substitution therapy continued without interruption, a ketamine bolus of 0.5 mg/kg IV at induction and an infusion of 0.1-0.25 mg/kg/h during surgery, which the author’s practice is to continue for 24-48 hours, regular paracetamol (with dose reduction in the cachectic or the patient with liver disease), surgeon infiltration, and an opioid patient-controlled analgesia set for the patient’s tolerance, not for an opioid-naive adult. The evidence for ketamine comes from opioid-dependent patients having clean lumbar surgery, where a bolus and an intra-operative infusion of 10 microg/kg/min, stopped at wound closure, reduced opioid consumption at 48 hours and at six weeks; the lower rate and the post-operative continuation used here were not tested in that trial and are author’s judgement. PROSPECT’s complex spine recommendations add an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, which is deferred here until the creatinine is stable and the nephrotoxic antibiotics are established.

For the patient on methadone, the daily dose continues and is not counted as analgesia. For the patient on buprenorphine, the author’s practice is to continue it, split the dose, and add a full agonist on top, which is the current consensus position and is covered in the opioid-tolerant patient. Under-treated pain in a patient who injects drugs leads to self-discharge and an abandoned antibiotic course, which is the outcome that matters.

Escalation: pain rising after day 3 with rising inflammatory markers is undrained infection or a failing construct, not tolerance. New radicular pain or weakness is re-accumulation. Rebound is not a feature because no block has been used.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The epidural abscess you caused. An abscess after an epidural or spinal catheter presents days later with back pain and fever before any deficit; the catheter-site examination and a low threshold for imaging are the prevention, and the case comes to theatre as on this card.
  • Endocarditis. Staphylococcus aureus bacteraemia with a spinal focus carries a real chance of a valve lesion. A new regurgitant murmur with pulmonary oedema changes the induction; an echocardiogram should not delay a decompression but is done the same day.
  • Tuberculosis. Slow, destructive, often thoracic with kyphosis and a cold paraspinal abscess; the operation is usually anterior and planned, the patient may need respiratory isolation, and rifampicin induces the metabolism of methadone and many anaesthetic adjuncts.
  • Cervical abscess. Airway first; the anterior approach shares the post-operative airway risks of any anterior cervical operation with infection added.
  • Dialysis and diabetes. Fluid balance, potassium and glucose are the anaesthetic; an insulin infusion is usual.
  • Psoas and paraspinal collections. Often drained by the radiologist under sedation, prone, in a septic patient: the same physiology in a worse room.
  • The return to theatre. Repeat washouts are common. Each one is a septic prone anaesthetic in a patient whose veins, nutrition and tolerance are a little worse than last time.
  • Steroids. They have no role in infective compression, in contrast to spinal metastasis decompression.

Key sources

The timing evidence is retrospective and confounded by indication, since the sickest patients are operated on fastest, yet three of four series still favour early surgery; no trial will be done. The antibiotic timing rule is a guideline recommendation resting on observational culture-yield data. The analgesic plan borrows from clean spine surgery in opioid-dependent patients; there are no trials in infected spines or in people who inject drugs. The pressure targets are the conventional ones for a compromised cord and are untested in this setting.
