104 Limb lengthening, deformity correction frames and guided growth

Osteotomy with a circular or monolateral external fixator or a motorised intramedullary lengthening nail for leg length discrepancy and deformity in the child of 5 years to skeletal maturity, the months of distraction that follow, frame removal, and tension-band (eight-plate) hemiepiphysiodesis and epiphysiodesis as day cases from 2 years to adolescence

5Severe and prolonged

Dominant mechanism: Joint re-tensioned. Lengthening with a frame or nail: an osteotomy that is deliberately pulled apart by 1 mm a day for weeks, so the bone gap, the muscles, the nerves and the skin are held under rising tension for months. Wires and half-pins transfix muscle and move with every step; pin sites inflame; stretched nerves add burning pain. The first three days are an ordinary osteotomy scoring 3-4; the 5 is earned by the months that follow. Guided growth with eight-plates is a different operation scoring 1-2

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Block
Femoral lengthening: suprainguinal fascia iliaca or femoral block, ropivacaine 0.2% 0.5 mL/kg (max 30 mL), single shot. Tibial osteotomy with a frame: no dense block; morphine patient-controlled analgesia (bolus 20 microg/kg, max 1 mg, lockout 5 min) with ketamine 0.1-0.2 mg/kg/h, paracetamol and, if the surgeon permits, a short NSAID course. Eight-plates: surgeon infiltration, ropivacaine 0.2% up to 0.5 mL/kg per limb
Catheter: selective A dilute femoral or sciatic catheter (ropivacaine 0.1% at 0.1-0.2 mL/kg/h, max 0.4 mg/kg/h) or a low-dose lumbar epidural for 48 h for bilateral surgery, combined femoral and tibial frames, and the adolescent whose previous frame went badly, with the surgeon's agreement and hourly neurovascular observations. Not routine: the acute pain is moderate, the tibia is a compartment at risk, and the long pain course is an outpatient problem that no catheter reaches
Alternatives: Lumbar epidural, ropivacaine 0.1% with clonidine 1 microg/mL at 0.2-0.3 mL/kg/h, for bilateral lower limb lengthening as in achondroplasia; Popliteal sciatic plus saphenous single-shot blocks with ropivacaine 0.1-0.2% for frame removal, pin exchange or foot frames, where no osteotomy has been made; General anaesthesia without a block for femoral nail lengthening, which was non-inferior for early pain in one retrospective series; Caudal ropivacaine 0.2% 1 mL/kg (max 20 mL) for bilateral eight-plates in the child under about 20 kg
Position
Supine on a radiolucent table with a bump under the buttock and the whole limb free to the iliac crest, the leg raised on bolsters so rings can pass round it; lateral or supine with traction for an antegrade femoral lengthening nail
Tourniquet
Sometimes. Used for eight-plates and epiphysiodesis (150-250 mmHg, 20-40 min). Usually avoided for frame application and corticotomy: wires heat as they are drilled and surgeons want a perfused limb to cool them and to show bleeding at the osteotomy
Cement
No
Blood loss
Under 5 mL/kg for a frame with tibial corticotomy; 5-10 mL/kg for a femoral osteotomy or a reamed lengthening nail; negligible for eight-plates
Duration
2-4 h for frame application with osteotomy, most of it assembly; 90-150 min for a lengthening nail; 20-40 min per physis for eight-plates; 30-60 min for frame removal
Length of stay
2-4 nights after frame or nail, set by pain, pin-site teaching and physiotherapy; day case for eight-plates, epiphysiodesis and most frame removals. Day case: selected
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 30 mg/kg IV (max 2 g) within 60 min of incision, repeated at 4 h; pin-site infections during treatment are managed with oral courses
Tranexamic acid
Not routine; tranexamic acid 15 mg/kg IV (max 1 g) is reasonable for a reamed femoral nail or bilateral femoral osteotomies
VTE
None before puberty; in the post-pubertal adolescent with a frame or nail and weeks of reduced mobility, mechanical measures and enoxaparin by unit protocol
Antiemetic
Ondansetron 0.15 mg/kg IV (max 4 mg) and dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV (max 8 mg)
Tell the patient
The operation itself is sore for a few days like any broken bone; the harder part is the weeks of gradual stretching afterwards, which bring aching, tight muscles and sore pin sites, and there is a plan for each of those.
The trap
The anaesthetic covers three hours and the pain lasts six months: a plan that ends at discharge leaves an adolescent turning struts four times a day on escalating opioids with burning in the foot that nobody has named as nerve stretch. Agree before the first operation who manages analgesia during distraction, what the neuropathic agent will be, and whether the surgeon allows NSAIDs

What the surgeon is doing and why

Two ideas share this card, and they sit at opposite ends of the effort scale.

The first is that living bone under slow, steady tension makes more bone. If a long bone is cut with its periosteum and blood supply preserved, left for five to seven days to form early callus, and then pulled apart at about 1 mm a day in three or four increments, the gap fills with a column of new bone (the regenerate) and the muscles, nerves, vessels and skin lengthen with it. When the target is reached, distraction stops and the regenerate is left to mature, which takes about a month for every centimetre gained. The device that does the pulling is either outside the limb (a circular frame of rings and tensioned wires, a hexapod frame whose six struts can correct angulation, rotation and length together, or a rail on half-pins) or inside it (a telescopic intramedullary nail driven by an external magnet). Children come to this at any age from about five years for congenital short femur or tibia, growth arrest after infection or fracture, Blount disease and the skeletal dysplasias; the nail needs a canal large enough, so it is mostly used from about 8-10 years.

The second idea is cheaper: let the child’s own growth do the correction. A small two-hole plate with a screw either side of the growth plate on the convex side of a bowed knee tethers that side while the other keeps growing, and the limb straightens at about a degree a month. When it is straight the plate comes out. The same principle across the whole plate, or a drill through it, stops a long leg growing so the short one catches up. These are 30-minute day cases from the age of two until a year or two before skeletal maturity.

Pain logic. The frame or nail operation is, on the day, a low-energy osteotomy plus a dozen wires or pins through muscle: bone and muscle, 3-4 for three days. Then distraction starts, and the mechanism becomes retension in its purest form: every tissue in the segment is longer today than yesterday. The prospective paediatric data describe high pain for months, through consolidation as well as distraction. That earns the 5, and it means the anaesthetist’s most useful contribution may be the plan made in clinic, not the block. Eight-plates are extraperiosteal metal on an intact bone, scoring 1-2.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Planning: the patient, the bone, the device

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon chooses the level of osteotomy, the device and whether deformity will be corrected acutely on the table or gradually by the frame.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Ask four things: femur or tibia, frame or nail, acute or gradual correction, and how many centimetres. The tibia with an acute correction is the compartment and peroneal nerve risk; the femur is the bleeding and the quadriceps. Then look at the child. Achondroplasia brings foramen magnum stenosis, sleep apnoea, a small midface, tubes chosen by weight and difficult veins, and bilateral lengthenings. Blount disease in the adolescent comes with severe obesity and its airway. Congenital limb deficiency may come with other anomalies. Most of these patients have had operations before and will have many more: needle phobia, anticipatory anxiety and existing opioid use are common by the second frame, and a premedication plan (midazolam 0.5 mg/kg orally, max 20 mg, or clonidine 4 microg/kg) and the same anaesthetist where possible are worth arranging.

  2. 2 Set-up and the block decision

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The limb must be free from iliac crest to toes on a radiolucent table for several hours of assembly and screening.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Supraglottic airway for the fit school-age child having a single frame; tracheal tube for long bilateral cases, obesity and the dysplasias. Relaxant matters here in an unusual way: surgeons watch for a muscle twitch as each wire passes near a nerve, so no neuromuscular block during wire and pin placement, and a block that abolishes the twitch (and the post-operative nerve examination) should be discussed first. For a femoral lengthening a fascia iliaca or femoral block is useful and low-risk. For a tibial osteotomy the author’s default is no dense block. Warm actively: an exposed limb held in the air for three hours is a radiator.

  3. 3 Rings, wires and half-pins

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A circular frame is built from the bone outwards. Smooth 1.5-1.8 mm wires are drilled through skin, muscle and bone along known safe corridors, tensioned to about 100-130 kg across each ring, and supplemented by threaded half-pins. A rail uses half-pins only. The construct must be stable enough to walk on.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Repeated brief stimuli over one to two hours, each a skin-to-skin transfixion: a steady remifentanil infusion (0.1-0.2 microg/kg/min) or fentanyl increments suit it better than a long-acting opioid given early. Wires are placed with the muscle they cross on stretch (knee extended for anterior wires, flexed for posterior) so the joint keeps its range afterwards. A jump of the foot as a wire passes the fibular neck is the peroneal nerve, and the wire is re-sited. Cefazolin is repeated at 4 h.

  4. 4 Fibular osteotomy and nerve decompression

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The tibia cannot lengthen or change angle while the fibula is intact, so a segment is removed or cut through a lateral incision. For tibial deformity correction, and above all for valgus-to-varus correction or double-level lengthening, many surgeons decompress the common peroneal nerve at the fibular neck and release the anterior compartment fascia prophylactically.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Note whether this was done: a prophylactic fasciotomy lowers the compartment risk and changes how much weight breakthrough pain carries afterwards. Nerve lesions during lengthening are frequent enough to have their own literature, with double-level tibial lengthening and skeletal dysplasia at highest risk, and they recover when found early.

  5. 5 Corticotomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    With the frame on, the surgeon cuts the bone through a 1-2 cm incision using multiple drill holes joined by an osteotome, then twists the rings to complete the break. The technique is deliberately low-energy to spare periosteum and marrow vessels, because the regenerate grows from them.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A brief intense stimulus and the start of the bleeding, which is into the limb and small in the tibia, larger in the femur (5-10 mL/kg). Heat kills osteocytes, so the drill is irrigated and no tourniquet is used. Paracetamol 15 mg/kg IV (max 1 g) and, if the surgeon accepts NSAIDs, the first dose now.

  6. 6 Acute correction and the foot at the end

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Some deformity is corrected on the table and the frame locked; the rest is dialled in over weeks. The surgeon checks pulses, compartments and the position of the foot before leaving theatre.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    An acute angular correction of the proximal tibia stretches the peroneal nerve and can kink the anterior tibial artery at the interosseous membrane, and bleeding from the osteotomy goes into a closed anterior compartment. This is the reason for the no-block default: the child should wake able to dorsiflex the ankle and great toe, with sensation in the first web space, and that examination should be written down in recovery. Pain on passive toe flexion, or pain rising through a working analgesic regimen in the first 24 hours, is a compartment until proven otherwise; see compartment syndrome and fasciotomy.

  7. 7 The lengthening nail

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    For the femur in the older child, the canal is reamed, vent holes and drill holes are made at the osteotomy level, the nail is inserted (antegrade through the trochanter, avoiding the piriformis fossa in the skeletally immature), the osteotomy is completed and the nail locked. Distraction is by a hand-held magnet at home.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    An adolescent anaesthetic of about two hours with reaming, so 5-10 mL/kg of blood loss and a small embolic load, as on the femoral nailing card. The retrospective comparison of 192 such patients found general anaesthesia alone non-inferior to general anaesthesia with a block for recovery-room pain (maximum 3.8 against 3.3), with fewer discharge opioid tablets in the block group. A single-shot fascia iliaca block is therefore optional and cheap; a catheter is not needed. Nails spare the pin sites, not the stretch: the months that follow still hurt, and complications still occur in about one segment in three.

  8. 8 Emergence and the first three days

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the child out of bed on day 1, weight-bearing in a frame, and the family taught pin-site care and how to turn the struts.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cover wire ends, and warn recovery staff that a frame weighs 2-4 kg and tears sheets and skin. Morphine patient-controlled analgesia from 6-7 years (bolus 20 microg/kg, max 1 mg, lockout 5 min) or nurse-controlled below that; ketamine 0.1-0.2 mg/kg/h for 24-48 h in the opioid-experienced adolescent; regular paracetamol 15 mg/kg every 6 h (max 1 g, max 60 mg/kg/day); diazepam 0.1 mg/kg orally (max 5 mg) every 8 h as required for spasm. The first physiotherapy session is the pain peak of the admission and should be pre-medicated.

  9. 9 Distraction and consolidation: the months outside theatre

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    From day 5-7 the family turns the frame or applies the magnet three or four times a day. The surgeon reviews length, alignment, regenerate quality, joint range and nerves every one to two weeks, and slows or pauses distraction if bone or nerve falls behind.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is where the pain is, and four mechanisms need four answers. Muscle stretch and joint tightness: physiotherapy, splinting, diazepam at night. Pin sites: a red, painful, discharging site is infection, treated with oral antibiotics and not with more opioid. Nerve stretch: burning, hypersensitivity or new weakness is reported to the surgeon the same day, distraction is slowed, and gabapentin 5-10 mg/kg (max 300 mg per dose initially) up to three times daily or amitriptyline 0.2-0.5 mg/kg at night (max 25 mg) is started. Background ache: paracetamol, and an NSAID if allowed. Opioid use should fall after the first two weeks; an adolescent whose use is rising at week six needs review by a pain service, not a repeat prescription. You will also see these children again in theatre for wire exchange, pin-site debridement, manipulation of a stiff knee or correction of a strut, each a 20-40 minute anaesthetic in a child who dreads them.

  10. 10 Frame removal

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    When three of four cortices are visible on radiographs the frame comes off, wires are cut and pulled, pin tracks are curetted, and a cast or brace protects the regenerate, which can fracture.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A day case of 30-60 minutes with a supraglottic airway. Wire and pin removal is briefly very stimulating and the tracks ache for a day; fentanyl 1-2 microg/kg IV, surgeon infiltration of the pin sites, or single-shot popliteal and saphenous blocks at 0.1-0.2% (now that there is no fresh osteotomy and no compartment question) all work. Gentle handling: the bone is weakest now.

  11. 11 Guided growth and epiphysiodesis

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Through a 2-3 cm incision over the physis, located by image intensifier, the plate is laid on top of the periosteum and a screw placed in the epiphysis and one in the metaphysis; the periosteum and perichondral ring are left untouched so growth resumes when the plate is removed. Bilateral, and both femur and tibia, are common. Epiphysiodesis destroys the physis with a drill or transphyseal screws through stab incisions.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Ages 2-14 years, a supraglottic airway, tourniquets, 20-40 minutes per physis. Surgeon infiltration with ropivacaine 0.2% (up to 0.5 mL/kg per limb, total within 3 mg/kg), paracetamol, an NSAID and fentanyl 1 microg/kg IV. No block is needed, and a femoral block that weakens the quadriceps delays walking out of the day unit; a caudal is reasonable for bilateral surgery in a small child. Knee effusion and stiffness for a few days is the usual complaint. Plate removal a year or two later is the same case in reverse.

Pain plan

For the lengthening operation the acute mechanism is bone with muscle transfixion, and the chronic mechanism is retension. They need different plans and the first should not compromise the limb.

Acute phase. Femur: a single-shot fascia iliaca or femoral block (ropivacaine 0.2% 0.5 mL/kg, max 30 mL) with dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV (max 8 mg), then patient-controlled opioid for 24-48 hours. Tibia: systemic multimodal analgesia without a dense block, because an acute correction creates the two things a block hides, a compartment at risk and a nerve on stretch. Where a continuous technique is wanted (bilateral surgery, femur plus tibia, a previous bad experience), use dilute solutions (ropivacaine 0.1%), tell the ward that breakthrough pain is a surgical call, and get the surgeon’s agreement. Paediatric advisory opinion is that regional anaesthesia has not been shown to delay the diagnosis of compartment syndrome in children; the author’s caution here is a judgement about an operation that specifically stretches nerve and loads a compartment. The principles are in nerve injury and compartment syndrome under regional anaesthesia.

NSAIDs. Many limb reconstruction surgeons prohibit them throughout distraction for fear of a poor regenerate. The one randomised trial in children, of ibuprofen in fracture healing, found no impairment; there is no equivalent trial in distraction osteogenesis. The plan must therefore be agreed with the surgeon in advance, and if NSAIDs are excluded the paracetamol, the adjuvants and the opioid taper carry more weight.

Distraction phase. Written, outpatient, and owned by someone: regular paracetamol; a small supply of immediate-release opioid for physiotherapy days with a stated end date; diazepam or baclofen at night for spasm; gabapentin or amitriptyline at the first sign of neuropathic pain; antibiotics, not analgesics, for the painful pin site. Rebound in this operation is not a 2 am event but the second and third week, when the regimen written for an osteotomy has run out and the stretch is at its height.

Eight-plates and epiphysiodesis: infiltration, paracetamol 15 mg/kg and ibuprofen 10 mg/kg (max 400 mg) for three to five days, and one or two doses of oxycodone 0.1 mg/kg (max 5 mg) for the first night.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Nerve stretch. The earliest sign is often pain or altered sensation, not weakness. Slowing the rate and early decompression gave complete recovery in 74 of 76 lesions in the largest series; delay lengthened recovery.
  • Pin-site infection. Nearly universal at some point with frames; presents as new pain at one site.
  • Joint contracture and subluxation. Knee flexion contracture and equinus develop as muscle lags behind bone; these children return for manipulation, botulinum toxin, tendon lengthening or extension of the frame across the joint.
  • Regenerate fracture or premature consolidation. Either brings the child back for an unplanned anaesthetic: re-osteotomy, or a cast or rod.
  • Later anaesthesia with a frame on. No MRI for most frames, awkward positioning and cuff placement, and a limb that must be supported by the frame, not the foot.
  • Achondroplasia. Bilateral tibial and femoral, sometimes humeral, lengthening in stages over years; airway and cervical precautions each time, and an epidural is technically harder with spinal stenosis.
  • Blount disease. Early-onset (2-5 years) is eight-plates or a proximal tibial osteotomy; adolescent Blount is a 100 kg patient having a tibial osteotomy and frame, with sleep apnoea and a high compartment risk.
  • Upper limb. Humeral and forearm lengthening follow the same rules, with the radial nerve as the one that complains.
  • Psychology. Months in a frame bring low mood, sleep loss and school absence. A child who is not coping reports more pain; the answer includes a psychologist.

Key sources

The pain course during distraction rests on one small prospective paediatric study from 1994 that every later description echoes. Nerve lesion data come from a single high-volume centre. Comparisons of nails with fixators are retrospective and confounded by age. The only analgesic comparison in this field is a retrospective series of femoral nails. NSAID safety is extrapolated from a fracture trial. The no-dense-block default for tibial osteotomy with acute correction, the adjuvant choices and their doses are the author’s judgement and standard paediatric formulary practice. General paediatric points are in the child having orthopaedic surgery.
