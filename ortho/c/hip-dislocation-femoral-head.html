81 Hip dislocation reduction and femoral head fracture fixation

Emergency closed reduction of the native traumatic hip dislocation, the irreducible hip and open reduction, and fixation or excision of Pipkin femoral head fractures through an anterior approach or a surgical dislocation

3Moderate

Dominant mechanism: Capsule. The injury tore the capsule, the labrum and the short rotators and bruised the head against the rim; reduction relieves most of that pain within minutes. Fixation of the head then adds an approach through the hip muscles, a second capsulotomy and screws in subchondral bone, with a trochanteric osteotomy if a surgical dislocation is used. Tell the patient
Once the hip is back in its socket most of the severe pain goes straight away; the hip stays bruised and sore to move for a week or two, and if the ball of the joint needs fixing the wound and the deep ache are worst over the first two days.
The trap
This is a time-critical reduction dressed up as a short case: the odds of avascular necrosis are more than five times higher when the hip is reduced after 12 h, a failed sedation attempt is the usual source of delay, and the sciatic nerve must be examined and written down before the anaesthetic and again after it, because nobody can later tell an injury palsy from a reduction palsy

What the surgeon is doing and why

A native hip is a deep, congruent ball and socket held by the strongest capsule in the body, so it takes a great deal of energy to dislocate it. The classic mechanism is a knee against a dashboard with the hip flexed and adducted: the femur is driven backwards, the head tears out through the posterior capsule and comes to rest behind the acetabulum, and the leg lies short, flexed, adducted and internally rotated. About nine in ten are posterior. On its way out the head can shear a fragment off itself against the rim (a Pipkin fracture), break off the posterior wall, and stretch the sciatic nerve, which lies directly behind the joint.

The surgeon’s first objective is to get the head back in, and quickly. While the head is out, the vessels that run along the neck to supply it are kinked and stretched, and the cartilage is deprived of synovial fluid. In the meta-analysis the odds of avascular necrosis were 5.6 times higher for hips reduced after 12 h than for those reduced earlier, and most units work to a target of 6 h from injury. Reduction also takes the head off the sciatic nerve, which is the only treatment that nerve has. The manoeuvre is simple: traction in line with the deformity with the pelvis held down, then gentle rotation, until the head climbs over the rim. What makes it fail is muscle. The gluteals, the short rotators, the hamstrings and iliopsoas are in spasm around a displaced joint, and the surgeon is pulling against them with the patient on a trolley. That is the anaesthetist’s whole contribution to the first operation: abolish the muscle tone, so that one controlled pull works and nobody levers on the femoral neck.

The second objective, hours to days later, is to deal with what the dislocation broke. After reduction a CT shows whether the joint is concentric, whether fragments are trapped in it, and the size and position of any head fracture. A Pipkin I fragment lies below the fovea, outside the weight-bearing dome, and a small one is excised; a Pipkin II fragment lies above the fovea and carries weight, so it is reduced anatomically and held with countersunk or headless screws; Pipkin III adds a femoral neck fracture and Pipkin IV an acetabular fracture. The approach is chosen by where the fragment is: an anterior approach with the patient supine reaches the usual anteromedial fragment directly, a surgical dislocation through a trochanteric flip shows everything and, in the review of approaches, had the lowest complication rate, and a posterior approach is used when a posterior wall needs fixing in the same sitting.

The pain logic has two phases. Before reduction the pain is extreme and is a stretched capsule and a head sitting on muscle and nerve; it answers poorly to opioids and completely to reduction. After fixation the pain is that of a hip approach, a capsulotomy and a little subchondral metalwork: capsule-dominant, sharp with rotation, a 3 that falls over a week.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 The trauma bay: examine the nerve, find the other injuries

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon confirms the dislocation on a pelvic radiograph, looks specifically for a femoral neck fracture (which makes closed reduction dangerous and changes the plan to open reduction), examines the knee on the same side, and documents the sciatic nerve.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The energy that dislocates a hip injures other things: the knee that hit the dashboard (patella, posterior cruciate ligament), the femoral shaft, the pelvis, the chest, the head and the cervical spine. Treat the patient as a trauma patient who happens to need a ten-minute procedure. Before any sedative or anaesthetic, test and write down ankle and great toe dorsiflexion, plantarflexion and sensation on the dorsum and sole of the foot; the peroneal division is the part usually affected, so a weak dorsiflexion is the sign. In the systematic review, sciatic palsy complicated about 4% of femoral head fracture-dislocations. A deficit found only after reduction cannot be attributed, and a new deficit after reduction means entrapment and an urgent exploration.

  2. 2 Where and how: sedation or theatre

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the earliest successful reduction with the fewest attempts. Repeated forceful attempts under inadequate relaxation damage cartilage and can fracture the femoral neck, a complication reported after emergency department reductions.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    In the cohort of 67 native dislocations, half were reduced under procedural sedation and about a quarter of the sedation attempts failed and went on to general anaesthesia; a hip that had been out for more than 6 h was nearly 20 times more likely to fail sedation. The reasoning for the author’s default follows: if theatre and an anaesthetist are available within the time it would take to organise safe sedation, go straight to general anaesthesia with paralysis; accept one attempt under deep propofol sedation only when that is clearly faster, the patient is otherwise uninjured and the hip has been out for a short time; and do not allow a second sedation attempt. Do not let the case queue behind elective work. It is an emergency of the same order as a threatened limb.

  3. 3 Induction and relaxation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs the hip muscles completely slack for one or two minutes.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The patient is unfasted by definition (injury and opioids stopped gastric emptying), so a rapid sequence induction with a tracheal tube is the default, with manual in-line stabilisation if the cervical spine is not cleared. Suxamethonium gives dense relaxation for exactly the right duration; rocuronium at an intubating dose with sugammadex available does the same. Check the twitch response is abolished before telling the surgeon to pull, because a reduction attempted through partial relaxation is the failed attempt you are trying to avoid. A spinal gives excellent relaxation and suits the rare isolated, fasted, stable patient, but it takes time, needs the patient positioned on a dislocated hip, and delays the post-reduction nerve check. Anterior dislocations (leg extended and externally rotated) need the same relaxation and a different manoeuvre.

  4. 4 The reduction manoeuvre

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    With the patient supine and an assistant pressing down on both anterior superior iliac spines, the surgeon flexes the hip and knee to 90 degrees and pulls upward in line with the femur, adding gentle internal and external rotation until the head clears the rim and reduces with a palpable clunk. The surgeon may stand on the table or trolley to do it.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Lower the trolley, lock the brakes, and guard the airway and lines: the whole patient lifts with the pull. A person standing on the table is at risk of falling. If two attempts with full paralysis fail, the hip is irreducible and the next step is open reduction now, not a third pull.

  5. 5 Stability check, imaging and the second nerve examination

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    With the patient still relaxed the surgeon tests stability (flexion to 90 degrees with slight adduction and internal rotation and a posterior push) and takes radiographs or uses the image intensifier to confirm a concentric joint. A hip that redislocates easily has a posterior wall defect and may need skeletal traction or early fixation.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Keep the patient anaesthetised until the surgeon has finished the stability examination; it is done once and done properly. Wake the patient with the leg in extension, slight abduction and external rotation, with a pillow between the knees. Repeat the foot examination as soon as commands are obeyed and record it next to the first. Then a CT, which the surgeon wants within hours.

  6. 6 The irreducible hip and open reduction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A hip that will not reduce is blocked by something: the head buttonholed through the capsule or the short rotators, piriformis or a labral flap in the way, or a fragment in the socket. The surgeon opens the hip urgently, usually through a posterior approach, clears the obstruction and reduces under vision. A non-concentric reduction with a trapped fragment is the same problem on a slightly slower clock.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The short case has become a hip operation in the lateral position in an unfasted trauma patient, so be ready for it from the start: a second cannula, a tube and not a supraglottic airway, and theatre staff who know the lateral supports may be needed. Cefazolin 2 g IV before the incision. The sciatic nerve is in the posterior field and may be tented over the head.

  7. 7 Definitive surgery for the femoral head: approach

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Days later, on a planned list, the surgeon fixes or excises the head fragment. An anterior (Smith-Petersen or Hueter) approach with the patient supine goes between sartorius and tensor fascia lata and opens the capsule over the anteromedial fragment. A surgical dislocation with the patient lateral uses the trochanteric flip described on the proximal femoral osteotomy card. A Kocher-Langenbeck approach is used when the posterior wall is fixed at the same time, as on the pelvic and acetabular card.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    General anaesthesia; a fascia iliaca block placed after the documented examination covers the anterior capsule and the anterior incision well. The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve crosses the anterior approach and is often numb afterwards, which the patient should hear from you as expected. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV at induction.

  8. 8 Fragment fixation or excision

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The fragment is reduced against the head under direct vision so the cartilage is flush, and held with two or three small screws buried beneath the cartilage surface. A small inferior fragment that blocks motion is removed. Loose bodies are washed out of the socket.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The surgeon needs the hip subluxed or dislocated to see the fragment, so relaxation is requested again. If a surgical dislocation is used the same two cautions apply as on that card: do not run the patient hypotensive when head bleeding is checked, and keep the knee flexed while the hip is out to spare the sciatic nerve.

  9. 9 Closure and aftercare

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Capsule and, where used, the trochanteric osteotomy are repaired. Most surgeons prescribe touch weight-bearing for six to eight weeks, hip precautions against the position of dislocation, and some give indometacin or another NSAID against heterotopic ossification, which followed about one in six head fractures in the review.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The trajectory on this card is 3 on the first evening, 5 on day 1 as the block recedes, 4, 3 and then 2 at a week. A third foot examination in recovery. Chemoprophylaxis for venous thromboembolism starts when the bleeding risk allows.

Pain plan

Before reduction, the plan is speed. Titrated intravenous opioid and, if there will be any wait, a fascia iliaca block takes the edge off, but the block is placed only after the sciatic examination is written down, and it does not replace the relaxation needed for the reduction. The femoral and obturator nerves supply the anterior capsule; the posterior capsule and the stretched sciatic nerve are sacral plexus, so no anterior block makes a dislocated hip comfortable. After a closed reduction of a simple dislocation, paracetamol, an anti-inflammatory and a few doses of oral opioid over 48 h are usually enough; pain that stays severe after reduction means a non-concentric joint, a trapped fragment or a missed fracture, and is a reason to look at the CT again.

For fixation of the head, the defensible default is general anaesthesia, a single-shot supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block (30-40 mL of ropivacaine 0.2-0.25%), dexamethasone 8 mg IV, paracetamol, and the surgeon’s capsular and wound infiltration, followed by regular paracetamol, an anti-inflammatory where the surgeon agrees, and an oral opioid for three to five nights. This is the author’s judgement from the mechanism: the pain is capsular and from a hip approach, comparable to other open hip procedures in young adults, and no analgesic trial exists for this injury. No catheter is needed for a 3. If the same anaesthetic includes acetabular fixation, the pelvic card’s plan, with its stronger case for a continuous technique, takes over.

Avoid long-acting sciatic or dense neuraxial blocks in the first 24 h: the sciatic nerve is the structure at risk from the injury, the reduction and the posterior approach, and at least partial recovery in 60-70% of injured nerves depends on recognising and decompressing the ones that are still trapped. Neuropathic pain in the foot after a sciatic injury is treated early with the usual agents and is referred on, because it outlasts everything else on this card.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The femoral neck fracture hiding under the dislocation. A neck fracture, displaced or not, forbids closed manipulation; the surgeon looks for it on the first film. A neck fracture produced by the reduction converts a salvageable hip into an arthroplasty question in a young patient.
  • Polytrauma. The hip competes with a laparotomy, a chest or a head. The reduction takes two minutes under an anaesthetic the patient is already having, and should be asked for at the earliest safe point in the sequence; see polytrauma sequencing.
  • Posterior wall fracture. Common with posterior dislocation; a large fragment makes the hip unstable after reduction and brings a pelvic operation.
  • Ipsilateral knee injury. The knee took the blow; examine it before pulling on it.
  • Pipkin III. Head and neck both fractured: fixation fails often, and in older patients the surgeon goes to arthroplasty.
  • The dislocated hip replacement. A different patient and a different problem: older, low energy, no vascular clock for a metal head, though the sciatic nerve and the skin still argue for promptness. A brief general anaesthetic with a relaxant or deep sedation with propofol is the usual choice; the arthroplasty cards cover the causes and the revision. See posterior hip arthroplasty and revision hip arthroplasty.
  • Late presentations. A hip out for days has shortened muscles and scar in the socket; closed reduction usually fails and open reduction is planned.
  • Later problems that return to you. Avascular necrosis (about one in eight after a head fracture) and post-traumatic arthritis (one in five) bring these patients back for arthroplasty within a few years, often with heterotopic bone and retained screws.
