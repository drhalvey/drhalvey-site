25 Hip arthroscopy: FAI and labral repair

Femoroacetabular impingement: cam and pincer resection, labral repair or debridement, capsulotomy and repair, with the hip in traction over a perineal post

2Mild to moderate

Dominant mechanism: Capsule. An interportal or T-shaped capsulotomy through the iliofemoral ligament, a labrum sutured back to a decorticated acetabular rim, and a femoral neck burred to reshape the cam; plus the joint distracted for an hour against a perineal post and the soft tissues filled with irrigation fluid. Capsule and labrum dominate; the bone work is a burr on a neck, not a reaming

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: moderate.

What the surgeon is doing and why

Femoroacetabular impingement is a hip whose ball and socket are the wrong shape for each other. A cam lesion is a bump on the femoral head-neck junction that is driven into the acetabular rim in flexion and internal rotation, shearing the cartilage off the rim from the inside; a pincer lesion is an over-covering acetabular rim that the neck strikes, crushing the labrum between them. Either way the labrum, the fibrocartilaginous ring that seals the joint and holds the fluid film, is torn, and the patient (a young adult, often an athlete, often a woman) has groin pain in flexion and a positive impingement test. The surgeon’s aim is to reshape the bone so that the two no longer collide and to repair the labrum so that it seals again. If the cartilage is already gone the operation does not work, which is why the surgeon is selective and why some of these patients come back years later for a total hip.

The whole operation is done through two or three portals with the joint pulled apart. The sequence:

  1. Traction. The hip is a deep ball-and-socket under negative pressure, and the surgeon cannot get a scope between the head and the acetabulum unless the joint is distracted 8-10 mm. That is done on a traction table against a perineal post, with the foot in a boot, and checked on the fluoroscope: the seal of the joint has to be broken (the vacuum sign) before the space opens, which takes a considerable force. The central compartment work (labrum, rim, cartilage) is all done under traction.
  2. Portals and capsulotomy. The anterolateral portal is placed under fluoroscopy, then an anterior or mid-anterior portal, and the two are joined by an interportal capsulotomy through the iliofemoral ligament, 2-4 cm long, to give the instruments room. Some surgeons extend it into a T down the neck. The capsulotomy is what makes the operation possible and what the patient feels afterwards; most surgeons now close or plicate it at the end because an unrepaired capsulotomy in a loose-jointed young woman produces instability.
  3. Rim and labrum. The pincer rim is burred back; the labrum is detached or elevated, the rim decorticated to bleeding bone, and the labrum sutured back with two to four anchors so that it seals against the head again. An irreparable labrum is debrided or, in a few hands, reconstructed with a graft.
  4. Traction off, cam resection. The hip is flexed, which exposes the head-neck junction in the peripheral compartment without traction, and the cam is burred away with a motorised burr until the head is round on the fluoroscope through a range of positions. Too much resection and the neck fractures; too little and the impingement persists, which is the commonest reason for a revision scope.
  5. Add-ons. A psoas tenotomy for a snapping or impinging iliopsoas, a trochanteric bursectomy or gluteal tendon repair through the peritrochanteric space, a ligamentum teres debridement. Each adds time and fluid.
  6. Capsular closure, portal closure, and home.

The anaesthetic reading. This is an operation whose pain is a 2 and whose hazards are all mechanical, and they come in three groups. The traction, applied against a perineal post, loads the pudendal nerve, the perineal skin and the genitals, and stretches the sciatic and peroneal nerves; in the large prospective series pudendal numbness occurred in 1.4%, foot sensory loss in 0.8%, and traction beyond 60 minutes raised the whole complication rate. The irrigation pump, run at high flow through a capsulotomy into the soft tissues of the hip, puts litres into the thigh and, in the reported cases, along the psoas into the retroperitoneum and the peritoneal cavity; abdominal compartment syndrome has been reported perhaps a dozen times, with a cardiac arrest, laparotomies and one paracentesis that drained 3 L, and the common features were a long case, a capsulotomy and a psoas release. And the surgeon’s requests for a low pressure and a relaxed patient are requests made of you for the surgeon’s view and the surgeon’s traction, and they are reasonable in a young patient lying flat and unreasonable at the extremes.

The pain logic: capsule is the dominant mechanism (a cut through the thickest ligament in the body, a labrum sutured to a raw rim), then the bone of the cam resection, which is a burr on a neck and not a reaming, and under both the traction and the fluid. It is sharp with movement and settles in days; it is not re-provoked by loading, because the patient is on crutches and the labrum is not weight-bearing. A 2 on the Part B scale, and in the 179-procedure cohort hip arthroscopy does not appear in the 40 highest-scoring operations. The young, female, opioid-naive population is the one with the highest nausea risk and, in the ambulatory block data, the highest rebound risk, which is an argument for an antiemetic plan and an oral regimen started before discharge rather than for a block.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 General anaesthesia, the airway, and the block question decided in advance

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs 60-150 minutes of a motionless, relaxed patient on a traction table with a C-arm over the pelvis, and a patient who can go home on crutches that afternoon.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    General anaesthesia with a tracheal tube and neuromuscular block is the author’s default: the surgeon’s request for relaxation at the traction step is a request for muscle relaxation (a tense hip needs more force to distract, and more force is more pudendal and sciatic injury), a tube protects the airway for a long case with the head away from you and a C-arm swinging over the chest, and the end of the case needs an abdominal examination before extubation. A spinal is a reasonable option for the patient who wants one, sized to two hours, with the understanding that a sedated spontaneously breathing patient under a traction and a pump is harder to assess at the end than a ventilated one; the one reported abdominal compartment syndrome under a spinal declared itself as dyspnoea and abdominal pain in the final minutes. The block decision belongs here and is covered in the pain plan; the short version is no fascia iliaca block, a PENG only for the selected patient, and surgeon-delivered infiltration for everyone.

  2. 2 Positioning on the traction table: the post, the boots and the leg

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Distraction needs a counter-force, which is the perineal post against the medial thigh; a large, well-padded post, lateralised against the operative thigh rather than centred on the perineum, spreads the load away from the pudendal nerve. The operative foot goes into a boot with the leg in slight flexion, abduction and internal rotation; the other leg goes into the contralateral boot in abduction to make room for the C-arm, or is left free.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The post is the pudendal nerve: in the pooled series pudendal injury occurred in 4.3% of patients where a post was used and 0.5% where it was not, all resolving within three months. Check that the post is padded, that it sits on the thigh and not the perineum, and that the genitals are clear of it and not trapped; in a man the scrotum and penis go away from the post and are looked at again after the traction is on. The boots are the feet: a loose boot allows the foot to slip and the traction to be applied through the ankle, a tight boot is a skin and a nerve problem; the contralateral foot in a boot under counter-traction is at the same risk. Arms across the chest on a rest, out of the C-arm’s path. Then the thing nobody else will do: look at the whole patient from the end of the table once the traction is on, because the pelvis will have shifted against the post and the head and the arms will have moved with it.

  3. 3 Traction applied and the vacuum broken

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Traction is increased until the joint seal breaks (seen as a crescent of air on the fluoroscope) and the joint space opens 8-10 mm. A stiff, muscular or guarded hip needs more force, and the surgeon cannot work in the central compartment with less than full distraction.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the moment the surgeon asks for relaxation, and under general anaesthesia the answer is neuromuscular block, because a relaxed hip distracts with less force and the force is the injury. Start the traction clock. The series are consistent that traction time predicts complications: over 60 minutes the rate rose significantly, and hips with pudendal neurapraxia had an hour of traction against 45 minutes in those without. The surgeon’s practice is to release traction when moving to the peripheral compartment and to re-apply it only if needed; your practice is to say the time out loud at 45 minutes and every 15 minutes after that, which is a request that every experienced hip arthroscopist will understand. The pressure often rises as the traction goes on and the pelvis loads the post; a young patient tolerates it and it is not a reason to deepen.

  4. 4 Portals, capsulotomy and the irrigation pump

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Portals under fluoroscopy, then the interportal capsulotomy to create working space. The irrigation pump is set at 40-60 mmHg and flows continuously; the capsulotomy means the fluid is no longer contained in the joint.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve runs across the anterior portal site and is disturbed in 16.5% of patients in the prospective series, persisting at six months in 1.6%; it is a portal injury, not a traction one, and the patient will report numbness over the anterolateral thigh. The capsulotomy opens the hip to the thigh and the pump fills it: from this point the thigh swells, the fluid tracks where the anatomy lets it, and the two reported routes to the abdomen are along the psoas into the retroperitoneum and from there into the peritoneum. Warm the irrigation or the patient, because 20 L of room-temperature fluid through the hip over two hours is the cooling system that the hypothermia in two of the reported compartment syndrome cases reflects. The surgeon’s request for a systolic of 90-100 for a clear view is reasonable in a flat, young, well patient, which is who is on the table; it is not reasonable in the older patient with hypertension having a scope for a different indication.

  5. 5 Rim trimming, labral takedown and repair under traction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The pincer rim is burred back, the labrum elevated, the rim decorticated to bleeding bone, and the labrum sutured back with anchors so that it seals against the femoral head. This is the longest part of the central compartment work and all of it is under traction.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The capsule and labrum mechanism is created here: a labrum sutured onto raw bone and a capsule held open by instruments, which the patient will feel on every flexion for a week. The anchors are implants, which is the reason for the antibiotic. Bone dust and bleeding from the decorticated rim fog the view, and this is the step that produces the request for a lower pressure and a higher pump; the pump is the surgeon’s lever. The traction clock is running; this step is where it is most often forgotten.

  6. 6 Traction released, cam resection in the peripheral compartment

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The joint is reduced, the hip flexed to 30-45 degrees to relax the anterior capsule and expose the head-neck junction, and the cam burred away under fluoroscopic and arthroscopic control until the head is spherical through flexion and rotation. The neck must not be over-resected (a femoral neck fracture after a scope is a resection of more than about 30% of the neck diameter, and the patient is told to protect it on crutches for that reason) and the retinacular vessels that supply the head run along the posterosuperior neck and are kept out of the field.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Traction off is the moment the perineum and the feet get their blood back; note the total traction time. The cam resection is the bone mechanism, a burr on a neck rather than a reaming, and in a young patient it is well tolerated. The flexed leg in a boot is a different loading of the sciatic nerve and the peroneal nerve at the fibular neck; the knee is checked. This is where the case runs long if the surgeon is thorough, and the fluid keeps running.

  7. 7 Add-ons: psoas release, peritrochanteric work and the fluid problem

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A psoas tenotomy at the lesser trochanter or through the capsule treats a snapping or impinging iliopsoas; trochanteric bursectomy, iliotibial band release and gluteus medius repair are done in the peritrochanteric space with the leg abducted and the traction off; ligamentum teres debridement in the central compartment.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The psoas release is the step most consistently present in the abdominal compartment syndrome reports, because it opens the fluid’s path along the psoas into the retroperitoneum; four of the seven cases reviewed had one. The peritrochanteric space is a large potential space that holds litres. From this point the examination is the abdomen: distension, a tense wall, rising airway pressures on the ventilator, a pressure that falls without a reason in the field, a core temperature that drops. The treatment of the established syndrome is drainage (a paracentesis drained 3 L in one case) or a laparotomy; the prevention is a surgeon who knows the clock and a pump that is not run at pressures and flows that the anatomy cannot drain. Say the fluid volume out loud when the bags are changed, and say the deficit if the pump displays one.

  8. 8 Capsular closure, portal closure and the abdomen before the drapes come off

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The capsulotomy is closed or plicated with two or three sutures (more in the patient with laxity), the portals closed, and a dressing applied; the leg comes out of the boot and the post is removed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Before the drapes come off and before any plan to extubate, examine the abdomen: a soft, flat abdomen with normal airway pressures and a normal temperature is the common finding, and anything else is not extubated until it is explained. Look at the perineum and the genitals for the pressure marks and the oedema the post leaves; look at both feet. Give the antiemetic. A patient under a spinal who complains of abdominal pain or breathlessness at this point has declared the diagnosis and the ultrasound probe is the next step, not reassurance.

  9. 9 Recovery, the oral regimen and home

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the patient home on crutches with partial weight bearing for two to six weeks depending on what was done, with hip flexion limited for a repaired labrum and a closed capsule, and a physiotherapy programme that starts within days.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The pain in recovery is capsular and sharp with movement, and settles with the first doses of paracetamol and an NSAID given before the patient leaves, plus a few oral opioid doses for the first two nights. Nausea in this population is the thing that stops discharge; the dexamethasone and ondansetron at induction and emergence are not optional. The anterolateral thigh numbness and the perineal numbness are expected and are explained before they are reported, because a young woman who wakes with a numb perineum and was not told has had a bad day. A quadriceps that will not hold the patient’s weight on the first standing attempt is a block, if one was given, and is the reason the fascia iliaca block was abandoned for this operation. The thigh will be swollen with fluid for a day or two; a thigh that is tense, with pain out of proportion and a numb foot, is a compartment syndrome of the thigh, reported after this operation, and is the surgeon’s problem now.

Pain plan

The mechanism is a capsulotomy, a labrum sutured to a raw rim and a burred femoral neck, in a joint distracted for an hour and filled with fluid: sharp with movement, settling in days, not re-provoked by loading. That is a 2, and the plan is built for a day case: paracetamol and an NSAID started before discharge and continued for a week, a few oral opioid doses for the first two nights, dexamethasone and ondansetron for the nausea that this population produces, and surgeon-delivered local anaesthetic into the joint and the portals at the end of the case.

The block question has a clear negative and an open positive. The negative is the fascia iliaca block: in the randomised sham-controlled trial of 80 patients having arthroscopy for impingement, a pre-operative fascia iliaca block changed neither the pain scores nor the opioid use over 24 hours, and produced more quadriceps weakness and more falls, and the authors could not recommend it; a femoral nerve block carries the same motor cost for the same absent benefit. The open positive is the PENG block: in the pooled trials across hip surgery it gave the same pain scores as fascia iliaca with less opioid at 24 hours, and in the one hip arthroscopy trial that combined it with a lateral femoral cutaneous nerve block it gave lower pain scores and less quadriceps weakness than fascia iliaca, but it is being compared with a block that does not work, and the trials that report motor function after PENG find quadriceps weakness in over a quarter of patients in some series. The author’s practice is no routine block: infiltration for everyone, and a single-shot PENG for the patient who predicts a bad course (chronic pain, a previous bad day-case night, opioid tolerance) or the larger operation (capsular plication, gluteal tendon repair, labral reconstruction), with the patient told that the leg may not hold them for the first evening. That is a judgement, not an evidence statement; there is no PROSPECT guideline for hip arthroscopy and the nearest one, for total hip arthroplasty, addresses a different mechanism.

No catheter. Rebound is moderate because the population is young, female and opioid-naive, which are the ambulatory block predictors, but the thing that wears off is infiltration rather than a dense block, and the first oral doses go in before discharge. Escalation triggers: a thigh that is tense and swollen with pain out of proportion is a thigh compartment syndrome; perineal or anterolateral thigh numbness is expected and settles; abdominal pain or distension at home on the evening of surgery is the compartment syndrome that was missed in theatre and needs an emergency department, not a phone call.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Traction time is the complication rate. Over 60 minutes the rate rises; the surgeon’s practice of releasing traction for the peripheral compartment and your practice of calling the time are the two controls.
  • The perineal post. Padded, large, lateralised onto the thigh; the genitals checked before and after traction. Post-free traction systems exist and the pooled pudendal injury rate without a post is a tenth of the rate with one.
  • Lateral decubitus. Some surgeons scope the hip on its side with traction applied through a boot on a lateral distractor; the airway access, the dependent arm and the post are the lateral-position issues, and the fluid tracks differently.
  • The obese patient. Harder to distract, longer traction, more force, a deeper wound for the fluid to fill; many surgeons will not scope above a BMI of about 35.
  • Fluid extravasation and the abdomen. The capsulotomy, the psoas release, the long case and the high-flow pump are the reported associations; the signs are distension, airway pressure, hypotension and hypothermia; the examination before extubation is the prevention of the catastrophe, and the hernia in the history is a reason to examine twice.
  • Thigh compartment syndrome. Reported after fluid extravasation into the thigh; a tense thigh with disproportionate pain in recovery.
  • The request for hypotension. Reasonable in a flat young patient; the pump pressure is the surgeon’s lever before yours.
  • Hypothermia. Litres of room-temperature irrigation over two hours; forced-air warming from the start and the fluid warmed where the pump allows.
  • The female athlete on the pill. Long traction, immobility on crutches and oestrogen: the one day-case arthroscopy population where chemoprophylaxis is discussed rather than dismissed.
  • Revision arthroscopy and conversion. Scar makes the capsule harder to open and the distraction harder; a scope that fails for adhesions or cartilage loss ends up as a periacetabular osteotomy or a total hip in the following years, and the surgeon will often say so at the time.
  • The dysplastic hip. A labral tear in a shallow socket is the symptom, not the disease; a scope that repairs the labrum without addressing the dysplasia makes it worse, and the right operation is the periacetabular osteotomy.

Key sources

The complication data are good: a 1,615-hip prospective multicentre series with a validated grading scheme and a pooled analysis of pudendal injury. The abdominal compartment syndrome literature is case reports, perhaps a dozen, and the card’s advice to examine the abdomen before extubation is drawn from their common features rather than from any trial. The block evidence is one well-designed negative randomised trial for fascia iliaca and a small, heterogeneous and early literature for PENG; the card’s no-routine-block default is the author’s reading of that and is labelled as such. There is no procedure-specific analgesic guideline.
