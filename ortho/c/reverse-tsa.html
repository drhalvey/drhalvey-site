2 Reverse total shoulder arthroplasty

For cuff tear arthropathy, massive irreparable cuff tears, fracture and revision

4Severe

Dominant mechanism: Joint re-tensioned. The arm is deliberately lengthened by 2-3 cm to tension the deltoid, which then carries the whole load of elevation and is stretched with every movement; underneath that is bone (humeral osteotomy, glenoid reaming, baseplate screws, canal preparation) and the stretch on the axillary nerve and plexus that lengthening produces

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Block
Interscalene brachial plexus block, single shot, with intravenous dexamethasone
Catheter: selective The threshold for a catheter is lower than for an anatomic replacement because the deltoid is loaded from day 1; a continuous technique is the defensible choice for revision, conversion of a failed arthroplasty, fracture, opioid-tolerant or chronic pain patients, and the patient who has had a bad night after a single-shot block before. Single shot with dexamethasone and a written night plan is the default for primary cuff arthropathy in a counselled patient
Alternatives: Superior trunk block (phrenic sparing, equivalent analgesia at 24 h); Suprascapular plus axillary nerve blocks (phrenic sparing, weaker, and the axillary component matters more here than in any other shoulder operation); Surgeon-delivered periarticular infiltration as a supplement
Position
Beach chair, 45-70 degrees, head fixed, operative arm free on a support or held by an assistant; some surgeons work at a lower incline for the anterosuperior approach
Tourniquet
No

Tell the patient
The arm will be numb and heavy into the night; the shoulder then aches deeply for several days because the arm has been made slightly longer to tighten the main shoulder muscle, and lifting the arm uses that muscle from the first week.
The trap
The reduction is the moment: lengthening the arm by 2-3 cm pulls on the axillary nerve and the plexus and drops the blood pressure on every trial reduction, and a deltoid that does not work afterwards is either the block, a nerve stretched by the lengthening, or an acromial stress fracture weeks later; document deltoid function and regimental badge sensation before the block

What the surgeon is doing and why

An anatomic replacement works only while the rotator cuff centres the humeral head on the glenoid. Take the cuff away and the deltoid, pulling almost vertically, drags the head upwards into the acromion instead of lifting the arm: the patient shrugs and cannot elevate (pseudoparalysis), the unroofed head wears the undersurface of the acromion and the glenoid into a single eroded socket (cuff tear arthropathy), and an anatomic implant in that shoulder rocks its glenoid loose within a few years. The reverse geometry solves the mechanical problem rather than the anatomical one. The ball goes on the glenoid side (a glenosphere on a screwed-in baseplate) and the socket goes on the humerus (a polyethylene insert on a tray or stem). That does two things. It moves the centre of rotation medially, onto the face of the glenoid, so that the deltoid works with a longer lever arm and the forces on the baseplate are compressive rather than shearing. And it lets the surgeon drop the humerus distally, lengthening the arm by two to three centimetres, so that the deltoid is put on stretch and more of its fibres are recruited into elevation. A tensioned deltoid is now the engine and the stabiliser of the joint; the implant is semi-constrained and stays reduced mainly because the deltoid compresses it. That one sentence is the mechanism difference from the anatomic card and it drives the pain profile, the nerve risk, the instability pattern and the stress fracture.

The indications have widened from Grammont’s original cuff arthropathy in the elderly to massive irreparable tears with pseudoparalysis, proximal humerus fractures in older patients (see proximal humerus fracture), inflammatory arthritis, osteoarthritis with so much posterior glenoid wear that an anatomic glenoid cannot be seated, failed anatomic or hemiarthroplasty, and increasingly primary osteoarthritis in patients over 70 with an intact cuff, on the argument that the cuff would fail before the implant did. The population is therefore older, frailer and more comorbid than the anatomic population, and a good proportion are revisions.

The operation in order:

  1. Get in. The deltopectoral interval is the common route, with the subscapularis taken down if it exists; many cuff arthropathy shoulders have no functioning subscapularis to take down. Some surgeons use an anterosuperior approach that splits the anterior deltoid off the acromion instead, which gives a direct view of the glenoid in a stiff shoulder at the cost of cutting into the muscle that the whole construct depends on.
  2. Cut the head off, lower and in less retroversion than for an anatomic replacement, to make room for the humeral socket to sit distal to the glenosphere.
  3. Expose the glenoid completely, including the inferior neck of the scapula, which means a circumferential capsular release with the axillary nerve running under the inferior capsule. Ream, seat the baseplate low and tilted slightly inferiorly, and fix it with a central peg or screw and peripheral locking screws driven into the scapular body. Impact the glenosphere onto the baseplate taper.
  4. Prepare the humerus (stem, short stem or stemless; press-fit or cemented), assemble a trial tray and insert, and reduce.
  5. Set the tension. This is the judgement at the heart of the operation. The surgeon pulls on the arm, reduces the trial, and assesses how hard it was to reduce, how taut the conjoint tendon is, whether the joint gapes when distracted and whether the arm hangs at the right length. Too loose dislocates; too tight stretches the nerves, raises the acromial stress and limits motion. Insert thickness and stem seating are adjusted until the surgeon is satisfied, and each adjustment is another dislocation and reduction.
  6. Fix the definitive components, decide about the subscapularis (surgeons vary: some repair it for stability, some leave it, some cannot), close, sling.

The pain logic follows. The dominant mechanism is retension: the arm has been lengthened and the deltoid is on stretch from the moment of the final reduction, and unlike the subscapularis after an anatomic replacement nobody can rest it, because every elevation of the arm from the first physiotherapy session is the deltoid doing its new job. That produces the deep, aching, stiffness-type pain that the Part B table assigns to re-tensioned joints, with night pain and a slower fall than pure bone pain. Bone contributes the first 48 hours: humeral osteotomy, glenoid reaming, screws into the scapular body, and canal preparation or metaphyseal impaction. Nerve contributes a flavour of its own: subclinical axillary and plexus lesions on electromyography were found in 9 of 19 reverse arthroplasties at 3.6 weeks in the one prospective study, mainly axillary, mostly resolving, against one lesion in 23 anatomic replacements, and the mean arm lengthening in that series was 2.7 cm. Dysaesthesia in the regimental badge area or a burning quality to the pain in the first weeks is that, not the block.

One correction to the folk wisdom, and it runs the opposite way to the one on the anatomic card. The reverse is often said to be the more painful arthroplasty, and the mechanism argument above supports a score of 4. The largest retrospective comparison does not: 412 reverse patients were less often still on opioids at two weeks than 278 anatomic patients (46% against 62%), needed fewer refills, and had a lower pain score at six weeks. Two explanations fit. The reverse population is older, and younger age is an independent predictor of rebound and of severe early pain; and in many reverse shoulders there is no subscapularis repair to protect and no residual cuff to load. The honest reading is that the retension mechanism earns the 4 for the subgroups that matter to you (revision, conversion, fracture, the younger patient with a massive tear, the opioid-tolerant patient), while the uncomplicated elderly cuff arthropathy patient often runs a course nearer a 3 with a slower tail. The plan is written for the first group and relaxed for the second, not the reverse.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Assessment, block, airway and baseline pressure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants an immobile head, a free arm, a working deltoid afterwards and a stable implant. The patient in front of you is older and sicker than the anatomic patient, and the operation is often a revision.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Two things are decided here that cannot be decided later. The first is the deltoid: a reverse without a working deltoid is a contraindication, and once the interscalene block is in you cannot test the axillary nerve until tomorrow, so document deltoid power and regimental badge sensation in words before the block. The second is the catheter (see the pain plan), decided with the indication in front of you: primary cuff arthropathy and conversion of a failed anatomic are not the same operation. Place the interscalene block (or a superior trunk block in the patient who cannot afford a hemidiaphragm, which is a larger fraction of this population than of the anatomic one) awake or lightly sedated, with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg at the same time. A tracheal tube is the author’s default: the head is away from you for two hours and the patient is often obese or has a poor chest. Cuff on the non-operative arm, awake baseline pressure recorded before any drug; that number is the target for the case.

  2. 2 Beach chair and head fixation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The sitting position drops the scapula and lets the surgeon work down a vertical humerus and across to the glenoid face, which every instrument in the set assumes. For the anterosuperior approach some surgeons sit the patient lower.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The pressure falls as the chair goes up, from venous pooling, the sympathetic effect of the block and the anaesthetic together, and the cuff reading overstates perfusion at the brain by roughly 1 mmHg for every 1.25 cm between cuff and external auditory meatus. This population carries more cerebrovascular disease than the anatomic one, so the margin matters more. Start a vasopressor infusion early rather than chasing with boluses, keep the cuff reading near the awake baseline, treat the bradycardia-hypotension episodes that the sitting position and the block provoke, and decline deliberate hypotension for a dry field; the reported strokes after shoulder surgery in beach chair happened at cuff systolics in the 80s and 90s. Check the head is neutral and strapped, the tube and circuit are not pulling on it, the eyes are free, and look again after the surgeon has set the arm, because the arm support moves the neck.

  3. 3 Antibiotic, tranexamic acid and skin preparation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Cutibacterium acnes lives in the dermal sebaceous glands of the shoulder and is the organism behind the late, indolent infections of shoulder arthroplasty; the surgeon prepares with benzoyl peroxide beforehand and avoids the dermal edge. Blood loss in a reverse is a little higher than in an anatomic because the glenoid exposure is wider and the scapular screw holes bleed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cefazolin 2 g IV finished before the knife, 3 g over 120 kg. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV at the same time. Nothing further is needed for C. acnes from your side. Have a group and hold for a revision or a conversion; a primary rarely needs blood.

  4. 4 Approach and subscapularis

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Deltopectoral: the internervous plane between deltoid and pectoralis major, cephalic vein as the marker, conjoint tendon retracted medially with the musculocutaneous nerve behind it, then the subscapularis taken down if it is there and repairable. Anterosuperior: the anterior deltoid is split or elevated off the acromion and the surgeon looks straight down onto the glenoid, which is faster in a stiff, scarred or revision shoulder but puts a knife into the engine of the construct, and is the reason some surgeons will not use it.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The cephalic vein is the first bleed. Retractors on the conjoint tendon are the first traction on the plexus. In a revision, the approach is through scar with retained metal, the vessels are displaced and the plexus is tethered; this step alone can take 45 minutes and is where the revision earns its blood loss. If the surgeon has chosen the anterosuperior approach, add muscle to the pain mechanisms for this patient: the split deltoid aches with every contraction, which is to say every elevation.

  5. 5 Humeral head osteotomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The head is delivered by extension, adduction and external rotation, osteophytes are cleared and the cut is made with a guide at the implant’s neck-shaft angle (155 degrees in the Grammont design, 135 degrees in the lateralised designs) and in less retroversion than anatomic, so that the humeral socket will sit below and lateral to the glenosphere.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The largest single bleed of the operation, from cancellous bone and the circumflex vessels. Forcible external rotation of a stiff, osteoporotic humerus is where a shaft fracture happens and the case doubles in length; the reverse population is more osteoporotic than the anatomic one. In a cuff arthropathy shoulder the head is already riding high and the delivery is often easier than in a stiff osteoarthritic one.

  6. 6 Glenoid exposure, reaming and baseplate

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The reverse baseplate has to sit flush on the inferior glenoid with its central fixation driven into the dense bone at the base of the scapular spine, so the whole glenoid face and the inferior scapular neck must be seen. That means excising the labrum, releasing the capsule all the way round, and placing a retractor under the inferior neck, where the axillary nerve runs across the subscapularis and under the capsule. The surface is reamed flat, the baseplate is positioned low with a few degrees of inferior tilt (both reduce scapular notching), the central peg or screw goes in, and peripheral locking screws are driven into the scapula. Glenoid bone loss is built up with a graft from the resected head or an augmented baseplate.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The most demanding step for the patient and the one you should be watching. The posterior humeral retractor levers on the plexus and the inferior retractor sits on the axillary nerve; the surgeon needs the arm held still in the position that opens the glenoid and a request for “something” usually means depth, not relaxant. Scapular screws bleed from cancellous bone and the irrigation obscures how much. Reaming throws blood and bone. The time at this step is set by exposure, and a revision glenoid with a failed component to remove and a defect to graft is the long version.

  7. 7 Glenosphere and humeral preparation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The glenosphere is impacted onto the baseplate’s taper and locked. The humerus is then reamed and broached for a stem, or the metaphysis impacted for a stemless or short-stem implant. Press-fit in good bone; in osteoporotic bone, in fracture and in revision the stem is cemented.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Impaction of the glenosphere is a sharp hammering with the arm levered aside. Humeral canal work is far less venting than a femur, but a cemented stem is a real cement step and the one BCIS-relevant moment in shoulder surgery: 16% of 173 cemented shoulder arthroplasties met any-grade criteria in a large series, against 31% of hip hemiarthroplasties, and the patients who do badly are over 75, ASA 3-4 or have renal impairment, which describes much of the reverse population. If cement is going in, the canal is lavaged and dried, the pressure is already supported, the oxygen is up and you are watching end-tidal carbon dioxide when the stem is pushed home. Press-fit stems carry no such moment and a higher rate of intraoperative fracture in soft bone, which is the trade the surgeon is making.

  8. 8 Trial reduction and deltoid tensioning

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon assembles a trial tray and insert, pulls the arm into traction and reduces the socket onto the glenosphere. The feel of that reduction, the tautness of the conjoint tendon, the amount of gap when the joint is distracted, the hang of the arm and the range through which it can be moved without the insert levering off the glenosphere are the information the surgeon uses to pick the insert thickness and, if needed, reseat the stem. Each change is another dislocation and reduction. The target is enough tension to be stable and no more; the systematic review of arm lengthening puts the goal at 0-2 cm beyond the contralateral side, with shortening raising dislocation risk and excessive lengthening raising nerve injury.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the step the trap on this card is about. Reduction stretches the axillary nerve and the plexus by the full length of the lengthening, in a patient whose nerves are often already tethered by scar, and the pressure dips with each reduction as the arm is pulled and the joint levered. A 10-second pause before each reduction is a reasonable request. Do not give a relaxant to make the reduction easier: a reduction that only goes in with the patient paralysed will dislocate on the ward when the deltoid tone returns, and the surgeon needs the tone to judge the tension. A working interscalene block already provides a flail arm; the deltoid tone the surgeon is feeling is largely resting tension in the muscle, not voluntary. When the surgeon says the reduction is “tight”, that is the mechanism of this card’s pain being created in front of you.

  9. 9 Definitive components and final reduction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The definitive stem or metaphyseal component is seated (impacted or cemented), the tray and insert are locked on, and the final reduction is performed and tested through the range, with particular attention to adduction (the insert must not impinge on the scapular neck) and to extension with internal rotation, the position that dislocates a reverse.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A cemented stem has its cement step here if it was not done at step 7, with the same precautions. The final reduction is the last stretch on the nerves. Note the time of the final reduction: pressure and heart rate settle afterwards, and this is where you start to lighten.

  10. 10 Subscapularis, closure and sling

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Surgeons vary. Some repair the subscapularis for anterior stability, some leave it because a repaired subscapularis can limit external rotation and may not heal to a lengthened humerus, and in many cuff arthropathy shoulders there is nothing to repair. The deltopectoral interval closes without sutures into muscle; the anterosuperior approach needs the deltoid repaired to the acromion with heavy sutures, which the surgeon will protect for six weeks. Drains are used by some. A sling, often with a small abduction wedge, goes on.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Give the antiemetic. The sling goes on before the patient lightens: an awake patient with a dense block will lift the dead arm by the wrist with the other hand, and the position that does it (arm swung across the body) is the dislocating position. Emergence is unremarkable; the arm is insensate.

  11. 11 Recovery, the first night and physiotherapy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The implant is stable from the moment of the final reduction, provided the arm stays out of extension, adduction and internal rotation. The surgeon wants early passive and active-assisted elevation because the deltoid is the operation, a radiograph before discharge, and no hand-behind-back for six weeks.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The block lasts 12-24 hours with dexamethasone. The first dose of regular paracetamol and NSAID goes in before the patient leaves recovery, and the ward is told that the arm comes back to life overnight with a deep ache rising over a few hours, which the regular regimen treats. Deltoid that does not work when the block has receded is the differential that matters: residual block (the commonest, and it has a timescale), an axillary nerve stretched at the reduction (subclinical in nearly half of reverse arthroplasties on EMG, usually recovering), or a true injury. Record what you find and when. Day 1 physiotherapy loads the deltoid, and this is where the reverse separates from the anatomic: the first elevation is pain through a stretched muscle, and a patient who cannot do it because the oral regimen was not given on time loses the day. Length of stay is one to three nights in this population; same-day discharge is reasonable for a younger patient with a primary and home support.

  12. 12 Later presentations that come to you first

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Three complications of the reverse present as new pain or a dead arm in the weeks after discharge, and each is sometimes attributed to the anaesthetic. The acromial or scapular spine stress fracture is the deltoid’s own tension breaking the bone it hangs from: pooled incidence 5%, usually in the first months, presenting as new pain over the acromion or spine, a sudden loss of elevation and sometimes a drooping acromion on the radiograph, with outcomes that are worse whether or not it is fixed. Dislocation (around 2% overall, higher in revision and in the absent-subscapularis shoulder) is early, anterior, and follows extension-adduction-internal rotation: reaching behind to push up from a chair, or the sling off. Scapular notching is the insert eroding the inferior scapular neck in adduction, radiographic rather than painful in the early years.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    You will be asked whether the block caused the weak deltoid at six weeks. It did not; the stress fracture and the stretched axillary nerve are the two candidates and the surgeon’s radiograph and an EMG decide between them. A reduction of an early dislocation is a short general anaesthetic with full relaxation, usually in the first weeks, and the surgeon may convert to an open revision of the insert if closed reduction fails, which it does more often than not.

Pain plan

The mechanism is a lengthened arm with a tensioned deltoid loaded from day 1, over bone work that hurts for 48 hours and nerves that have been stretched. Against that, the population is older and less rebound-prone than the anatomic one and the largest retrospective series shows reverse patients needing less opioid in the first weeks. The plan is built for the mechanism and relaxed for the patient, which is the same skeleton as the anatomic card with a lower threshold for a continuous technique.

The defensible default is an interscalene block with 15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% (or a superior trunk block with the same volume when the patient cannot afford a hemidiaphragm; it matches interscalene at 24 h with complete phrenic paresis in 5% rather than 73%, and this population has more chests that cannot afford it), intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg at induction, regular paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor where renal function allows (which it often does not in this population, and the loss of the NSAID is one reason the oral regimen is weaker here than on the anatomic card), and oral opioid for the first three days. PROSPECT has no reverse-specific guideline; the rotator cuff repair guideline is the nearest and recommends this skeleton with intravenous dexamethasone preferred over perineural.

The catheter. The one randomised comparison in shoulder arthroplasty found that a continuous interscalene infusion lowered day 1 pain scores and opioid use against a single shot, but did not shorten the stay, produced more adverse events and lost 10% of its catheters early; the trial that added an infusion on top of dexamethasone and full multimodal analgesia in a mixed arthroplasty and cuff repair population found no difference at all. The author’s reconciliation is the same as on the other shoulder cards: the catheter’s advantage shows when the single shot is bare and shrinks when it is prolonged with dexamethasone and backed by a regimen that is given. What is different on this card is who sits in the group the catheter helps. Conversion of a failed anatomic or hemiarthroplasty, revision of a reverse, the fracture reverse, the opioid-tolerant or chronic pain patient, the younger patient with a massive tear and an intact pain system, and anyone whose previous shoulder operation produced a catastrophic night: for these the catheter is the defensible choice, not an option, because the deltoid will be loaded on day 1 whether or not the pain is controlled. If a catheter goes in it needs fixation that survives a sling and a nurse who knows what a leaking dressing means, because the common failure is the catheter in the bed.

Rebound pain: half of ambulatory block patients after bone surgery describe it, with younger age, female sex and the absence of intravenous dexamethasone raising the odds. The reverse population is older, which helps, and has bone work, which does not. Escalation: pain above 7 after the block has receded with the sling on and the hand warm and moving is treated with the opioid already charted and a check that the regular doses were given; pain that is out of proportion with a tense, swelling deltopectoral wound is a haematoma for the surgeon; a new, sharp pain over the acromion in the first weeks with loss of elevation is a stress fracture, and the patient is sent back to the surgeon rather than to the pain clinic. A rescue block the next morning is rarely needed, and a second interscalene on the same side within 24 h in a patient who already has a phrenic palsy is not the reflex to have.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Primary cuff arthropathy versus everything else. The primary in a 75-year-old is a 90-minute case with 300 mL of blood loss and a course nearer a 3. Conversion of a failed anatomic, revision of a loose reverse and the fracture reverse are two- to three-hour cases through scar, with more bleeding, longer plexus traction, a cemented stem and a catheter-grade pain course. Book them differently.
  • Anterosuperior approach. Splits the deltoid: faster exposure, a deltoid repair to protect, and muscle added to the pain mechanisms.
  • Lateralised designs. Lateralising the glenosphere (bone graft, metal) or the humerus (135-degree neck-shaft angle, offset tray) reduces notching and improves rotation but changes how the deltoid is tensioned and where it is loaded; surgeons vary and the pain plan does not change.
  • Stemless and short-stem reverse. No canal work, no cement, less bleeding, shorter case; needs good metaphyseal bone and the surgeon may convert to a stem after the osteotomy.
  • Cemented stem. Fracture, revision and osteoporotic bone. The BCIS risk is lower than a hip but it is the same syndrome in the same frail patient; the precautions are the same. The 2016 systematic review found no difference in loosening or revision between cemented and press-fit stems and non-significant trends to more infection, nerve injury and thromboembolism with cement, so the choice is the surgeon’s and the bone’s.
  • Nerve. Subclinical EMG lesions in nearly half of reverse arthroplasties, mainly axillary, mostly resolving by six months. Pre-block documentation is the only defence against the dense block being read as an injury, and vice versa.
  • Instability. Early, anterior, in the extension-adduction-internal rotation position; commoner without a subscapularis, after revision and in patients with poor tone (Parkinson’s disease, neuromuscular disease, heavy alcohol use). Closed reduction fails more often than it succeeds.
  • Acromial and scapular spine stress fracture. The deltoid breaking the bone it hangs from; pooled incidence 5%; osteoporosis, rheumatoid arthritis and over-tensioning are the suspected contributors. Presents to whoever is nearest with new pain and a drop in elevation.
  • Beach chair physiology in the patient with carotid disease, uncontrolled hypertension or a previous stroke. The cuff-to-brain gradient is the problem and this population has more of these patients. An arterial line transduced at the ear is the honest monitor.
  • Renal impairment. Takes away the NSAID, raises the BCIS risk and the dexamethasone glucose effect in a diabetic matters for a day. The oral regimen is paracetamol and opioid, which is weaker, and that argues for the block being the best one you can place.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis. Cervical spine for the head holder, steroid cover, osteoporotic bone for a cemented stem and for the acromion, more bleeding.
  • Obesity. Beach chair airway access, a diaphragm that cannot afford the phrenic palsy, a long reach for the surgeon and a swollen neck from retraction.

Key sources

There is no PROSPECT guideline for shoulder arthroplasty of either geometry; the rotator cuff repair guideline is extrapolated. The mechanism claims on this card rest on the Lädermann group’s work on arm lengthening and on nerve lesions after reverse arthroplasty, which are small prospective series rather than trials. The acromial fracture incidence is a systematic review of heterogeneous reports and the instability figure is a review of case series. The block evidence is the 2019 randomised comparison of continuous and single-shot interscalene block in arthroplasty and the 2024 trial showing no added benefit from an infusion once dexamethasone and multimodal analgesia are in place; the catheter advice is the author’s reconciliation of those two with the mechanism. The claim that reverse patients use less opioid than anatomic patients is a single-centre retrospective series and should be read as a corrective to folklore, not as evidence that the reverse is a mild operation.
