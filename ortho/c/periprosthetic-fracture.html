38 Periprosthetic fractures of the hip and knee

Vancouver classification; plate and cable fixation versus revision stem; distal femoral fractures above a knee replacement

4Severe

Dominant mechanism: Periosteum. A long lateral exposure with vastus lateralis lifted off the femur, a plate laid along most of the bone and cables passed around it, or a loose stem extracted and a longer one driven into the diaphysis; on top of a fracture that was already painful, in a patient who has to stand on it the next day

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: moderate.

Block
Fascia iliaca or femoral nerve block on admission, repeated or replaced by a suprainguinal fascia iliaca catheter at surgery; spinal, combined spinal-epidural or general anaesthesia chosen by expected duration
Catheter: selective A suprainguinal fascia iliaca catheter for 48-72 h is reasonable for revision of the stem and for long plate fixation, where the periosteal pain outlasts a single shot and the patient tolerates opioids badly; a single shot repeated before surgery is enough for a short distal fixation. Position
Lateral decubitus for most fractures around a hip stem, fixed or revised; supine on a radiolucent table with a bump under the thigh for distal femoral fractures above a knee replacement and for some lateral plating

Tell the patient
The bone has broken around your joint replacement; the operation either holds the bone with a plate or replaces the loose part with a longer one, and the aim is the same as after a broken hip: to have you standing with the physiotherapist the next day.
The trap
It is booked as a fracture fixation and it behaves like a revision arthroplasty in a hip fracture patient: the stem may prove loose on the table, the case doubles in length, the spinal runs out, the blood loss triples and cement goes into a broken femur

What the surgeon is doing and why

A joint replacement turns a tapering, flexible bone into a bone with a rigid metal rod in one end. The stiffness changes abruptly at the tip of the implant, the bone around it thins with age and stress shielding, and a simple fall concentrates its energy at that junction. The patient is on average in the mid-80s, had the hip or knee done 10 or 15 years ago, and now cannot stand. In every respect that matters to you this is a hip fracture patient, with the same frailty, the same anticoagulants and the same one-year mortality (11% in the matched series against 16.5% for hip fracture and 2.9% for primary arthroplasty), who needs a bigger operation than a hip fracture patient gets.

The surgeon’s first question is not where the fracture is but whether the implant is still fixed to the bone. The Vancouver classification for fractures around a hip stem is built on it:

  A   trochanters only            usually non-operative or wires
  B1  around the stem, stem SOLID  plate and cables
  B2  around the stem, stem LOOSE  revise to a longer stem
  B3  loose stem, bone stock poor  revise, with allograft or
                                   proximal femoral replacement
  C   well below the stem tip      plate, as for any femoral fracture

A solid stem is left alone and the fracture is fixed around it: a long lateral locking plate from the trochanter to the condyles, held proximally with cerclage cables and unicortical screws, because the stem fills the canal and ordinary screws cannot pass. A loose stem cannot be fixed around, because the bone will not unite about a moving implant: it is pulled out and replaced with a longer stem that bypasses the fracture by at least two cortical diameters and takes its grip in the intact diaphysis below, usually an uncemented tapered fluted stem, sometimes a cemented long stem, with cables to hold the fragments around it. Poor bone stock adds strut allograft or a tumour-type proximal femoral replacement.

Two things blur that clean division. First, stability is often uncertain on radiographs and is decided on the table, so the consent, the kit and the anaesthetic have to cover both operations. Second, practice is shifting for one common implant: a fracture around a polished tapered cemented stem with an intact cement mantle is increasingly reduced and fixed, or revised cement-in-cement, with the logic that a shorter operation suits a frail patient. The pooled comparison of fixation against revision for B2 and B3 fractures (1,629 patients) found similar complication and reoperation rates with less blood loss, shorter operating time and shorter stay after fixation. The mortality data point in both directions and are dominated by selection: surgeons fix the oldest and frailest.

Around a knee replacement the usual fracture is supracondylar, in the distal femur just above the femoral component. The options are a lateral locking plate, a retrograde nail passed through the box of the femoral component where its design allows, or, when the component is loose or the distal fragment too short and soft to hold screws, a hinged distal femoral replacement that discards the fragment altogether. The systematic review found no difference in mortality or reoperation between fixation and replacement.

Whatever the construct, the surgical aim is the one that drives hip fracture surgery: full weight-bearing on the first day. A frail patient cannot partial weight-bear, and in the prospective multicentre cohort, frail patients whose fixation allowed full weight-bearing had lower one-year mortality.

The pain logic: the fracture itself has been painful since the fall. The operation adds a long lateral incision, vastus lateralis elevated from the femur over 20-30 cm, cables passed circumferentially under the periosteum, and either a plate along the bone or reaming and impaction of the distal canal. The dominant mechanism is periosteum, as in revision hip arthroplasty, with bone behind it. It sits at a 4: more than the 3 of a neck of femur fracture because of the extent of the dissection, in a patient whose tolerance of opioids is the limiting factor.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Admission: run the hip fracture pathway

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgical team needs time to image the fracture, find the original operation note and implant details, order a long stem or plate set and often a company representative, and arrange a surgeon who does revisions. That takes one to three days more often than not.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Everything that a hip fracture patient gets on admission applies: a fascia iliaca or femoral block in the emergency department and repeated while waiting, orthogeriatric review, anticoagulant reversal planning, fluids, and attention to delirium, pressure areas and nutrition. The one departure is timing. For native hip fractures early surgery is a standard. For periprosthetic fractures the evidence does not show that a short wait costs lives: a series of 82 patients with a mean delay of four days found no link between delay and mortality, and a target trial emulation of 4,564 patients found no difference in 30- or 90-day mortality between surgery within two days and surgery at three to ten days. The defensible reading is that waiting a day or two for the right surgeon and the right implant is acceptable, and that waiting unblocked, starved and immobile is not. Use the delay: repeat the block or place a catheter, correct the haemoglobin and the INR, and stop the patient being fasted on successive days for a case that is not going to happen.

  2. 2 The plan: what the stem is doing and how big the operation is

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon classifies the fracture and prepares for the next size of operation up. A B1 booked for plating becomes a B2 revision if the stem moves when it is tested.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Ask three questions and plan on the answers: is the stem thought to be solid or loose, is cement going in, and how long would the longer operation take. Then prepare for the longer one. Cross-match two units as a minimum and more for a planned revision; check the pre-operative haemoglobin, which has usually fallen since admission; arrange cell salvage for a revision where the unit has it and infection is not suspected. Arterial line for a revision in a frail patient or for any cement step; a second large cannula. Read the pacemaker, the echo and the anticoagulant chart as for any hip fracture.

  3. 3 Choice of anaesthetic

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs a still patient in the lateral position for an operation of uncertain length.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A single-shot spinal covers plate fixation of a fracture with a solid stem and a distal femoral plate, and little else. In a retrospective comparison of 95 B2 fractures, the patients who had a spinal did no worse than those who had general anaesthesia, but they were fitter and their operations shorter, so the finding supports spinal as an option for the selected patient and no more. For a planned or possible revision the choice is a combined spinal-epidural, which can be extended, or general anaesthesia with a regional block; the author’s default for a revision in a frail patient is general anaesthesia with a suprainguinal fascia iliaca block or catheter and an arterial line, because the duration is unpredictable and cement and blood loss are easier to manage with a controlled airway. Dose the spinal for the patient’s age, and position for it with the block already working.

  4. 4 Positioning

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Lateral decubitus gives access to the whole lateral femur and the hip, and allows dislocation if the stem is revised. Distal fractures above a knee replacement are done supine on a radiolucent table.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A broken femur rolled into the lateral position is a moment of severe pain under spinal or light anaesthesia and of bleeding into the thigh. The skin is thin, the shoulders are arthritic and the neck is stiff: pad the dependent side for a four-hour case even if the booking says two. Warm the patient from the start; an 85-year-old with a long lateral incision cools quickly.

  5. 5 Exposure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The old incision is extended down the lateral thigh, the fascia lata is split, and vastus lateralis is lifted forwards off the lateral intermuscular septum, ligating the perforating vessels as they come through. The fracture haematoma is evacuated and the fracture ends cleaned.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The perforators are the first real bleeding and there are several of them; a retracted, unligated one bleeds from behind the septum. The haematoma that the surgeon scoops out is blood the patient lost days ago, so the pre-operative haemoglobin already accounts for it and the suction volume overstates today’s loss. Give tranexamic acid before the incision.

  6. 6 Testing the stem

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon looks at the stem-bone or cement-bone interface through the fracture and tries to move the stem by hand or by gentle distraction. Movement, or a broken cement mantle, means the implant is loose and has to be revised.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the decision point for the whole anaesthetic, and it is worth asking for the answer aloud. If the stem is loose, the operation is now a revision: extend the epidural or accept that the spinal will run out, send for blood, repeat the antibiotic at four hours, and tell the recovery unit and the high dependency unit that the plan has changed.

  7. 7 Fixation with plate and cables

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The fracture is reduced with clamps and held with one or two cerclage cables, then a long plate is contoured to the lateral femur. Around the stem it is held with cables and short locking screws; below the stem with bicortical screws. Each cable is passed around the femur with a curved passer kept on bone.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cable passage strips periosteum circumferentially and is the periosteal pain of this card. The passer travels round the back of the femur, where the profunda perforators and, more proximally, the sciatic nerve lie; sudden bleeding during cable passage comes from there. Reduction manoeuvres on a spiral fracture squeeze marrow contents into the circulation, with a brief fall in pressure or saturation in some patients. Expect 90-150 min in total.

  8. 8 Revision of the stem

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The hip is dislocated, the loose stem and any cement are removed, working through the fracture, which serves as its own access window. A prophylactic cable is placed below the fracture, the distal canal is reamed, and a long tapered fluted stem is impacted until it is axially and rotationally stable in the diaphysis. The proximal fragments are then wrapped around it with cables, leg length and stability are tested with trial components, and the hip is reduced.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the longest and bloodiest phase: a canal that is open at the fracture bleeds continuously while it is reamed, and loss runs past a litre in a long case. Run the cell saver, track the loss against the haemoglobin every hour, and give the second dose of tranexamic acid. Reaming and impaction pressurise the distal canal with the same marrow embolisation as any stem, in a patient with less reserve. Repeated trial reductions and dislocations pull on the sciatic nerve.

  9. 9 Cement, when it is used

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A cemented long stem, a cement-in-cement revision into an intact mantle, or cement around a distal femoral replacement. Surgeons choose cement for the oldest patients and widest canals because it gives immediate fixation that does not depend on bone quality.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Treat it exactly as the cemented stem in a hip fracture (see bone cement implantation syndrome): the surgeon announces it, the canal is washed, dried and vented, the pressure is already supported with a vasopressor running, the inspired oxygen is up, and a sudden fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide is the right ventricle. Two features are particular to this operation. Cement pressurised into a fractured femur escapes through the fracture lines into the soft tissues, so the surgeon either fixes the fracture first with cables or pressurises less. And the stem is long, so the cement volume and the length of canal exposed are greater than in a primary. Cement-in-cement revision is the gentle version: a small volume into an old mantle with no bone interface.

  10. 10 Distal femoral fractures above a knee replacement

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A lateral locking plate through a lateral or minimally invasive approach, a retrograde nail through the knee, or a distal femoral replacement in which the fracture fragment and the old femoral component are excised and a hinged, cemented, stemmed implant is put in.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Plating and nailing are 90-120 min supine operations that suit a spinal with a femoral or adductor canal block. A distal femoral replacement is a revision knee arthroplasty with a cemented femoral stem, sometimes done under tourniquet, with blood loss and a cement step to match; in the pooled series its 30-day mortality was 4.1% and its two-year mortality 14.6%, which describes the patient more than the operation. Its advantage to the patient is immediate full weight-bearing.

  11. 11 Closure and the blood count

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Vastus is allowed to fall back over the plate, the fascia lata is closed, a drain is used by some surgeons.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Add up the loss before the drapes come off and check a haemoglobin: bleeding continues into the thigh for 24-48 h and the day 2 value is the lowest. Transfuse in theatre the patient who is already at the threshold, since a frail patient who is anaemic cannot stand for the physiotherapist. Bolus the block or the catheter now. Avoid long-acting opioid and anything anticholinergic at the end of the case in a patient this prone to delirium.

  12. 12 Recovery and the first days

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the patient out of bed on day 1, weight-bearing as the construct allows, and the wound dry.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Level 2 care for a revision, a cemented stem or a transfusion beyond two units. The orthogeriatric pathway resumes: regular paracetamol, the regional technique continued for 48-72 h where a catheter is in place, small doses of oral opioid with laxatives, no NSAID in most of this population because of renal function and anticoagulants, delirium screening, and early food. Thromboprophylaxis is timed against the neuraxial block or catheter. Hypotension on the first stand is common after a long operation, a transfusion and an epidural.

Pain plan

The mechanism is periosteal stripping along most of the femur in a patient for whom opioids are the main source of post-operative harm, so the plan is built on regional analgesia started at admission and carried through the first two or three days. The anterolateral thigh and the femoral periosteum are covered by the femoral and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves, which a suprainguinal fascia iliaca block reaches reliably; the posterior femur and the hip capsule (sciatic and obturator contributions) are not covered, which is why the block reduces opioid need and does not abolish it.

The defensible default: a fascia iliaca or femoral block on admission, repeated every 12-24 h or converted to a catheter while the patient waits; at surgery, a fresh suprainguinal fascia iliaca block with ropivacaine 0.2-0.375% 30-40 mL, dose-reduced for a small frail patient; regular paracetamol; oral opioid in small, age-adjusted doses as rescue; and surgeon infiltration along the plate as a supplement. A fascia iliaca catheter for 48-72 h is the author’s choice for a stem revision or a full-length plate, and a low-dose epidural for 24-48 h after a combined spinal-epidural is the alternative in a unit that can nurse it; the epidural gives better analgesia, with more hypotension, a urinary catheter and a delayed anticoagulant. All of this is extrapolated from the hip fracture and revision arthroplasty evidence. There is no analgesic trial in periprosthetic fracture.

Quadriceps weakness from a femoral or fascia iliaca block matters less here than in elective arthroplasty, because the alternative is an opioid-induced delirium that stops mobilisation altogether; use a dilute infusion and tell the physiotherapist. Rebound is moderate: the pain is not as sharp-peaked as after elective bone surgery in a young patient, and the catheter or repeated block smooths it.

Escalation: pain that prevents sitting out on day 1 despite a working block is usually the posterior thigh and responds to a small opioid dose before physiotherapy. A tense, enlarging thigh with a falling haemoglobin is a haematoma. New pain on weight-bearing with shortening or rotation is loss of fixation or a dislocation. New confusion is treated as delirium with a cause to find (pain, retention, opioids, hypoxia, infection) before it is treated with a drug.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Conversion on the table. The commonest surprise: a solid stem proves loose. Plan the anaesthetic for the revision every time.
  • The spinal that runs out. A two-hour fixation becomes a four-hour revision. Use a combined spinal-epidural or general anaesthesia when revision is possible.
  • Infection as the cause of loosening. A stem that has been painful for months before the fall may be infected; the surgeon will want samples before antibiotics, cell salvage is not used, and the operation may become the first stage of a two-stage revision.
  • Anticoagulants and antiplatelets. As common as in hip fracture and managed the same way; a direct oral anticoagulant in a patient with poor renal function is the usual reason neuraxial anaesthesia is not available on the day.
  • Fixation against revision in the frail. Fixation is shorter and bleeds less; revision gives a construct that can be loaded at once. The mortality figures in the literature mostly reflect who was chosen for which. Your contribution to the decision is an honest estimate of what the patient will tolerate.
  • Proximal and distal femoral replacement. Tumour-type implants for bone that cannot be reconstructed: large exposures, cemented stems, high blood loss, and the fastest route to full weight-bearing.
  • Fractures around a knee replacement on the tibial side, and around shoulder or elbow implants, are uncommon and follow the same principle: fix around a solid implant, revise a loose one.
  • Interprosthetic fractures. A femur with a hip stem above and a knee stem below has a short segment of unprotected bone between them; the plate spans the whole femur and the exposure is correspondingly long.
  • Intra-operative periprosthetic fracture. A crack in the calcar or the shaft during a primary or revision hip is the same problem discovered early: the surgeon adds a cable or changes to a longer stem, and the case lengthens by 30-60 min (see posterior approach hip arthroplasty).
  • Limitation of care. A one-year mortality in the teens means the conversation about escalation, resuscitation and what the patient wants belongs before the operation.

Key sources

The outcome literature is retrospective series, one prospective multicentre cohort and administrative database studies, and it is consistent on two points: these patients die at the rate of hip fracture patients, and the fixation-against-revision comparisons are confounded by who is selected for each. The timing evidence is the notable difference from native hip fracture: the single-centre series and the 2026 target trial emulation both fail to show harm from a wait of a few days, though neither examined the quality of care during the wait. The anaesthetic literature amounts to one retrospective comparison of spinal and general anaesthesia. The block and catheter advice is the author’s extrapolation from the hip fracture and revision arthroplasty cards.
