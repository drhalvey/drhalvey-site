103 Paediatric septic arthritis and osteomyelitis

Aspiration, arthrotomy or arthroscopic washout of the septic hip, knee, ankle and shoulder, and drainage of subperiosteal and intraosseous abscess in acute haematogenous osteomyelitis, from the neonate to the adolescent

2Mild to moderate

Dominant mechanism: Capsule. The pain of a septic joint is a capsule distended with pus under pressure, and of osteomyelitis a periosteum lifted off bone by an abscess. The operation decompresses both, so the child is usually more comfortable in recovery than in the emergency department. What the surgery adds is a small arthrotomy or portals, a drain, and drill holes in a metaphysis. Drainage of established osteomyelitis is a 3, with bone pain and regular opioid for two days

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: low.

Block
No block as the default: IV paracetamol 15 mg/kg (max 1 g) if not given in the last 4-6 h, fentanyl 1-2 microg/kg IV in increments, an NSAID once the child is rehydrated and renal function is known, and oral opioid as required afterwards
Catheter: no No indication, and an indwelling catheter in a bacteraemic child is an avoidable risk; pain falls with drainage
Alternatives: Single-shot fascia iliaca or femoral block, ropivacaine 0.2% 0.5 mL/kg (max 30 mL), for hip or femoral drainage in the child who is haemodynamically stable with a normal platelet count, through clean skin away from the infected compartment; Surgeon infiltration of skin incisions with ropivacaine 0.2% up to 0.5 mL/kg, knowing local anaesthetic works poorly in inflamed acidic tissue; Neuraxial block is avoided in the febrile bacteraemic child
Position
Supine with a bump under the affected buttock for an anterior hip arthrotomy; supine with a thigh support for the knee; beach-chair tilt or lateral for the shoulder; whatever exposes the abscess for osteomyelitis
Tourniquet
Sometimes. Used by some surgeons for knee, ankle and tibial procedures, after elevation only: an infected limb is not exsanguinated with a compressive bandage. Never needed for hip or shoulder
Cement
No
Blood loss
Under 2 mL/kg for a joint washout; 5-10 mL/kg for a large subperiosteal or thigh abscess with bone drilling. The child is often already anaemic from infection and haemodilution
Duration
20-45 min for a hip or knee washout; 45-90 min for abscess drainage and bone windowing; add 45-60 min if an MRI is done under the same anaesthetic
Length of stay
3-7 nights, set by the fever, the C-reactive protein trend and the switch to oral antibiotics, not by the surgery; weeks for disseminated staphylococcal disease. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Therapeutic, not prophylactic. Empirical cover by age and local guideline, for example cefazolin 50 mg/kg IV (max 2 g) 8-hourly or flucloxacillin 50 mg/kg IV (max 2 g) 6-hourly, with vancomycin 15 mg/kg IV (max 750 mg per dose initially) where MRSA is likely; given as soon as blood cultures are taken in the unwell child, and after joint or bone samples in the stable child only if that costs minutes
Tranexamic acid
Not used
VTE
None routinely; deep vein thrombosis next to the infected bone is a recognised complication of severe staphylococcal osteomyelitis in older children and is looked for, not prevented, at the first operation
Antiemetic
Ondansetron 0.15 mg/kg IV (max 4 mg); a single dose of dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV (max 8 mg) is not contraindicated by the infection
Tell the patient
The joint is washed out through a small cut to remove the infection and the pressure; children are usually more comfortable afterwards, and the antibiotics do the rest over the following weeks.
The trap
Two children share this booking: a toddler with a Kingella knee who is well enough to be eating chips, and a ten-year-old with staphylococcal osteomyelitis of the femur who is in septic shock with a heart rate of 160 that everyone has attributed to fever and pain. Decide which one is in front of you before induction, because the second needs fluid, antibiotics and a vasopressor drawn up, not a slot at the end of the list

What the surgeon is doing and why

Bacteria reach a child’s bones and joints through the blood. They lodge in the metaphysis, where looped capillaries beside the growth plate have slow flow and few phagocytes, and from there the infection takes one of three routes: it stays in bone (osteomyelitis), it breaks out under the periosteum and strips it off the shaft (subperiosteal abscess), or, where the metaphysis lies inside the joint capsule (hip, shoulder, elbow, ankle), it empties into the joint (septic arthritis). In the infant, vessels cross the growth plate and take the infection into the epiphysis and joint directly.

Pus in a joint does two kinds of damage on a clock measured in hours to days. Bacterial and neutrophil enzymes dissolve articular cartilage, which does not regenerate. In the hip the capsule is tight, so pressure rises until it exceeds the perfusion pressure of the vessels that run up the neck to the femoral head, and the head dies. That is why a septic hip is a surgical emergency in a way that a septic knee, which can be aspirated repeatedly and is not at risk of avascular necrosis, is not quite. Under the periosteum the same pressure strips the blood supply from the cortex and produces dead bone (sequestrum).

The surgeon’s aims are therefore simple: prove the diagnosis and get an organism (aspiration), let the pus out and wash the enzymes away (arthrotomy, arthroscopy or needle lavage), relieve pressure in bone where there is an abscess (drainage, with drill holes or a small cortical window into the metaphysis), and leave a route for it to keep draining (a drain, or a capsule left open). Antibiotics do the rest: with early treatment many cases of osteomyelitis never need an operation.

The organism sets the tempo. Kingella kingae, a mouth commensal of the child aged 6 months to 4 years, is the commonest cause in that age group once molecular tests are used (45% of documented infections in one series, against 29% for Staphylococcus aureus) and gives a mild illness: low-grade fever, a modest C-reactive protein, cultures often negative. Staphylococcus aureus is the organism of the school-age child and of severe disease, and strains carrying Panton-Valentine leukocidin or methicillin resistance cause multifocal infection, pyomyositis, venous thrombosis, septic lung emboli and shock. Neonates get group B streptococcus and Gram-negative organisms, often at several sites with few signs.

Pain logic: capsule and periosteum under pressure, relieved by the operation. This is a score of 2 and falling. Increasing pain after a washout is not a pain problem; it is undrained pus.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Which child, which joint, how soon

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon is separating septic arthritis from transient synovitis, and a hip (same night) from a knee (same day). Fever, refusal to bear weight and raised inflammatory markers together make sepsis likely; ultrasound shows the effusion; MRI finds the adjacent bone or muscle focus.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Sort the child physiologically before discussing fasting. Heart rate above the fever-adjusted range, capillary refill over 2 s, cool peripheries, a wide pulse pressure, lethargy, lactate above 2 mmol/L: any of these means sepsis, and the operation is source control that should not wait six hours for a stomach. The well toddler with a limp can wait the two or three hours that make the stomach safer and the team available, but not until the morning if it is a hip. Ask what antipyretics were given and when: parents and the emergency department have usually given both paracetamol and ibuprofen, and your doses must fit around them. Ask about a recent viral illness, which commonly precedes Kingella infection and brings an irritable airway.

  2. 2 Resuscitation and the antibiotic argument

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants an organism and has traditionally asked for antibiotics to be withheld until the joint is sampled.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    In the septic child that request is refused politely: blood cultures, then antibiotics, then theatre. The evidence supports this: in 131 children with positive blood cultures who went to surgery, antibiotics before surgical sampling did not lower culture yield, and molecular tests find Kingella whatever has been given. In the stable child, withholding until the needle is in the joint is harmless if it is a matter of minutes with the syringe drawn up. Fluid: 10-20 mL/kg of balanced crystalloid before induction for the febrile child who has not been drinking, repeated to a response; glucose checked in the under-fives. If MRSA is likely, vancomycin 15 mg/kg IV is infused over at least an hour (faster rates cause flushing and hypotension), so it is started before the child reaches you where possible.

  3. 3 MRI under the same anaesthetic

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A young child cannot lie still for MRI, and the scan changes the operation: it shows whether a hip effusion comes with femoral or pelvic osteomyelitis, an obturator or psoas abscess, or pyomyositis that needs its own drainage.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    “MRI and proceed” is efficient and has costs: an hour of anaesthesia in a remote site for a child who may be septic, a transfer while anaesthetised, and a temptation to scan first when the hip should be drained first. A fair rule is that a hot hip with pus on ultrasound goes to theatre and is scanned afterwards if the course demands, while the child with an unclear focus is scanned first. Secure the airway with a tube if the child is unwell or unfasted; use the same weight-based doses and keep warming modest, since these children are febrile.

  4. 4 Induction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A 30-minute operation in a child who may have little reserve.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    For the well child: intravenous or inhalational induction, a supraglottic airway if fasted, a tube after a modified rapid sequence if not (rocuronium 1 mg/kg IV, or suxamethonium 2 mg/kg IV; 3 mg/kg under 1 year). For the septic child: ketamine 1-2 mg/kg IV, or propofol in small increments, with a vasopressor ready. Peripheral adrenaline 0.05-0.3 microg/kg/min or noradrenaline at the same rate is acceptable through a good cannula while central access waits. Fever and tachycardia shorten apnoea time; pre-oxygenate properly. A child with staphylococcal sepsis and a cough or oxygen requirement may have septic lung emboli or necrotising pneumonia: look at the chest film before, not after.

  5. 5 Aspiration and samples

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A wide-bore needle into the joint under ultrasound or image intensifier, or direct puncture of a subperiosteal collection. Fluid goes for cell count, Gram stain, culture in blood-culture bottles (which is how Kingella grows) and PCR.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the moment for the withheld antibiotic: give it as the needle comes out. A dry tap in a child with convincing signs means the pus is thick, the focus is in bone, or the needle is in the wrong place, and the surgeon will usually proceed to open the joint. Paracetamol 15 mg/kg IV (max 1 g) if the last dose was more than four to six hours ago.

  6. 6 Hip arthrotomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A short anterior incision in the groin crease, the interval between sartorius and tensor developed, rectus retracted, and a window cut in the anterior capsule so it cannot seal. Pus is released under pressure, the joint is irrigated with one or more litres through a catheter, and a drain is left. Some surgeons drill the femoral neck if MRI shows metaphyseal infection. Arthroscopic drainage and needle lavage are alternatives in some hands, with a shorter stay in one small comparison.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Brief and not bloody. Manipulation of an infected, hyperaemic joint can release a bacteraemic shower: a rise in heart rate and a fall in pressure and temperature control over the next 15 minutes is that, treated with fluid and, if needed, the vasopressor. The femoral nerve is under the medial retractor.

  7. 7 Knee, ankle and shoulder

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The knee and ankle are washed arthroscopically through two portals with several litres of saline, or through a small arthrotomy in the infant; the shoulder through an anterior deltopectoral stab or arthroscope, remembering that proximal humeral osteomyelitis is usually the source.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Irrigation fluid at room temperature will cool a febrile toddler from 39 to 36 degrees C in half an hour, which is a swing, not a benefit: monitor temperature and warm the fluid. If a tourniquet is used, the limb is elevated and not wrapped, and deflation returns blood from an infected field, with the same transient bacteraemic response as in step 6.

  8. 8 Osteomyelitis: abscess drainage and bone decompression

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Where MRI shows a subperiosteal or soft-tissue collection, or the child has not improved after 48-72 hours of antibiotics, the surgeon incises over the metaphysis, drains the abscess, and makes drill holes or a small cortical window to decompress the medullary cavity, staying away from the growth plate. Dead bone is removed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    These are the sick children. A femoral or pelvic abscess in a child with disseminated staphylococcal infection can hold a surprising volume and bleed from inflamed muscle and bone: second cannula, a haemoglobin before starting (often 80-100 g/L already), blood available for the large collection, and an arterial line if vasopressors are running. Platelets may be low or the coagulation deranged, which is a further argument against neuraxial or deep blocks. Ask whether a central or peripherally inserted central line is wanted while the child is asleep.

  9. 9 Emergence and the next 72 hours

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon follows fever, pain, movement of the joint and C-reactive protein, which should fall by a third or more in two to three days. A child who is not improving has undrained pus or a second focus and goes back for a repeat washout, further imaging or both. Antibiotics are switched to oral once the child is afebrile and improving, usually within days.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Extubate the well child awake in theatre; send the septic child who needed vasopressors to intensive or high dependency care, intubated if the trajectory is uncertain. Afterwards chart regular paracetamol 15 mg/kg every 6 h (max 60 mg/kg/day; max 1 g per dose), ibuprofen 10 mg/kg every 8 h (max 400 mg; from 3 months of age, once drinking and with normal creatinine), and oxycodone 0.1 mg/kg orally (max 5 mg) as required. Expect to see some of these children again in 48 hours: keep the airway and access notes, and plan the repeat anaesthetic as a continuation, with the same weight, the antibiotic schedule in hand, and a question about whether long-term access is still needed now that early oral switch is usual.

Pain plan

The mechanism is pressure in a capsule or under periosteum, relieved by surgery, so the plan is simple analgesia and the score is 2. Paracetamol, an NSAID when the kidneys are perfused, and small opioid doses for the first day cover a joint washout. Children with osteomyelitis have more background bone pain for several days and may need regular oral opioid for 48 hours. The antipyretic effect of regular paracetamol and ibuprofen blunts the fever curve the surgeon and the infectious diseases team are following; that is acceptable, and the C-reactive protein is the better marker, but they should know it is charted.

Regional anaesthesia has a small role. The author’s default is none, for three reasons that follow from the disease and not from evidence: the analgesic need is modest, the child is or has been bacteraemic, and worsening pain is the signal for a repeat washout. Neuraxial techniques are avoided outright in the febrile bacteraemic child, as they would be in an adult. A single-shot fascia iliaca or femoral block through clean skin is reasonable for the stable child with a septic hip or femoral drainage and a normal platelet count, at ropivacaine 0.2% 0.5 mL/kg (max 30 mL). No catheters.

Compartment syndrome deserves a sentence because infection can cause it without trauma: a subperiosteal or intramuscular abscess in the forearm or leg raises compartment pressure, and a child with a tense, exquisitely painful limb needs fasciotomy with the drainage. That is another reason not to block the limb; see compartment syndrome and fasciotomy.

Escalation: pain or fever that rises after the first 24 hours, a joint that is again held still, or a C-reactive protein that fails to fall means re-imaging and re-operation, not a stronger opioid.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The neonate. Often afebrile, with a limb that is not moved (pseudoparalysis) and several sites involved; the hip is commonly one of them. Neonatal doses and intervals apply, glucose and temperature are yours to guard, and regional techniques are not used.
  • Disseminated staphylococcal disease. Multifocal bone and muscle infection, deep vein thrombosis, septic pulmonary emboli, pericardial effusion and endocarditis are all described. Echocardiography and chest imaging before a non-urgent repeat anaesthetic are reasonable; anticoagulation for a thrombosis changes the timing of everything.
  • Kingella. Mild, culture-negative, age 6 months to 4 years. Resistant to vancomycin and clindamycin, so the empirical regimen in this age group includes a beta-lactam.
  • Sickle cell disease. Salmonella as well as staphylococci, and osteomyelitis that is hard to tell from infarction. Keep the child warm, hydrated and oxygenated and avoid tourniquets where possible.
  • Pelvic, sacroiliac and spinal infection. Present as abdominal pain, limp or refusal to sit; usually treated without surgery, but come to you for MRI or biopsy.
  • Transient synovitis sent for aspiration. A well child with a small effusion and normal markers; the procedure is a diagnostic tap under a short anaesthetic, and antibiotics are not given.
  • The partially treated child. Oral antibiotics from a general practitioner mask the signs and delay the diagnosis; assume the infection is more advanced than it looks.
  • Late sequelae. Growth arrest, avascular necrosis and joint destruction bring these children back years later for the reconstructive operations on other cards, including limb lengthening.
  • The adult counterpart is on the osteomyelitis and septic arthritis card.

Key sources

The organism data and the hip prediction rule are observational but replicated. The antibiotic-timing study is retrospective; the position taken here (never delay antibiotics in an unwell child, and a delay of minutes is harmless in a well one) is consistent with it and with the paediatric osteomyelitis guideline, whose detailed antibiotic choices should be read with local resistance patterns. There is no trial evidence on anaesthetic or analgesic technique in this condition: the no-block default and the avoidance of neuraxial block in bacteraemia are the author’s judgement and standard caution respectively. Doses of antibiotics are examples of common empirical regimens and vary with age and local guideline. General paediatric points are in the child having orthopaedic surgery.
