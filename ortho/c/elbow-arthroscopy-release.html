Block

Prone or lateral decubitus with the arm over a padded bolster and the elbow flexed to 90 degrees for arthroscopy; supine on an arm table with the arm across the chest for some surgeons and for most open tennis elbow and open release surgery

The trap
The nerves sit millimetres from the anterior portals and a stiff or rheumatoid elbow pulls them closer: a transient palsy after arthroscopy is common enough that the surgeon may want the hand examined before any block, and a dense block in recovery after a release can hide both a nerve injury and the forearm that is swelling from extravasated irrigation

What the surgeon is doing and why

Three operations share this card because they share a joint, a position and a pain mechanism, and because the hazards they carry for you are the same: nerves that sit within millimetres of the instruments, a forearm that fills with irrigation fluid, and a surgeon who wants the arm moved afterwards.

The elbow is a tight, congruent joint with a capsule that scars readily. Any insult (a fracture, a dislocation, a haemarthrosis, weeks in a splint, rheumatoid synovitis) produces a thickened anterior capsule that blocks extension and a thickened posterior capsule and olecranon fossa osteophytes that block flexion; the patient loses the functional arc of 30-130 degrees and cannot reach their face or their pocket. Loose bodies, osteophytes on the coronoid and olecranon tips, synovitis and osteochondritis of the capitellum in a young thrower are the other reasons to look inside. Elbow arthroscopy is the way in. The joint is distended with 20-30 mL of saline through the lateral soft spot, which pushes the anterior neurovascular bundle away from the capsule, and portals are made above and below the medial and lateral epicondyles with the elbow flexed to 90 degrees, because flexion relaxes the anterior structures and extension brings the radial nerve 3-7 mm closer to the joint. The distances are small whatever is done: the proximal anteromedial portal sits about 4 mm from the ulnar nerve and 7-20 mm from the median nerve, and the proximal anterolateral portal a mean 13.7 mm from the radial nerve. A stiff elbow pulls the nerves onto the capsule and a rheumatoid elbow has a thin, friable capsule with no safe plane, which is why those two diagnoses predicted transient nerve palsy in the 473-case Mayo series (p less than 0.001 and p less than 0.05), where 12 palsies occurred in 10 patients, all temporary, alongside four deep infections (0.8%) and a minor complication rate of 11%, most of it portal drainage. The pooled modern figure across 14,213 arthroscopies is a total complication rate of 8% and a nerve injury rate of 1.95%, mostly transient.

A release for stiffness takes the same route and goes further. Arthroscopically, the surgeon excises the anterior capsule off the humerus (a capsulectomy, not a capsulotomy, because an incised capsule rescars), clears the coronoid and olecranon fossae of osteophytes and scar, releases the posterior capsule, and often decompresses or releases the ulnar nerve through a small medial incision, because an elbow regaining flexion will stretch a nerve that has been sitting in a scarred cubital tunnel. In the 30-patient arthroscopic series the arc went from 68 to 123 degrees with three transient nerve palsies. An open release does the same through a lateral column incision, a medial over-the-top approach, or both: a muscle interval is split, the capsule excised under direct vision, heterotopic bone removed if there is any, and the ulnar nerve transposed. In the 52-patient open series the arc went from 57 to 116 degrees, but 27% needed a manipulation under anaesthesia in the early weeks and five needed a second release, which is the honest description of what the surgeon is fighting: a capsule that wants to scar back, and a movement programme that has to beat it.

Tennis elbow is a degenerate tendon origin, not an inflamed one. The extensor carpi radialis brevis origin on the lateral epicondyle develops angiofibroblastic degeneration and microtears under repetitive load, and after a year of failed non-operative treatment the surgeon excises the degenerate tissue and either decorticates the epicondyle and repairs the origin (the open Nirschl-type procedure), releases it arthroscopically from inside the joint, or simply divides it percutaneously through a 1 cm stab. The three give the same pain relief and function in the pooled 1,622 elbows (VAS 1.45 open versus 1.62 arthroscopic), with more minor complications after open surgery (57% versus 33%, mostly stiffness, haematoma and superficial infection), and percutaneous release under infiltration alone returned 87% of patients to complete pain relief in a 30-elbow series with a three-week return to work.

The pain logic: the dominant mechanism for arthroscopy and release is capsule, which is sharp with movement and settles over days, and in a release it is made worse by the fact that the joint is then deliberately pushed through a range it has not had for months, so the movement-evoked component is the whole point of the operation rather than a side effect. Irrigation fluid in the periarticular tissues adds a tight, swollen elbow for 48 hours. Osteophyte removal and capitellar drilling add a small bone surface. Open release adds a muscle split and a handled ulnar nerve with a neuropathic edge. Tennis elbow surgery is a surface-and-periosteum operation, a decorticated epicondyle and a debrided tendon origin, and it hurts in the way a decompression does: a few bad days, then a sore elbow for weeks that responds to simple analgesia. None of these operations produces the deep bone pain of an arthroplasty or the prolonged tendon-tension pain of a cuff repair; a score of 2 fits all three, with the release at the top of that band.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Pre-operative neurology, the block decision and the position

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon is about to put instruments within millimetres of three nerves, in a stiff or rheumatoid elbow that has pulled them closer, and will want to know afterwards whether a weak hand is the block or the operation.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Document ulnar, median, radial and posterior interosseous function before anything is given. For a diagnostic or loose body arthroscopy and for tennis elbow surgery the author’s practice is general anaesthesia with surgeon infiltration and no block: the pain is a 2, the case is short, and a dense block buys nothing but a delay in the nerve check. For a release, where the surgeon wants the new range used from the first day, a single-shot infraclavicular or supraclavicular block with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg is placed before induction, after the surgeon has agreed to examine the hand when it recedes rather than in recovery. The position decides the airway: prone and lateral need a tracheal tube and the usual care of the dependent eye, plexus and the face in a cradle; supine with the arm across the chest accepts a supraglottic airway in a short case.

  2. 2 Positioning and the tourniquet

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Prone or lateral with the arm over a bolster and the elbow hanging at 90 degrees gives the surgeon access to the posterior compartment, gravity working for them, and the forearm free to move; supine with the arm across the chest is quicker but the arm has to be held. The tourniquet keeps the view clear.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    In prone, the bolster is under the mid-humerus and the shoulder is abducted to 90 degrees and the elbow flexed; check that the shoulder is not forced and that the bolster does not press the radial nerve against the shaft of the humerus, because a radial palsy from a bolster is indistinguishable from one from a portal. The tourniquet goes high on the arm, inflated after exsanguination. In lateral, the same hazards as a shoulder in lateral, with the dependent arm on a board and the head neutral. Tourniquet pain under general anaesthesia is a slow rise in pressure and heart rate from 45 min onwards that will not answer opioid; a release can run to 120 min, an arthroscopy for loose bodies to 45.

  3. 3 Antibiotic and the first distension

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The joint is filled through the lateral soft spot with 20-30 mL of saline before any portal is made; a distended joint moves the brachial artery and median nerve away from the anterior capsule and makes the capsule taut enough to pierce cleanly. Deep infection is 0.4-0.8% and most surgeons give an antibiotic for an arthroscopy that may become a release.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cefazolin 2 g IV before inflation. Nothing else at this step, but note the pump pressure the surgeon sets and the irrigation volume at the start, because this joint holds far less than a shoulder and extravasates earlier.

  4. 4 Anterior compartment: portals, loose bodies, synovectomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon enters through a proximal anteromedial or anterolateral portal with the elbow flexed, inspects the coronoid, the radial head and capitellum, removes loose bodies and synovitis with a shaver, and clears coronoid osteophytes with a burr. The trocar is kept flexed and aimed at the centre of the joint because extension brings the radial nerve closer.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The shaver and burr are the moments of risk for the posterior interosseous and median nerves, and a sudden request to stop the patient moving is a request for depth, not neuromuscular block: the surgeon cannot see the nerves and is relying on the elbow not shifting on the bolster. A retractor may be put through a second portal to hold the capsule off the nerves. Irrigation now begins to leave the joint; the forearm and the anterior elbow swell steadily from this point.

  5. 5 Capsulectomy and the formal release

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    For a stiff elbow the anterior capsule is excised from its humeral attachment, with a retractor holding the brachialis and the bundle away; the posterior capsule is released from the posterior compartment and the olecranon fossa cleared of bone and scar. The surgeon then extends and flexes the elbow on the table to confirm the gain and to tear whatever adhesions are left. A medial incision is often added to decompress or transpose the ulnar nerve, because regained flexion stretches a nerve trapped in scar.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the capsule mechanism being created and the step that fixes the pain course. The manipulation at the end is forceful; in a patient without a block it is the most stimulating moment of the operation, and in an osteoporotic post-traumatic elbow it is when a supracondylar fracture happens. The ulnar nerve work means a neuropathic element in the hand afterwards and another reason the surgeon wanted the pre-operative documentation.

  6. 6 Posterior compartment and the olecranon

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Through a posterior and posterolateral portal the surgeon clears the olecranon fossa, trims the olecranon tip, removes posterior loose bodies and releases the posterior capsule; this is what restores flexion. The ulnar nerve is a few millimetres medial to the posteromedial gutter.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    In prone the surgeon is now working with gravity, the view is good and the step is quick. Check the forearm: by now a release has run 60-90 min of irrigation, and a forearm that is tense and hard rather than soft and swollen has a compartment filling with saline. The surgeon will usually stop the pump and wait; it resolves within hours and the reported experience is that it does not progress to a compartment syndrome, but a forearm that is still tense after the drapes come off is examined and documented before any block, and the block that was placed for a release is the one circumstance in which you cannot use pain to follow it.

  7. 7 Tennis elbow: the lateral epicondylar release

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A different operation, usually supine on an arm table. Open: a 3-5 cm incision over the lateral epicondyle, the interval between extensor carpi radialis longus and the common extensor origin opened, the degenerate grey tissue at the extensor carpi radialis brevis origin excised, the epicondyle decorticated to bleeding bone to provoke a healing response, and the origin repaired or left. Arthroscopic: the same origin released from inside the joint, which avoids the skin incision and the extensor repair. Percutaneous: a stab and a division of the origin, under local infiltration alone, no tourniquet. The lateral collateral ligament is a few millimetres posterior and the surgeon stays anterior to the equator of the radial head to protect it.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    20-40 min under a tourniquet for the open and arthroscopic versions, and the author’s practice is general anaesthesia with surgeon infiltration, or infiltration with sedation for the percutaneous release, because the pain is a 2 that simple analgesia covers. The posterior interosseous nerve is at risk only if the surgeon dissects distally; a wrist drop afterwards is surgical. The decorticated epicondyle is a raw bone surface and the reason the elbow aches for weeks.

  8. 8 Closure, dressing and the splint or no splint

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Portals are closed with a suture rather than a strip because the elbow extravasates and leaks; a bulky dressing goes on. After a release the surgeon decides between a splint in extension overnight, a hinged brace, or nothing, and some use continuous passive motion from day 1. After tennis elbow surgery a sling for comfort and no immobilisation.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Give the antiemetic. The elbow is swollen and the forearm full; a dressing that is already tight will be tighter in two hours. If a release patient has a block, the arm goes into whatever the surgeon wants before the patient wakes, and the forearm is examined one more time at the end.

  9. 9 Recovery and the first night

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the arm elevated, the swelling down, the nerves checked, and for a release, movement started within 24 h, because the capsule begins to scar back from the first day.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    For the unblocked arthroscopy and tennis elbow patient, paracetamol and an NSAID started in recovery with a few doses of oral opioid for the first two nights; the pain peaks on the evening of surgery and day 1 and settles over a week. For the blocked release patient, the hand is examined when the block recedes at 12-24 h, at the time agreed with the surgeon, and the oral regimen is started in recovery, not when the block wears off. Half of ambulatory block patients describe rebound pain when the block goes, with bone surgery and no intravenous dexamethasone raising the odds; a release has a small bone component and the dexamethasone was given for that. Nearly every patient on this card goes home the same day; a release may stay a night where the surgeon runs a supervised movement programme.

Pain plan

The mechanism is capsule for arthroscopy and release, surface and periosteum for tennis elbow surgery, and neither is a mechanism that a long-acting block changes much after the first night. There is no PROSPECT guideline for any of the three and the analgesia literature is thin, so the plan is author’s judgement built on the mechanism, on the general rebound-pain data and on what the surgeon needs afterwards.

For a diagnostic or loose body arthroscopy and for tennis elbow surgery, the defensible default is general anaesthesia (or sedation for a percutaneous release), surgeon infiltration of the portals or the wound with 10-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% or bupivacaine 0.25%, paracetamol and an NSAID started pre-operatively and continued for a week, intravenous dexamethasone 4-8 mg at induction, and an oral opioid for two nights. No block: it would hide the nerve check for the one complication that matters, and it would not change the day 1 pain in an operation whose peak is reached with simple analgesia.

For a release the calculation changes, because the operation only works if the elbow is moved from the first day, and movement through a freshly excised capsule is the one thing a single-shot block makes possible on day 0 and the oral regimen makes tolerable from day 1. The defensible default is a single-shot infraclavicular or supraclavicular block with 20-25 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% and intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, placed after the surgeon has agreed to a delayed nerve check, with the same oral regimen. A catheter is author’s judgement for the severely stiff elbow (arc under 60 degrees), the revision release, the patient who had a manipulation after a previous release, and the unit that runs continuous passive motion or a twice-daily physiotherapy programme in hospital, provided the surgeon accepts two days without a nerve examination and the forearm is examined by eye and by hand rather than by pain. The catheter’s cost is a flail arm during the exercises that it exists to make possible; the surgeon decides whether the trade is worth it.

Escalation: pain above 7 after the block has receded, or on day 1 in an unblocked patient, is treated with the charted opioid and a check that the regular doses were given. Pain with a tense forearm, pain on passive finger extension, or a hand that is cooler than the other is a compartment filling, and after an arthroscopy it is usually saline rather than blood, but it is examined by a surgeon, not treated with more opioid. Pain that is better on day 1 than on day 3, in a hot swollen joint, is one of the 0.4-0.8% of deep infections.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Prone versus lateral versus supine. Prone and lateral mean a tracheal tube and the bolster hazards; supine with the arm across the chest means an assistant holding the arm and a surgeon who finds the posterior compartment harder. Ask which, because it decides the airway.
  • The rheumatoid elbow. Thin friable capsule, no safe plane, the strongest predictor of nerve palsy in the Mayo series, plus the neck, airway and steroid questions of every rheumatoid patient.
  • The post-traumatic stiff elbow with retained metal or heterotopic bone. The nerves are in scar and may be adherent to the capsule; the surgeon may convert to open, the case lengthens to two hours, and the ulnar nerve is transposed. A supracondylar fracture during the end-of-case manipulation is the disaster that turns a day case into a fixation.
  • Fluid extravasation. The elbow holds 20-30 mL and leaks from the first portal; a release with a high pump pressure fills the forearm. It resolves, but a tense forearm is examined before any block or discharge, and the block placed for a release removes pain as a sign.
  • Nerve palsy. 1.95% pooled, mostly transient, ulnar commonest; documented before the case, examined after the block recedes, and treated as the surgeon’s problem if it is a posterior interosseous or median palsy after an anterior portal.
  • The ulnar nerve in a release. Decompressed or transposed by most surgeons when flexion is being regained; dysaesthesia in the ring and little fingers afterwards is expected for weeks and is not the block.
  • Osteochondritis dissecans of the capitellum. A young thrower, a short arthroscopy with drilling or a graft, day case; the bone surface adds a little to the first two days.
  • Tennis elbow: open versus arthroscopic versus percutaneous. Same outcome, fewer minor complications arthroscopically, and the percutaneous release needs nothing but infiltration. Iatrogenic lateral collateral instability is the complication that presents late and to the surgeon, not to you.
  • Continuous passive motion and early physiotherapy after a release. Where the surgeon uses it, the block and the regimen have to be matched to a programme, and the catheter question is decided by the programme, not by the operation.
  • Day-case discharge with a block after a release. A flail arm, a splint or brace, written instructions, oral analgesia already started and a named person for the night; the same list as any ambulatory block patient, with the forearm examined and documented before the patient leaves.

Key sources

The complication data for elbow arthroscopy are good: a single-institution series of 473 cases and a meta-analysis of 14,213, agreeing on a transient nerve palsy rate of about 2% and a deep infection rate under 1%. The stiffness data are small single-surgeon series, one arthroscopic and one open, that show the same 50-60 degree gain and the same tendency to recur. The tennis elbow data come from a systematic review of 1,622 elbows showing no difference between open and arthroscopic release and a small series of percutaneous release under local anaesthesia alone. There is no analgesia trial for any of these operations and no PROSPECT guideline; the block, catheter and day-case advice is author’s judgement from the mechanism and from the general ambulatory block literature.
