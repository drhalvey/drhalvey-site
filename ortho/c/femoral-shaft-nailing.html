40 Femoral shaft nailing

Antegrade (piriformis or trochanteric entry) and retrograde reamed intramedullary nailing; traction table or free leg; the polytrauma patient and the damage control decision

3Moderate

Dominant mechanism: Bone. A fractured, mobile long bone with a litre of blood in the thigh; then a reamed canal, an entry point through gluteus medius or the patellar tendon, and a fracture compressed and held rigid by the nail. The fixation itself is the analgesic: a splinted femur hurts far less than a mobile one, and the residual pain is reaming, the entry point and the swollen thigh

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: moderate.

Block
Femoral nerve or fascia iliaca block for the pre-operative period and for traction; the operation itself under general anaesthesia, with a single-shot femoral block or fascia iliaca block as the analgesic component where there is no compartment syndrome concern
Catheter: rarely A single shot covers the first night and the thigh is not a compartment syndrome site in the way the leg is; a catheter is for the patient with bilateral femurs, a floating knee or opioid tolerance, and never in a patient whose other injuries need serial neurological examination
Alternatives: Spinal anaesthesia for the isolated fracture in a fasted, stable patient who can tolerate the traction table; Surgeon's infiltration at the entry point and portals; Multimodal with ketamine for the polytrauma patient without a block
Position
Supine on a traction table with a perineal post for most antegrade nails; supine on a radiolucent table with the leg free and manual traction for the polytrauma patient and for retrograde nailing with the knee flexed over a bolster; lateral on a traction table in a few surgeons' hands

The fracture has already lost 1-1.5 L into the thigh before you meet the patient; the operation adds 300-600 mL, more with reaming of a comminuted fracture and much more in a polytrauma patient with a coagulopathy. Tell the patient
Once the nail is in, the bone is held still and most of the deep pain of the break goes; the thigh stays swollen and bruised for a couple of weeks and the hip or knee entry point is sore when you walk on it, which you will be asked to do straight away.
The trap
Reaming is a marrow embolisation step, and the patient who cannot afford it is the one with a chest injury or a borderline physiology after resuscitation: in the randomised trial of borderline polytrauma patients, primary nailing carried 6.7 times the odds of acute lung injury compared with external fixation and nailing later, so the question to settle before the reamer is whether this patient should be nailed today at all

What the surgeon is doing and why

A femoral shaft fracture is a high-energy injury in a young patient or a low-energy one in an osteoporotic or pathological femur, and both get the same implant. The femur is the largest tube of bone in the body inside the largest muscle mass, and when it breaks the muscles shorten and angulate the fragments, the thigh fills with a litre or more of blood, and the patient cannot be moved without the fragments grinding. The surgeon’s aim is to restore length, alignment and rotation and hold them with an implant that lets the patient weight-bear at once. A reamed, locked intramedullary nail does that from inside the bone: a load-sharing splint down the canal, locked above and below so the fragments cannot shorten or rotate, inserted through small incisions away from the fracture so the haematoma and periosteum that will heal the bone are left alone. The Canadian trial of 224 polytrauma patients found nonunion five times more likely with an unreamed nail and no excess of lung injury from reaming, which is why nearly every femoral nail is now reamed.

The nail can go in from the top or the bottom. Antegrade nailing enters at the piriformis fossa or the tip of the greater trochanter, through gluteus medius, and is the standard for most shaft fractures. Retrograde nailing enters through the intercondylar notch of the knee, through the patellar tendon, with the knee flexed over a bolster, and is chosen for the obese patient whose trochanter cannot be reached, the patient with an ipsilateral femoral neck or acetabular fracture that needs the hip left alone, the floating knee (ipsilateral tibial and femoral fractures, nailed through one knee incision), the distal third or supracondylar fracture, and the periprosthetic fracture above a knee replacement. Union rates are equivalent; the retrograde knee is sorer for longer.

The second decision is the table. A traction table with a perineal post holds the fracture out to length with the surgeon’s hands free, at the cost of a post pressing on the pudendal nerve and the perineum, a pull on the sciatic and peroneal nerves, and a patient who cannot be turned or reached quickly. The free-leg technique on a radiolucent table, with an assistant pulling, is slower but is the choice for the polytrauma patient who may need a chest drain or a laparotomy, for bilateral femurs, and for retrograde nailing.

The third decision belongs to both of you and is the heart of this card: whether to nail today at all. Bone’s 1989 randomised trial showed that in multiply injured patients delayed femoral fixation carried more ARDS, fat embolism and pneumonia and a longer ICU stay, and early total care became the rule. Then it became clear that the operation itself, reaming a litre of marrow and fat into the circulation during a 2-h case with its own blood loss, is a second inflammatory hit, and that in the patient whose first hit has already primed the lungs, the second hit is what tips them into ARDS. Scalea’s damage control orthopaedics (external fixation in minutes, nailing days later when the physiology has recovered) was the answer, and Pape’s randomised trial of 165 polytrauma patients put numbers on it: in stable patients, primary nailing shortened ventilation; in the 44 borderline patients, primary nailing carried 6.7 times the odds of acute lung injury compared with an external fixator and a nail later. The full physiology of the decision is in the polytrauma card; for this card the rule is that the stable patient is nailed within 24 h and the borderline or unstable patient gets a fixator and a reamer in three to five days’ time.

The pain logic is bone, and it runs backwards from most operations: the patient is in more pain before surgery than after it. The fracture haematoma, the mobile fragments and the muscle spasm hurt in a way splinting cannot fully control, and the nail, by locking the fracture, removes most of that. What remains is the reamed canal (a deep ache for two or three days), the entry point (gluteus medius split at the trochanter, or the patellar tendon and knee joint for a retrograde nail), the locking screw incisions through vastus lateralis, and a thigh swollen with a litre of old blood. The score is 3: a block or neuraxial technique is expected, opioids are needed for two or three days, and the pain falls as bone pain falls. The young patient after a motorcycle crash sits at the top of the range and will need a great deal of opioid in the first 48 h; the older patient after a fall has the same operation with the hip fracture pathway’s problems.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 The trauma bay: splint, block, blood and the decision

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the fracture splinted in traction to reduce bleeding and pain, the limb’s circulation and skin documented, an ipsilateral femoral neck fracture excluded on a dedicated hip view or CT (it is missed in a meaningful minority of shaft fractures and changes the nail), and the patient’s physiology classified as stable, borderline or unstable.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A femoral nerve or fascia iliaca block in the trauma bay is the analgesic that lets the traction splint go on; the thigh is not the compartment syndrome site that the leg is, so the block is not withheld on those grounds. The femur has already bled a litre or more into the thigh, so a young patient with a normal pressure and a heart rate of 110 is a litre down and compensating; tranexamic acid 1 g IV within 3 h of injury if there is any bleeding concern, a group and hold or crossmatch, and a lactate and base deficit as the honest measures of how far behind the resuscitation is. Decide the damage control question with the surgeon now, with the lactate, the chest radiograph, the temperature and the coagulation in front of you, not in the anaesthetic room with the patient asleep.

  2. 2 Induction and the unfasted trauma patient

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The isolated closed femoral fracture in a stable patient is nailed on the next available list within 24 h, which is almost never a fasted patient.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Trauma, opioids and pain stop gastric emptying at the moment of injury, so the fasting interval is counted from the crash, not the last meal, and a rapid sequence induction is the default. Spinal anaesthesia is an option for the isolated fracture in a stable, fasted and cooperative patient on a traction table, with a block for the positioning; it is not the choice for a polytrauma patient who may need a laparotomy, a chest drain or a long case. Two large-bore cannulae and an arterial line for anything beyond an isolated fracture. Cefazolin 2 g IV before the incision; for an open fracture the antibiotic was due within an hour of injury and follows the open fracture card. Dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction.

  3. 3 Positioning: traction table or free leg

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    On the traction table the surgeon puts the foot in a boot, the perineum against the post, adducts the leg to clear the trochanter, swings the C-arm and reduces the fracture with traction and rotation before scrubbing. For a free-leg nailing the patient lies on a radiolucent table with a bump under the buttock and an assistant holds traction by hand.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The post is the injury you can prevent. In the systematic review of 351 traction table patients, mostly femoral shafts, erectile dysfunction occurred in 10%, genital sensory disturbance in 6% and perineal skin necrosis in 3%, with pudendal palsy correlating with the summed pressure on the perineum rather than operative time alone. Pad the post, check the genitalia are not trapped between post and thigh, ask for traction to be released whenever the surgeon is not reducing or imaging, and note the traction time. The pull also stretches the sciatic nerve and the peroneal nerve at the fibular neck; the well leg, abducted and elevated for the C-arm, is at risk of a calf compartment syndrome in a long case. The arm on the operative side is across the chest and must not drag on the plexus. Forced-air warming on the upper body, because the trauma patient arrives cold and the traction table exposes everything.

  4. 4 Entry point: piriformis fossa, trochanteric tip or intercondylar notch

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Antegrade: a 5-cm incision proximal to the trochanter, a guidewire into the piriformis fossa (in line with the canal, but with the medial femoral circumflex artery and the abductors in the way) or the trochanteric tip (easier, used with a bent nail, through gluteus medius), confirmed on two views, then an entry reamer opens the canal. Retrograde: a medial parapatellar or transtendinous incision with the knee flexed 40-50 degrees, a guidewire into the notch just anterior to the posterior cruciate ligament origin, and the entry reamer through the articular cartilage.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Opening the canal is the first marrow venting, smaller than the reaming to come. The trochanteric entry disrupts gluteus medius and is the abductor limp and the hip pain on stairs that follow antegrade nailing for months. The retrograde entry is intra-articular, so the knee will be a haemarthrosis and a stiff, sore joint for weeks.

  5. 5 Reduction and passage of the guidewire across the fracture

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The fracture is reduced with traction, rotation and manipulation of the fragments by a crutch, a mallet or a percutaneous reduction tool, and a ball-tipped guidewire is passed down the canal across the fracture to the distal metaphysis. A fracture that will not reduce closed needs a small open reduction at the fracture site, which disturbs the haematoma and lengthens the case.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the most physically violent part of the operation and the one that needs a relaxed patient: muscle spasm is what holds the fragments shortened and angulated, so the surgeon’s request for relaxation here is a real one, and a bolus of neuromuscular blocker makes a difference to the reduction. It is also the step most likely to run long. Malrotation is the common malunion after femoral nailing and the surgeon will compare the lesser trochanter profile against the other side, which explains a pause with the C-arm over the well leg.

  6. 6 Reaming

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Flexible reamers are passed over the guidewire in 0.5-mm increments until cortical chatter, to a diameter 1-1.5 mm over the planned nail, so that the nail fits the isthmus tightly and the reaming debris acts as a bone graft at the fracture. Reaming raises intramedullary pressure and forces marrow fat into the venous sinusoids; sharp reamers, slow advancement and a venting hole reduce the peak.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Every pass of the reamer sends marrow fat and debris into the femoral veins and through the right heart; transoesophageal echocardiography shows embolic material in 88% of patients during reaming. In the healthy young patient the lungs filter it with a transient fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide and saturation and nothing else. In the patient with a chest injury, a second fractured femur, a pelvic fracture or a pre-existing lung, this is the second hit: fat embolism syndrome (hypoxaemia, confusion and petechiae 12-72 h later, supportive treatment, 1-10% mortality in the reviews) and ARDS are the consequences. During reaming, watch end-tidal carbon dioxide, saturation and pressure together; an abrupt fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide with a rise in central venous pressure is a large embolic load and the reaming should pause. The Canadian trial found no increase in ARDS with reaming over unreamed nails, so the question is not whether to ream but whether this patient should have had a nail today; see fat embolism.

  7. 7 Nail insertion and proximal locking

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The nail is driven over the guidewire with a mallet, the fracture reduced around it, and locked proximally through a jig (antegrade) or distally through a jig (retrograde). Nail length and rotation are checked under imaging.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Hammering a nail down a reamed canal is a second pressurisation and a second embolic load, smaller than reaming. The blood loss of the operation is mostly from the reamed canal and the entry point, and a comminuted or segmental fracture bleeds more because the reaming debris and the haematoma have nowhere to go; check a haemoglobin in a long case. A nail that incarcerates in a canal reamed too narrow is a long delay and a surgeon reaming again.

  8. 8 Distal locking and release of traction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Distal interlocking is done freehand under the image intensifier (“perfect circles”), through small lateral incisions and vastus lateralis, two screws in most fractures; the traction is released so the fracture compresses onto the nail, and rotation and length are compared with the other leg.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Freehand locking is 10-20 min of fluoroscopy with the surgeon’s hands in the beam, a still leg and a patient the surgeon wants left alone. Release of traction is the moment to look at the perineum and the well leg, and to feel the calf of the operated leg; a thigh that is tense after nailing is a haematoma, and compartment syndrome of the thigh, rare but real after high-energy fractures and prolonged traction, presents as pain out of proportion and a tense anterior compartment.

  9. 9 Closure, emergence and recovery

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The incisions are small and closed in layers; there is no drain and no cast. The surgeon wants a check radiograph, full weight-bearing as pain allows from day 1 for a stable fracture pattern with a reamed locked nail, and an early rotational profile check.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Emergence from a rapid sequence induction in a full-stomach patient is awake extubation. The oral regimen starts in recovery. The young patient will need substantial opioid in the first 24 h; the haemoglobin on the morning after is the one that counts, after the thigh has finished redistributing. Low molecular weight heparin starts the night of surgery unless the bleeding risk says otherwise; a femoral fracture is a high-risk injury for VTE and the clot is forming in the femoral vein from the night of the crash.

  10. 10 Day 1 and the ward

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the patient standing on day 1 with physiotherapy, a hip and knee range started, and the other injuries managed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Fat embolism syndrome declares itself on day 1-3 as confusion, hypoxaemia, a fever and petechiae on the chest and in the axillae, and the ward will call it pneumonia or opioid toxicity; the treatment is oxygen and supportive care, and the diagnosis is yours to make when the night registrar asks. NSAIDs: the case-control literature associates them with femoral nonunion and many surgeons ask for them to be withheld after shaft fractures; the evidence is observational, but it is the surgeon’s call and worth asking before the chart is written.

Pain plan

The mechanism is bone with the operation removing most of the pre-operative generator, so the plan is a block for the pre-operative period and the traction, a general anaesthetic for the operation, and a multimodal regimen with substantial early opioid. There is no PROSPECT guideline for femoral shaft nailing; the nearest is the hip fracture recommendation, and its skeleton (single-shot femoral or fascia iliaca block, paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor if the surgeon permits, opioid rescue) is the author’s starting point here.

The block: a femoral nerve block or fascia iliaca block in the trauma bay covers the anterior thigh and the fracture haematoma, lets the splint and the traction go on, and is repeated before the operation if more than 6 h have passed. It does not cover the posterior thigh or the retrograde knee entry, and a sciatic block in a trauma patient with a possible sciatic nerve injury from the fracture obscures an examination. The catheter decision: a single shot is enough for an isolated fracture, because the nail is the analgesic and the thigh is not the compartment syndrome site the lower leg is. A femoral catheter earns its place for bilateral femurs, the floating knee, the patient who waits days with a fixator on, and the opioid-tolerant patient; it is withheld in the polytrauma patient with a head or spine injury who needs serial neurological examination.

The oral regimen shape: regular paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor for the first few days where the surgeon accepts it (many will not, citing the nonunion association), and an opioid at regular intervals for the first 48 h with a patient-controlled analgesia pump for the young high-energy patient, who will need more than the ward expects. Ketamine at low dose is the author’s adjunct for the opioid-tolerant or the patient without a block. Rebound is moderate: when the block recedes the fracture is fixed and the pain is a sore entry point and a bruised thigh.

Escalation: pain that rises on day 1-2 with a tense thigh is a haematoma or, rarely, a thigh compartment syndrome; pain with new confusion and hypoxaemia is fat embolism syndrome; pain in the calf of either leg is a clot until proved otherwise. For the retrograde nail, a knee that is hot and swollen at a week is a haemarthrosis or a septic knee, and the surgeon sees it first.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The ipsilateral femoral neck fracture. Present in a minority of high-energy shaft fractures and missed on the trauma series; it changes the operation to a retrograde nail with screws into the neck, or a cephalomedullary device, and adds an hour.
  • Bilateral femoral fractures. Two litres in the thighs before the operation, two reamings, a higher fat embolism rate (4.6% against 2.9% in the systematic review), a free-leg technique, and a patient who is borderline by definition. One or both fixators first is a reasonable call.
  • The floating knee. Ipsilateral femoral and tibial fractures, usually nailed through one knee incision (retrograde femur, antegrade tibia); a long case with two reamings and a sore knee for months.
  • The obese patient. The trochanter cannot be reached antegrade, so retrograde nailing; a traction table that may not take the weight; a difficult airway and a difficult arterial line.
  • The older patient after a fall. The same nail with a hip fracture physiology: anticoagulants to reverse, a dry patient, a long reaming of an osteoporotic canal that bleeds, and delirium on the ward. The hip fracture card applies.
  • The pathological fracture. A metastasis in the shaft means a long nail reamed through tumour, with embolisation of tumour and fat together, often a larger blood loss, and a conversation about the rest of the skeleton and the prognosis before the operation.
  • The open femoral fracture. Antibiotics within an hour of injury, debridement, and either a primary nail (most Gustilo I-IIIA fractures) or an external fixator for the contaminated or vascular injury; see the open fracture card.
  • The head-injured patient. Early fixation does not cause secondary brain injury; hypoxia, hypotension and raised intracranial pressure do, and a reaming-induced desaturation is a brain insult in this patient.
  • The perineal post complaint. Numbness of the genitalia or erectile dysfunction after the traction table is a pudendal neuropraxia; it resolves over weeks to months, needs documentation, and is prevented by padding and short traction times.
  • Nail removal. Elective removal of a femoral nail after union is harder than it looks and bleeds more than expected; see removal of metalwork.

Key sources

The evidence on timing is the strongest part of this card and the weakest part of most anaesthetists’ knowledge of it: Bone’s randomised trial for early fixation, Scalea’s series for damage control, and Pape’s randomised trial for the borderline patient between them define who is nailed on the day. The reamed versus unreamed question is settled by the Canadian trial. The fat embolism literature is observational and the incidence has fallen with early fixation. The perineal post review is recent and sobering. There is no procedure-specific analgesia guideline; the block and catheter advice here is extrapolated from the hip fracture recommendations and is the author’s judgement where the card says so.
