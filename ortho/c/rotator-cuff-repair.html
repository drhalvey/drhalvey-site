3 Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair

With or without subacromial decompression, biceps tenodesis and distal clavicle excision

5Severe and prolonged

Dominant mechanism: Tendon under tension. A retracted, degenerate tendon pulled back onto a decorticated, bleeding footprint under tension and loaded by every movement for weeks; plus raw bone from acromioplasty, an inflamed bursa stripped out, and arthroscopic fluid in the soft tissues

What the surgeon is doing and why

A rotator cuff tear is a tendon that has pulled off bone. Supraspinatus is the usual victim, because it runs under the acromion and over the humeral head at the point of maximum load and minimum blood supply. Once the tendon end is off the greater tuberosity it retracts medially, the muscle behind it atrophies and fills with fat, and the humeral head loses the depressor that kept it centred under the acromion, so the patient gets weakness, night pain, and a head that rides up and impinges. The tissue the surgeon is working with is not healthy tendon that happened to tear; it is a degenerate, retracted, poorly vascular structure that has to be dragged back to where it came from and persuaded to heal to bone over about twelve weeks. Re-tear rates after repair are substantial and rise with tear size and age, which is why the protection afterwards is so cautious and why the surgeon cares about every detail of fixation.

The operation has a fixed logic:

  1. See the problem: diagnostic arthroscopy of the joint, then the subacromial space. The long head of biceps is often cut or tenodesed on the way because it is a pain generator and anchors nothing useful.
  2. Clear the workspace: the subacromial bursa, thick and inflamed, is stripped out so the tendon and the acromion can be seen. Many surgeons then shave the undersurface of the acromion (acromioplasty) and some excise the distal clavicle. The randomised trials show acromioplasty adds nothing to re-tear or function, but it is still common and it leaves a raw bleeding bone surface.
  3. Free the tendon: releases of the capsule and the coracohumeral ligament above and below the tendon so that it can be pulled laterally without excess tension. The more retracted the tear, the more release, the longer the case.
  4. Prepare the footprint: the cortical surface of the greater tuberosity where the tendon used to insert is decorticated with a burr to bleeding bone, because tendon heals to raw bone and not to cortex.
  5. Fix it: suture anchors are placed in the tuberosity (one row at the medial edge, or two rows in a “transosseous-equivalent” bridge), sutures are passed through the tendon, and the tendon is reduced onto the footprint and tied or locked under tension. The tension is the point: a lax repair does not heal and a tight one re-tears, and the surgeon judges it with the arm in a set position.
  6. Protect it: a sling, often with an abduction pillow, for six weeks, with no active use of the repaired tendon. Every movement that loads the tendon in those weeks is pain and risk.

The pain logic is the opposite of an anatomic shoulder replacement. There the tissues are left at native tension and bone pain fades once nothing is being loaded through the cut. Here the dominant mechanism is tendon-tension: a repaired, poorly vascular tendon that is pulled onto bone and then loaded, however gently, by every reach and every roll in bed for weeks. Underneath it is bone (the decorticated footprint and the acromioplasty, which hurt like any osteotomy for two or three days), capsule (the releases), and arthroscopy itself (portals, irrigation fluid in the deltoid and subcutaneous tissues, and a shoulder that has been held in traction or abduction for an hour and a half). The early peak is no higher than after arthroplasty: in the one prospective comparison both groups peaked on day 2, at a mean of 4.4 for cuff repair and 5.1 for arthroplasty under long-acting interscalene blocks. The difference is afterwards. Cuff repair patients were still using more opioid at a week, and the night pain that defines this operation runs for one to two weeks. A plan that only covers the first night has covered the easy part.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Block, airway and the night plan before the patient is asleep

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The whole operation is done through 5 mm portals under irrigation; nothing about it needs your attention as much as the hours after it.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the operation PROSPECT wrote its shoulder guideline for: interscalene block first choice, intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg with it, paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started pre-operatively and continued. Decide the catheter question now with the surgeon’s tear size in front of you (see the pain plan). Airway: a tracheal tube is the author’s default in beach chair, because the head is behind a screen and the neck may swell with irrigation fluid over a long case; a second-generation supraglottic device is reasonable for a short repair in a slim patient. Cuff on the non-operative arm, awake baseline pressure recorded, eyes taped and padded, because the surgeon’s arm and the camera cable will be passing over the face.

  2. 2 Positioning: beach chair or lateral decubitus

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Beach chair gives a familiar anatomy and easy conversion to open; lateral decubitus with the arm in longitudinal traction opens the joint and the subacromial space wider and some surgeons will not operate any other way.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Beach chair: the cuff overstates brain perfusion pressure by roughly 1 mmHg per 1.25 cm of height between cuff and ear, so keep the cuff reading near the awake baseline with a vasopressor infusion and treat the bradycardia-hypotension episodes that the sitting position plus interscalene block provoke. Lateral: the airway is now accessed from the side with the patient’s face in a cradle, the dependent arm and eye need padding, the traction (5-7 kg) is a sustained pull on the plexus that you cannot see, and hypotension is less of a problem but a shift in the patient’s position mid-case is harder to correct. In either position, check that the head is neutral once the surgeon has set the arm, because they will move it.

  3. 3 Portals, irrigation pump and the request for a low pressure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Visibility in the subacromial space depends on the balance between irrigation pump pressure (typically 40-60 mmHg) and the patient’s systolic pressure; bleeding from the bursa and the acromion fogs the view. Surgeons raise the pump, add adrenaline to the irrigation bags, or ask you to lower the pressure.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The request for a systolic of 90 is the central conflict of this operation. In beach chair it is a request for a brainstem perfusion pressure in the 50s, and PROSPECT lists controlled hypotension as not recommended on exactly those grounds. The honest answer is that you will keep the pressure near baseline, that adrenaline in the irrigation (1 mL of 1:1000 per 3 L bag is a common recipe) and a higher pump pressure are the surgeon’s tools, and that a patient with a working interscalene block and an adequate depth of anaesthesia will not be hypertensive. Adrenaline absorbed from the irrigation can produce transient tachycardia and hypertension; a sudden pressure rise shortly after a new bag goes up is that, not light anaesthesia.

  4. 4 Diagnostic arthroscopy, biceps and labrum

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The glenohumeral joint is inspected for the things that change the plan (a subscapularis tear, a SLAP lesion, cartilage loss that would make repair pointless) and the long head of biceps is tenotomised or tenodesed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Nothing to do but note the time. A tenodesis into the bicipital groove adds an anchor and twenty minutes; a tenotomy adds nothing.

  5. 5 Subacromial bursectomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The inflamed bursa is thick, vascular and obscures everything. It is resected with a shaver and radiofrequency device until the acromion, the coracoacromial ligament and the tendon edge can be seen.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is where the fluid starts to go. Irrigation extravasates into the deltoid and the subcutaneous tissues, the shoulder swells, and over a long case in a heavy patient the swelling tracks to the neck. Watch the neck and the airway pressures as a matter of routine from this point, and warm the irrigation or the patient, because 20 L of room-temperature saline through the shoulder is a cooling system.

  6. 6 Acromioplasty and distal clavicle excision

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The undersurface of the acromion is burred flat to remove a spur or a hooked morphology and to make room for the repaired tendon; the coracoacromial ligament is released. Some surgeons excise the distal 5-10 mm of the clavicle if the acromioclavicular joint is arthritic.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Bone work with a burr: bleeding, bone dust, and the pump going up. The pain contribution is a raw cancellous surface under the deltoid that behaves like a small osteotomy for two or three days. Nothing about the step needs intervention, but it is the step most likely to produce the low-pressure request.

  7. 7 Tendon mobilisation and releases

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A retracted tendon will not reach its footprint without releasing the adhesions, the capsule beneath it and the coracohumeral ligament above it. Massive tears need extensive releases, sometimes a margin convergence (side-to-side sutures to bring the tear edges together), and some get a patch or a superior capsular reconstruction instead of a repair.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The length of this step is the length of the case. A tear that the surgeon describes as “retracted to the glenoid” means releases, more anchors, more fluid, more traction, and a pain course at the top of the range. This is the information you need for the catheter decision, and it arrives now if it did not arrive in the pre-operative note.

  8. 8 Footprint preparation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The greater tuberosity is decorticated with a burr to a bleeding bed, because tendon heals to marrow and not to cortical bone. Some surgeons add bone marrow vents (small drill holes) to bring in a healing response.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Raw bleeding bone, the second of the three bone surfaces this operation creates. It is the reason that cuff repair qualifies as bone surgery for rebound pain purposes, with the higher odds that carries.

  9. 9 Anchors, suture passage and tendon reduction under tension

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Anchors go into the tuberosity, sutures are passed through the tendon with a dedicated instrument, and the tendon is reduced onto the footprint and fixed with knots or a knotless locking mechanism, in single-row or double-row configurations. The surgeon sets the tension with the arm in a specific degree of abduction and rotation and will want it held exactly there for the tying.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The arm position is set by the surgeon or an assistant and must not drift; a shift of 10 degrees changes the tension on a repair that is about to be locked. This is not a request for neuromuscular block (a working interscalene block already provides a flail arm) but for a steady patient and a steady table. The tendon pulled onto bone under tension here is what the patient will feel for the next fortnight; the moment the knots go down, the mechanism this card is built around exists.

  10. 10 Closure, sling and the swollen shoulder

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Portals are closed with a stitch or a strip, a bulky dressing goes on, and the arm goes into a sling, usually with an abduction pillow that takes the tension off the repair.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Put the sling on before the patient lightens; an awake patient with a dense block will otherwise lift the dead arm by the wrist and load the repair. Examine the neck before extubation after a long case in beach chair: subcutaneous fluid tracking from the deltoid to the supraclavicular fossa and anterior neck is common, usually harmless, and occasionally enough to compromise the airway. A patient with a visibly swollen neck, a long case and a difficult airway earns a longer wait and a careful look rather than a reflex extubation.

  11. 11 Recovery and the first night

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The repair is protected by the sling and nothing else; the surgeon wants pendular movements only, cryotherapy and sleep.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The first paracetamol and NSAID dose goes in before the patient leaves recovery. The block lasts 12 to 24 hours with dexamethasone, longer with liposomal bupivacaine, and when it recedes the pain rises over an hour or two to its day 1 and 2 level; half of ambulatory block patients after bone surgery describe exactly this. The written night plan (what to take at bedtime, what to take when the fingers start to tingle, when the opioid is for) is the single intervention that decides whether this patient telephones at 3 am. Night pain persists for one to two weeks because the patient cannot lie on either side without loading the shoulder; sleeping propped up and the sling worn in bed are surgical advice worth repeating. Day case is the norm for small and medium tears with a single-shot block; a catheter patient can also go home if the unit runs ambulatory infusions, or stays a night if it does not.

Pain plan

The mechanism is tendon under tension plus two raw bone surfaces, loaded by movement for weeks, so this is the shoulder operation where a continuous technique has the strongest claim and the operation PROSPECT’s guideline was written for. The guideline recommends interscalene block as first choice, with single-shot and continuous both graded A and the continuous technique favoured; intravenous dexamethasone (grade B) and preferred over perineural; paracetamol with an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started before surgery; and suprascapular with or without axillary nerve block as a phrenic-sparing alternative that is inferior to interscalene. It recommends against subacromial or intra-articular infusions, stellate ganglion and cervical epidural blocks, gabapentinoids, and controlled hypotension.

Against that stands the randomised trial that added a ropivacaine 0.2% infusion to day 2 on top of dexamethasone, magnesium, paracetamol and ketorolac, in a mixed arthroplasty and cuff repair population, and found no difference in morphine at any time point or in pain scores, with four of thirty catheters falling out. The two are reconcilable: the catheter’s advantage shows when the alternative is a bare single shot, and shrinks when the single shot is prolonged with dexamethasone and backed by a regimen that is actually taken.

The defensible default, then, is a single-shot interscalene block (15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%, or a superior trunk block with the same volume when the patient cannot afford a hemidiaphragm, which matches it at 24 h), intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, paracetamol and an NSAID started before the block recedes, oral opioid for the nights of the first week, and a night plan written down for the patient. The catheter is the right call for the large or massive tear with extensive releases, the revision repair, the opioid-tolerant or chronic pain patient, the patient who has had a catastrophic rebound night before, and the unit that cannot guarantee a 2 am dose on a ward. If a catheter is placed, it needs a fixation that survives a sling and a patient who knows what a leaking dressing looks like, because the common failure is the catheter in the bed rather than the catheter in the patient.

Rebound pain is the planning target: in 972 ambulatory block patients it occurred in half, with bone surgery (OR 1.82), female sex (OR 1.52) and younger age raising the odds and intravenous dexamethasone lowering them (its absence OR 1.78). Every cuff repair involves bone. Escalation triggers: pain above 7 after the block has gone, with a sling correctly fitted and the hand warm and moving, is treated with the oral opioid that is already charted and a check that the regular analgesia was actually given; pain with a tense, hugely swollen shoulder in the first hours is extravasated irrigation fluid and settles; new severe pain days later with a hot joint is an infection until proven otherwise.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Tear size is the variable that matters. Small tears with one anchor and no releases run 60 minutes and behave like a decompression with a sore tuberosity; massive tears with margin convergence, patches or a superior capsular reconstruction run over two hours and sit at the top of this card’s range. The pre-operative MRI report, not the booking slip, tells you which you have.
  • Open and mini-open repair. Still used for some massive tears and by some surgeons: a deltoid split, more muscle pain, more bleeding, and PROSPECT’s recommendation to prefer the arthroscopic approach is on pain grounds.
  • Lateral decubitus. Changes airway access and brings sustained plexus traction; the dependent brachial plexus and peroneal nerve need padding and the eye on the pillow needs checking. Hypotension matters less, head position matters more.
  • The request for hypotension in beach chair. The reported strokes happened at cuff systolics in the 80s and 90s. Adrenaline in the irrigation and the pump are the surgeon’s levers; the vasopressor infusion is yours.
  • Fluid extravasation. Long case, high pump pressure, heavy patient, lateral position: the neck swells. Inspect before extubation; if in doubt, wait.
  • Hypothermia. Litres of room-temperature irrigation through the shoulder; forced-air warming from the start.
  • Phrenic palsy. Near-universal after interscalene block; use a superior trunk block for the patient with COPD, obesity with poor reserve, or a contralateral problem, and expect slightly less from a suprascapular plus axillary combination.
  • Chronic pain and opioid-tolerant patients. This operation is the one where their course is worst; a catheter and a pre-agreed plan with the pain service are both reasonable.
  • Day-case discharge criteria. A working block is not a reason to delay discharge, but a patient going home with a dense block needs the sling, written instructions, someone at home, and oral analgesia already started.
  • Infection. Cutibacterium acnes and anchors; presentation is late and indolent.

Key sources

This is the best-evidenced shoulder operation for analgesia, because PROSPECT built its guideline on it. The guideline’s preference for a continuous interscalene technique predates the 2024 randomised trial showing no added benefit from an infusion once dexamethasone and multimodal analgesia are in place, and the 2025 prospective comparison showing early pain equal to arthroplasty; the card’s catheter advice is the author’s reconciliation of the three, not a guideline statement. The acromioplasty meta-analysis is cited for the surgical variant, not for pain.
