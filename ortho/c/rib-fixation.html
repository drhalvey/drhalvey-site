85 Surgical stabilisation of rib fractures

Plate fixation of flail segments and multiple displaced rib fractures, with or without thoracoscopic evacuation of haemothorax and intercostal cryoablation; the ventilated patient, the anticoagulated patient and the choice of chest wall catheter

4Severe

Dominant mechanism: Bone. Fractured ribs that move with every breath and grate on the intercostal nerve beneath each one; this is the pain the operation is done to treat. Plating stops the movement, at the price of a muscle-sparing thoracic incision, periosteum cleared along several ribs, a chest drain and often thoracoscopy ports. Unfixed neighbouring fractures, the contused lung and the patient's other injuries continue, so the curve falls more slowly than after a limb fixation

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: moderate.

Block
A continuous chest wall technique that is in place before incision and stays for 3-5 days: an erector spinae plane or paravertebral catheter as the default, with a thoracic epidural for bilateral injuries when coagulation, the spine and the blood pressure allow
Catheter: routine Every patient having this operation already meets the threshold for a chest wall catheter (three or more fractures, flail or failing analgesia). The fixation shortens the need but does not remove it; the catheter is sited before surgery or by the surgeon under direct vision at the end
Alternatives: Thoracic epidural (the best-studied technique; conditionally recommended in the trauma guideline; barred by anticoagulation, spinal fracture and hypotension); Serratus anterior plane catheter for anterolateral fractures in the anticoagulated or supine ventilated patient (does not cover posterior fractures); Surgeon's intercostal blocks or intercostal cryoablation under direct vision at the end of the fixation; Ketamine and lidocaine infusions with opioid patient-controlled analgesia where no regional technique is possible
Position
Lateral decubitus with the operative side up for lateral and most posterolateral fractures; prone with the arm hanging over the table edge to swing the scapula off the posterior ribs; supine with the arm abducted for anterior fractures and costal cartilage. Bilateral fixation needs a turn

Blood loss
100-300 mL from the chest wall; any retained haemothorax evacuated at thoracoscopy is old blood already lost and shows in the suction, not in the haemodynamics. Bleeding from an intercostal artery or a lacerated lung is the exception that can be brisk
Duration
90-180 min for four to six ribs on one side; add 30-45 min for thoracoscopy and washout, and a turn for bilateral or anterior and posterior fractures
Length of stay
Set by the injuries: typically 5-10 nights for an isolated chest injury, with the first one or two in a high-dependency or intensive care bed. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV within 60 min of incision (3 g over 120 kg), repeated at 4 h

TXA 1 g IV at induction is reasonable; the patient may already have received it at injury. Withhold where a retained clotted haemothorax is the main problem only if the surgeon asks
VTE
The trauma patient is usually already on low molecular weight heparin, and its timing decides which catheter can be placed and when it can be removed: plan the dose around a paravertebral or epidural insertion as for neuraxial techniques; continue it through an erector spinae or serratus plane catheter under local policy
Antiemetic
Dexamethasone 8 mg IV and ondansetron 4 mg IV; retching with fixed ribs is survivable, retching with unfixed ones is misery
Tell the patient
The broken ribs have been held still with small plates, which takes away the grating and catching with each breath; the wound and the chest drain are sore for several days, and the bruised lung and the ribs that were not plated still ache with deep breaths and coughing for a few weeks.
The trap
The lung under the fractures is contused and sometimes leaking: positive pressure ventilation without a working drain turns a small pneumothorax into a tension pneumothorax in the lateral position, one-lung ventilation on a contused lung desaturates quickly, and the awake patient you extubate at the end must have an analgesic catheter that works, or the fixation has bought nothing

What the surgeon is doing and why

Ribs are the only bones that cannot be rested. A fractured rib moves about 20,000 times a day, and each movement shears the fracture ends against the intercostal nerve that runs in the groove beneath it. The patient responds by breathing shallowly and refusing to cough; secretions are retained, the contused lung under the fractures consolidates, and pneumonia and respiratory failure follow. When three or more adjacent ribs are each broken in two places the segment between the fractures floats free and moves inward on inspiration: a flail chest, which is a marker of a much larger injury. In the pooled studies, patients with flail patterns had more ribs broken, pulmonary contusion in three quarters, haemothorax in nearly half and five times the in-hospital mortality of those with simple multiple fractures.

Surgical stabilisation treats the mechanics. The surgeon exposes the fractured ribs through a muscle-sparing incision, reduces each one, and bridges it with a low-profile precontoured titanium plate held by locking screws. Four to six ribs are usually fixed, from the third to the tenth; the first two are protected by the shoulder girdle and the last two float. With the chest wall rigid again, the segment stops moving paradoxically, breathing becomes less painful, and a ventilated patient may come off the ventilator sooner. Many surgeons add thoracoscopy through the same field to evacuate retained haemothorax, inspect the lung and diaphragm and place the drain under vision, and some freeze or block the intercostal nerves before closing.

Who benefits is narrower than enthusiasm suggests. In the randomised trial of 207 patients with unstable chest wall injuries, fixation gave two more ventilator-free days overall, which was not significant, and the gain was confined to patients who were already ventilated at randomisation; those breathing for themselves did not benefit on that measure, although mortality was lower in the operative group. In the trial of severe non-flail patterns the gain was in pain: a numeric score of 2.9 against 4.5 at two weeks, with fewer pleural complications. Pooled data suggest the benefit is greatest when fixation happens within 72 h and smaller in patients over 60. So you will meet two patients: the ventilated flail chest in intensive care, operated on to get off the ventilator, and the awake patient with displaced fractures and failing analgesia, operated on for pain.

The pain logic is unusual, because the operation is itself an analgesic intervention. The dominant mechanism before and after is bone that moves; plating removes the movement at the plated ribs. What it adds is a thoracic incision, periosteal clearing along each rib, a drain between two ribs, and ports. What it leaves is every fracture that was not plated, the contusion, the pleura and the rest of the patient’s injuries. The net effect is a 4: a patient who needed a catheter before the operation still needs one after it, for a shorter time.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Selection, timing and the state of the patient

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon selects on fracture pattern (flail, three or more bicortically displaced fractures, chest wall deformity), on failure of non-operative care (pain, falling vital capacity, failure to wean) and on timing, aiming for the first 72 h before inflammation and callus make reduction harder.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Read the CT yourself: which ribs, where along their length (anterior, lateral, posterior decides the position and which block can reach), how much contusion, whether there is a pneumothorax or haemothorax and whether a drain is in. Look for the associated injuries that change your plan: thoracic and cervical spine fractures, head injury, clavicle and scapula, liver and spleen under the lower ribs, and blunt cardiac injury. Get an arterial blood gas on the current oxygen. Find out what anticoagulant or antiplatelet the older patient takes and when the last prophylactic heparin dose was given.

  2. 2 The analgesic catheter before theatre

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The patient was admitted for pain control days before the decision to operate; the surgeon is operating partly because that control failed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    If a working epidural, paravertebral or erector spinae catheter is already in place, it is your intra-operative analgesia: check its level and bolus it. If not, place one before induction with the patient sitting or lateral, fractured side up, or after induction in the lateral position. An erector spinae plane catheter at the mid-level of the fractures is the practical default: it covers posterior and lateral fractures, in a cohort of rib fixations it was associated with less intra-operative fentanyl and fewer analgesic doses in the first 24 h, and it lies in a compressible plane away from the neuraxis. A catheter inserted lateral to the incision line can end up in the surgical field, so agree the site with the surgeon; the alternative is for the surgeon to place a paravertebral or subpleural catheter under direct vision at the end.

  3. 3 Airway and the lung isolation decision

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    For extrathoracic plating alone the surgeon does not need the lung down. For thoracoscopy, evacuation of a clotted haemothorax or any intrathoracic plating, a collapsed lung gives the view.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Ask the question at the briefing, because the answer sets the tube. No thoracoscopy: a single-lumen tube, lung-protective ventilation, and brief apnoea on request while screws are drilled over the pleura. Thoracoscopy planned: a double-lumen tube in the patient who arrives awake, or a bronchial blocker through the existing tube or tracheostomy in the ventilated intensive care patient, which avoids two tube changes in an oedematous airway. One-lung ventilation is tolerated badly here: the dependent lung may itself be contused, and shunt through the operative lung is added to it. Have a plan for hypoxaemia that the surgeon has agreed to (intermittent two-lung ventilation, low-flow oxygen or continuous positive pressure to the operative lung), and accept thoracoscopy done in short periods of apnoea when isolation fails. Before positive pressure starts, confirm that any pneumothorax is drained and the drain is unclamped and bubbling or swinging. A cervical spine that has not been cleared needs in-line stabilisation and makes a double-lumen tube harder still.

  4. 4 Positioning

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The fractures decide it. Lateral decubitus reaches most. Posterior fractures under the scapula are reached prone, with the arm hanging forward off the table so the scapula rotates away. Anterior fractures and costal cartilage are done supine.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Turning a patient with a flail chest, an unstable spine or a pelvic fracture is a team manoeuvre with spinal precautions if the spine is not cleared. In the lateral position the good lung is dependent, which is the favourable arrangement for gas exchange, but the drain on the operative side must stay patent and visible. Secure the tube and any chest drain during the turn; re-check tube position afterwards. Pad the dependent side, which may also have fractures. An arterial line is standard; central access only if the other injuries require it.

  5. 5 Incision and muscle-sparing exposure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The incision is centred over the fractures, often along the border of latissimus dorsi. Latissimus is lifted and retracted rather than cut; serratus anterior is split along its fibres over each rib. For high posterior ribs the surgeon works under the scapula in the triangle of auscultation.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Moderate stimulation. The long thoracic and thoracodorsal nerves are in the field and the surgeon may use a stimulator to find them, so avoid deep paralysis at this point if asked. A serratus plane block or catheter placed before surgery lies in exactly this layer and will be opened; that is one reason to prefer a paravertebral site of action.

  6. 6 Reduction and plating

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Each fracture is cleared of interposed muscle and periosteum for a few centimetres, reduced with clamps, and spanned with a plate contoured to the rib, fixed with three locking screws each side. The surgeon measures rib thickness so that screws engage both cortices without projecting into the pleura. Each rib takes 10-20 min.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Drilling a rib with lung moving beneath it is the surgeon’s hazard; hold ventilation for the few seconds of each drill pass if asked, and keep tidal volumes low throughout. A sudden increase in airway pressure, a fall in saturation or an air leak into the wound after a drill or a clamp is a lung laceration: tell the surgeon. Bleeding from an intercostal vessel is usually controlled directly; it is the one source in this operation that can lose several hundred millilitres quickly. As the chest wall becomes rigid, compliance and tidal volumes on pressure-controlled ventilation change, so re-set the ventilator.

  7. 7 Thoracoscopy, washout and intercostal nerve treatment

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Through a port or a gap between ribs the surgeon removes clot and fluid, looks for a diaphragmatic tear or a lung laceration, and positions the drain. Some then apply a cryoprobe to each intercostal nerve from two levels above to two below the fractures, or inject local anaesthetic at each nerve under vision.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the period of lung isolation, usually 20-40 min. The evacuated haemothorax can be a litre of old blood and looks alarming in the canister without any haemodynamic change. Cryoablation gives weeks to months of chest wall numbness; pooled retrospective data associate it with lower opioid use and shorter stays, a later analysis questions the benefit in patients who are not ventilated, and intercostal neuralgia is the recognised harm. It does not replace the catheter in the first days, because it does not cover the pleura, the drain or fractures outside the treated levels. If the surgeon injects intercostal local anaesthetic, add that dose to your running total: absorption from the intercostal space is the fastest of any block site.

  8. 8 Drain, re-expansion and closure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A chest drain is placed or re-sited, the lung is re-inflated under vision, and the muscle layers are closed over the plates without tension.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Recruit the operative lung gently with a sustained manual inflation while the surgeon watches for an air leak, then return to protective settings. Confirm the drain is connected to an underwater seal and swinging before the patient is turned. If the surgeon is placing the analgesic catheter under vision, this is when it happens; have the infusion ready.

  9. 9 Extubate or not

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon’s aim for the awake patient is to breathe and cough comfortably that evening; for the ventilated patient it is an earlier wean.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The patient who came from the ward breathing spontaneously is extubated in theatre, sitting up, with the catheter bolused and working, and goes to a monitored bed. The patient who came from intensive care ventilated returns ventilated; fixation shortens ventilation by days, not hours, and a contused lung sets its own timetable. Between the two is the older patient with borderline gas exchange after a period of one-lung ventilation: a trial of extubation onto high-flow nasal oxygen or non-invasive support is reasonable, with a low threshold to wait.

  10. 10 The first days

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Physiotherapy, incentive spirometry and mobilisation are the purpose of the operation; the drain stays until output and air leak settle.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The trajectory on this card is 4 on the first evening with the catheter running, 5 on days 1 and 2 with the drain in and physiotherapy starting, 4 on day 3 and 3 at a week, in line with the two-week score of about 3 in the non-flail trial. The targets are functional: a cough that clears secretions, a vital capacity that rises day by day. Drain removal usually drops the pain by a point or two. Plan the catheter removal against the heparin doses.

Pain plan

Start from the innervation and the constraints. Each rib and its pleura are supplied by one intercostal nerve; fractures are usually at four to eight levels on one side, sometimes both. A technique has to cover those levels continuously for several days in a patient who is often anticoagulated, may have a spinal fracture, and may be hypovolaemic.

Thoracic epidural analgesia is the best-studied option and the only regional technique the trauma guideline felt able to recommend, conditionally and on very low-quality evidence; the systematic reviews of randomised trials found no firm benefit on mortality or length of stay and a possible reduction in ventilation time. It covers both sides. It is barred or made awkward by prophylactic heparin timing, therapeutic anticoagulation, vertebral fractures, sedation that prevents neurological monitoring, and hypotension. A paravertebral catheter gives unilateral cover of similar quality with less hypotension and is treated like a neuraxial technique for anticoagulation purposes. The erector spinae plane catheter is the newer default: its site of action is posterior enough to cover posterior and lateral fractures, it can be placed with the patient sitting, lateral or prone, and it is superficial to the paraspinal muscle; in a randomised comparison in rib fracture patients it gave better early analgesia than a serratus anterior plane block. The serratus anterior plane catheter can be placed in a supine, ventilated patient and covers the lateral cutaneous branches, which means anterolateral fractures only.

The defensible default, which is the author’s judgement applied to that evidence, is: for unilateral fractures, an erector spinae plane or paravertebral catheter running ropivacaine 0.2% at 8-12 mL/h or as programmed intermittent boluses, in place before incision; for bilateral flail in a patient who meets the neuraxial rules, a thoracic epidural; for the anticoagulated or spine-injured patient, an erector spinae or serratus plane catheter by the location of the fractures; and for the patient in whom nothing regional is possible, a ketamine infusion, an intravenous lidocaine infusion where local policy supports it, and opioid patient-controlled analgesia. Around every option: regular paracetamol, an NSAID once renal function, bleeding risk and the surgeon allow, and a short-acting opioid. Gabapentinoids add sedation in older patients for doubtful gain.

Bilateral plane catheters double the local anaesthetic dose; calculate the 24 h total before starting two infusions, particularly in a small older patient. Rebound is moderate because the technique is continuous; the equivalent event is a displaced catheter, recognised by a patient who stops coughing. Escalation triggers: rising pain with rising oxygen requirement is a blocked drain, a re-accumulating haemothorax or pneumonia before it is an analgesic failure; sharp, electric pain in a band is intercostal nerve irritation from a plate or after cryoablation; pain at the tip of the shoulder is the drain or the diaphragm.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The ventilated flail chest. The group with trial evidence of benefit. They arrive with a tube or tracheostomy, sedation and often a spinal fracture; use a bronchial blocker if isolation is needed, and expect to return them ventilated.
  • The awake patient operated on for pain. The benefit in the trials was in pain scores and pleural complications, not ventilation. An analgesic catheter that works at extubation is the difference between a good and a pointless operation.
  • The older patient on an anticoagulant. Pooled data show less benefit from fixation over 60. Reversal follows the bleeding risk of the whole injury; choose a compressible-site catheter.
  • Undrained pneumothorax. Small occult pneumothoraces on CT are common. Positive pressure ventilation, especially on one lung in the lateral position, can convert one to tension; know whether a drain is in before induction.
  • Pulmonary contusion. Peaks at 24-72 h, which is exactly when the surgeon wants to operate. A patient on high inspired oxygen before surgery will not tolerate lung isolation.
  • Posterior fractures and the prone position. Access under the scapula, with the airway away from you and all the prone hazards on a patient with fractures on the dependent chest wall.
  • Associated clavicle, scapula and sternal fractures. Sometimes fixed in the same sitting, adding time and a second field; see the scapula card.
  • Polytrauma. The chest competes with the limbs and the abdomen for theatre time and for the patient’s reserve; sequencing is on the polytrauma card.
  • Chronic chest wall pain and plate irritation. A minority have plates removed later for pain or prominence under the scapula.

Key sources

The surgical evidence is one substantial randomised trial with a null primary outcome and a positive ventilated subgroup, one prospective controlled trial in non-flail patterns, and meta-analyses dominated by observational data. The analgesic evidence for rib fractures is weak for every technique, by the guideline’s own grading; the comparisons between plane blocks are recent and small, and the evidence specific to analgesia during and after fixation is a single cohort. There is no trial of lung isolation strategy for this operation: the airway advice is the author’s practice. The pain score of 4 and the trajectory are calibrated to the two-week pain score in the non-flail trial and to the polytrauma card.
