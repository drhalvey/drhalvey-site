64 Ankle, subtalar and triple fusion

Open and arthroscopic ankle arthrodesis, subtalar fusion, double and triple arthrodesis, tibiotalocalcaneal fusion with a hindfoot nail, and midfoot fusion, with bone graft

4Severe

Dominant mechanism: Bone. Every joint surface in the fusion is stripped of cartilage and subchondral plate to bleeding cancellous bone, then the two raw faces are forced together under screw compression; a triple fusion does this to three joints through two incisions and adds a graft donor site. Pure bone mechanism at its largest scale in the foot, in a dependent limb that swells. The catheter covers days 0-2 and the peak arrives when it is removed

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Block
Popliteal sciatic perineural catheter (ropivacaine 0.2% for 48-72 h) plus a single-shot saphenous block in the adductor canal, with intravenous dexamethasone, under general or spinal anaesthesia
Catheter: routine Foot arthrodesis ranked seventh of 179 operations for day 1 pain without blocks and the pain outlasts any single shot; the exception is the isolated arthroscopic ankle or single-joint fusion in a low-risk patient, where a single shot with dexamethasone and an overnight stay is acceptable
Alternatives: Single-shot popliteal sciatic plus saphenous blocks with intravenous dexamethasone and a planned inpatient oral regimen; Spinal anaesthesia with the blocks for analgesia, which also covers the thigh cuff for up to 2 h; General anaesthesia with opioid PCA where regional techniques are declined or contraindicated
Position
Supine with a large bump under the ipsilateral buttock to bring the lateral hindfoot up for subtalar and triple fusion, removed for the medial incision; supine with the foot at the table end for ankle fusion and the hindfoot nail; lateral for a transfibular ankle or isolated subtalar fusion in some hands; prone for posterior arthroscopic subtalar fusion
Tourniquet
Yes. Thigh cuff set from the limb occlusion pressure; an isolated fusion fits inside 90-120 min, a triple or a nail often does not, and the plan for a deflation interval or for finishing without the cuff is agreed before the start
Cement
No
Blood loss
100-300 mL, most of it after tourniquet release from the raw bone surfaces; more with iliac crest graft or a reamed hindfoot nail
Duration
Arthroscopic or open ankle fusion 90-120 min; subtalar fusion 60-90 min; triple fusion 150-210 min; hindfoot nail 120-180 min
Length of stay
1-3 nights; selected single-joint fusions as a day case with an ambulatory catheter. Day case: selected
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV, complete before the cuff is inflated (3 g over 120 kg); repeat at 4 h in a long triple fusion
Tranexamic acid
Optional; TXA 1 g IV before inflation is reasonable for a triple fusion, a nail or an iliac crest graft
VTE
Individual risk assessment, with a low threshold for low molecular weight heparin: 6-12 weeks non-weight-bearing in a cast, and thromboembolic events in 4.9% of the fusion arm of the TARVA trial
Antiemetic
Dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction and ondansetron 4 mg IV
Tell the patient
A fine tube behind the knee keeps the foot numb for two to three days; the day it comes out is the sore one, the tablets are taken by the clock for the first week, and the leg stays up and off the ground for at least six weeks because the bones have to grow together.
The trap
This is among the most painful operations in surgery when it is done without regional analgesia, and the catheter only moves that pain to the day of removal: the oral regimen has to be running before the infusion stops, and the NSAID decision, which the surgeon may have made on non-union grounds, has to be known before the plan is written because it removes the most effective oral drug

What the surgeon is doing and why

A fusion trades a painful, deformed or unstable joint for no joint. The surgeon removes the cartilage from both sides, puts the two raw bone faces together in the position the foot will hold for life, and compresses them until they heal as one bone. It is the standard answer to end-stage arthritis of the ankle (usually post-traumatic), the subtalar joint (usually after a calcaneal fracture), and the triple joint complex (subtalar, talonavicular and calcaneocuboid) in a rigid flatfoot, a cavovarus foot, rheumatoid disease or a neuromuscular deformity.

Whatever the joint, the operation has four steps, and all four are bone:

  1. Expose the joint and open it wide, usually with a lamina spreader.
  2. Remove every trace of cartilage and then break through the hard subchondral plate with curettes, osteotomes, a burr and multiple drill holes, until both faces are bleeding cancellous bone. This is the step that determines union, and the step that determines pain.
  3. Fill gaps with bone graft: local shavings, cancellous graft from the calcaneus, proximal tibia or iliac crest, or allograft and substitutes.
  4. Set the position (ankle at neutral, heel in about 5 degrees of valgus, foot slightly externally rotated and plantigrade) and compress with large cannulated screws, staples or plates.

The variants differ in how many joints and how they are reached. An ankle fusion is done arthroscopically through two anterior portals with a burr and percutaneous screws, or open through an anterior or transfibular approach. A subtalar fusion is done through a short incision over the sinus tarsi with one or two screws driven from the heel into the talus. A triple fusion needs a lateral incision for the subtalar and calcaneocuboid joints and a medial one for the talonavicular joint; many surgeons now do a double fusion of the subtalar and talonavicular joints through the medial incision alone. A tibiotalocalcaneal fusion sacrifices both ankle and subtalar joints and holds them with a retrograde nail inserted through the sole of the heel, for combined arthritis, failed ankle replacement, severe deformity or a Charcot hindfoot.

The pain logic is the simplest in the book and the most severe in the foot. Each fused joint is two freshly decorticated bone surfaces compressed together; a triple fusion is six. There is no tendon under tension and nothing is loaded afterwards, so the pain is intense for three to four days and then falls steadily, with a week or two of throbbing whenever the leg hangs. In the 179-procedure cohort, in which regional techniques were rarely used, foot arthrodesis ranked seventh for worst pain on day 1, ahead of most abdominal and thoracic surgery. That ranking is what the operation does without a block. With a popliteal catheter and a saphenous block in place it scores 4, and the practical problem moves from the first night to the day the catheter is removed.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Reading the booking: which joints, which approach, which graft

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    “Fusion” covers a 60-min subtalar fusion and a 3-h triple with iliac crest graft. The approach decides the position, the number of joints decides the time, and the graft site decides whether there is a second painful wound.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Establish the joints, open or arthroscopic, and the graft source the day before. An iliac crest harvest lies outside every leg block and often hurts more than the foot; it needs the surgeon’s infiltration into the periosteum and a plan for it in the oral regimen. Proximal tibial graft is saphenous and lateral sural cutaneous territory and is covered only partly by an adductor canal block. Ask now what the surgeon’s rule on NSAIDs is. The patient population carries its own information: smokers and diabetics (non-union and wound risk), rheumatoid patients (airway, steroids), and hereditary neuropathies with cavovarus feet, in whom a block is not contraindicated but the baseline deficit must be recorded.

  2. 2 Anaesthetic, catheter and saphenous block

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs a still, bloodless hindfoot for up to 3 h and a leg that can be lifted and inspected for alignment.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A popliteal sciatic catheter placed awake or lightly sedated before induction, bolused with 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%, with the tip a few centimetres proximal to the bifurcation and the dressing on the lateral thigh clear of the cuff. The saphenous block (10 mL in the adductor canal) is required for anything with a medial incision or a medial screw: the talonavicular joint, the medial ankle gutter, the medial malleolus and the skin over them are saphenous. General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway suits most cases; a spinal covers a thigh cuff well for an isolated fusion but its duration and the tourniquet pain that breaks through at 90 min make it a poor fit for a triple. Dexamethasone 8 mg IV and cefazolin 2 g IV before inflation.

  3. 3 Position and the tourniquet clock

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A large bump under the buttock rolls the leg inward so the sinus tarsi and lateral hindfoot face the ceiling; it is pulled out when the surgeon moves to the medial side. Ankle fusion and nail cases have the foot at the end of the table for lateral fluoroscopy and for the plantar entry point.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Removing the bump mid-case shifts the pelvis and the catheter dressing; look at both. Measure the limb occlusion pressure and set the cuff from it (the tourniquet). A triple fusion or a nail will reach 120 min with the fixation unfinished. The options, agreed beforehand, are a 10-15 min deflation interval followed by a second inflation of no more than 60 min, or completing the joint preparation under tourniquet and the fixation without it. Under general anaesthesia expect tourniquet hypertension from about 60 min; in a small randomised trial, moving the compression from a thigh cuff to a calf cuff at 60 min prevented it, which is an option for forefoot and midfoot fusions where the calf is out of the field.

  4. 4 Approach and joint exposure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Sinus tarsi incision from the tip of the fibula towards the fourth metatarsal base for the subtalar joint, with the peroneal tendons and sural nerve below it; a medial incision between tibialis anterior and posterior for the talonavicular joint; anterior or transfibular for the ankle. The joints are levered open with a spreader.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Little happens physiologically. The sural and superficial peroneal nerves are in the lateral field and the saphenous vein and nerve in the medial one, which is relevant to post-operative numbness and to the record you made in step 1.

  5. 5 Joint preparation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Cartilage is removed with osteotomes and curettes, the subchondral plate is feathered or burred, and both faces are drilled with multiple small holes until punctate bleeding is seen. In a triple fusion this is repeated for each joint, and wedges are removed where deformity has to be corrected.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the mechanism of the card being created, one joint at a time: each prepared joint adds to the first week’s pain in proportion to its area. The surgeon may ask for the tourniquet to be released briefly to confirm that the bone bleeds. Irrigation is cold and prolonged; keep forced-air warming on from the start, because a 3-h case with an exposed limb and a cuff release at the end produces a cold patient.

  6. 6 Bone graft

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Gaps left by deformity correction or bone loss are packed with cancellous graft. Local bone and calcaneal or proximal tibial graft are taken through small windows in the same limb; iliac crest is taken through a separate incision when structural or large-volume graft is needed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    An iliac crest harvest is the one part of this operation that the leg blocks do not touch. Ask for 20 mL of long-acting local anaesthetic into the periosteum and muscle layers at closure, include it in the dose budget shared with the blocks (the block menu), and tell the patient that the hip may be the sorer site when they first stand.

  7. 7 Setting the position

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The fusion position is permanent. The surgeon holds the foot plantigrade, checks heel valgus and rotation against the tibial crest and the opposite leg, and confirms on fluoroscopy before any definitive screw is placed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The surgeon needs both legs visible and the table flat and level; remove tilt and lower the drapes if asked. The foot is held by an assistant against the surgeon’s guide wires, and any movement of the patient changes a position that cannot be revised later. Maintain depth; neuromuscular block is not needed in a limb with a dense sciatic block.

  8. 8 Compression and fixation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Guide wires are placed across each joint, measured, and large partially threaded cannulated screws are driven over them so the threads cross the joint and the head compresses the faces together. Plates or staples supplement screws where bone is soft. Each screw is checked in two planes.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Fluoroscopy time is substantial. Compression of raw cancellous faces is the last element of the pain mechanism. The tourniquet clock is usually at its limit here; say the time aloud.

  9. 9 The hindfoot nail variant

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    For a tibiotalocalcaneal fusion the joints are prepared through a lateral transfibular approach (the fibula is often resected and used as graft), a guide wire is passed from the plantar heel through calcaneus and talus into the tibial canal, the path is reamed, and a nail is inserted and locked with screws in the calcaneus, talus and tibia.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The patients are the frailest on this card: Charcot hindfeet with renal and cardiac disease, failed replacements, elderly unreconstructable fractures. Reaming a short path through tarsal bones and distal tibia produces little embolic load compared with a femoral nail, and no pulmonary event is expected. Blood loss is greater after cuff release. In a neuropathic foot the block is for the anaesthetic and haemodynamic stability more than for pain, and its duration will be prolonged; see diabetic foot surgery.

  10. 10 The arthroscopic variant

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Ankle fusion through two anterior portals with non-invasive distraction, a burr and curettes for the surfaces, and percutaneous screws. Used for arthritis without major deformity. Pooled comparative studies report less blood loss, a shorter tourniquet time and about a day and a half less in hospital than open fusion, with at least as good a fusion rate.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The thigh holder and strap bring the same peroneal nerve padding concerns as in ankle arthroscopy. The bone work is the same as the open operation, so the pain mechanism is unchanged; what is spared is the incision and the soft-tissue stripping. This is the fusion for which a single-shot block with dexamethasone and an overnight stay is a defensible alternative to a catheter.

  11. 11 Release, closure and backslab

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The cuff is released, bleeding from the bone surfaces is accepted and the skin closed in layers, sometimes over a drain. A bulky padded backslab holds the foot at neutral.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Reperfusion after 2 h or more gives a sharper fall in pressure and a larger carbon dioxide and potassium load than the short foot cases; stay with the patient for the ten minutes after release and increase ventilation. Most of the measured blood loss appears now and into the dressing. The slab goes on before emergence and must be padded at the heel and the fibular head, because the patient will not feel either for three days.

  12. 12 The first three days and the day the catheter comes out

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants strict elevation, an untouched cast and no weight through the leg for 6-12 weeks until union.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Run ropivacaine 0.2% at 5-8 mL/h with or without a patient bolus. Start regular paracetamol on day 0 and, according to the surgeon’s rule, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor for a limited course. Give a modified-release or regular oral opioid from the evening before the catheter is removed, remove it in the morning, and review the patient that afternoon: the hours after removal are the peak of this operation with the plan in place. Leakage (about one in seven catheters in the pooled trials) is managed by reinforcing the dressing, a catheter that has fallen out by converting to the oral plan early. Falls are a real risk in a patient on crutches with an insensate leg in a heavy cast.

Pain plan

The mechanism is bone, in quantity, in a limb whose nerve supply is reachable from the popliteal fossa and the adductor canal. The pain is severe for longer than a single-shot block lasts even with dexamethasone, and the patient is in hospital or closely followed regardless, so this is the foot operation with the clearest claim to a catheter. The block menu lists it as such.

The trial evidence supports the direction and is modest about the size. Pooled randomised trials in foot and ankle surgery show lower pain at 24 and 48 h with a continuous popliteal block than with a single injection, with no infections or nerve injuries and leakage in 13.9%. The original home-infusion trial showed a drop in day 1 rest pain from 4 to 0 and in opioid tablets from 8 to 0. The one trial restricted to major ankle and hindfoot surgery found significantly lower pain and opioid use with a 72-h infusion, but with mean scores between 1.1 and 3.6 in both arms, and its authors doubted the cost was justified. None of these compared a catheter with a dexamethasone-extended single shot. The recommendation for a routine catheter is therefore the author’s judgement from the mechanism and the cohort ranking, not a trial result, and a single shot with intravenous dexamethasone and a properly run inpatient oral regimen is a defensible second choice for single-joint and arthroscopic fusions.

The oral regimen has one decision in it that belongs to the surgeon. NSAIDs are the best oral drug for bone pain and are restricted by many fusion surgeons because of non-union, which occurred radiographically in 12.1% of ankle fusions in the TARVA trial. The clinical evidence for harm is weak: a retrospective series of 161 foot and ankle fusions found no more NSAID use among non-unions but a longer time to union, and a current review advises limiting NSAIDs to under 2 weeks and avoiding them in high-risk patients (smokers, diabetics, revisions). Surgeons vary between no NSAID, a 3-5 day course, and no restriction. Where NSAIDs are withheld, the plan needs regular paracetamol, a regular oral opioid for the first week with a laxative, and the catheter for as long as the service can run it. Gabapentinoids have no specific evidence here.

Escalation triggers: severe pain with a running catheter is a displaced catheter (test with a bolus and look at the dressing), saphenous territory pain on the medial side, the iliac crest, or a tight cast, in that order of likelihood; split the cast before increasing opioids if the toes are swollen or dusky. Rebound after removal responds to the opioid already charted. Calf or thigh pain in the weeks afterwards is a thrombosis until excluded.

Pitfalls and variants

  • NSAIDs withheld without telling you. Find out before the plan is written.
  • The tourniquet at 120 min with a joint still open. Agree the deflation plan at the start; do not negotiate it at 115 min.
  • Iliac crest graft. Outside the block and frequently the patient’s main complaint on day 1.
  • Smokers. Higher non-union and wound complication rates; some surgeons will not list them. Nicotine replacement is not neutral for bone either, and the surgeon’s rule applies.
  • Charcot and neuropathic hindfeet. Little pain, long block duration, major comorbidity, and a long operation; the anaesthetic risk is cardiac and renal.
  • Rheumatoid patients. Cervical spine, temporomandibular joints, steroid cover and thin skin under a tourniquet.
  • Hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy with a cavovarus foot. Triple fusion with tendon transfers and osteotomies; record the baseline deficit, use the lowest effective local anaesthetic concentration, and expect a block that lasts longer than planned.
  • Midfoot fusion. Tarsometatarsal and naviculocuneiform fusions through dorsal incisions are the same mechanism on a smaller scale; a single-shot popliteal and saphenous block usually suffices, and a calf cuff is possible.
  • Day-case fusion. Possible for single joints where an ambulatory catheter pathway exists; otherwise the first two nights belong in hospital.
  • Venous thromboembolism. A long period of cast immobilisation; the rate in the trial’s fusion arm was 4.9%.

Key sources

The pain ranking is from the Gerbershagen cohort, which grouped foot fusions together and seldom used regional analgesia; it describes the untreated operation. The catheter evidence is from small trials in mixed foot and ankle populations before routine dexamethasone, and the only hindfoot-specific trial is equivocal about clinical importance. Fusion complication rates are from the fusion arm of a randomised trial against ankle replacement. The arthroscopic comparison is a meta-analysis of non-randomised studies. The NSAID question has no randomised evidence in foot fusion; the cited series is retrospective and the review is expert opinion, which is why the card describes surgeon variation and does not pick a side.
