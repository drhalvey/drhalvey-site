76 Hand infection: flexor sheath washout, abscess and bite injuries

Pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis, deep space and web space abscess, septic arthritis of the finger joints, fight bites and animal bites, felon and paronychia

2Mild to moderate

Dominant mechanism: Capsule. The severe pain is pre-operative: pus under pressure inside a closed synovial sheath, joint or fascial space, worst on passive stretch. Surgery decompresses it, so pain falls after the operation. What is left is an inflamed synovial lining, wounds deliberately left open, and movement started early; plus a second and sometimes third washout 24-48 h apart

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: moderate.

Block
Axillary or infraclavicular brachial plexus block as the sole anaesthetic, placed through clean skin proximal to the infection, in the stable patient; general anaesthesia with rapid sequence induction in the septic, uncooperative or spreading case
Catheter: rarely A single shot per washout is usual. An infraclavicular catheter is defensible when two or more returns to theatre are planned and the insertion site is well clear of cellulitis and nodes; an indwelling catheter in a bacteraemic patient is a foreign body, so it comes out at the first sign of site inflammation and is not placed in the febrile, rigoring patient
Alternatives: Supraclavicular block when the axilla has tender nodes or lymphangitis reaches the upper arm; General anaesthesia with rapid sequence induction for the septic or unfasted patient who cannot have a block; Wrist or digital block, or WALANT, for a localised distal infection; local anaesthetic is infiltrated proximal to, never into, inflamed tissue; Sedation plus local infiltration is the weakest option: acidic, hyperaemic tissue blocks poorly and sedation in an unfasted patient has the risks of general anaesthesia without the airway
Position
Supine with the arm on a hand table; the limb is elevated before the tourniquet is inflated and not wrapped
Tourniquet
Yes. Upper arm tourniquet at limb occlusion pressure plus the tiered margin (about 200 mmHg in a normotensive arm where it cannot be measured) after elevation for 2-3 min; no Esmarch or compressive exsanguination, which would milk pus and bacteria proximally
Cement
No
Blood loss
Negligible under tourniquet; open wounds ooze afterwards
Duration
15-30 min for an abscess, felon or single joint washout; 30-60 min for a flexor sheath washout; longer for a deep space infection with several incisions or necrotic tendon
Length of stay
1-3 nights on intravenous antibiotics with a planned second look; day case for a drained superficial abscess or a bite washed out early in a reliable patient. Day case: selected
Antibiotic
Therapeutic, not prophylactic, and usually already started. If it has not been and the patient is stable, surgeons often ask for it to be held until pus and tissue are sent; in sepsis it is given at once. Bites need cover for oral flora and anaerobes as well as staphylococci and streptococci, and the agent follows local guidance
Tranexamic acid
Not needed
VTE
None for a short stay; chemoprophylaxis for the septic inpatient
Antiemetic
Ondansetron 4 mg IV; dexamethasone 4-8 mg IV is reasonable for a block in a patient on effective antibiotics and is omitted by the author in uncontrolled diabetes or established sepsis
Tell the patient
The throbbing you have now comes from pressure, and it eases once the infection is washed out; the arm will be numb for some hours, the wound is left partly open on purpose, and you are likely to come back to theatre in a day or two for a second wash.
The trap
It is booked as a quick washout at the end of the list, and the patient has diabetes, has not eaten properly for two days, is tachycardic and has red streaks to the elbow: read the observations and the glucose before choosing between a block in the anaesthetic room and a rapid sequence induction

What the surgeon is doing and why

The hand is built from closed compartments: synovial sheaths around the flexor tendons, small joints with tight capsules, the pulp of the fingertip divided by septa, and fascial spaces in the palm. Bacteria put into any of them by a thorn, a tooth or a needle multiply in a space that cannot expand. Pressure rises, the blood supply to the contents is squeezed off (a tendon inside its sheath lives on synovial fluid and a few fine vessels), and within a day or two the tendon or cartilage begins to die. Antibiotics do not penetrate a tense avascular collection. The surgical principle is therefore the oldest one: open it, wash it out, leave it open, move it early, look again.

The presentations you meet:

  • Pyogenic flexor tenosynovitis. Infection inside the flexor sheath, recognised by Kanavel’s four signs: a finger held flexed, swollen along its whole length, tender along the sheath, and exquisitely painful on passive extension. The surgeon opens the sheath proximally in the palm at the A1 pulley and distally at the distal interphalangeal crease and irrigates through a catheter from one opening to the other; a sheath full of thick pus or dead tendon is laid open through a longer incision. A systematic review could not show that open washout, closed catheter irrigation or antibiotics alone is better, because the studies are poor; in practice established infection is washed out.
  • Septic arthritis of a finger joint and the fight bite. A knuckle struck against a tooth: the tooth goes through skin, extensor tendon and capsule into the metacarpophalangeal joint with the fist clenched, and when the hand opens the layers slide and seal the inoculum inside. The wound looks trivial. The surgeon extends it, opens the joint, washes it and leaves it open.
  • Abscesses. A felon (the pulp), a paronychia (the nail fold), a web space collar-stud abscess with a dorsal and a volar pocket, and deep palmar space infections of the thenar and midpalmar spaces. Each has its incision; all are drained and left open.
  • Animal bites. Cat teeth inject deeply into joints and sheaths through a pinhole; dog bites crush and tear.

The patient matters as much as the organism. In a 341-patient series the independent predictors of a bad result were delay, diabetes and the stage at operation, and in another series about a quarter went back to theatre and one in 15-20 lost the finger. Diabetes, renal failure, immunosuppression and intravenous drug use are over-represented. The urgency is real: this is a same-day operation, and it should not wait for the end of an elective list or for a particular surgeon.

The pain logic is the reverse of most cards. The pre-operative pain is severe (pressure in a closed space) and the operation relieves it. Afterwards the mechanism is an inflamed synovial lining, open wounds and early movement: sharp with motion and dressing changes, settling over days. That is the capsule pattern, a 2. The pain plan is aimed more at the repeat procedures and the dressing changes than at the first night.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Assess for sepsis and the patient behind the finger

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon has made a diagnosis in one digit. Whether the infection is contained there decides the anaesthetic.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Temperature, heart rate, pressure, respiratory rate, and how far up the arm the redness and tender nodes go. Blood glucose and ketones in anyone with diabetes: an infected hand is a common trigger for ketoacidosis, and the patient may have stopped eating and kept or stopped taking insulin. Creatinine and potassium in the dialysis patient, and which arm has the fistula. In the intravenous drug user: venous access, tolerance, blood-borne virus precautions and the possibility that this is one of several seeded sites. A patient with rigors, hypotension or a lactate above 2 mmol/L is a sepsis case first: fluids, cultures, antibiotics now, and theatre without delay; rapidly spreading pain and swelling with systemic toxicity raises necrotising infection, which is a different operation.

  2. 2 Fasting, timing and the antibiotic

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants source control within hours and often wants cultures before antibiotics.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Do not let fasting status push a threatened tendon to the next day. A plexus block as the sole anaesthetic makes fasting less critical, though not irrelevant; a septic patient with pain and opioids on board has delayed gastric emptying whatever the clock says. On antibiotics: if they are already running, continue. If they are not and the patient is well, holding them until the first specimen is taken (10 min into the case) is reasonable; if there are signs of sepsis they are given before induction and the cultures take their chance.

  3. 3 The choice of anaesthetic

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The operation needs a still hand, a tourniquet for 20-40 min and a surgeon who can extend the incision proximally if the pus tracks.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The defensible default in a stable patient is an axillary or infraclavicular block with a short- or intermediate-acting agent (lidocaine 1.5% or mepivacaine 1.5% with adrenaline, 20-30 mL, which is 300-450 mg; the ceiling is 7 mg/kg of lean body weight with adrenaline, so 30 mL is too much for anyone under 64 kg, and the dose is cut further in the frail, septic or renally impaired patient this operation tends to bring): it avoids airway instrumentation in an unfasted patient, gives tourniquet tolerance, and wears off in time for the surgeon to assess the hand that evening. The needle goes through clean skin with no cellulitis, no lymphangitic streak across it and no tender mass of nodes beneath it; when the axilla is involved, move to the supraclavicular fossa, and when redness has reached the shoulder, use general anaesthesia. There is no trial evidence that a plexus block through clean skin proximal to an infection spreads it, and none that it is safe; this is consensus practice and the author’s. Local infiltration into inflamed tissue fails (the tissue is acidic and hyperaemic, so less drug reaches the nerve in its active form and more is carried away) and is not attempted. General anaesthesia with rapid sequence induction is the choice for sepsis, a spreading infection, a child, or a patient who cannot cooperate.

  4. 4 Tourniquet without exsanguination

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A bloodless field is needed to see the sheath and the neurovascular bundles, but squeezing the limb would drive infected material proximally.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Elevate the arm for two to three minutes and then inflate. Cuff time is short. Sickle cell disease and a fistula in that arm are the two reasons to operate without one.

  5. 5 Incision, specimens and decompression

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The space is opened, fluid and tissue are sent for microscopy and culture, and the pressure is released.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    If the antibiotic was held, give it now and say that you have. Bacteraemia with manipulation of an abscess is expected: a rise in temperature or a rigor in recovery is common after drainage and does not by itself mean deterioration, but hypotension does.

  6. 6 Irrigation of the sheath, joint or space

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A fine catheter is passed into the sheath at the palm and saline flushed until it runs clear from the distal opening; a joint is opened and washed; an abscess cavity is curetted and its loculi broken down. Some surgeons leave the catheter for continued irrigation on the ward.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A litre or more of saline under modest pressure into a finger: the digit swells and blanches, and the surgeon watches perfusion. Nothing systemic.

  7. 7 Assessment of what is dead

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A grey, frayed tendon or soft, eroded cartilage will not survive and harbours infection. Limited debridement now, with a decision about tendon excision or amputation usually deferred to the second look.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Ask what was found: it tells you whether the patient is coming back once or several times, and that drives the catheter decision and the fasting plan for tomorrow.

  8. 8 Wounds left open, dressing and splint

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Closure would re-create the closed space. Wounds are left open or loosely tacked over a wick, the hand is dressed in a bulky bandage in the safe position and elevated.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Tourniquet down before the dressing so that bleeding and finger perfusion are seen. The surgeon will not infiltrate local anaesthetic; if the block is patchy or general anaesthesia was used, a wrist block through clean skin at the end gives several hours of analgesia.

  9. 9 Recovery and the first 24 hours

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    High elevation, intravenous antibiotics, and hand therapy with active movement from day 1 so that the tendon does not adhere.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Pain should be lower than before surgery. Regular paracetamol, an NSAID if the kidneys allow (they often do not: sepsis, diabetes, nephrotoxic antibiotics), and oral opioid for movement and dressing changes. Pain that is rising at 12-24 hours means undrained pus or a missed space and is reported to the surgeon as a surgical sign. Restart diabetic medication with the first meal and check the glucose; if dexamethasone was given, it will be higher for a day.

  10. 10 The second look

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Planned return to theatre at 24-48 hours to re-irrigate, debride what has declared itself and decide on closure. About a quarter of flexor sheath infections return in published series; in some units the second look is routine.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The same patient, now fed, hydrated and on antibiotics: usually an easier anaesthetic. Repeat what worked. Total local anaesthetic dose across procedures a day apart is not a concern. If the hand is clearly improving, some second looks are replaced by a dressing change on the ward with oral analgesia, or under a wrist block that you may be asked to place.

Pain plan

The mechanism is pressure before surgery and an inflamed, open, moving wound after it, so the plan is front-loaded. Before theatre: elevation, a splint, intravenous paracetamol and titrated opioid; these patients are often under-treated while they wait. For the operation: a short- or intermediate-acting plexus block through clean skin, or general anaesthesia with a wrist block at the end. After it: regular paracetamol, an NSAID only when renal function, sepsis and the antibiotic regimen permit, and oral opioid timed for therapy and dressing changes for two to four days.

The author’s practice is not to use a long-acting agent with a perineural or high-dose intravenous adjunct for the first washout. A dense 18-hour block hides the one symptom that tells the surgeon the drainage was inadequate, and the operation relieves most of the pain by itself. This is judgement, not evidence. For a second or third procedure in a hand that is settling, a longer block is reasonable.

A catheter is rarely needed. The defensible case is the patient facing several returns to theatre and painful daily dressings, with an infraclavicular site clear of infection; it is removed at any redness or discharge at the site, and it is not placed while the patient is febrile and untreated.

In the patient with opioid dependence, continue the usual methadone or buprenorphine, expect to need more short-acting opioid than usual on top, and lean on the block (the opioid-tolerant patient).

Pitfalls and variants

  • Necrotising soft tissue infection. Pain out of proportion, rapid proximal spread, skin that is dusky or blistered, a sick patient. This is resuscitation, general anaesthesia, wide debridement and intensive care, not a hand washout.
  • The fight bite presented as a fall. The history is often withheld, and the patient may be intoxicated or in custody. A block needs cooperation; see the same point in hand trauma.
  • Diabetes. The largest modifiable risk for amputation in the series, and the commonest reason the short case becomes a medical admission.
  • The dialysis patient. No tourniquet, cannula or blood pressure cuff on the fistula arm; if the fistula arm is the infected one, the surgeon and nephrologist decide about the cuff, and a block is usually still possible.
  • Intravenous drug users. Access may need ultrasound; endocarditis and other seeded sites are on the list of causes of a fever that does not settle.
  • Children. A felon or a paronychia is a brief general anaesthetic; a septic joint in a child follows paediatric bone and joint infection.
  • Continuous sheath irrigation on the ward. A catheter left in the sheath with saline running: painful if it blocks and the finger distends, and the pain is treated by fixing the outflow.
  • Amputation at the second look. A ray or digit amputation for a dead finger is a different pain problem, with a nerve mechanism; see amputation.
  • Osteomyelitis and chronic infection of the hand follow the principles in osteomyelitis and septic arthritis and antibiotics and infection.

Key sources

The surgical literature on hand infection is observational and of low quality, to the point that a systematic review could not rank the standard treatments. The prognostic data (delay, diabetes, stage) are consistent across series. On the anaesthetic questions there is almost nothing: one cohort describes wide-awake local anaesthesia for hand infections without harm, and no study addresses plexus block proximal to infection, catheter use in the bacteraemic patient, or the choice of local anaesthetic duration. Those recommendations are consensus and the author’s judgement and are labelled as such in the text.
