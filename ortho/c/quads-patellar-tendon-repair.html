36 Quadriceps and patellar tendon repair

Acute repair by transosseous tunnels or suture anchors; augmentation; chronic reconstruction with autograft or allograft; the extensor mechanism rupture after knee arthroplasty

4Severe

Dominant mechanism: Tendon under tension. A retracted, usually degenerate tendon pulled back onto a decorticated, bleeding patellar pole and locked there under tension with heavy sutures, then loaded by every quadriceps contraction, every transfer and every step for six weeks; the torn retinaculum repaired on either side; plus drill tunnels through the patella and a tourniquet

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Block
Spinal anaesthesia or general anaesthesia with a single-shot adductor canal block and IV dexamethasone; surgeon's infiltration of the wound edges and retinaculum
Catheter: selective An adductor canal catheter for the bilateral rupture, the chronic reconstruction with a graft, the repair after knee arthroplasty, and the opioid-tolerant or dialysis-dependent patient; a single shot with a written night plan for the acute unilateral repair in a braced leg that will not be bent for weeks
Alternatives: Femoral nerve block: acceptable for this operation because the quadriceps is braced in extension and not asked to work, at the cost of a numb, weak leg in a locked brace on the first night; General anaesthesia with infiltration only for the short acute repair in a young patient when a block is declined; Epidural for the bilateral repair in the patient who cannot have two catheters
Position
Supine, leg flat on the table with a bump under the ipsilateral buttock in some hands; the surgeon needs full extension for the repair and the ability to flex the knee to 60-90 degrees to test it

Sometimes. A thigh tourniquet is common for the exposure but it tethers the retracted quadriceps and makes the tendon harder to bring down to the patella, so surgeons inflate it with the tendon pulled distally, or let it down for the reduction, or do without

No

Tell the patient
The tendon has been stitched back onto the kneecap and locked straight in a brace; the knee will ache hard for the first few days and then every time the leg is loaded for about six weeks, and the brace is what protects the repair while it heals.
The trap
This is a tendon repaired under tension, not a knee that has had a bone cut: the first night is the easy part, the pain is re-provoked for six weeks by every step in the brace, and the patient who tore the tendon has the disease that makes tendons tear, so expect diabetes, renal failure, steroids or a fluoroquinolone in the history and plan the NSAID, the dexamethasone and the dialysis timing accordingly

What the surgeon is doing and why

The extensor mechanism is a single chain, quadriceps to quadriceps tendon to patella to patellar tendon to tibial tubercle, and it fails at whichever link is weakest for that patient. The two tendon ruptures are different diseases that get the same operation. The quadriceps tendon tears in the older, heavier man, at a mean age of 61 and a body mass index of 30 in the largest series, through a tendon that was already degenerate: in the zone of poor blood supply just above the patella, in a patient with diabetes, renal failure, gout, hyperparathyroidism, long-term corticosteroids, or a recent fluoroquinolone, after a trivial load such as a stumble on a stair. The patellar tendon tears in the younger man, mean age 39.5, under a high eccentric load with the knee flexed, in a tendon that is usually healthier but may carry a tendinopathy or a previous graft harvest. Both are 91-95% male. The two things the anaesthetist needs from that sentence are that the quadriceps patient has the comorbidities that make tendons fail, and that a woman with either rupture almost always has an underlying medical disease (96% in the series), which may not have been diagnosed yet. In dialysis patients the quadriceps and patellar tendons account for three quarters of spontaneous ruptures, and frailty, years on dialysis, a high calcium-phosphate product and a recent quinolone predict them; after knee arthroplasty, testosterone replacement, fluoroquinolones and smoking do.

The injured knee cannot extend against gravity, there is a palpable gap, and the patella sits low (quadriceps rupture) or high (patellar tendon rupture) on the lateral radiograph. The diagnosis is missed at first presentation in a surprising fraction, which matters because the tendon retracts and the muscle shortens, and a repair that is easy at a week is a reconstruction at six. The surgeon’s aim is simple: put the tendon end back onto the bone it came from, at a tension that restores full active extension without over-tightening the patella into the trochlea, and hold it there for the six to twelve weeks that tendon takes to heal to bone.

The sequence, for either tendon, is:

  1. Midline incision over the gap, evacuation of the haematoma, and identification of the torn tendon end and of the medial and lateral retinaculum, which tear with it in a complete rupture (that is why the knee cannot extend at all rather than weakly).
  2. Debridement of the frayed, degenerate tendon end to healthy tissue, which in the older quadriceps rupture can remove a centimetre and increase the gap.
  3. Preparation of the patellar pole: the proximal pole for a quadriceps rupture, the distal pole for a patellar tendon rupture, decorticated with a burr or rongeur to a bleeding cancellous surface, because tendon heals to bone and not to cortex.
  4. Fixation. Two or three heavy non-absorbable locking sutures (Krackow stitches) run up and down the tendon so the tension is shared along its length; then either three longitudinal drill tunnels through the patella with the suture ends passed through and tied over the opposite pole, or two or three suture anchors in the pole with the sutures tied down onto the tendon. The cadaveric studies show anchors form a smaller gap under cyclic load (0.9-4.1 mm against 2.9-10.3 mm for a transpatellar patellar tendon repair) with a similar load to failure, and the clinical reviews show no difference in re-rupture (0-17% with tunnels, 0-15% with anchors) or function. Transosseous tunnels remain the commoner technique, anchors are increasing, and the choice is the surgeon’s.
  5. The tendon reduced onto the pole with the knee in full extension and the sutures tied under tension; the retinaculum repaired on both sides, which adds to the strength more than it appears to; sometimes an augmentation (a cerclage of suture tape or wire from patella to tubercle, or a strip of tendon) to protect the repair while it heals.
  6. The repair tested by flexing the knee to the angle at which it gaps, usually 60-90 degrees, which sets the limit the brace will allow in the first weeks.
  7. Closure over no drain, and a hinged brace locked in extension. Weight-bearing is allowed in the brace from day 1; flexion is restricted and advanced over six weeks; active extension against resistance waits longer.

A chronic rupture (beyond about six weeks) is a different operation: the retracted tendon will not reach, and the surgeon reconstructs with hamstring autograft, Achilles allograft or synthetic mesh, often with a quadriceps lengthening, over two to three hours. The rupture after knee arthroplasty is worse again: direct repair of a patellar tendon rupture around an implant failed in 63% of pooled cases, which is why those patients get a reconstruction with allograft or mesh instead, and they are older, heavier (mean body mass index 34.5 in the European review) and more comorbid than any other patient on this card.

The pain logic is tendon-tension, which puts this operation in a different family from the arthroplasty whose anaesthetic skeleton it borrows. A degenerate tendon has been pulled back onto a raw, bleeding bony pole and locked there with sutures under tension, and from the first transfer out of bed it is loaded by every quadriceps contraction, including the ones the patient makes to steady the leg in its brace. The retinacular repair is loaded the same way. Underneath it is bone, from the decorticated pole and the drill tunnels, which hurts for two or three days and then settles, and a tourniquet. The score is 4: the first night with a block working is unremarkable, the peak is on day 1 and 2 as the block and the infiltration recede and the patient starts moving, and the ache is re-provoked by loading for weeks, so a single-shot block covers the easy part. It is not a 5 because the knee is held still: nobody bends it on day 1, the brace takes most of the load off the repair, and by a week most patients are on simple analgesia and the opioid is for sleep.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Diagnosis, the patient behind the tendon, and the timing

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the tendon repaired within a week or two of the injury, before retraction and muscle shortening turn a repair into a reconstruction; it is urgent, not an emergency, and a quadriceps rupture in a dialysis patient waits for a dialysis session rather than the reverse.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The assessment is of the disease that tore the tendon. For the quadriceps rupture: diabetes and its control, chronic kidney disease or dialysis (timing of the last session, potassium, the calcium-phosphate axis and the frailty that goes with years of dialysis), long-term corticosteroids needing cover, gout, hyperparathyroidism, a recent fluoroquinolone course, obesity with its airway and positioning consequences, and anticoagulants in a population where atrial fibrillation is common. A woman with either rupture needs a medical cause looked for if none is known. For the patellar tendon: a young, fit patient, sometimes a bodybuilder on anabolic steroids or testosterone, who will be the rebound pain phenotype. Bilateral simultaneous rupture, which happens in the steroid, renal and gout patients, means a patient who cannot stand at all afterwards and a different plan for both the anaesthetic and the ward. The NSAID decision is made here, because the typical quadriceps patient has the kidneys that forbid it.

  2. 2 Anaesthetic plan: spinal or general, the block, dexamethasone

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs a still, fully relaxed leg while the retracted tendon is brought down and tied, a dry field for the suture work, and a patient who can stand in a locked brace the next morning.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A spinal with sedation or a general anaesthetic are both reasonable; the spinal suits the older comorbid quadriceps patient and the general anaesthetic the young patellar tendon patient who wants to go home, and neither has evidence over the other for this operation. The analgesic skeleton is borrowed from the arthroplasty guideline because there is none for tendon repair: paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor where the kidneys allow, a single-shot adductor canal block with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, surgeon’s infiltration, and opioid for rescue. The adductor canal block is the author’s default because the patient will stand and transfer in the brace with a quadriceps that fires, which is safer than a femoral block’s numb, weak leg in a locked brace, and because the brace does the job of keeping the quadriceps quiet that the femoral block would otherwise do. A femoral block is acceptable for this operation in a way it is not for an arthroplasty. The single dose of dexamethasone has no evidence of harm to a tendon repair and prolongs the block; it is given.

  3. 3 Positioning, the tourniquet and the retracted quadriceps

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Supine with the leg flat, because the repair is tied in full extension; a tourniquet on the thigh gives a dry field for the suture work but compresses the quadriceps bunched up proximally and tethers it, so that a retracted tendon which would reach the patella with the muscle free will not reach with the cuff up. Surgeons inflate the cuff with the tendon pulled distally, let it down for the reduction, or do without.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cefazolin 2 g IV before the cuff. The tourniquet time is short, 45-75 minutes, and tourniquet pain through a spinal arrives at the end of it, if at all. Full relaxation matters at the reduction (a general anaesthetic needs neuromuscular block here, a spinal supplies it) because a quadriceps with tone pulls against the surgeon bringing the tendon down and the repair is tied under more tension than it needs. Pad the heel and the contralateral leg; the obese quadriceps patient on a flat table for an hour and a half is a pressure-area patient.

  4. 4 Exposure, haematoma and debridement of the tendon end

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The midline incision opens a haematoma that has been under tension since the injury; the torn tendon end is found retracted, frayed and, in the older patient, visibly degenerate, and it is trimmed back to tissue that will hold a suture. The torn retinaculum on either side is identified for repair later.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The first bleeding of the case, modest under a tourniquet and steady without one. The debridement is the step at which the surgeon learns whether this is a repair or a reconstruction: a tendon that is trimmed back 2 cm and will not reach the pole with the muscle freed means a lengthening or a graft and an extra hour, and the catheter conversation reopens here.

  5. 5 Preparation of the patellar pole and the tunnels or anchors

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The pole is decorticated to bleeding bone, then either drilled with three parallel longitudinal tunnels for the suture ends, or fitted with two or three anchors. Tunnels through a small patella risk a fracture through the drill holes, particularly in the osteoporotic patient; anchors avoid the tunnels but can pull out of poor bone.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the bone step: a raw cancellous surface and drill holes through the patella, which is the component of the pain that settles in 48 hours and the reason the operation qualifies as bone surgery in the rebound cohort’s terms. Nothing to do but note that a patient with a fractured patella from the drill holes has just acquired a longer case and a tension-band fixation.

  6. 6 Locking sutures, reduction and the repair under tension

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Heavy locking sutures are run along the tendon, passed through the tunnels or anchored, and tied with the knee in full extension and the tendon pulled down onto the pole; the retinaculum is repaired; the surgeon then flexes the knee to find the angle at which the repair gaps, and that angle sets the brace limit. The tension is the point: a lax repair leaves an extension lag and a tight one over-compresses the patella and re-tears.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The leg must not move while the knots go down; a working spinal or full block delivers this without being asked. The mechanism this card is built on exists from this moment: tendon on bone under tension, loaded by every contraction from tomorrow. The test flexion is a brief, sharp nociceptive stimulus that a lightly anaesthetised patient will show. If the surgeon augments with a cerclage, there is more bone work at the tubercle.

  7. 7 Closure, infiltration and the brace

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Layered closure over no drain, infiltration of the wound edges and retinaculum where the surgeon does it, a bulky dressing, and a hinged brace locked at zero degrees that the patient will wear day and night for the first weeks.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Tourniquet release, if the cuff was up, brings the usual reperfusion dip and the bleeding from the decorticated pole; a vasopressor to hand. Add the surgeon’s infiltration to the block in the ropivacaine arithmetic. The brace goes on before the patient lightens, because an awake patient with a dense block and a leg that feels like someone else’s will try to bend it. Give the ondansetron.

  8. 8 Recovery, the first night and standing in a brace with a blocked leg

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Weight-bearing as tolerated in the locked brace from day 1 is the usual instruction, with crutches; no active extension against resistance, no flexion beyond the brace setting, and the brace worn in bed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The trajectory on this card is 3 on the first evening with the block working, 6 on day 1 and 5 on day 2 as the block recedes and the patient starts transferring, 4 on day 3 and 3 at a week. The regular paracetamol and NSAID (or paracetamol alone, in the renal patient) go in before the block recedes; the opioid is charted for the nights and for the first transfers; and the night plan is explained, because the first night at home or on the ward is when rebound arrives, in half of ambulatory block patients, more in the young and in those without dexamethasone. The first stand is the hazard with a block still working: a numb leg in a locked brace is a fall risk, and the physiotherapist is told which block was done. Observations: a knee that cannot be actively extended against gravity when the block has gone and the brace is off for the check is a failed repair; a tense, hot wound over the next week in a diabetic or renal patient is an infection, which is the common serious complication in the patellar tendon series.

  9. 9 The six weeks and what fails

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The brace is unlocked in stages (commonly 30 degrees more flexion every two weeks), active extension is allowed from about six weeks, and the repair is protected from a fall or a forced flexion throughout. Re-rupture (0-17% in the pooled series), arthrofibrosis, an extension lag, wound infection and venous thromboembolism are the complications.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The pain tail is a tendon tail: an ache with each day’s loading that outlasts the bone pain by weeks, which is why the oral regimen is written for ten days rather than three, with the opioid reserved for sleep after the first week. Venous thromboembolism in a braced, immobile, obese patient with renal disease or diabetes is the medical complication of this operation and the chemoprophylaxis plan is for six weeks, not the inpatient stay. The patient who returns with a re-rupture gets a reconstruction, the longer and more painful operation on this card.

Pain plan

The mechanism is a tendon repaired under tension on a decorticated bone surface and loaded for six weeks, with the loading limited by a brace, in a patient who is either young and fit (patellar tendon) or old, heavy and comorbid (quadriceps). There is no procedure-specific guideline. PROSPECT’s knee arthroplasty guideline is the nearest and its skeleton transfers: paracetamol with an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor where the kidneys allow, a single-shot adductor canal block, surgeon’s infiltration, intravenous dexamethasone and opioid as rescue. The adductor canal block covers the anteromedial knee and the incision; the lateral retinaculum and the tubercle are the surgeon’s infiltration to cover. The arthroplasty guideline’s objection to the femoral block is functional (a weak quadriceps on a knee that must be bent on day 1) and does not apply with the same force here, because the knee is locked straight and the quadriceps is deliberately rested; the author still prefers the adductor canal block because the patient has to stand on the leg in a brace, but a femoral block for this operation is a defensible choice and some surgeons prefer the quieter quadriceps it gives on the first night.

The catheter decision is author’s judgement on no direct evidence. For an acute unilateral repair in a braced leg, a single shot with dexamethasone, infiltration and a written night plan is enough, because the knee is not moved for the first days and the re-provocation is standing and transferring rather than bending. An adductor canal catheter earns its place for the bilateral rupture (two numb legs and no way to stand, so the plan is pain control and bed-based rehabilitation for several days), the chronic reconstruction with a graft, the rupture after arthroplasty (older, heavier, a bigger operation and a worse tissue bed), the dialysis patient who cannot have an NSAID, and the opioid-tolerant patient. An epidural is the alternative for the bilateral case where two catheters are impractical, accepting the immobility that the bilateral brace already imposes.

Rebound: the young patellar tendon patient is the ambulatory cohort’s rebound phenotype (bone surgery, young, often sent home the same day), and intravenous dexamethasone is the single intervention with evidence for reducing it. Escalation triggers: pain above 7 after the block has gone with the brace on and the calf soft is a prescription problem and is treated with the charted opioid; a tense, swollen knee with pain out of proportion is a haematoma under a repair and the surgeon decides whether to evacuate it; calf pain or a swollen leg at any point in the six weeks is a clot; and any loss of active extension is a re-rupture until the surgeon has examined it.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Transosseous versus anchors. Anchors gap less under cyclic load in the laboratory and are not better clinically; tunnels through a small or osteoporotic patella can fracture it; neither changes the anaesthetic.
  • The tourniquet and the tendon. A cuff inflated with the quadriceps retracted makes the reduction harder; expect the surgeon to let it down mid-case or to work without one, and expect the bleeding to be at the end.
  • Bilateral rupture. The renal, steroid and gout patient; both legs braced, no standing for days, catheters or an epidural, a high venous thromboembolism risk and a long stay; the medical disease is usually the bigger problem.
  • Dialysis. Operate the day after a session; potassium and fluid status on the day; no NSAID; cefazolin dosed for renal failure on repeat; the fistula arm protected; expect poor tissue and a higher re-rupture rate.
  • Steroids and diabetes. Steroid cover; glucose control for a day after the dexamethasone, which is still worth giving; a wound that heals slowly and infects more often.
  • Fluoroquinolones. The patient on one when the tendon tore, or given one post-operatively, has the other tendons at risk; the association appears in the arthroplasty disruption data (OR 1.58) and the drug class is avoided afterwards.
  • Chronic rupture and reconstruction. Hamstring autograft, Achilles allograft or mesh, with a quadriceps lengthening: a 2-3 h case, more bleeding, a graft harvest site that hurts in its own right, and the catheter case.
  • Rupture after knee arthroplasty. Direct repair fails in most patellar tendon cases; the operation is a reconstruction in an older, heavier patient with a 16% pooled complication rate; cefazolin timing and the infection risk are those of an arthroplasty revision.
  • The missed diagnosis. A patient presenting weeks late with a “sprain” has a retracted tendon; the booking says repair and the operation becomes a reconstruction, and the surgeon finds out at step 4.
  • Obesity. The quadriceps patient’s mean body mass index is 30 and the post-arthroplasty patient’s 34.5; positioning, pressure areas, airway under sedation, and dosing of the chemoprophylaxis.
  • Anticoagulation. Atrial fibrillation is common in the quadriceps population; the haematoma is bigger, the spinal timing applies, and the brace hides a bleeding wound.
  • The young patient going home. Day case is reasonable for an acute patellar tendon repair with a block, a brace, crutches practised, and a written night plan that assumes rebound.

Key sources

The surgical evidence is case series and systematic reviews of them, consistent on the demographics, the comorbidities, the equivalence of tunnels and anchors in practice, and the poor results of direct repair after arthroplasty. The biomechanical data are cadaveric. There is no analgesia trial and no procedure-specific guideline for extensor mechanism repair; the block choice is extrapolated from PROSPECT’s arthroplasty guideline with the functional argument adjusted for a braced knee, and the catheter advice, the dexamethasone statement and the score are the author’s judgement from the mechanism and are labelled as such.
