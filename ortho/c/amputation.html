47 Lower limb amputation

Below-knee and above-knee amputation for vascular disease, infection, trauma and tumour; through-knee and guillotine variants; stump revision

5Severe and prolonged

Dominant mechanism: Nerve. Four or five named nerves cut under tension and left to form neuromas, a deafferented cord segment and cortex that will generate phantom sensation and, in half or more, phantom pain; under that, a tibia or femur transected with a saw, a muscle flap sutured over the bone end under tension so the stump has a shape, and an ischaemic or infected limb that was hurting before the knife

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Block
Spinal anaesthesia for the operation where coagulation and sepsis allow, with a sciatic (tibial) perineural catheter placed by the surgeon under direct vision before closure or by ultrasound before the spinal; a single-shot femoral or saphenous block for the anteromedial skin
Catheter: routine The author's default is a sciatic or tibial perineural catheter running ropivacaine 0.2% for three to five days, on the grounds of the mechanism and of a frail population that tolerates opioid badly; the pooled evidence shows a modest reduction in acute pain and opioid use and no effect on phantom pain, so the catheter is a judgement, not a proven preventive
Alternatives: Lumbar epidural started before surgery and run for several days, which gives better stump analgesia in the first three days than a perineural catheter but does not prevent phantom pain and sits badly with heparin infusions and antiplatelet drugs; General anaesthesia with pre-operative ultrasound-guided sciatic and femoral blocks when a spinal is contraindicated; Low-dose ketamine infusion for the opioid-tolerant patient and the young trauma amputee, as analgesia rather than as prevention
Position
Supine with the limb on a bolster or held by an assistant, the sound heel padded and off the table, and a tourniquet on the thigh in some hands; above-knee amputation sometimes with a sandbag under the buttock to lift the thigh
Tourniquet
Sometimes. Used for trauma, tumour and infection where the vessels are healthy; in vascular disease surgeons vary, some using a thigh tourniquet for a below-knee amputation to cut blood loss and some refusing it to protect calcified vessels and flap perfusion
Cement
No
Blood loss
200-500 mL for a below-knee amputation, 300-800 mL for an above-knee amputation, more without a tourniquet, in trauma and in tumour resection; the vascular patient arrives anaemic from sepsis and renal disease, so a group and hold always and blood available for an above-knee amputation
Duration
45-90 min for a below-knee amputation, 60-120 min for an above-knee amputation; a guillotine amputation 20-30 min; tumour resections and traumatic amputations with flap reconstruction run far longer
Length of stay
7-14 nights, set by wound healing, comorbidity and the rehabilitation destination rather than by the operation; a revision to a higher level resets the clock. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV within 60 min of incision (3 g over 120 kg) for a clean elective amputation; where infection is the indication the treatment regimen already running is continued and timed to cover the incision, with Gram-negative and anaerobic cover for wet gangrene and diabetic foot sepsis
Tranexamic acid
Tranexamic acid 1 g IV at induction for trauma, tumour and any amputation without a tourniquet; in the patient with a fresh stent or graft or an acute arterial thrombosis the author weighs the thrombotic risk and often withholds it, a judgement rather than evidence
VTE
Low molecular weight heparin from the evening of surgery, continued through the immobile weeks; a mechanical sleeve on the sound leg; a femoral vein that has just been ligated and a patient who will not walk for a fortnight are a high-risk combination, and many vascular patients are already on a heparin infusion that is simply resumed
Antiemetic
Ondansetron 4 mg IV; dexamethasone 4-8 mg IV at induction with the lower dose in the septic diabetic, where the glucose rise is the cost and the antiemetic and analgesic benefit still worth having
Tell the patient
The leg will be numb for the first day or two while the local anaesthetic runs; the stump then aches and is sore to touch for a week or two, and most people feel the missing foot as if it were still there, which is normal, expected, and treated if it hurts.
The trap
The patient is the risk, not the operation: 30-day mortality after major amputation for vascular disease runs between 10 and 17% in the series on this card, the mode of anaesthesia has never been shown to change it, and the decisions that matter (sepsis control, the heparin infusion, the glucose, the heart and the kidneys) are made the day before, so a smooth spinal and a well-placed catheter are the easy part

What the surgeon is doing and why

An amputation is offered when the limb is killing the patient, cannot be saved, or is worse than no limb. Four diseases bring four different patients to the same operation. Peripheral arterial disease with rest pain, tissue loss or wet gangrene after failed or impossible revascularisation is the commonest: that patient is old, diabetic, often on dialysis, usually on an antiplatelet drug or a heparin infusion, with coronary and cerebrovascular disease in proportion to the disease in the leg. Infection (diabetic foot sepsis, necrotising fasciitis, an infected non-union or arthroplasty beyond reconstruction) brings a patient who is septic now and needs source control today. Trauma brings a young patient with a mangled limb, blood loss and other injuries; tumour brings a planned resection with a flap and a long operation.

The surgeon’s aims pull against each other: remove everything dead and infected, leave a stump that will heal on the blood supply the patient has, and build an end that can take a prosthesis. The level is the compromise. A below-knee amputation keeps the knee and roughly halves the energy cost of walking compared with an above-knee amputation, so it is the level every surgeon wants, but it needs a calf with enough perfusion to heal a long posterior flap. An above-knee amputation heals more reliably in poor perfusion and is the level for the patient who will never walk, for a knee fixed in flexion, and for sepsis that has reached the calf. A guillotine amputation, a straight cut through everything with the wound left open, is the sepsis operation: 20 minutes in a patient too sick for more, with the formal amputation days later once the physiology has recovered.

The below-knee operation is the spine of the card. Skin flaps are cut a hand’s breadth below the tibial tubercle: a long posterior flap carrying gastrocnemius and soleus on their sural artery supply, or skew flaps on their own perforators. The anterior and lateral compartments are divided and the anterior tibial vessels tied. The tibia is cut with an oscillating saw and bevelled at the front; the fibula is cut a centimetre or two shorter so it takes no load. The posterior tibial and peroneal vessels are ligated and the deep posterior muscles divided. The nerves are dealt with deliberately: the tibial, superficial and deep peroneal, sural and saphenous nerves are each drawn down under gentle traction and divided sharply so the cut end retracts into muscle well above the bone and away from the socket; the tibial nerve is ligated with its vasa nervorum because it bleeds. The posterior flap is brought forward and sutured to the anterior tibial periosteum or through drill holes under tension (a myodesis) or to the anterior muscles (a myoplasty), so the bone end is padded, the muscles keep a length to contract against, and the stump is cylindrical.

The above-knee operation follows the same logic a storey higher: fish-mouth flaps, the quadriceps divided, the femoral vessels ligated in the adductor canal (where the blood loss happens if it happens), the femur cut as long as the flaps allow, the sciatic nerve drawn down, divided and ligated, and adductor magnus sutured across the end of the femur under tension so it does not drift into abduction.

The pain logic is why this card sits with rotator cuff repair and scoliosis at a 5 rather than with the fractures at a 3. The dominant mechanism is nerve. Four or five named nerves are cut, each will form a neuroma, and a cord segment and a cortical map have lost their input and will reorganise over weeks. The result is three pains the ward will chart as one: stump pain (wound, bone end and neuromas, nociceptive and then neuropathic), phantom sensation (the foot is still there, nearly universal, not painful), and phantom pain (the missing foot hurts, cramps or burns), which in the trials on this card affected half the patients at one week and two thirds or more at a year. Beneath the nerve mechanism is bone (a tibia and fibula or a femur sawn through, worst for two days like any osteotomy), muscle under tension (a myodesis loads the muscle with every contraction and is why the stump aches for weeks rather than days), and a leg that was generating pain for weeks before the knife, so the sensitisation is in place before the first incision. The shape of the curve earns the score: a stump sore to touch and to the socket, and a phantom that appears in week one and may persist for years.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 The decision, the level and the day before

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon has judged the limb unsalvageable and chosen a level from the perfusion, the infection and the patient’s prospects of walking. They want sepsis controlled, the heparin infusion or antiplatelet drug managed, the glucose in range and the heart assessed, because the operation is short and the physiology is what kills.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    In the series on this card 30-day mortality after major amputation for vascular disease ran at 10-17%, and in none of them did the mode of anaesthesia change it. The work is the assessment: a functional history in a patient who has not walked far enough to have angina; renal function and the dialysis schedule; glucose and ketones in the septic diabetic; a haemoglobin low from chronic disease and sepsis; and the anticoagulation, which decides between a spinal and a general anaesthetic. Take the pain history in milligrams: weeks of rest pain on an opioid means a tolerant patient whose baseline dose continues and whose plan is in the opioid-tolerant chapter. Agree the technique and who places the perineural catheter now.

  2. 2 The anaesthetic: spinal or general, and the lines

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs 60-90 min of a still, perfused patient, a leg that can be lifted, and bleeding skin edges to judge the flaps by.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Spinal anaesthesia is the author’s default for the vascular amputee who can have one: no airway in a patient with bad lungs, a dense block for the nerve division and the saw, and in the 407-patient single-centre series regional anaesthesia was associated with fewer pulmonary complications (15% against 24%), fewer arrhythmias and a shorter intensive care stay. The obstacles are the ones this population carries: a heparin infusion that must be stopped for the guideline interval, clopidogrel or ticagrelor that cannot be, platelets low from sepsis, and bacteraemia. General anaesthesia with ultrasound-guided sciatic and femoral blocks before induction is the alternative, and in the Japanese nationwide cohort a nerve block technique carried the same mortality and morbidity as general anaesthesia with less delirium. Either way: an arterial line for heart failure, dialysis or sepsis, and a vasopressor running early, because a spinal in a dry septic patient with a stiff aorta drops the pressure further and faster than in an arthroplasty.

  3. 3 Positioning, tourniquet and antibiotic

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Supine, leg on a bolster so the posterior flap can be cut, sound heel off the table, and in some hands a thigh tourniquet. Surgeons vary on the tourniquet in vascular disease: some use it for every below-knee amputation to cut blood loss, others refuse it because calcified vessels do not compress and flap perfusion cannot be judged with it inflated.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The sound heel is the one the patient will stand on, and a pressure ulcer from an hour on the table in a neuropathic diabetic is the start of the second amputation; pad it. Cefazolin 2 g IV before the knife for the clean case; where infection is the indication the treatment antibiotics already running are the prophylaxis, timed so the incision falls inside the cover. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV where blood loss is expected and nothing fresh sits in the arterial tree; with an acute thrombosis or a stent placed days ago the author often withholds it, a judgement.

  4. 4 Skin flaps and the viability test

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The posterior flap is cut long and deep to carry the calf muscles on their supply. The surgeon judges viability by the skin edge, which must bleed bright, or the flap necroses and the amputation is revised higher in a week.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A pale, vasoconstricted, hypotensive patient gives a false read, and a flap condemned for not bleeding at a systolic of 80 is a level lost for nothing. Keep the pressure near the patient’s own baseline and the patient warm through this step; a request to bring the pressure up to judge a flap is a reasonable one.

  5. 5 Muscle division and vessel ligation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The compartments are divided to bone and the tibial and peroneal vessels (or the superficial femoral vessels in the adductor canal) are isolated and ligated. In vascular disease the arteries are calcified pipes and the veins are what bleed; in trauma and tumour the vessels are healthy and the ligation is the haemostasis of the case.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the blood loss of an above-knee amputation and the time to have the first unit checked in a patient who started at 90 g/L. A ligature that slips off the femoral vein after the tourniquet comes down is a sudden loss into the thigh.

  6. 6 The nerves: division under tension, ligation and reinnervation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Each nerve is drawn down several centimetres and cut sharply so the end retracts into muscle above the bone and away from where the socket will press; the tibial and sciatic nerves are ligated for their vessels. Some add targeted muscle reinnervation or a regenerative peripheral nerve interface, because a nerve with a target forms a smaller neuroma; the randomised trial behind this treated established pain, and primary use at amputation rests on observational data.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the mechanism being created. A nerve pulled and cut is the one step a patient under a receding spinal may feel as a shooting pain into the foot; check the block level before it. A surgeon-placed perineural catheter goes in now, laid along the tibial nerve (below knee) or the sciatic nerve (above knee) and brought out through separate skin, with the first bolus of ropivacaine 0.2% 10-20 mL given before closure so the infusion starts in a blocked limb.

  7. 7 The bone cut

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The tibia is transected with an oscillating saw, bevelled anteriorly and rasped smooth, the fibula cut shorter; the femur is cut as long as the flaps allow. The periosteum is stripped no further than needed, because bare bone under skin is a pressure point and an osteomyelitis in waiting.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cancellous bone bleeds and is sealed with wax or pressure; this is the bone mechanism, worst for two days and then gone. The saw is the moment the patient under a spinal hears, feels through the table and understands, and it is where the one sentence on the emotional side of this operation belongs: the patient has lost a limb, grieves for it, and rehabilitates faster when the people around them say so plainly and then talk about the prosthesis.

  8. 8 Myodesis, myoplasty and the posterior flap

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The gastrocnemius-soleus flap is brought over the bone end and sutured to the anterior tibial periosteum or through drill holes under tension; above the knee, adductor magnus is fixed through drill holes to the lateral femur. The tension is the point: a muscle sutured at length keeps its bulk and gives the stump a shape a socket will fit, and a floppy stump with bone against skin never takes a prosthesis.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A muscle sutured to bone under tension is loaded by every contraction and every attempt to move the knee or hip, and it is why the stump aches for weeks after the bone pain has gone.

  9. 9 Closure, drain and dressing

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A drain in most hands because the dead space under the flap fills with blood; skin closed without tension; a soft bulky dressing or a rigid removable dressing that protects the stump from the fall every new amputee has in the first week.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The perineural catheter is secured and labelled before the dressing goes on, because a catheter under a crepe bandage is one nobody can inspect for a leak or a disconnected filter. The dressing is applied before the spinal recedes or the patient lightens. The first regular analgesia is charted now, not in recovery.

  10. 10 Emergence, recovery and the first night

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants a warm, pink stump, a dry dressing, the knee straight and the patient sitting up by the evening.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The vascular amputee after a spinal is hypotensive for hours with a coronary and cerebral circulation that cannot afford it; a vasopressor infusion in recovery rather than fluid boluses into a bad heart and bad kidneys is the author’s practice. Start the catheter infusion (ropivacaine 0.2% at 5-8 mL/h) as the spinal or single-shot block recedes, with regular paracetamol and the opioid charted as patient-controlled analgesia for the patient who can use it. Delirium is the common complication of the first night, and the lower delirium rate with a block technique in the Japanese cohort is one of the few outcome differences the anaesthetic has been shown to make.

  11. 11 Days 1-5: the catheter, the flap and the phantom

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the flap watched for a dusky edge or dehiscence, the drain out at a day or two, the patient transferring to a chair with the knee straight, and the prosthetic rehabilitation conversation started.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The catheter runs three to five days and comes out when the stump tolerates handling; the pooled evidence is that it lowers pain scores modestly and opioid use somewhat while it runs and does nothing to the phantom pain afterwards, so its removal is not a loss. Phantom sensation appears in nearly everyone within days and phantom pain in about half within the week; tell the patient on day 1 what it is, because a patient who was not warned believes the leg has been left in the bed. Early phantom pain is treated as neuropathic pain (a gabapentinoid or a tricyclic, mirror therapy, the pain service), on the author’s judgement.

Pain plan

The mechanism is nerve, with bone and muscle under tension beneath it and a sensitised nervous system before the first incision, so the plan is a dense block for the operation, a continuous technique for the first days, and an honest account of what the plan cannot do. There is no PROSPECT guideline for amputation; the evidence is a set of small randomised trials of phantom pain prevention, one meta-analysis of perineural catheters and a few cohort studies of the mode of anaesthesia, and the recommendations are the author’s reading of them.

The anaesthetic: spinal where coagulation and sepsis allow, general anaesthesia with pre-operative ultrasound-guided sciatic and femoral (or saphenous) blocks where they do not. The catheter: the author’s default is a sciatic or tibial perineural catheter, surgeon-placed before closure or ultrasound-guided before the spinal, running ropivacaine 0.2% for three to five days. The grounds are the mechanism and the patient: a limb that has had four nerves cut, a bone sawn and a muscle flap sutured under tension is as strong a case for continuous analgesia as the book contains, and the vascular amputee tolerates opioid worst of all patients. The evidence is modest. In the pooled analysis of 10 studies and 731 patients a perineural catheter lowered pain scores by a standardised mean difference of 0.30 and morphine use by 0.63, the second effect disappearing when only the randomised trials were pooled, with no effect on mortality, phantom pain or chronic stump pain. An epidural does the acute job better: in the 30-patient comparison an epidural started 24 h before surgery gave lower stump pain for the first three days than a surgeon-placed perineural catheter and the same phantom pain at a year. Its problem is the population (heparin infusions, dual antiplatelet therapy, bacteraemia), which is why the perineural catheter is the default.

What the plan cannot do is prevent phantom pain, whatever the folklore says. The 60-patient Lancet trial of an epidural started 18 h before amputation and continued afterwards found phantom pain in 52% at a week and 75% at a year against 56% and 69% with systemic morphine. The 45-patient trial of ketamine at 0.5 mg/kg/h for 72 h found phantom pain at six months in 47% against 71%, not significant in a trial powered for a large effect, with more stump pain on day 3. The 2025 pooling of 20 trials with follow-up of six months or more found a 0.63-point reduction in phantom pain intensity with low certainty and no individual intervention (ketamine, gabapentin, valproate, calcitonin, epidural or perineural local anaesthetic) that reduced it. Ketamine stays in the plan as analgesia for the opioid-tolerant and the young trauma amputee, not as prevention. The surgical answer, primary targeted muscle reinnervation, has a randomised trial for established pain (phantom pain down about three points at a year against none with neuroma excision) and observational data for prevention, and is the surgeon’s call.

The oral regimen: regular paracetamol; an NSAID only where kidneys and vascular tree allow, which in practice means the trauma and tumour patients; the baseline opioid continued in the tolerant patient and an immediate-release opioid or a patient-controlled pump for the first three to five days; a gabapentinoid or a tricyclic when phantom pain declares itself rather than before. Rebound is high: when the catheter comes out on day 3-5, or a single-shot block recedes overnight in a patient without one, the stump pain arrives in a limb that has already begun to generate phantom pain, and that night needs a written plan.

Escalation: stump pain rising under a tense dressing in the first 24 h is a haematoma under the flap; a dusky edge, a discharging wound and a fever at days 3-7 is a failing flap heading for revision at a higher level, the same anaesthetic in a sicker patient; shooting pain into the missing foot is the neuroma and the phantom, a pain service problem rather than a surgical one.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Below-knee versus above-knee. The below-knee amputation is the one the patient wants and the one that fails to heal in poor perfusion; a proportion of vascular below-knee amputations are revised above the knee within weeks, and that second operation finds a patient sicker, more anaemic and already on opioids. The above-knee amputation bleeds more, hurts the same, and is the level for the patient who will not walk.
  • The guillotine amputation. Twenty minutes in a septic patient who cannot tolerate more, often at night and under general anaesthesia because the coagulation is deranged; the formal amputation follows in three to five days. The physiology is septic shock: vasopressor, arterial line, and intensive care told before the knife.
  • The anticoagulated patient. Heparin infusions for acute ischaemia, clopidogrel for a stent, apixaban for atrial fibrillation: the spinal follows the guideline intervals, and the surgeon-placed perineural catheter in an open wound is the technique that does not care.
  • The dialysis patient. Fluid balance, potassium, a fistula arm that takes no cuff or cannula; dialysis the day before and no NSAID ever.
  • The septic diabetic. Glucose and ketones, treatment antibiotics rather than prophylaxis, a relative contraindication to the spinal while bacteraemic, and dexamethasone at the lower dose.
  • Trauma. A young patient with other injuries, a limb that has been under a field tourniquet for hours (acidosis and potassium when it comes off), blood loss and a long reconstruction with flaps; the catheter and the ketamine both earn their place.
  • The opioid-tolerant patient with weeks of rest pain. The commonest phenotype among the vascular amputees and the one for whom the catheter matters most, because the alternative is an opioid dose the ward will not give.

Key sources

The evidence is better than its reputation and worse than its folklore. The three randomised trials of phantom pain prevention (epidural before and after, epidural against perineural catheter, ketamine for 72 h) are small, old and consistent in finding nothing, and the 2025 meta-analysis of 20 trials agrees. The perineural catheter meta-analysis is the only pooled estimate of what the catheter does and its effect on acute pain is modest; the PLACEMENT feasibility trial was built to answer the question properly and the effectiveness trial has not reported. The mode-of-anaesthesia data are retrospective cohorts in which regional techniques were associated with fewer complications and never with lower mortality.

  7. Chery J, Semaan E, Darji S, Briggs WT, Yarmush J, D'Ayala M. Impact of regional versus general anesthesia on the clinical outcomes of patients undergoing major lower extremity amputation. Ann Vasc Surg 2014;28:1149-1156
    407 major amputations at one centre, 259 under regional anaesthesia: fewer pulmonary complications (15% versus 24%) and arrhythmias (14% versus 25%), shorter intensive care and hospital stay; mortality 10% versus 13% and myocardial infarction 12% versus 9%, neither different; the regional group was older, lighter and less often anticoagulated
  8. Abe H, Sumitani M, Matsui H, et al. Comparing outcomes after peripheral nerve block versus general anesthesia for lower extremity amputation: a nationwide exploratory retrospective cohort study in Japan. Reg Anesth Pain Med 2020;45:399-404
    11,796 below-knee and foot amputations, 747 under peripheral nerve block, propensity matched one to four: 30-day mortality odds ratio 1.11 (0.75-1.64) and composite morbidity 1.15, neither different; delirium odds ratio 0.75 (0.57-0.98) with the block
  9. Lin R, Hingorani A, Marks N, et al. Effects of anesthesia versus regional nerve block on major leg amputation mortality rate. Vascular 2013;21:83-86
    158 above- and below-knee amputations, mean age 74: overall 30-day mortality 17.1%; 15.2% with peripheral nerve blocks versus 17.9% with general or spinal anaesthesia, not different
  10. Dumanian GA, Potter BK, Mioton LM, et al. Targeted muscle reinnervation treats neuroma and phantom pain in major limb amputees: a randomized clinical trial. Ann Surg 2019;270:238-246
    28 amputees with established pain randomised to targeted muscle reinnervation or neuroma excision and burial in muscle: phantom pain fell by 3.2 points with reinnervation against 0.2 with standard treatment at one year (adjusted p=0.06 in the primary analysis, p=0.03 in the longitudinal model); the surgical basis for primary reinnervation at the time of amputation
  11. Hsu E, Cohen SP. Postamputation pain: epidemiology, mechanisms, and treatment. J Pain Res 2013;6:121-136
    Review of mechanisms: cortical reorganisation behind phantom pain, dorsal horn changes after deafferentation, and neuroma ectopic firing behind residual limb pain; the trials of epidural and perineural prevention are inconsistent, with any benefit confined to epidurals started more than 24 h before surgery against non-optimised controls
