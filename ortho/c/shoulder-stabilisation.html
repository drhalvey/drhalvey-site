5 Shoulder stabilisation: Bankart, remplissage, Latarjet

Arthroscopic labral repair with or without remplissage; open coracoid transfer and free bone block for glenoid bone loss

4Severe

Dominant mechanism: Bone. For the Latarjet: an osteotomised coracoid screwed onto a decorticated glenoid neck through a split subscapularis, with the conjoint tendon pulled through the split under tension; for the arthroscopic Bankart: anchors in the glenoid rim and a capsulolabral complex re-tensioned onto it, plus an infraspinatus tenodesis into the humeral head if remplissage is added; a young male population with the highest rebound odds in the book

What the surgeon is doing and why

An anterior dislocation in a young person tears the anterior-inferior labrum off the glenoid rim with its capsule and inferior glenohumeral ligament (the Bankart lesion), and the posterolateral humeral head is dented against the rim on the way out (the Hill-Sachs lesion). Each further dislocation stretches the capsule and chips the rim. The surgeon’s problem is therefore three things in one joint: a detached, lax soft-tissue restraint; a glenoid that has lost bone from its front edge and is shaped like an inverted pear; and a humeral defect that can engage the front of the glenoid in abduction and external rotation and lever the head out. The operation chosen depends on how much bone has gone, and the two main operations create pain by completely different mechanisms.

The arthroscopic Bankart repair treats the soft tissue. The labrum and capsule are mobilised off the glenoid neck, the rim is lightly decorticated, three or four suture anchors are placed in the glenoid face at the edge of the cartilage, and the capsulolabral complex is sutured back up onto the rim with a deliberate shift, so that the capsule is tighter than it was. If the Hill-Sachs lesion is large enough to engage, a remplissage is added: the infraspinatus tendon and posterior capsule are sutured down into the humeral defect with anchors, filling it so that the dent can no longer catch the rim. The tissues created are a re-tensioned capsule, three or four small anchor holes in cortical bone, and in remplissage a tenodesis under tension. Recurrence in contact athletes runs about 15% after a Bankart alone and is reduced by remplissage to the point where, in subcritical bone loss, the pooled comparisons find no difference from a Latarjet, with fewer complications.

The Latarjet treats the bone. The coracoid process is osteotomised at its base, with the conjoint tendon (short head of biceps and coracobrachialis) still attached, rotated, and screwed flat onto the decorticated anterior-inferior glenoid neck through a horizontal split in the subscapularis. That does three things at once: the coracoid widens the glenoid arc so the head has further to travel before it falls off; the conjoint tendon, now running through the subscapularis split, becomes a sling that tightens across the front of the joint exactly when the arm is abducted and externally rotated; and the inferior half of the subscapularis is tensioned over the graft. It is the better operation for critical bone loss, for the collision athlete and for the failed Bankart (recurrence 3.5% against 14.4% for a revision Bankart), at the cost of a 30% complication rate in the pooled open series, more late pain, less external rotation, and the two nerves that run within a centimetre of the work: the musculocutaneous nerve entering the conjoint tendon 3-8 cm below the coracoid tip, and the axillary nerve crossing the inferior subscapularis on its way under the joint. In the hands of a surgeon who keeps the split muscular and does not pull the conjoint tendon hard, the clinical nerve injury rate is under 1%; in the pooled literature it is higher, and the transferred tendon changes the course of both nerves permanently, so that electrodiagnostic changes appear in most patients when the arm is tested in the dislocating position. The free bone block (iliac crest or distal tibial allograft) is the Latarjet without the sling, fixed with screws or suture buttons, and the comparative data show the same recurrence; it adds a harvest site when autograft is used.

The pain logic separates the two operations. The arthroscopic Bankart is capsule and small bone: anchors in cortical bone and a re-tensioned capsule that hurts sharply with movement for a few days and settles. Remplissage adds a tenodesis under tension on the back of the joint, which is a small version of the cuff repair mechanism and extends the night pain. The Latarjet is bone and muscle: an osteotomy of the coracoid, a decorticated glenoid neck, two screws, a split and re-tensioned subscapularis, and a conjoint tendon that has been pulled through a muscle and now takes load every time the arm goes out to the side. That is why the Latarjet hurts more, bleeds more and is a 4, and why the pooled comparisons show worse early pain after Latarjet than after Bankart with remplissage, converging later. What both share is the population: young, male, bone surgery, day case, which is three of the four rebound risk factors in the ambulatory cohort before you have done anything. The trajectory on this card is for the Latarjet. The arthroscopic Bankart runs a point lower at every time and the remplissage adds a day to its tail.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Assessment, block, magnesium and the night plan

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon has usually decided between Bankart and Latarjet on the CT measurement of glenoid bone loss, the Hill-Sachs size and the sport, and the plan does not change on the table except that an arthroscopic Latarjet converts to open in a few per cent. The patient is young, fit, and has had the shoulder out several times.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is a healthy patient and the whole anaesthetic conversation is about the night. Interscalene block (or superior trunk block if there is a reason to protect the diaphragm, which in this population is rare) with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, placed before induction. For the open Latarjet the author adds intravenous magnesium 50 mg/kg before induction: in the one randomised trial in exactly this operation it reduced the rebound pain on block resolution from 6.2 to 4.0 and lowered pain at 24 h. The catheter decision is made now (see the pain plan). Airway: a second-generation supraglottic device for a short arthroscopic Bankart in a slim patient; a tracheal tube for the open Latarjet and for any case that might run long. Awake baseline pressure before any drug; these are patients with low resting pressures and the chair will take them lower. The written night plan is drafted now, because the patient will be home by the time it matters.

  2. 2 Beach chair or lateral decubitus

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Beach chair for the open Latarjet and for most arthroscopic Bankarts; lateral decubitus with 5-7 kg of longitudinal and lateral traction opens the anterior-inferior joint wider for the labral work and some surgeons prefer it, especially for posterior labral repairs.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Beach chair: the cuff overstates brain perfusion by about 1 mmHg per 1.25 cm between cuff and ear, and a 22-year-old with a resting systolic of 105 and an interscalene block can sit in the chair at a cuff systolic of 80 without anyone being alarmed; that is a brainstem pressure in the 50s and the strokes in the literature happened in this range. Start the vasopressor early and treat the bradycardia-hypotension episodes. Lateral: airway from the side, face in a cradle, dependent arm and peroneal nerve padded, and the traction is a sustained pull on the plexus for 90 minutes that a block will conceal.

  3. 3 Antibiotic and examination under anaesthesia

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon examines the relaxed shoulder for the direction and degree of translation, which confirms the diagnosis and sometimes finds a posterior or multidirectional component that changes the repair.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cefazolin 2 g IV finished before the knife, 3 g over 120 kg; sebaceous young male skin carries the highest Cutibacterium acnes load and the anchors or screws are implants. The examination needs a relaxed patient and no muscle tone, so the surgeon may ask for the depth that a block alone does not provide; it is the one moment a short-acting relaxant is reasonable if the airway is a tube.

  4. 4 Arthroscopy, labral mobilisation and glenoid preparation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The joint is inspected, the Hill-Sachs lesion is assessed for engagement with the arm abducted and externally rotated, and the Bankart lesion is mobilised with an elevator off the glenoid neck until the capsulolabral complex floats freely and can be brought up to the rim. The rim and neck are rasped or burred to bleeding bone so the labrum will heal.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Irrigation starts to go into the tissues. The arm is moved repeatedly into abduction and external rotation to test engagement, which pulls on the plexus and will dislocate the shoulder on the table in a relaxed patient; the surgeon expects this. In the arthroscopic Latarjet this is also where the coracoid is exposed from inside, through the rotator interval, with a much longer instrument time.

  5. 5 Anchors and labral repair

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Three or four anchors go into the glenoid face at the cartilage edge from 5:30 to 3 o’clock, sutures are passed through the capsule and labrum with a deliberate inferior-to-superior shift so the capsule ends up tighter, and the complex is tied or locked onto the rim. The tension is set with the arm in a specific rotation and the surgeon will want it held exactly there.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The anchor holes are the bone component and the shift is the capsule component. Position is set by the assistant and must not drift; this is not a request for relaxant but for a still table. In a young patient without a working block the pressure and heart rate will tell you this step has started.

  6. 6 Remplissage

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    If the Hill-Sachs lesion engages, one or two anchors are placed in the floor of the defect from a posterior portal and the infraspinatus tendon and posterior capsule are tied down into it, converting the dent into an extra-articular surface that cannot catch the rim. It costs a few degrees of external rotation and adds 20-30 minutes.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A tenodesis under tension on the back of the shoulder, loaded by every external rotation. It is a small cuff repair, and the patient will describe a posterior ache that outlasts the anterior pain by a few days. The posterior portal and the anchor sit in axillary nerve and suprascapular territory, both covered by an interscalene block.

  7. 7 Open Latarjet: deltopectoral approach and coracoid osteotomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A short deltopectoral incision, the conjoint tendon identified and the coracoid exposed by releasing pectoralis minor from its medial border and the coracoacromial ligament from its lateral. The coracoid is osteotomised at the knee with a saw or osteotome, with the conjoint tendon attached, and prepared with two drill holes and a decorticated undersurface. The musculocutaneous nerve enters the conjoint tendon 3-8 cm below the tip and is at risk from traction on the graft, not from the saw.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The first real blood of the operation, from the coracoid and from the branches of the thoracoacromial artery along the pectoralis minor edge. Retraction on the conjoint tendon with the coracoid attached is traction on the musculocutaneous nerve; the surgeon knows and keeps it gentle. A patient in a light plane will tense the pectoralis and the surgeon will say so. Deepen rather than relax, because the subscapularis split that follows is easier to judge in a muscle with tone.

  8. 8 Subscapularis split, glenoid neck preparation and graft fixation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The subscapularis is split horizontally at the junction of its upper two-thirds and lower third, in the muscular portion rather than at the tendon, which is the step that protects the axillary nerve running across its inferior edge. The capsule beneath is opened vertically, the anterior-inferior glenoid neck is cleared of soft tissue and decorticated to bleeding bone, and the coracoid is laid flat on it, flush with the glenoid face, and fixed with two screws (or suture buttons in some techniques). A graft that sits proud makes arthritis; one that sits medial does not stabilise. The capsule is then repaired to the native glenoid, leaving the graft extra-articular.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is where the Latarjet earns its pain score: an osteotomised bone screwed onto a decorticated bed through a muscle that is being levered open with retractors in the axillary nerve’s territory, with the arm in external rotation to open the split. The glenoid neck bleeds from cancellous bone and the subscapularis from muscle; this is the 200 mL of the operation. Levers under the subscapularis sit on the axillary nerve and the posterior lever on the plexus; a 90-minute Latarjet in a muscular athlete produces plenty of retractor force. The axillary vessels and the plexus are a finger’s breadth medial to the field, which is why surgeons are careful with the medial retractor and why arterial injury, though rare, is on the consent form.

  9. 9 Closure, sling and emergence

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The subscapularis split closes itself when the arm returns to neutral and is not sutured by most surgeons; the deltopectoral interval is closed in layers; the skin is a 5 cm scar. For the arthroscopic Bankart the portals are closed with a stitch or a strip. A sling goes on and stays for four to six weeks, with external rotation limited to protect the repair, and the patient is told not to reach behind.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Give the antiemetic. The sling before the patient lightens, because the first thing a young patient with a dead arm does is pick it up by the wrist and swing it, and the position that does it loads the repair. Emergence is unremarkable. Examine and document hand function and regimental badge sensation as far as the block allows and write down what could not be tested; the Latarjet is the shoulder operation most likely to produce a real musculocutaneous or axillary nerve problem, and the block makes it invisible for 16 hours.

  10. 10 Recovery, discharge and the first night at home

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The repair is protected by the sling and nothing else. The surgeon wants the patient home, elbow and wrist moving, and back at the first follow-up with a radiograph showing the graft in place.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The block lasts 12-24 hours with dexamethasone, 17 hours in the Latarjet trial, and the worst pain comes on average 14-15 hours after it was placed, which is 2-4 am for a morning list. The first regular paracetamol and NSAID dose goes in before the patient leaves recovery, the oral opioid is dispensed with a written instruction for the first two nights, and the patient and whoever is taking them home are told that the arm coming back to life brings a sharp rise in pain over an hour that is treated by the regimen, not a reason to attend the emergency department. Pre-emptive opioid at six hours did not help in the one trial that tested it; regular non-opioid analgesia started on time and a rescue dose taken at the first tingle is the plan. A dense block is not a reason to keep the patient in. For a Latarjet done as a day case, surgeons vary, and the honest default in a patient without home support is one night.

Pain plan

The arthroscopic Bankart is capsule and small bone and runs a point below the trajectory on this card at every time; a single-shot interscalene or superior trunk block with intravenous dexamethasone, regular paracetamol and NSAID, and two nights of oral opioid is the defensible default and nothing more is needed. The remplissage adds a tenodesis under tension and a posterior ache that lasts a few days longer; it does not change the plan. There is no procedure-specific PROSPECT guideline; the rotator cuff repair guideline is the nearest and its skeleton applies.

The open Latarjet is the operation this card is scored for. The mechanism is bone (coracoid osteotomy, decorticated neck, screws) and muscle (a split and re-tensioned subscapularis, a conjoint tendon that now takes load with every abduction), which is severe for three days and then settles, because nothing is being loaded through healing tendon. That is a 4 with a quick fall, not a 5. The defensible default is a single-shot interscalene block with 15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%, intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, intravenous magnesium 50 mg/kg before induction, regular paracetamol and an NSAID started in recovery, and oral opioid for three nights, with a written plan. The author’s practice is that this is enough for a primary Latarjet in a counselled young patient, and that a catheter is the right call for the revision Latarjet, the bone block with iliac crest harvest (a second painful site that the interscalene block does not touch), the patient with a chronic pain history or opioid tolerance, and the patient who describes a catastrophic rebound after a previous shoulder operation. A catheter in a 24-year-old going home with a sling needs a fixation that will survive a weekend and a phone number that answers; the common failure is the catheter in the bed by the second morning.

The suprascapular plus axillary combination is adequate for the arthroscopic Bankart and wrong for the Latarjet: the anterior wound, the coracoid and the conjoint tendon sit in musculocutaneous and lateral pectoral territory that neither block covers.

Rebound is the enemy on this card more than on any other in the shoulder chapter. In the 972-patient ambulatory cohort the independent risk factors were bone surgery (OR 1.82), younger age, female sex (OR 1.52) and no intravenous dexamethasone (OR 1.78); a Latarjet in a 22-year-old has the first two before you start and the fourth is your choice. Escalation: pain above 7 after the block has gone with the hand warm, the fingers moving and the sling on is treated with the charted opioid and a check that the regular doses were taken; pain with a tense, swelling deltopectoral wound in the first hours is a haematoma and the surgeon’s problem; weakness of elbow flexion or numbness of the lateral forearm once the block has receded is the musculocutaneous nerve and needs documenting and the surgeon told the same day.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Arthroscopic versus open Latarjet. Arthroscopic Latarjet has a long learning curve, longer operating times (110-170 minutes in a recent series), a conversion rate to open of a few per cent in early hands, and the same nerves at risk from a different direction. The pain is somewhat less; the block choice is the same.
  • Free bone block. Iliac crest autograft brings a second wound in a territory no shoulder block covers; it hurts as much as the shoulder for the first days and is the strongest argument for a catheter or at least local infiltration at the crest. Distal tibial allograft avoids the harvest.
  • Posterior labral repair and posterior bone block. Lateral decubitus more often, the posterior portals in axillary and suprascapular territory (both covered by interscalene), and a sling in neutral rather than internal rotation; the same capsule and small bone mechanism.
  • Capsular shift for multidirectional instability. Plication of a lax capsule without a labral tear, usually in a hyperlax young woman; capsule mechanism, settles in days, and a population with a higher rebound and anxiety profile than the collision athlete.
  • Revision surgery. A failed Bankart going to Latarjet, or a failed Latarjet going to a bone block, is scar, retained metal, more bleeding and a longer case; the catheter earns its place.
  • The nerves. Interscalene block makes the musculocutaneous and axillary nerves untestable for 16 hours after the one shoulder operation that genuinely injures them. Document before, document what could not be tested after, and examine the next morning.
  • Beach chair in the young hypotensive patient. A resting systolic of 100 and a cuff systolic of 75 in the chair is the brainstem pressure the literature’s strokes happened at; youth is not protection, and the short case tempts people to skip the vasopressor infusion.
  • Epilepsy. Seizure-related dislocations are an indication for the Latarjet and the patient will dislocate again with the next seizure; anticonvulsant timing and a plan for a seizure in recovery are part of the anaesthetic.
  • Pain catastrophising and kinesiophobia. In Bankart patients followed beyond two years, pain intensity tracked with unhelpful thinking rather than with bone loss or the operation; the pre-operative conversation about what the pain will be, and that it is not the repair failing, is analgesia.

Key sources

The surgical evidence is strong on recurrence and weak on pain: the meta-analyses compare Bankart, remplissage and Latarjet on redislocation and return to sport and mention pain only as a late VAS score. The Latarjet complication rate of 30% is pooled from level IV series across four decades and overstates modern open practice, where a single-surgeon series of 164 cases had no clinical musculocutaneous or axillary deficit. The one analgesic trial specific to the Latarjet is the magnesium study, small and single-centre but randomised and blinded. The block evidence is extrapolated from the PROSPECT rotator cuff repair guideline. The pain scores and the catheter advice are the author’s calibration against the Part B scale and the mechanism.
