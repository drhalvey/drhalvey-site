102 Cerebral palsy: single-event multilevel surgery and hip reconstruction

Bilateral muscle-tendon lengthening, tendon transfer, femoral and tibial derotation osteotomy and foot stabilisation in the ambulant child of 6-14 years, and adductor release, femoral varus derotation osteotomy and pelvic osteotomy for the displaced hip of the non-ambulant child of 3-16 years

5Severe and prolonged

Dominant mechanism: Muscle. Six to sixteen separate procedures in one sitting: spastic muscles lengthened or transferred at hip, knee and ankle, femur and often pelvis or tibia cut and plated, both sides. The dominant and prolonged driver is spasm: a spastic muscle responds to pain by contracting across the fresh osteotomy and the lengthened tendon, which produces more pain. Bone and capsule run underneath; sciatic stretch from newly straightened knees adds a neuropathic element in some children. Spasm is re-provoked by handling, casts and physiotherapy well beyond the first week, which is what earns a 5 on a muscle mechanism

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Block
General anaesthesia with a lumbar epidural infusion for 3-4 days: ropivacaine 0.1-0.2% or bupivacaine 0.1-0.125% at 0.2-0.4 mg/kg/h (max 0.4 mg/kg/h) with clonidine added to deliver 0.1-0.2 microg/kg/h, plus regular diazepam for spasm, continued baclofen, paracetamol and an NSAID
Catheter: routine Bilateral multilevel surgery and bilateral hip reconstruction are the strongest indication for an epidural catheter in children's orthopaedics: both legs, several dermatomes, and spasm that clonidine and local anaesthetic damp at the cord. Run it 72-96 h, with the rate reviewed at 48-72 h and reduced if it has sat at the ceiling. For unilateral hip reconstruction a lumbar plexus or suprainguinal fascia iliaca catheter is an equal choice
Alternatives: Lumbar plexus or suprainguinal fascia iliaca block or catheter (ropivacaine 0.2% 0.5 mL/kg, max 30 mL, then 0.05-0.15 mL/kg/h) for unilateral hip reconstruction, with shorter stay in retrospective series; Bilateral sciatic and femoral catheters where the back is fused or instrumented, within a total of 0.3 mg/kg/h; Morphine nurse-controlled analgesia (bolus 10-20 microg/kg, background 0-10 microg/kg/h) with ketamine 0.1-0.2 mg/kg/h and regular diazepam where no regional technique is possible; Epidural fentanyl 1-2 microg/mL in place of clonidine: equal analgesia and spasm control, more vomiting and desaturation
Position
Supine on a radiolucent table with both legs and the pelvis prepared free; some surgeons turn the child prone for hamstring, rectus and calf surgery or for femoral derotation; fixed contractures and a windswept pelvis mean every limb is padded where it lies, not where the table expects it
Tourniquet
Sometimes. Thigh or calf tourniquets for the below-knee components (calf lengthening, tibial osteotomy, foot stabilisation), 150-250 mmHg; none for hip and femoral work
Cement
No
Blood loss
10-25 mL/kg for bilateral femoral osteotomies with soft-tissue surgery; more with pelvic osteotomies. In hip reconstruction, transfusion in about one child in five overall, rising from under 10% with one or two osteotomies to a third with three and over half with four
Duration
3-4 h for unilateral hip reconstruction; 4-8 h for bilateral hip reconstruction or full multilevel surgery, often with two surgical teams
Length of stay
5-7 nights; the first night in a high dependency area for the non-ambulant child with respiratory or seizure comorbidity. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 30 mg/kg IV (max 2 g) within 60 min of incision, repeated every 4 h
Tranexamic acid
Tranexamic acid 15 mg/kg IV (max 1 g) before incision then 5 mg/kg/h whenever two or more osteotomies are planned; reduced measured bleeding in one small randomised trial, with a meta-analysis finding the evidence insufficient for transfusion
VTE
Mechanical prophylaxis where the legs allow; chemoprophylaxis only for the post-pubertal adolescent with added risk factors, timed around the epidural
Antiemetic
Ondansetron 0.15 mg/kg IV (max 4 mg) and dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV (max 8 mg); vomiting in a child with reflux, no fundoplication and poor airway reflexes is an aspiration risk, so prophylaxis is not optional
Tell the patient
This is a long operation on several muscles and bones at once so that there is one recovery, not five; the first three or four days are the hardest, mostly because of muscle spasms, which can return with movement for some weeks, and an epidural and spasm medicines are used to keep those under control.
The trap
Pain in this child presents as spasm, and spasm is treated at the cord and the muscle, not with more opioid: a child who cannot report pain, whose legs jump under the casts every few minutes, and who is given morphine boluses until the respiratory rate falls has been sedated, not treated. Chart the diazepam regularly from the first evening, keep the baclofen going, and score pain with a tool the carers have individualised

What the surgeon is doing and why

Cerebral palsy is a static brain injury with a progressive musculoskeletal consequence. Spastic muscle does not grow as fast as the bone it spans, so with each growth spurt the muscle-tendon units become relatively shorter, the joints they cross are pulled into flexion, and the bones, which model to the forces on them, grow twisted: the femur stays anteverted, the tibia rotates outwards, the foot collapses or inverts. Two populations come to surgery and the operations look similar on the list and differ in purpose.

The ambulant child (GMFCS I-III, typically spastic diplegia, aged 6-14 years) walks in crouch or on tiptoe with knees knocking. Gait analysis identifies every level at which a short muscle or a rotated bone is costing energy, and the surgeon corrects all of them at once so that the child has one rehabilitation: psoas lengthened over the pelvic brim, adductors released, hamstrings lengthened, rectus femoris transferred or released, the calf lengthened at the gastrocnemius aponeurosis, the femur and tibia cut and derotated, the foot stabilised. That is single-event multilevel surgery: 6-16 procedures, both legs, 4-8 hours.

The non-ambulant child (GMFCS IV-V, spastic quadriplegia, aged 3-16 years) has hips that are being pulled out of joint by adductors and psoas, against abductors that are weak and a socket that is shallow because nobody has walked on it. A displaced hip becomes painful and makes sitting and hygiene impossible. Reconstruction removes the deforming force (adductor and psoas release), points the femoral head back into the socket by a varus derotation osteotomy with 1-2 cm of shortening, and, if the socket is deficient, levers its roof down over the head with a Dega-type pelvic osteotomy. It is often bilateral.

Pain logic: this is a 5, and not because any single component is extreme. A femoral osteotomy in a typical child is a 3-4. Here it is bilateral, at several levels, and performed in a nervous system whose response to nociception is a reflex contraction of the very muscles that have just been cut, across bones that have just been cut. Spasm drives pain and pain drives spasm for days. The plan therefore has to act at three sites: the afferent signal (epidural local anaesthetic), the cord reflex (clonidine, baclofen, diazepam) and the tissue (paracetamol and an NSAID).

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Assessment with the carer in the room

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgical plan is fixed weeks ahead from gait analysis or hip surveillance radiographs; what varies is the child.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The carer knows how this child communicates, shows pain and takes medicine: ask, and write the pain behaviours on the chart, because the revised FLACC score works best when individualised that way. Do not infer intellect from speech or movement. Work through the list. Seizures: type, frequency, last one, and every anticonvulsant given on the morning (valproate lowers platelet count and function, so check a platelet count before an epidural; enzyme inducers shorten the action of rocuronium and vecuronium). Reflux and swallowing: gastrostomy, fundoplication, drooling, chest infections in the last year. Respiratory reserve: scoliosis, night-time oximetry, home suction. Spasticity treatment: oral baclofen dose, and whether there is an intrathecal baclofen pump, its last refill and alarm dates. Latex: these children have had many operations and some have shunts, so a history of reaction to gloves or balloons is asked for and a latex-free set-up used where there is doubt. Weight is often well below centile for age; dose everything on the measured weight.

  2. 2 Premedication and induction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A frightened child with spasticity becomes rigid, which makes cannulation, positioning and mask ventilation harder.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Usual medicines on the morning with a sip or through the gastrostomy, including baclofen and anticonvulsants. Midazolam 0.5 mg/kg orally (max 15-20 mg) or clonidine 4 microg/kg orally works, in smaller doses for the hypotonic child with poor airway control. Veins are small and fragile and limbs contracted; an inhalational induction with the carer present, then cannulation, is often the kindest route, with a modified rapid sequence for active reflux. Anaesthetic requirement is lower than in other children, more so with anticonvulsants, so titrate with processed EEG and expect a slow emergence. Suxamethonium is not contraindicated in cerebral palsy. Tracheal tube for every case on this card; glycopyrronium 5 microg/kg IV for secretions.

  3. 3 Lines, epidural, position and heat

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeons need both legs and the pelvis free for hours, and often an image intensifier from each side.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Two cannulae, an arterial line for bilateral osteotomies or the frail child, a urinary catheter and a temperature probe. Site the epidural at L2-4 after induction, lateral, before the hips are fixed in plaster or abduction. With an intrathecal baclofen pump the spinal catheter usually enters at L2-4 and tunnels round the flank: check the radiograph or use the image intensifier, and go above or below it. In a series of 44 children with pumps, image-guided epidurals caused no pump problems. These children arrive cold and cool quickly: little fat, impaired vasomotor control, a preparation from umbilicus to toes. Forced-air warming over the chest and arms, underbody warming, warmed fluids and a warm theatre from the start. Contractures decide the position: pad every limb as it lies, do not force a windswept pelvis flat, and remember the bone is osteopenic and breaks at a careless transfer.

  4. 4 Hip: adductor and psoas lengthening

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Through a groin-crease incision adductor longus and gracilis are divided and brevis partly released, protecting the anterior branch of the obturator nerve; psoas tendon is divided at the pelvic brim (leaving iliacus) in the walker, or at the lesser trochanter in the non-walker.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Short and not bloody. If the surgeon is judging muscle tone or testing for the obturator nerve, relaxant is avoided. Some surgeons inject botulinum toxin into the lengthened muscles here to blunt spasm over the following weeks; it needs no change from you.

  5. 5 Femoral osteotomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A lateral incision, vastus lateralis lifted, and the femur cut below the trochanter. In hip reconstruction the shaft is tilted into varus, derotated and shortened, then fixed with a blade plate or locking plate, so the head points into the socket without tension. In the walker a derotation without varus corrects in-toeing.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The main source of blood loss: marrow, periosteum and muscle, 5-10 mL/kg per femur, some of it hidden in the thigh. Tranexamic acid 15 mg/kg IV (max 1 g) should have gone in before incision. Keep a running total and a haemoglobin at each hour and after each osteotomy, because the transfusion rate is set by the number of bones cut. Cefazolin is repeated at 4 h.

  6. 6 Pelvic osteotomy and capsulorrhaphy in the displaced hip

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Through an anterior incision the outer table of the ilium is exposed and a curved cut made above the acetabulum, stopping at the triradiate cartilage; the roof is levered down and held with wedges of bone, usually the piece removed from the femur. The stretched capsule may be opened, the head reduced and the capsule tightened.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cancellous iliac bone bleeds steadily and cannot be sealed. With three osteotomies a third of children are transfused and with four over half: have blood in the room for bilateral femoral plus pelvic surgery, and at about 20% of blood volume lost with surgery continuing measure the haemoglobin; in the stable child transfuse packed cells 10-15 mL/kg at 70 g/L, sooner if the bleeding is brisk or the child is unstable. This is also the point where the epidural earns its place intra-operatively; a bolus of ropivacaine 0.2% 0.2-0.3 mL/kg before it blunts the response.

  7. 7 Knee: hamstrings and rectus femoris

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Medial hamstrings are lengthened through a posteromedial incision to let the knee extend; rectus femoris is released or transferred to a hamstring to stop it holding the knee stiff in swing. Fixed flexion beyond about 20 degrees may need a distal femoral extension osteotomy or guided growth.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The sciatic nerve is shorter than it looks. When a knee that has been flexed for years is straightened on the table and held in a cast or splint, the nerve is stretched, and the result is burning foot pain or weakness that appears on day 2-5. Surgeons limit the correction and splint in slight flexion; you can make sure the epidural is dilute enough for the child to move the toes and report or show dysaesthesia, and treat early with gabapentin 5-10 mg/kg (max 300 mg per dose) up to three times daily.

  8. 8 Calf, tibia and foot

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Gastrocnemius recession for equinus (never an over-lengthened Achilles, which produces crouch), supramalleolar tibial derotation, and lateral column lengthening or subtalar fusion to restore the foot as a lever.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Tourniquets go on for this segment. Deflation in a small, cold, vasodilated child with an epidural drops the pressure; give volume before release. Below-knee casts are applied at the end of each side, and from now on the heels cannot be seen.

  9. 9 The second side and the running totals

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    One rehabilitation requires both legs corrected at one sitting, and two teams working simultaneously halve the time.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    At the changeover review four numbers: temperature (act below 36 degrees C), blood loss against the allowable figure calculated at the start, time since the last antibiotic and tranexamic acid dose, and time since the last anticonvulsant dose if any is due, which can be given intravenously or through the gastrostomy. Glucose is checked in the small or ketogenic-diet child. A child who has been still and uncovered for six hours also has pressure areas at the occiput, sacrum and elbows.

  10. 10 Casts, splints and emergence

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Below-knee casts or broomstick casts with an abduction bar, knee immobilisers, sometimes a spica after hip reconstruction in a small child.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Load the epidural, start the infusion before waking, and give the first diazepam dose (0.05-0.1 mg/kg IV, max 5 mg) before emergence, since the first spasm on waking sets the pattern. Extubate awake and suctioned, upright where the casts allow; slow emergence is expected and is not a reason to reverse sedation that is doing a job. Bring the carer into recovery as soon as the airway is secure.

  11. 11 Days 0 to 4

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeons want comfortable positioning, early sitting and no pressure sores; rehabilitation starts in the first week.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Epidural for 72-96 h, with the rate reviewed at 48-72 h, then a planned step-down. Diazepam 0.05-0.1 mg/kg (max 5 mg) orally or by gastrostomy every 6 h regularly for 48-72 h, then as required and weaned over one to two weeks. Baclofen continued at the home dose. Paracetamol 15 mg/kg every 6 h (max 1 g, max 60 mg/kg/day) and ibuprofen 10 mg/kg every 8 h (max 400 mg) if renal function and platelets allow. Opioid in small doses for breakthrough, with oximetry. Score pain with the individualised revised FLACC every 2-4 h. Bladder catheter until the epidural is out, laxatives from day 1, heels floated off the bed.

Pain plan

The mechanism is muscle spasm amplifying bone, capsule and tendon pain on both sides for days, so the catheter decision is the easiest in this book: an epidural, routinely, for 72-96 hours. The trial evidence concerns what to put in it. Fifty children randomised to clonidine or fentanyl added to epidural bupivacaine had the same low diazepam use, the same spasm rates and the same pain scores; fentanyl caused repeated vomiting in a third and more desaturation. In a population with reflux, poor airway reflexes and limited respiratory reserve that settles it: clonidine, added to ropivacaine 0.1-0.2% or bupivacaine 0.1-0.125% so that an infusion run at 0.2-0.4 mg/kg/h of local anaesthetic delivers 0.1-0.2 microg/kg/h of clonidine, accepting some sedation and a lower blood pressure. Epidural baclofen added nothing in a retrospective comparison and lowered the blood pressure.

Unilateral hip reconstruction is different. An epidural beat high-volume infiltration decisively in a small trial (pain 0.7 against 4.8 at four hours), but two retrospective series found that fascia iliaca or lumbar plexus blocks gave equal or lower pain scores, less opioid and a shorter stay than epidurals. These are confounded by era and pathway, yet the signal is plausible: one leg, no urinary catheter, earlier sitting. For one hip the author’s practice is a lumbar plexus or suprainguinal fascia iliaca catheter; for two hips or full multilevel surgery, the epidural. With an intrathecal baclofen pump in place the epidural remains usable under image guidance and gave better analgesia than plexus block in the one series that looked.

Spasm is treated in its own right. Regular diazepam from the first evening, not as required; baclofen never interrupted; clonidine in the epidural or, without one, 1-2 microg/kg orally every 6-8 h. Opioids do not stop spasm and in doses that appear to, they have stopped breathing instead.

Rebound comes on the day the epidural stops. Step down deliberately: halve the rate for six hours, give oral oxycodone 0.1 mg/kg (max 5 mg) or morphine 0.2 mg/kg (max 10 mg) before the catheter is removed, keep the diazepam regular for another day. Escalation triggers: pain in one heel or over a malleolus under a cast is a pressure sore until the cast is windowed; burning foot pain with allodynia after knee straightening is sciatic stretch, treated by flexing the knee 20 degrees and starting gabapentin; a sudden rise in tone with fever, itch and agitation in a child with a pump is baclofen withdrawal and an emergency.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Intrathecal baclofen pumps. Keep the infusion running, know the refill date, keep diathermy plates away from it. Withdrawal (rebound spasticity, hyperthermia, itch, seizures, rhabdomyolysis) looks like sepsis or malignant hyperthermia and is treated with high-dose oral or intravenous benzodiazepine and restoration of delivery. Overdose looks like a slow emergence with hypotonia.
  • Seizures in recovery. Missed doses, hypocarbia, tramadol and pethidine all lower the threshold; avoid the last two.
  • Reflux and the chest. The non-ambulant child aspirates silently. Head-up nursing, suction, physiotherapy from day 1, and a low threshold for high dependency care after 6-hour bilateral surgery.
  • Hypothermia. Predictable and largely preventable; it worsens bleeding from cut bone and delays waking.
  • Hidden blood loss. Thighs and pelvis conceal several mL/kg; the haemoglobin on the morning of day 1 is lower than the one at closure.
  • Pain that is not surgical. Constipation, urinary retention after catheter removal, reflux, a gastrostomy that has been pulled, and hunger all present as the same distress score.
  • Neuropathic pain. Reported in a series of six children after multilevel surgery; it should be mentioned at consent and looked for when pain worsens after day 3.
  • Staged or single-level surgery. Adductor release alone in a 3-year-old, or a calf recession, is a day-case score of 2 with a caudal or infiltration; this card’s plan is not for those.
  • Hip salvage. For the painful, long-dislocated hip in an adolescent the options are femoral head resection or valgus osteotomy: bloodier and more painful than reconstruction, with the same plan.
  • Later spinal fusion. Many of these children come back for the operation on the neuromuscular scoliosis card, where a previous epidural record and pump details save time.

Key sources

This is one of the few paediatric orthopaedic operations with a randomised trial of the epidural mixture, and its result (clonidine for tolerability, not for better spasm control) is the basis of the default. Comparisons of epidural with plexus and fascia iliaca techniques are retrospective and point both ways depending on whether a pump was present and whether surgery was bilateral. Transfusion risk by osteotomy count comes from a single 180-patient cohort. Tranexamic acid has one small positive trial for measured bleeding and a meta-analysis that could not show a transfusion benefit; its routine use here is the author’s judgement by analogy with spine surgery. The regular-diazepam regimen is long-standing practice without a trial. General paediatric points are in the child having orthopaedic surgery.
