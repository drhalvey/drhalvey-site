75 Small joint arthroplasty and fusion of the hand; rheumatoid hand surgery

Silicone metacarpophalangeal and proximal interphalangeal arthroplasty, interphalangeal and thumb fusion, synovectomy, ulnar head excision and extensor tendon reconstruction

3Moderate

Dominant mechanism: Bone. Metacarpal heads sawn off and the medullary canals of metacarpal and phalanx broached for a silicone hinge, usually four in a row; or the two surfaces of a finger joint cut to cancellous bone and compressed with a screw or wires. Small bone with thin cover and dense innervation. Soft tissue rebalancing, synovectomy and a tendon transfer add a capsule and muscle component, and the patient brings chronic joint pain and long-term analgesic use to the baseline

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: moderate.

Block
Axillary or infraclavicular brachial plexus block with intravenous dexamethasone as the sole anaesthetic, with light sedation; the airway is left alone
Catheter: rarely A single shot with dexamethasone and regular oral analgesia is the defensible default; a continuous infraclavicular catheter is reserved for the opioid-tolerant patient or for a surgeon who starts dynamic splint therapy in hospital on day 1-2
Alternatives: Supraclavicular block, accepting a phrenic palsy in some, which matters in rheumatoid lung disease; Wrist or digital block for a single distal interphalangeal or thumb fusion; WALANT for metacarpophalangeal arthroplasty with intrinsic transfer in units that set tension awake; General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway or videolaryngoscopic intubation in neutral, with a block for analgesia, when the patient cannot lie flat and still
Position
Supine, arm abducted on a hand table with the shoulder and elbow in whatever range they allow; the patient positions their own neck and other joints before sedation, with a pillow shaped to the neck and padding under every bony point
Tourniquet
Yes. Upper arm tourniquet at 250 mmHg (lower in a thin arm) over generous padding: steroid-thinned skin shears under a cuff and under adhesive drapes
Cement
No. Silicone hinges are uncemented spacers; a surface replacement interphalangeal implant may be cemented with a few millilitres, of no systemic consequence
Blood loss
Negligible under tourniquet
Duration
20-40 min for a single interphalangeal or thumb fusion; 90-150 min for four metacarpophalangeal arthroplasties with soft tissue rebalancing; similar for a dorsal wrist synovectomy with ulnar head excision and tendon transfers
Length of stay
Day case for a single joint; one night for multi-joint surgery in a patient who lives alone or whose other hand does not work. Day case: selected
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV before tourniquet inflation; an implant in an immunosuppressed patient is the reason it is not omitted
Tranexamic acid
Not needed
VTE
None for a day case; mechanical for the inpatient with limited mobility
Antiemetic
Dexamethasone 8 mg IV is given for the block in a patient not already on a glucocorticoid and is reasonable in one who is; ondansetron 4 mg IV if opioid or general anaesthesia is used
Tell the patient
The arm will be numb until the evening and you can stay awake or doze, without a breathing tube; the knuckles ache for the first two or three days, and you will keep taking your usual arthritis tablets unless your rheumatologist has told you to pause one of them.
The trap
The hand is the easy part: the dangerous joint is the atlantoaxial one, and the safest management of an unstable rheumatoid neck is a block that makes laryngoscopy unnecessary, with the patient placing their own head before any sedation

What the surgeon is doing and why

Rheumatoid synovitis destroys a joint from the soft tissue inwards. The synovium swells, stretches the capsule and collateral ligaments, erodes the bone at their attachments and invades the tendons that cross the joint. Once the restraints have gone the tendons no longer pull through the centre of the joint, and the deformity they produce is predictable: at the metacarpophalangeal joints the extensor tendons slip into the ulnar gutters and the fingers drift ulnarwards and sublux volarly; at the wrist the ulnar head dislocates dorsally and saws through the extensor tendons of the little and ring fingers; in the fingers the imbalance produces swan neck and boutonniere deformities. Biologic drugs have made all of this much less common, so the patient who still comes to surgery tends to have long-standing, multi-system disease. Osteoarthritis sends a different, fitter population for the same small-joint operations, mostly at the interphalangeal joints.

There are three kinds of procedure, often combined in one sitting.

  • Replace a joint whose movement matters. The metacarpophalangeal joints are the usual target: through a transverse dorsal incision each joint is opened, the synovium cleared, the metacarpal head resected, the ulnar intrinsic tendon released, the canals of metacarpal and proximal phalanx broached, and a one-piece silicone hinge inserted. The implant is a spacer; the result depends on what the surgeon does next, which is to reef the radial sagittal band and centralise the extensor tendon, sometimes transferring the released ulnar intrinsic to the neighbouring finger. In the multicentre cohort function, appearance and drift improved for years while strength did not, and 9.5% of implants fractured. Proximal interphalangeal joints are replaced with silicone or surface implants in the ulnar fingers where grip needs flexion.
  • Fuse a joint whose stability matters. The thumb metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joints, the distal interphalangeal joints and the index proximal interphalangeal joint, which must resist pinch. The surfaces are cut to cancellous bone in the chosen angle and held with a headless compression screw, tension band or crossed wires.
  • Rescue the tendons. Dorsal wrist synovectomy, excision of the dislocated ulnar head (the Darrach procedure) or fusion of the distal radioulnar joint with a pseudarthrosis proximal to it, and reconstruction of ruptured extensors by side-to-side suture or tendon transfer.

The order across the limb is proximal before distal and painful before deformed, so the wrist is usually stabilised first (wrist fusion), and the surgeon operates on one hand at a time because the patient needs the other.

The pain logic: four metacarpal heads resected and eight canals broached is bone, in a row, under thin dorsal skin, and it behaves like other small-bone surgery with a day 1 peak that surprises people. Small-joint arthrodesis ranked among the most painful operations in the 179-procedure cohort. This is a 3. Soft tissue work adds a capsule and muscle component, and dynamic splinting from the first week reloads the reconstruction daily. Against that, these patients are older, which protects against rebound, and are already taking regular analgesics.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Pre-operative assessment: the patient, not the hand

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon has assessed the hand. What can hurt the patient is elsewhere.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cervical spine first. Atlantoaxial subluxation, subaxial steps and cranial settling are features of long-standing erosive disease and can be silent; neck pain radiating to the occiput, hand clumsiness beyond what the joints explain, and electric shocks on flexion are the symptoms, and severe hand deformity itself is a marker of the erosive phenotype. Flexion-extension radiographs are reasonable before a planned general anaesthetic in a patient with symptoms or long-standing disease; if the plan is a block with the patient awake and self-positioned, the neck is protected by the patient and imaging changes nothing. Then the airway beyond the neck: temporomandibular opening, cricoarytenoid involvement (hoarseness, stridor). Then the systems: interstitial lung disease, pleural effusions, pericardial disease, anaemia, renal function before an NSAID is added to the one already taken.

    Medication: the hip and knee guideline of the American rheumatology and arthroplasty societies advises continuing methotrexate and the other conventional agents through surgery, withholding biologics so that surgery falls at the end of a dosing interval, withholding JAK inhibitors for at least three days, and restarting at about 14 days when the wound is healing. Hand surgeons vary in how strictly they apply this to small-joint surgery, and for a soft tissue procedure many continue everything. The plan should be the rheumatologist’s and surgeon’s, made weeks before, and your task is to know what was decided. Glucocorticoids: the same guideline advises giving the patient’s usual daily dose and not routine supraphysiological cover; for a tourniquet operation under a block that is clearly sufficient, with hydrocortisone 100 mg IV kept for unexplained hypotension.

  2. 2 Positioning by the patient

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs the hand flat and pronated on the table for the dorsal approach, which asks for shoulder abduction, internal rotation and elbow extension that a rheumatoid limb may not have.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Before any sedation, let the patient lie down, arrange their own pillow and neck, and place the arm; adjust the table to the arm and not the reverse. Pad heels, sacrum, elbows and the occiput; nodules and thin skin break down in an hour. Cannulation on the other hand or the forearm may be difficult through fragile veins and deformity: use a small cannula and a transparent dressing with minimal adhesive.

  3. 3 Block

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A dense block of the whole hand with tourniquet tolerance for up to two and a half hours.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Axillary or infraclavicular block with ropivacaine 0.5% 20-30 mL; both avoid the phrenic nerve, which matters when the lungs are fibrotic. The axillary approach needs abduction the shoulder may not allow, so the infraclavicular route is often the practical one. Dexamethasone 8 mg IV unless the diabetic or infection risk is a specific concern. Light sedation only: a patient who is deeply sedated loses the protection of their own neck muscles and may obstruct in a position you cannot easily correct.

  4. 4 Tourniquet and incision

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A bloodless field for small-joint work; a transverse incision across the metacarpal heads or a longitudinal one over a single joint, preserving the dorsal veins.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Antibiotic before inflation. Soft padding without creases under the cuff, and the lowest pressure that keeps the field dry. Note the time.

  5. 5 Synovectomy, release and bone resection

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Synovium is cleared, the ulnar intrinsic and collateral ligaments released, and the metacarpal head removed at the neck with a saw to make room and relax the contracted volar tissues. For a fusion the two surfaces are shaped as cup and cone or flat cuts.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Nothing to do under a working block. If the patient has chosen general anaesthesia, this and the next step are the stimulating part.

  6. 6 Canal preparation and implant or fixation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Metacarpal and phalangeal canals are opened with an awl and broaches to the largest size that fits; trial, then the definitive silicone hinge with a no-touch technique. A fusion is compressed with a screw or wires at the chosen angle.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Repeated for each joint: four joints is 60-90 min of this cycle. The mini image intensifier comes and goes. Wires left protruding from a fused fingertip mean a pin site and a later removal.

  7. 7 Soft tissue rebalancing

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The radial collateral ligament is reattached through a drill hole, the radial sagittal band reefed to hold the extensor tendon over the joint, and an intrinsic transfer added if drift is severe. This step decides whether the deformity recurs.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A surgeon who works wide awake will ask the patient to move here; that is a planned WALANT case with no tourniquet and no plexus block, and belongs to the tendon transfer card. Under a block the surgeon uses passive tenodesis.

  8. 8 Wrist-level procedures when combined

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Through a dorsal longitudinal incision: extensor tenosynovectomy, ulnar head excision with stabilisation of the stump, and side-to-side transfer of ruptured tendons.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Adds 45-60 min and takes the tourniquet towards two hours; agree the limit (the tourniquet). The ulnar head excision is the more painful part and is inside the block.

  9. 9 Closure, dressing and splint

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Skin is thin and closed without tension; a bulky dressing and volar slab hold the fingers extended and radially aligned. A dynamic outrigger splint follows within the week.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Tourniquet down, capillary return observed in every finger before the dressing: vasculitic or previously operated digits can be slow. Tape is kept off the skin where possible.

  10. 10 Recovery and going home

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Elevation, finger tips visible, therapy appointment booked.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The practical question is function, not physiology: a patient whose good hand is in a cast and whose other hand is also deformed may be unable to dress, toilet or open a blister pack. Chart analgesia the patient can physically take (liquid or dispersible forms, not child-resistant caps), and admit overnight when there is no one at home.

Pain plan

The mechanism is small bone under thin cover, so the plan is the standard one for bony hand surgery with three adjustments for the patient.

The block is the anaesthetic and covers the first 12-18 hours with dexamethasone. Regular paracetamol continues. The patient’s own NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor continues if renal function allows; do not add a second. Oral opioid for two to three days after multi-joint arthroplasty, in small doses: frail, older, sometimes on gabapentinoids already. A single distal fusion under a digital or wrist block needs simple analgesia only.

The adjustments. First, baseline analgesics and any long-term opioid are continued on the day and afterwards, and the discharge plan is written as additions to that baseline. Second, a patient on prednisolone 5-10 mg who has been given dexamethasone 8 mg will feel their other joints improve for a day and flare on day 2-3; warn them, since that pain is not from the operated hand. Third, where biologics have been paused, a disease flare in the second or third week is the cost, and it is managed by the rheumatologist, not with opioid.

No catheter by default; the pain has a 48-hour hump and the population is at low rebound risk. Author’s judgement, with no arthroplasty-specific trial.

Escalation: pain with a tight dressing and dusky fingers is the dressing. A hot, increasingly painful joint with a silicone implant in an immunosuppressed patient at day 4-10 is infection, presenting with fewer signs than expected, and goes back to the surgeon the same day.

Pitfalls and variants

  • General anaesthesia in the rheumatoid neck. If it cannot be avoided: neutral position maintained by an assistant, videolaryngoscopy or a supraglottic airway, a smaller tube for cricoarytenoid disease, and the head positioned by the patient beforehand with the pillow marked. Awake fibreoptic intubation is for known instability with cord signs, a patient who should probably be seeing a spinal surgeon first.
  • The other injection sites. A shoulder that will not abduct, an elbow replacement, a previous axillary clearance: the infraclavicular approach gets round most of them.
  • Osteoarthritis and post-traumatic cases. Distal and proximal interphalangeal fusion or arthroplasty in a patient without systemic disease: the same block, none of the medical content, a day case.
  • Psoriatic arthritis and lupus. The same operations; skin plaques over the block site in the first, and antiphospholipid antibodies, renal disease and a lower threshold for steroid cover in the second.
  • Bilateral or staged surgery. The second hand is done months later; the record of what worked matters.
  • Pyrocarbon or surface replacement implants in place of silicone: a longer, more exacting operation with the same anaesthetic.
  • Wound healing. Thin skin, steroids and withheld-then-restarted biologics; wound breakdown over an implant is the complication the surgeon fears, and it is the reason the drug timing is decided in advance.

Key sources

The medication advice is a consensus guideline written for hip and knee arthroplasty, graded by its authors as low-quality evidence, and applied to hand surgery by extrapolation; practice among hand surgeons varies. The outcome data for silicone arthroplasty come from one well-conducted prospective cohort. There is no trial of anaesthetic technique in rheumatoid hand surgery: the preference for a block as the sole anaesthetic is reasoning from the cervical spine and airway, and is the author’s practice. The cervical spine advice is consensus, and no number is given for the prevalence of instability because the figures predate modern drug treatment.
