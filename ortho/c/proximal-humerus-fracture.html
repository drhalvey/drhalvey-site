9 Proximal humerus fracture: ORIF versus reverse TSA

Locking plate fixation, fracture reverse arthroplasty, fracture hemiarthroplasty and the non-operative decision in the elderly

4Severe

Dominant mechanism: Bone. A fractured, osteoporotic metaphysis, days old, with its haematoma, either plated through a deltoid-splitting or deltopectoral approach with periosteal stripping and screws into soft bone, or resected and replaced with a cemented stem, with the tuberosities and their cuff attachments sutured back around the implant under tension. The fracture pain is already present before you start; the operation adds periosteum, a cemented canal and, for the reverse, a lengthened arm

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: moderate.

Block
Interscalene brachial plexus block, single shot, with intravenous dexamethasone, placed for the fracture pain before induction; a catheter where the unit can run one on a frail inpatient
Catheter: selective The population is the argument for a catheter and against it at once: a frail 80-year-old cannot tolerate opioid rescue and benefits most from a continuous block, and cannot tolerate a phrenic palsy from a running infusion if the chest is poor. The author's practice is a catheter for the reverse and the ORIF of a 3- or 4-part fracture in a patient with a good chest and an intact mind, and a single shot with a low-opioid oral regimen where the chest or the cognition rules it out
Alternatives: Superior trunk block (phrenic sparing, the sensible choice in the patient with COPD or a contralateral problem); Suprascapular plus axillary nerve blocks, weaker, for the patient who cannot have any phrenic involvement; Pre-operative single-shot block in the emergency department or the ward for the fracture pain while the decision is made
Position
Beach chair, 30-60 degrees, head fixed, arm free; some surgeons plate supine with a bump and the image intensifier across the table, which puts the C-arm where you would stand
Tourniquet
No
Cement
Sometimes. ORIF: none. Fracture hemiarthroplasty: the stem is cemented, because there is no metaphysis to press-fit into and the tuberosities need a fixed height to be sutured against. Fracture reverse: cemented in most series and press-fit in a growing minority; the pooled comparison finds the same function and fewer complications with cement; this is the one cement step in shoulder surgery that behaves like a hip
Blood loss
300-500 mL for a plate or a reverse through a deltopectoral approach in a fracture that has already bled into the arm; transfusion is a real possibility in the anaemic elderly patient and a group and hold is standard
Duration
ORIF 90-150 min; fracture reverse 90-150 min; fracture hemiarthroplasty 90-120 min; all longer than their elective equivalents because the anatomy is in pieces and the image intensifier is used
Length of stay
2-5 nights, set by the frailty and the mobilisation rather than the shoulder. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV within 60 min of incision (3 g over 120 kg); an arthroplasty in an elderly patient with a haematoma is the setting for a deep infection
Tranexamic acid
Tranexamic acid 1 g IV before incision; the elective shoulder arthroplasty data show less drain output and a smaller haemoglobin fall, and the fracture patient has already lost blood
VTE
Mechanical, plus chemoprophylaxis for the inpatient who is not mobilising, which is most of this population for the first days
Antiemetic
Dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction and ondansetron 4 mg IV; droperidol is reasonable; the elderly patient who vomits with a sling on is the one who aspirates
Tell the patient
The arm will be numb and heavy into the night; the shoulder then hurts deeply for several days, more than the fracture did, because the bone has been put back together or replaced, and the first week is about getting up and walking more than about the arm.
The trap
The cemented stem into an osteoporotic canal in an 80-year-old with renal impairment is the one cement moment in shoulder surgery that behaves like a hip: bone cement implantation syndrome is commoner in this patient than in any elective shoulder, severe cases triple the 30-day mortality, and the frailty that makes the operation marginal is the same frailty that makes the cement dangerous

What the surgeon is doing and why

A proximal humerus fracture in an older person is an osteoporotic fracture, the third commonest after the hip and the wrist, and it carries the same message about the patient as a hip fracture does: in 18,452 patients in the Swedish register, 1.7% were dead within 30 days and 7.8% within a year, five times the matched population in the first month, and the mortality tracked age, male sex, low-energy mechanism and other fractures rather than the fracture pattern. The shoulder is the injury; the frailty is the disease. The surgeon’s problem is that the head, the two tuberosities with the cuff attached, and the shaft have separated along the lines of the old growth plate into two, three or four parts in bone that does not hold screws, and that the blood supply to the head enters through the tuberosities and the calcar, so that a four-part fracture or a fracture-dislocation often has a head that will die whatever is done to it.

Three decisions, in order, and the evidence for each has moved in the last decade:

  1. Operate at all. PROFHER randomised 250 patients with displaced surgical neck fractures to surgery (fixation or hemiarthroplasty) or a sling and found no difference in the Oxford Shoulder Score over two years, in quality of life or in complications. Most two-part and many three-part fractures in the elderly are now treated in a sling. The Spanish trial that randomised 81 patients over 70 with three- and four-part fractures to a reverse arthroplasty or a sling found a Constant score advantage of about nine points for the reverse at one year, near the clinical threshold, with two major complications in the surgical arm and none in the sling arm; the pooled retrospective data say the same, slightly better pain and range for the reverse and a higher revision rate. In a patient with a Charlson index above 5, one retrospective series found surgery associated with a hazard ratio of nearly 7 for death against non-operative treatment in the same comorbidity band. The honest reading is that the sling is the default, surgery is for the fracture that will not function and the patient who will live to use it, and the decision is made by the geriatrician as much as the surgeon.
  2. Fix or replace. The DelPhi trial randomised 124 patients aged 65-85 with displaced three- and four-part fractures to a locking plate or a reverse arthroplasty and found a Constant score 13 points better after the reverse at two years, with the whole of the advantage in the four-part fractures and none of it in the three-part; the five-year report maintains it. The locking plate fails in osteoporotic bone by screw cut-out into the joint and by avascular necrosis of a head that was never going to survive, both of which lead to a second operation that is a reverse anyway. Fixation is for the younger patient, the valgus-impacted fracture with an intact medial hinge, and the three-part fracture in good bone.
  3. Which replacement. The hemiarthroplasty, the historical answer, depends entirely on the tuberosities healing back around the stem in the right place, which in osteoporotic bone they often do not, leaving a painless but useless shoulder; the reverse works without the cuff and the pooled comparisons give it better function with the same complications. The fracture hemiarthroplasty survives as an operation for the younger patient with a head-splitting fracture and good tuberosities, and in the hands of surgeons who still believe in it.

What the surgeon does to tissue. For the plate: a deltopectoral or a deltoid-splitting approach, the haematoma evacuated, the fragments reduced with sutures through the cuff insertions and the tuberosities, the head elevated out of impaction and the calcar supported, a locking plate laid laterally with screws into the head and the shaft, and the cuff sutures tied to the plate. For the reverse: the deltopectoral approach, the head removed (it is loose and comes out in the surgeon’s hand), the tuberosities tagged, the glenoid exposed and prepared as for an elective reverse, the stem cemented into a canal with no metaphysis above it at a height judged from the shaft and the contralateral arm, the reduction tensioned, and the tuberosities sutured back around the stem with heavy sutures, because a healed greater tuberosity gives the reverse its external rotation. For the hemiarthroplasty, the same, with an anatomic head on a cemented stem and the whole result riding on the tuberosity repair.

The pain logic. The patient arrives with a fracture that has hurt for days, a haematoma in the arm and a sling, and the operation adds to it. The dominant mechanism is bone: a fractured metaphysis manipulated, drilled and plated, or resected and replaced through a cemented canal. For the plate, periosteum (stripping and a lateral plate) and muscle (the deltoid split) are added; for the reverse, retension (a lengthened arm, as on the reverse TSA card) and the tuberosities sutured under tension. For the hemiarthroplasty the tuberosity repair is the operation and a cuff repair’s mechanism in miniature. That is a 4: worse than an elective replacement because the fracture pain is already there and the tissues were injured before the knife, and with a slower fall because the patient is old, moves little and is often confused by the opioid. The trajectory on this card is for the reverse and the plate; the hemiarthroplasty runs the same shape with a longer tendon tail.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 The decision, the delay and the block before anything else

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon’s decision is made from the CT, the patient’s age, hand dominance, cognition and living situation, and increasingly from a geriatrician’s assessment of what the patient will do with a reconstructed shoulder. The fracture does not need to be operated on tonight; it needs to be operated on within about two weeks, after which the tuberosities retract and the reverse becomes harder.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The delay is yours to use. This is a hip fracture patient with a different bone: anaemia, dehydration, a delirium already starting, anticoagulation, a chest, a heart that has not been assessed for years, and a pressure sore starting under the sling. A single-shot interscalene or superior trunk block in the emergency department or on the ward treats the fracture pain, takes the opioid out of a confused 85-year-old, and does no harm to the surgeon’s decision; it is the author’s practice to offer it on admission. Blood, group and hold, electrolytes, an ECG, a medication reconciliation and a conversation with the family about what the operation is for and what it is not are the pre-operative work, and the Swedish register’s 30-day mortality is the number that justifies the time. The catheter decision for the operation itself is made with the chest and the cognition in front of you (see the pain plan). Airway: a tracheal tube; the case is long, the patient is old and the head is behind a screen. Awake baseline pressure before any drug, from a patient who may be hypertensive from pain or hypotensive from dehydration, and neither number is the true baseline.

  2. 2 Beach chair in the frail patient

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Beach chair gives the surgeon the vertical humerus and the free arm, and the image intensifier can come in from the side for the plate. Some surgeons plate supine with a bump, with the C-arm across the table.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Every beach chair risk on the elective cards, in a patient with less reserve: the cuff overstates brain perfusion by about 1 mmHg per 1.25 cm between cuff and ear, this population has carotid disease and uncontrolled hypertension more often than any other on the shoulder list, and the strokes in the literature happened at cuff systolics that an 85-year-old with a block and a dehydrated circulation will reach on sitting up. An arterial line transduced at the ear is the honest monitor for the reverse in a frail patient and the author uses one; a vasopressor infusion started before the chair goes up, not after. Pressure areas on a patient who has been in a sling on a trolley for two days. The C-arm across the table for a plate puts the machine where you would stand and the radiation where you would not want it.

  3. 3 Antibiotic, tranexamic acid and the approach through a haematoma

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Deltopectoral for the arthroplasty and for most plates; a deltoid split from the acromion for the lateral plate in some hands, with the axillary nerve crossing the split 5-7 cm below the acromion and protected by a stay suture. The haematoma is evacuated and the fragments identified, which in a four-part fracture means finding the head loose in the joint and the tuberosities retracted under the deltoid.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cefazolin 2 g IV finished before the knife, 3 g over 120 kg; tranexamic acid 1 g IV at the same time. The haematoma evacuation is the first blood loss and it is old blood the patient has already lost; the surgical loss adds to it and the group and hold is for this case rather than the elective one. The deltoid split adds muscle to the pain mechanism. The fracture has been bleeding into the arm for days and a swollen, tense upper arm with a blocked, insensate limb is the setting for a compartment problem nobody will notice; look at the forearm and hand before the drapes go on.

  4. 4 Reduction and plate fixation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The tuberosities and the head are reduced with sutures through the cuff insertions used as handles, the head is elevated out of impaction and the medial calcar restored with a bone graft or a calcar screw, and a lateral locking plate is placed with multiple screws into the head, each one checked on the image intensifier to be sure it has not penetrated the joint. The cuff sutures are tied to the plate to hold the tuberosities.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Bone and periosteum under a deltoid retracted for 90 minutes. The image intensifier is used repeatedly and the table is tilted and the arm rotated for each view; each rotation of the arm is traction on a plexus that is already compressed by a haematoma. Screw penetration into the joint is the common failure and the surgeon’s repeated screening is the cost of avoiding it. In soft bone the surgeon’s language changes (“that one’s not biting”) and the case moves towards a replacement or a longer plate; be ready for the operation to change.

  5. 5 Head removal, glenoid and the cemented stem

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    For the arthroplasty the head is removed, the tuberosities tagged with sutures through the cuff, and the glenoid prepared as for an elective reverse. The stem goes into a canal that has no metaphysis above it, so its height is set from the shaft, a jig or the contralateral radiograph, and it is cemented in most series because there is nothing for a press-fit to grip and because the tuberosities need a stem that will not subside to be sutured against; the pooled comparison finds the same function with cement and press-fit and fewer complications with cement, and a growing minority of surgeons press-fit fracture-specific stems.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the trap. A cemented stem into an osteoporotic canal in a dehydrated, anaemic patient over 75 with renal impairment and ASA 3-4 status is the one cement step in shoulder surgery that behaves like a hip: in the largest series, any-grade bone cement implantation syndrome occurred in 16% of shoulder arthroplasties against 31% of hip hemiarthroplasties, severe cases carried a hazard ratio of 3.5 for death within 30 days, and the independent risk factors were exactly this patient. The precautions are the hip’s: the canal lavaged and dried, the patient filled and the pressure supported before the cement goes in, oxygen up, the surgeon told to avoid over-pressurisation and to vent, and your eyes on the end-tidal carbon dioxide and the pressure for the three minutes after the stem goes home. If the surgeon press-fits, the risk is a shaft fracture through soft bone instead.

  6. 6 Reduction, tensioning and the tuberosity repair

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The reverse is reduced and tensioned as on the elective card, with the height now a judgement rather than a measurement, and the tuberosities are brought around the stem and sutured to each other, to the stem and to the shaft with heavy sutures, often with the resected head as bone graft between them. A healed greater tuberosity gives the reverse its external rotation; a healed tuberosity pair is the whole result of a hemiarthroplasty.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The lengthened arm stretches the axillary nerve and the plexus as on the elective reverse, in a patient whose plexus has been sitting in a haematoma for a week; subclinical lesions were found on EMG in nearly half of elective reverses and the fracture setting is worse. Each trial reduction drops the pressure. No relaxant for the reduction, for the same reason as the elective card: a reduction that only goes in paralysed will dislocate on the ward. The tuberosity repair wants the arm held exactly where the surgeon puts it, in neutral rotation, for several minutes of suturing.

  7. 7 Closure, sling and emergence

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Layered closure, often a drain for the arthroplasty because the haematoma bed bleeds, and a sling with the arm in neutral rotation to protect the tuberosity repair for six weeks.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Give the antiemetic; an elderly patient who vomits in a sling aspirates. The sling on before the patient lightens. Emergence in a frail, cold, anaemic patient after two hours in beach chair is the moment to be sure the pressure is near the baseline and the haemoglobin is known. Document what sensation and movement can be tested; the block hides the axillary nerve until tomorrow and this is the shoulder operation where it is most likely to have been damaged.

  8. 8 The ward, delirium and the mobilisation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the tuberosities protected, a radiograph, and the patient out of bed; the geriatrician wants the delirium prevented and the bone protected for the next fracture.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The first regular paracetamol goes in before the patient leaves recovery; the NSAID is often unavailable because of the kidneys, which leaves paracetamol and a block as the whole non-opioid plan and is the argument for the catheter. The block recedes overnight and the elderly patient’s rebound is quieter than a young patient’s but is treated with an opioid that produces delirium; small doses of a short-acting opioid, no slow-release preparations, and a regular review. Day 1 is about standing, walking with the sling and the bowels, not the shoulder. Haemoglobin on day 1, because the fracture bled before the operation and the drain afterwards. A reverse patient who cannot lift the arm at a week has a block that has worn off, a stretched axillary nerve or a tuberosity that has pulled off, in that order of likelihood, and the surgeon’s radiograph decides.

  9. 9 The patient who is not operated on

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Most two-part and many three-part fractures in the elderly go into a sling for three weeks and then physiotherapy, on the PROFHER result, and some four-part fractures in the frailest patients are managed the same way on the ground that the operation would kill them.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    You will still meet them: for a block for the fracture pain, which is a reasonable request in a patient who cannot take opioids, and for the late reverse when the sling fails. A single-shot interscalene or superior trunk block for the pain of a fresh fracture is a kindness that lasts a day and does not change the surgical options; a catheter on a ward for a non-operative fracture is not standard practice and the author does not offer it. The late reverse for a malunion or a nonunion is a longer, harder operation through scar with retracted tuberosities, and sits with the revision reverses on the elective card.

Pain plan

The mechanism is bone (a fractured metaphysis plated or replaced through a cemented canal) with periosteum and muscle added for the plate and retension and tuberosity tension added for the arthroplasty, on top of fracture pain that is already present, in a patient who is old, often confused, usually unable to take an NSAID and intolerant of opioid. The plan is therefore the elective reverse plan with the oral regimen weaker and the block more important. There is no PROSPECT guideline for proximal humerus fracture; the rotator cuff repair guideline’s skeleton applies and the hip fracture guideline’s logic (block early, opioid-spare, mobilise) applies more.

The defensible default is an interscalene block with 15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%, or a superior trunk block with the same volume where the chest cannot afford a hemidiaphragm (complete paresis in 5% rather than 73%, and this population has more chests that cannot), with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, regular paracetamol, an NSAID only where the kidneys permit, and small doses of a short-acting oral opioid with no slow-release preparation. The block goes in before induction, and ideally before the operation day, on the ward, for the fracture pain.

The catheter is the decision on this card, and the population argues both ways. For: the oral regimen is paracetamol alone in many of these patients, the opioid rescue produces delirium, the pain is a 4 and the deltoid is loaded from day 1 after a reverse. Against: a running interscalene infusion is a running phrenic palsy in a patient who may have a poor chest and no reserve, a confused patient pulls catheters out, and the elective trials show that the infusion’s advantage over a single shot shrinks once dexamethasone and a full oral regimen are in place, which this patient cannot have. The author’s practice is a catheter for the reverse and for the three- or four-part ORIF in a patient with a good chest and an intact mind, run at a low rate for 48 hours with the physiotherapy timed to it, and a single shot with the ward warned where the chest or the cognition rules it out. A superior trunk catheter is the compromise some units use for the poor chest; the evidence for it is extrapolated from the single-shot comparison.

Escalation: pain above 7 after the block has receded, with the hand warm and moving and the sling on, is treated with a small dose of short-acting opioid and a check that paracetamol was given; agitation in a patient who cannot say where it hurts is pain until proven otherwise, and a confused patient with a swollen arm has a haematoma or a compartment problem that the block has hidden; a sudden fall in pressure or saturation at the cement is BCIS and is treated as such before it is called anything else; a deltoid that does not work on day 2 is the axillary nerve or a tuberosity failure and the surgeon is told.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Non-operative is the default. PROFHER for the two-part, and increasingly for the three-part; the sling patient still needs a block for the fracture pain and a geriatrician for the rest.
  • Plate versus reverse. DelPhi favours the reverse for the four-part fracture in the elderly and finds no advantage in the three-part; the plate fails by cut-out and avascular necrosis in soft bone and the second operation is a reverse through scar.
  • The fracture hemiarthroplasty. The historical operation, still done by some surgeons for the younger patient with a head-splitting fracture; a cemented stem with the same BCIS moment, and the whole result riding on a tuberosity repair that is a cuff repair in miniature, which lengthens the tail of the pain curve.
  • Cemented versus press-fit stem. The pooled fracture data show the same function with fewer complications for cement; the cost is the BCIS moment in the frail patient, and the choice is the surgeon’s and the bone’s. Whichever it is, the precautions are decided before the canal is opened.
  • Fracture-dislocation. The head is out of the joint, often for days, with the axillary nerve and the vessels stretched over it; the reduction is harder, the nerve injury rate higher, and the block makes the pre-operative examination the last chance to document what was already damaged.
  • The deltoid-splitting approach. Less dissection, a direct line to the lateral plate, muscle added to the pain mechanism, and an axillary nerve crossing the split that the surgeon protects with a suture.
  • Delay. Two weeks is the surgeon’s window before the tuberosities retract; the first days of it are yours for the anaemia, the dehydration, the anticoagulation and the chest, and the register mortality figures are the argument for using them.
  • Anticoagulation. A third of this population is on something; the block timing and the operation timing are set by it, and a fracture that has already bled into the arm is the setting for a haematoma under a plate.
  • Delirium. The commonest complication of the first week, driven by pain, opioid, sleep loss and a strange ward; the block is the single intervention that addresses the first two.
  • Renal impairment. Takes away the NSAID and raises the BCIS risk; the plan becomes paracetamol and a block, and the catheter case strengthens.
  • Beach chair in the hypertensive, carotid-diseased, dehydrated elderly patient. The arterial line at the ear and the vasopressor infusion before the chair goes up are the defences; a cuff systolic of 90 in this patient is not a number to accept.

Key sources

The surgical evidence is unusually good for an orthopaedic decision: PROFHER, DelPhi and the Spanish reverse-or-nothing trial are randomised and address the three decisions in order, and the pooled data on cemented versus press-fit stems and reverse versus hemiarthroplasty are large. The mortality and frailty data come from a national register and a retrospective comorbidity series. The BCIS figures are from the largest series to score the syndrome across arthroplasty types, and the nerve lesion data are from elective reverse arthroplasty and extrapolated to the fracture setting. There is no analgesic trial in this population; the block and catheter advice is the elective reverse card’s reasoning applied to a frailer patient and is the author’s judgement.
