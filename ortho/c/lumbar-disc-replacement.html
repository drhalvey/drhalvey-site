89 Lumbar disc replacement

Lumbar total disc arthroplasty through an anterior retroperitoneal approach, one or two levels, and hybrid constructs with an anterior fusion at the adjacent level

3Moderate

Dominant mechanism: Visceral. A lower abdominal wall incision with the rectus retracted, the peritoneal sac swept off the retroperitoneum and the iliac vessels held aside for an hour; then a collapsed disc space jacked open, which stretches the annulus, the facet capsules and the roots; the posterior muscles are never touched and nothing is fused, so the patient is moving through the segment from day 1

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: low.

Block
Bilateral transversus abdominis plane or rectus sheath blocks (or surgeon infiltration of the sheath and skin with long-acting local anaesthetic), regular paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, and oral opioid as rescue
Catheter: rarely A single-shot wall block covers the first night, which is the only period in which the wall dominates; a catheter is for the opioid-tolerant patient or the two-level hybrid, and is then a rectus sheath catheter placed by the access surgeon at closure, not an epidural
Alternatives: Quadratus lumborum block on the left for a paramedian or oblique incision; Intrathecal morphine 0.1-0.15 mg before induction where the ward can monitor it; Low-dose ketamine infusion for the patient on long-term opioid, who is common in this population
Position
Supine on a radiolucent table, lumbar spine gently extended over a bolster or the table break, arms out on boards or across the chest clear of the fluoroscopy arc, the pelvis exactly level because the implant is placed to the millimetre on a true anteroposterior view; slight Trendelenburg to drop the bowel away
Tourniquet
No
Cement
No
Blood loss
50-300 mL for an uncomplicated level; a torn iliac or iliolumbar vein produces litres in minutes, and the published range for the mini-open anterior approach runs to 3,000 mL, so two large cannulae and blood available are the default
Duration
90-150 min for one level; 150-240 min for two levels or a hybrid
Length of stay
2-3 nights; one night in selected single-level cases. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV within 60 min of incision (3 g over 120 kg); an implant, so repeat at 4 h
Tranexamic acid
Tranexamic acid 1 g IV before incision is widely used and reasonable; the expected loss is small and the benefit is insurance against venous ooze, not evidence from this operation
VTE
Mechanical from induction; low molecular weight heparin from the first evening or day 1, because the left common iliac vein has been compressed under a retractor and no spinal canal has been opened
Antiemetic
Dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction and ondansetron 4 mg IV before emergence; ileus is the gut's response to retroperitoneal retraction and opioid makes it worse
Tell the patient
The disc is replaced from the front through a cut low on the tummy, so the back muscles are left alone; expect a sore abdomen for a few days, a bloated feeling until the bowel wakes up, and a left foot that feels warmer than the right.
The trap
The bleeding in this operation is venous, sudden and at L4-5: the left common iliac vein tears during retraction or when the implant is impacted, the person who can repair it is the access surgeon, and they must still be in the building when the trial implants are going in

What the surgeon is doing and why

A degenerate lumbar disc that is believed to be the source of chronic axial back pain can be removed and the segment fused, or removed and replaced with a bearing that still moves. The argument for the bearing is mechanical: a fused segment transfers its motion to the neighbours, and the hope is that preserving motion protects them. The Cochrane review of seven randomised trials is the honest summary of what has been shown: against fusion, disc replacement gave back pain and disability scores at two years that were statistically better and clinically no different (5.2 mm on a 100 mm pain scale, 4.27 Oswestry points), in a highly selected population, and the claim about adjacent segments was never tested. So the patient in front of you is young, selected, usually has a single degenerate level at L4-5 or L5-S1 with healthy facets, and has often been on analgesics for years.

The operation is an anterior one for a reason. The disc occupies the front of the column, the implant is wider than any posterior window would admit, and the facets and posterior muscles must be left intact because they are what will control the new bearing. The exposure is the same as for an anterior fusion (anterior and lateral lumbar fusion): a low transverse or left paramedian incision, the rectus retracted, the peritoneal sac swept off the abdominal wall and the psoas to the right, the left ureter lifted with it, and the great vessels mobilised off the front of the spine. At L5-S1 the disc lies in the fork below the aortic bifurcation and the median sacral vessels are divided. At L4-5 the left common iliac vein and artery lie across the disc and must be retracted to the right after the iliolumbar vein is ligated, and that is where the bleeding comes from.

Three things then differ from a fusion, and each has an anaesthetic consequence.

  1. The discectomy is complete, back to the posterior longitudinal ligament and out to both uncinate margins, because the bearing needs a balanced, mobile space. That means more time with the vessels under retractors.
  2. The implant must sit exactly in the midline and far enough posterior for its centre of rotation to match the facets. The surgeon checks this on a true anteroposterior image, so the patient’s pelvis must not rotate and the table must not be tilted to help the exposure.
  3. The endplates are cut for a keel or spiked for teeth and the implant is impacted between vertebrae that are being held apart by a distractor. The impaction is the moment a retractor slips or a tethered vein tears.

The pain logic: the back muscles are untouched and no bone graft is harvested, so the mechanism is wall and viscera (incision, rectus retraction, peritoneal traction, ileus) with a second component from distraction, which stretches facet capsules and nerve roots that have adapted to a collapsed disc for years. The wall is covered by a fascial plane block. The distraction ache and leg dysaesthesia are not, and are what the patient reports on day 2.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Pre-operative planning and the brief

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The access surgeon (vascular or general) reads the imaging for the level of the bifurcation, calcified or adherent vessels and previous abdominal surgery. In 246 consecutive exposures, Modic type 2 endplate change and any smoking history each doubled the odds of vessels stuck to the annulus.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Take the same history: smoker, previous abdominal or retroperitoneal surgery, previous anterior spine surgery, radiotherapy. Any of them moves the case from a small-bleed operation to one where a crossmatch is ready. Two large cannulae, a group and screen as a minimum and blood available for L4-5, and an arterial line for a revision or a hostile abdomen. Ask at the brief how long the access surgeon is staying; the implant impaction is a vascular step.

  2. 2 Induction, lines and monitoring

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs a soft abdominal wall, an empty bladder and stomach, and a patient who will not move while a retractor blade is holding the iliac vein.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    General anaesthesia with a tracheal tube and full neuromuscular blockade, monitored and maintained through the exposure. Orogastric tube to decompress the stomach and a urinary catheter. Put a pulse oximeter probe on the left great toe as well as a finger: the retractor compresses the left common iliac artery, and loss of the toe trace is the earliest sign of occlusion or thrombosis, which the vascular literature recommends monitoring routinely. Neuromonitoring is not usually used, so there is no restriction on volatile or relaxant.

  3. 3 Positioning and fluoroscopy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Extension opens the front of the disc space; a level pelvis makes the anteroposterior image true so the implant can be centred.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Arms out on boards or folded across the chest high enough to clear the lateral image. Extension over a bolster in a patient with a stiff, painful spine is done after induction and released before emergence. Check that the image intensifier can pass under the table in both planes before draping; a lateral image blocked by an arm board costs retractor time later.

  4. 4 Retroperitoneal exposure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The peritoneal sac is swept intact off the left abdominal wall and across the midline, taking the ureter with it, so that bowel never enters the field. A table-mounted retractor system then holds four blades around the disc.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Peritoneal traction produces bradycardia and occasionally asystole; the response is to ask for the traction to stop, then an anticholinergic. A peritoneal breach (2-16% of retroperitoneal exposures in the pooled series) is repaired and matters only because bowel then crowds the field. Keep the patient fully relaxed: a cough with blades on the vein is how it tears. Fluid restraint is sensible, since a wet gut contributes to ileus.

  5. 5 Vessel mobilisation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    At L4-5 the iliolumbar and ascending lumbar veins are ligated so the left common iliac vein can roll to the right without avulsing them. At L5-S1 the median sacral vessels are divided in the fork. Blunt dissection is preferred over diathermy on the front of the disc because the superior hypogastric plexus lies there and its injury causes retrograde ejaculation.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the step to be watching. In the systematic review of the mini-open approach, venous injury occurred in 0.8-4.3% and arterial injury in 0.4-4.3%. Venous bleeding from a common iliac tear is fast, wells up from the bottom of a deep hole and is controlled by pressure with sponge sticks above and below while the access surgeon gets sutures in. During that minute the venous return from the left leg and sometimes the cava is being compressed, so the pressure falls more than the measured loss predicts and rises again when the packs come off. Call for blood early, give it through the upper limb lines, and do not chase the pressure with volume into the leg veins. Air embolism through an open iliac vein in Trendelenburg is rare and real.

  6. 6 Discectomy and distraction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The whole nucleus and the cartilaginous endplates are removed and the space is progressively opened with a distractor until the annulus and posterior ligament are taut and the foramina are restored.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The longest quiet period. The retractors stay on the vessels throughout; watch the toe trace and tell the surgeon if it flattens, because a few minutes’ release usually restores it. Bleeding now is from epidural veins behind the posterior ligament and from the endplates: low-volume and persistent. Normothermia and a normal mean pressure matter more than any drug.

  7. 7 Endplate preparation, trial and implant impaction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Trials set the footprint, height and lordosis under fluoroscopy; a keel slot is chiselled if the design needs one; the implant is driven in between the distracted endplates and its position confirmed in two planes.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Impaction jolts the whole patient and the retractor frame. If the patient has been allowed to lighten or the relaxant to wear off, this is when they move. It is also the second vascular moment: a blade shifts, a tethered vein tears, or the vein has been stretched across an implant that is taller than the disc it replaced. Be at the head with blood in the room, not signing out.

  8. 8 Retractor release, inspection and closure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Blades are removed one at a time while the access surgeon watches the vessels refill and checks for bleeding and for a pulse in the left iliac artery; the peritoneal sac falls back and the rectus sheath is closed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Reperfusion of the left leg after an hour of partial compression is usually silent. Confirm the toe trace has returned and feel the foot before the drapes come off; a cold, pulseless left foot is an iliac artery thrombosis and is treated before the patient leaves theatre. The wall block or the surgeon’s sheath infiltration goes in now. Reverse the relaxant fully.

  9. 9 Emergence and the first night

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The bearing is stable in compression from the moment it is seated; the surgeon wants the patient walking the next morning and avoiding extension.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A smooth emergence without coughing against a fresh abdominal closure. Expect a left leg that is warmer and pinker than the right: the lumbar sympathetic chain runs along the edge of the exposure and is stretched or divided, the patient reads it as a cold right leg, and it needs explanation, not investigation. Ileus for 24-72 hours is common (14.8% in one series with a transperitoneal exposure, about 3% in pooled retroperitoneal data) and is the reason to limit opioid from the start. Deep vein thrombosis in the left leg is the delayed complication of the retracted vein.

Pain plan

The mechanism is wall and viscera with a distraction component, so the plan is a wall block for the first night and a regimen that keeps opioid low enough for the bowel to restart. The defensible default is bilateral transversus abdominis plane or rectus sheath blocks with 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.375% a side after closure (or generous sheath infiltration by the surgeon), regular paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor from the first evening since there is no fusion to protect, and oral opioid as rescue. There are no procedure-specific trials of wall blocks for disc replacement; the recommendation is author’s judgement extrapolated from abdominal surgery and from the anterior fusion card. Intrathecal morphine 0.1-0.15 mg is a reasonable alternative that covers the visceral component better than a wall block does, at the cost of pruritus, nausea in a patient already prone to ileus, and overnight monitoring.

A catheter is rarely justified. The wall pain is over in 48 hours, and the pain that persists (deep midline ache and bilateral buttock or leg dysaesthesia from distraction) is outside any wall block’s territory. For the patient on long-term opioid, which describes many candidates for this operation, add an intra-operative ketamine infusion and continue the baseline opioid on the day (see the opioid-tolerant patient); a rectus sheath catheter placed at closure is the regional escalation if one is wanted.

Escalation: abdominal distension with rising pain and tachycardia on day 1-2 is ileus first, retroperitoneal haematoma second, and a missed bowel or ureteric injury a distant third. New leg pain in a root distribution after a taller implant is root stretch and usually settles; new weakness needs imaging for a posteriorly displaced implant or fragment.

Pitfalls and variants

  • L4-5 against L5-S1. L5-S1 below the bifurcation is the easier exposure with the hypogastric plexus most at risk. L4-5 needs the iliac vein moved and carries nearly all the major bleeding.
  • Two-level and hybrid constructs. A disc replacement at L4-5 over a fusion cage at L5-S1 is common: longer retraction, both vascular zones, the same pain plan. NSAIDs are then the surgeon’s decision because one level is expected to fuse.
  • Revision anterior surgery. The vessels are encased in scar. Ureteric stents, a vascular surgeon scrubbed from the start, an arterial line, rapid infusion capacity and blood in the room are the standard, and some surgeons choose a posterior fusion over the implant precisely to avoid this.
  • Obesity. A deeper hole, longer blades, more retraction pressure on the vessels, and more difficult fluoroscopy.
  • Retrograde ejaculation. A sympathetic plexus injury that presents weeks later; it is a consent item that the patient may raise with you because it was mentioned alongside the anaesthetic.
  • Implant migration or subsidence. Early anterior migration is an emergency revision through a scarred field with the vein now adherent to the implant; treat it as a vascular case.
  • The patient told this is a small operation. It is a short admission with a real abdominal wound; the first 24 hours are an abdominal operation’s, not a keyhole one’s.

Key sources

The effectiveness evidence is the Cochrane review and it is lukewarm. The vascular complication rates come from a systematic review of retrospective series with wide ranges, and from single-centre cohorts with dedicated access surgeons whose results are the best case. The predictors of adherent vessels come from one prospective Australian cohort. There is no procedure-specific analgesic trial; the pain score of 3 matches the anterior fusion card because the approach, and therefore the mechanism, is identical.
