What the surgeon is doing and why

A spinal metastasis matters to the surgeon for two reasons only: it is pressing on the cord or the roots, or it has eaten enough of the vertebra that the spine is unstable. Pain alone is a radiotherapy and oncology problem. The operation on this card is never a cancer operation; the surgeon is buying walking, continence and the absence of mechanical pain, and the randomised evidence says the purchase is worth making. In the defining trial, 101 patients with metastatic cord compression were randomised to direct decompressive surgery followed by radiotherapy or to radiotherapy alone: 84% against 57% could walk after treatment, those who could walk kept walking for a median of 122 days against 13, and 62% against 19% of the patients who arrived unable to walk regained it.

Two ideas run the decision. The Spine Instability Neoplastic Score grades location, mechanical pain on loading, lytic or blastic bone, alignment, body collapse and posterolateral involvement; a high score is a surgical referral regardless of neurology. The second is separation. Stereotactic radiotherapy can control almost any tumour if there are a few millimetres between tumour and cord, so the surgeon no longer attempts an oncological resection: the operation is a laminectomy, removal of the epidural tumour to create that margin around the dura, and pedicle screws two levels above and two below to hold a spine that tumour and laminectomy have made unstable, with radiotherapy within a fortnight. An irradiated, steroid-treated patient will not fuse, so the construct carries the load alone for the patient’s remaining life.

Steroids come first. Dexamethasone reduces the vasogenic oedema in the compressed cord and buys time: in the one randomised trial, 57 patients given a 96 mg bolus and three days of high-dose oral steroid were ambulant after treatment in 81% of cases against 63% without, and at six months in 59% against 33%, with significant side effects in about one in ten. Most units now use 16 mg a day; the patient has been on it for days by the time they reach you, and it continues through the operation.

The other half of the card is vertebral augmentation. Vertebroplasty injects polymethylmethacrylate through a transpedicular needle into a fractured body; balloon kyphoplasty first inflates a balloon to create a cavity and partly restore height, then fills it with thicker cement at lower pressure. A fracture that does not move does not hurt. For osteoporotic fractures the Cochrane pooling of five sham-controlled trials found vertebroplasty improved pain by 0.7 of 10 points at one month, below any minimal important difference, with no benefit in disability or quality of life. For pathological fractures in cancer the fracture is part of a lytic process that will not heal, and in the CAFE trial 134 cancer patients randomised to balloon kyphoplasty or non-surgical care saw their Roland-Morris disability score fall from 17.6 to 9.1 at one month with kyphoplasty and not move without it. That trial was unblinded and industry funded; one of its 70 kyphoplasty patients had an intra-operative myocardial infarction attributed to the anaesthetic.

The pain logic separates the two procedures and then rejoins them. The open decompression is a posterior midline exposure with bilateral stripping over four or five vertebrae, a laminectomy and screws through four pedicles: the tissue injury of an instrumented fusion, which sits among the most painful operations in the 179-procedure cohort. Three things pull the score down to a 3: the mechanical pain generator, a collapsing vertebra that hurts on every movement, is removed by the construct; the patient arrives on dexamethasone and usually a baseline opioid, so the surgical pain is layered on a system already running; and the percutaneous screw variant leaves the muscle unstripped. Augmentation is bone-mechanism, brief and often net analgesic by recovery. An open five-level stripping in a patient with no mechanical pain beforehand behaves as a 4 for two days, and the plan assumes it.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 The call: urgency, steroids, neurology and the decision

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Cord compression is measured in hours. The surgeon wants the patient decompressed before a progressing deficit becomes complete; a dense paraplegia of more than a day or two rarely recovers. Prognosis under three months argues for radiotherapy alone.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The case jumps the queue and is done within 24 h of a new deficit. Dexamethasone is charted as a continuing drug, not stopped for fasting. Examine and record the neurology yourself (level, power by myotome, sensation, bladder): it is the baseline for every later check. Then the oncology history: the primary (the hypervascular ones and myeloma are under pitfalls), recent chemotherapy (neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, anthracycline cardiomyopathy, bleomycin lung), immunotherapy (adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, pneumonitis), bevacizumab (impaired healing and bleeding), a calcium, a sodium, a haemoglobin, platelets, clotting, and the anticoagulant most of these patients take for a previous thrombosis. Crossmatch two to four units for a hypervascular primary, two for the rest. The goals-of-care conversation happens before the anaesthetic and is written down.

  2. 2 Pre-operative embolisation for the vascular metastasis

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A renal cell, thyroid, hepatocellular or melanoma metastasis has an arterial supply that can bleed litres when entered, and the decompression enters it. Interventional radiology can embolise the segmental feeders within 24-48 h of surgery.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The evidence is thinner than the practice. In the one randomised trial, 45 patients lost a mean of 618 mL with embolisation and 735 mL without, not significantly different, with no difference in transfusion; the operation was shorter (median 90 against 124 min), and in the hypervascular subgroup the reduction was significant (645 against 902 mL). The author’s reading: worth the delay for a renal or thyroid primary, not for the rest. The embolised patient arrives with a femoral puncture site, a contrast load in a kidney that may already be poor, and a small risk of cord ischaemia if the artery of Adamkiewicz shared a feeder, so repeat the neurological examination after it.

  3. 3 Induction, lines, monitoring and the turn with an unstable spine

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A prone, immobile, warm patient with the abdomen free and a clear fluoroscopy arc, turned without moving a spine that may be held together by soft tissue. Motor evoked potentials, if used, are baselined before the turn.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Reinforced tracheal tube, bite block if potentials are planned, an arterial line before induction for a vascular primary, a poor heart or any deficit, two large cannulae, a urinary catheter, warming and a rapid infuser primed for the hypervascular case. Blood in the room, not the fridge. Cell salvage in malignancy is accepted by many units with a leucocyte depletion filter and refused by others; settle it before the day. Rocuronium at intubation, reversed with sugammadex once the exposure is done, if potentials are followed. The turn: log roll with the neck neutral, a collar for any cervical or upper thoracic lesion, abdomen free so the epidural veins drain, arms below 90 degrees, eyes checked after the turn and again after the fluoroscopy arm has swung. Keep the mean pressure at or above the awake level through the turn: the compressed cord has lost autoregulation and a 60 mmHg mean during the roll is an ischaemic insult you cannot see.

  4. 4 Posterior exposure and pedicle screws two levels above and below

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A midline incision, paraspinal muscle stripped two levels above and two below; some surgeons place percutaneous screws through a short midline window to cut the stripping and the bleeding. Screws go in before the laminectomy so that the spine is held while the decompression destabilises it further, and the construct is long because the bone is poor and the screws carry the load for life. In osteoporotic or irradiated bone the surgeon uses fenestrated screws and injects a few millilitres of cement down each.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The first bleeding, steady rather than dramatic, from stripped muscle in a patient on steroids; a tumour that has broken through the posterior elements is entered now and the bleeding changes character at once. Tranexamic acid, if chosen, is in before the knife. Cement-augmented screws are a cement step without the hip’s physiology: small volumes, no pressurised canal, but a direct route into the basivertebral and epidural veins and from there to the lungs. A fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide with a rise in airway pressure during screw cementation is pulmonary cement embolism, handled as in step 10, and the surgeon stops injecting.

  5. 5 Laminectomy and circumferential epidural decompression

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Lamina and spinous process at the lesion are removed, then the epidural tumour is taken off the dura all round, including the ventral space reached through the pedicle; a full corpectomy is reserved for the collapsed body that cannot be reconstructed otherwise. This is separation surgery: a few millimetres for the radiotherapy to work in.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The bleed of the case. Epidural tumour is vascular, the plexus is engorged by any abdominal pressure, and tumour in the body has no vessels to clip; haemostasis is packing, haemostatic agents and cement. Keep the mean pressure up through this step, not down: the author’s practice is a mean of 80 mmHg or above while the cord is handled, extrapolated from the spinal cord injury literature rather than any trial in metastatic disease. A drop in motor evoked potentials during the ventral decompression is the cord being retracted or made ischaemic: tell the surgeon, raise the pressure, check the haemoglobin and the temperature, and wait. Dural tears are commoner than in degenerative surgery because tumour is adherent to dura.

  6. 6 Reconstruction, rods and the decision not to fuse

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    After a corpectomy the gap is filled with an expandable cage or with cement poured into a mould, and the rods are locked to the screws. Decortication and graft are usually omitted: radiotherapy follows within two weeks and the biology will not fuse.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cement into a corpectomy defect is poured, not pressurised, and a cement reaction here is a case report. This is the quiet part of the case: catch up on blood and warmth, and measure the coagulopathy a two-litre loss has produced rather than assume it.

  7. 7 Closure, emergence and the first night

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Layered closure over a drain in a wound that will be irradiated within a fortnight, with steroid and often chemotherapy working against it; wound breakdown is the commonest complication and the one that delays radiotherapy. The surgeon wants an immediate neurological check and the patient sitting up within a day.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Reverse fully and wake the patient to a neurological examination in theatre against the step 1 baseline; a new deficit is a haematoma or a displaced construct and goes back at once. Dexamethasone continues and is tapered by the oncologists over weeks, not by the ward on day 2; hyperglycaemia gets insulin, not a stopped steroid. The baseline opioid restarts on the day of surgery as a regular drug. Mechanical prophylaxis from induction and low molecular weight heparin from day 1 once the surgeon is content. Stay is a week or more and the destination is as often rehabilitation or palliative care as home; involve those teams on day 1.

  8. 8 Vertebral augmentation: the patient and the decision

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A fractured body that hurts on loading, in an osteoporotic patient who has failed weeks of conservative care or a cancer patient whose lytic fracture will not heal, with a posterior wall intact enough that cement will stay in the body. Kyphoplasty is chosen where height matters or the posterior wall is doubtful, because the balloon cavity takes cement at lower pressure; vertebroplasty is faster and cheaper.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The procedure is small; the patient is not. The osteoporotic fracture patient is in their eighties, has had weeks of immobility and often a chest, and now has to lie prone for 45-90 min. Sedation or a general anaesthetic is decided on whether the patient can lie prone awake, keep still for the one painful moment (cement injection) and protect their own airway face-down with a sedative on board. The author’s practice is a general anaesthetic with a tracheal tube for more than one level, any patient who cannot lie prone comfortably on the trolley beforehand, any difficult airway and the cancer patient with a long comorbidity list; sedation for the single-level fracture in a patient who demonstrates the position first.

  9. 9 Prone positioning and transpedicular access under fluoroscopy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Prone on bolsters with the fracture extended slightly to open the anterior column. Biplanar fluoroscopy guides a trocar through the pedicle into the anterior third of the body, one or both sides, without breaching the medial pedicle wall and the canal.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The turn is the painful part for the awake patient and is done with their help before the sedation, not after it. Under sedation: nasal oxygen with capnography, the airway visible to you in the prone pillow, and an agreed signal for pain. The operator infiltrates skin, muscle and the periosteum of the pedicle entry; the periosteal injection is the one that matters and the one that is rushed. A trocar through the medial pedicle wall presents as sudden radicular pain in the awake patient and as nothing at all in the anaesthetised one, which is the strongest argument for keeping the single-level patient awake.

  10. 10 Balloon inflation and cement injection

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    For kyphoplasty, a balloon is inflated under fluoroscopy to create a cavity and lift the endplate, then removed, and viscous cement is injected into the cavity. For vertebroplasty, thinner cement goes directly into the trabecular bone under continuous screening, and the operator stops when it reaches the posterior quarter of the body or appears outside it. Leak is the complication the technique is designed around: into the disc (common, harmless), the paravertebral veins and from there the lungs, or the canal and foramina.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cement injection is the painful moment and the dangerous one. Leak rates depend on how hard anyone looks: 39% after vertebroplasty and 29% after kyphoplasty in the comparative studies, none producing neural compromise, pulmonary embolism or emergency decompression; 20.7% in the metastatic series (28.6% with CT), with pulmonary cement embolism in about 1% and symptomatic complications of any kind in 3.4%; and pulmonary cement embolism in 3.5-23% of osteoporotic augmentations depending on whether the lungs were imaged. The one that matters presents in the room: a fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide, a drop in saturation, hypotension and tachycardia during or minutes after the injection, the picture of any pulmonary embolus. Tell the operator to stop, give oxygen, support the pressure, turn the patient supine if it does not settle, and get an echocardiogram; symptomatic or central emboli are anticoagulated as thrombotic emboli and cement in the heart is a cardiothoracic problem. New radicular pain during injection in the awake patient is cement in the foramen and the injection stops; under general anaesthesia it is found at the wake-up, so the neurological check happens before the patient leaves the room.

  11. 11 Recovery and discharge after augmentation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The fracture is fixed the moment the cement sets; the operator wants the patient sitting, walking and home the same day or the next.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Relief is immediate when it comes: a patient who could not sit beforehand often sits up in recovery. Pain worse than before, new radicular pain or new weakness is a leak or a canal haematoma and needs imaging. The opioid the patient came in on is reduced over days rather than continued unchanged, because the pain generator has gone and an elderly patient on the same dose will be obtunded by day 2. Day case for the single-level fracture with support at home; one night for the cancer patient and anyone who needed a general anaesthetic for the position.

Pain plan

Two procedures, two plans. For the open decompression the mechanism is muscle stripping with a laminectomy and screws in a patient already on steroid and opioid. The regional options that serve a degenerative fusion are mostly closed: an epidural catheter through a space with tumour in it, under a planned radiotherapy field, with steroid thinning the skin and often a clotting screen that would not pass, is not a technique the author would place. Intrathecal morphine 0.1 mg is possible when the puncture level is clear of tumour on the MRI, the clotting is normal and the ward can monitor it, which is a minority of these patients. The defensible default is systemic: a low-dose ketamine infusion from induction to closure (the opioid-dependent spine trial model of 0.5 mg/kg at induction and 10 microg/kg/min, which cut opioid use at 48 h and pain at six weeks in this phenotype), regular paracetamol, an NSAID only where kidneys, platelets and the oncologist allow (which excludes myeloma and most with a raised creatinine), the baseline opioid continued as a regular drug from the day of surgery, and an immediate-release opioid charted as rescue at a dose scaled to that baseline (see the opioid-tolerant chapter). Bilateral erector spinae plane blocks before the turn are the author’s adjunct for the open exposure, on the general spine literature rather than any trial in metastatic disease; they do not touch the epidural space or mask a deficit. Surgeon infiltration at closure is asked for by name.

For augmentation, the pain is the trocar through the periosteum and the cement injection, covered by the operator’s local anaesthetic or the general anaesthetic, then a fracture that no longer moves: regular paracetamol, the opioid the patient was already taking reduced as the fracture pain goes, and no new drugs.

Rebound is not a feature of either plan, because nothing long-acting is wearing off. Escalation: rising back pain in the first 24 h after a decompression with new leg symptoms or retention is an epidural haematoma until imaged; pain after augmentation that is worse than the pre-operative pain is a leak. Both go to the surgeon, not to the rescue dose.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The hypervascular primary. Renal cell, thyroid, hepatocellular and melanoma metastases bleed from the moment they are entered and do not stop with pressure. Know the primary before the day, ask about embolisation, and have an arterial line, a rapid infuser, blood in the room and an agreement with the surgeon on the loss that shortens the operation.
  • Myeloma. Renal impairment, hypercalcaemia, hyperviscosity, anaemia, a bleeding tendency from paraproteins and thrombocytopenia, and no NSAIDs.
  • The cervical metastasis. An unstable neck in a collar, a possible anterior approach with a retropharyngeal haematoma risk, and an airway that may be secured awake and left in overnight; the posterior cervical fusion and ACDF cards carry the detail.
  • Anterior and lateral approaches. A corpectomy through a thoracotomy or the retroperitoneum brings a double-lumen tube, a chest drain and a visceral pain component; rarer now that separation surgery exists, still done for a solitary metastasis with a long prognosis.
  • The patient on immunotherapy. Checkpoint inhibitors cause adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism and pneumonitis, any of which can present as an unexplained collapse under anaesthesia; the augmentation patient on no steroid may need hydrocortisone.
  • Wound breakdown and infection. Weeks of dexamethasone, thin skin and a wound in a radiotherapy field: the infection rate is several times that of degenerative fusion and prophylaxis is not where the difference is made.
  • Vertebroplasty under sedation in the patient who cannot lie prone. The sedative dose creeps up, the airway is face-down, and the capnograph is the only early warning. Convert to a general anaesthetic beforehand, not halfway through the cement.
  • Cement where it should not be. Into the disc (harmless), the paravertebral veins (watch the capnograph), the canal (a laminectomy at once), the heart (a case report). Injection technique and viscosity are the prevention; your contribution is to notice.
  • The goals-of-care conversation. A patient with weeks to live or a complete paraplegia of several days may be better served by radiotherapy and analgesia, and the anaesthetist is often the person who notices the question has not been asked.

Key sources

The surgical evidence is one trial, stopped early, and it is enough; the steroid evidence is one 57-patient trial of a regimen nobody now uses. Pre-operative embolisation has one randomised trial, negative overall and positive in the hypervascular subgroup, and the advice to embolise the renal and thyroid primaries only is the author’s reading of it. The augmentation evidence runs in opposite directions: a Cochrane review against routine vertebroplasty for osteoporotic fractures, and an unblinded industry-funded trial for kyphoplasty in cancer. The leak and embolism figures depend on the imaging used. The cord perfusion target and the anaesthetic choice for augmentation are author’s judgement.
