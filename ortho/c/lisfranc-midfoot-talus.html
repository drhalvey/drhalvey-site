67 Lisfranc, midfoot, metatarsal and talus fracture fixation

Tarsometatarsal fracture-dislocation (open reduction or primary arthrodesis), navicular and cuboid fractures, metatarsal fixation including the fifth metatarsal base, and talar neck and body fractures

3Moderate

Dominant mechanism: Bone. Small bones and joints reduced and held with screws or bridge plates through dorsal incisions over thin skin, in a foot swollen by the injury. Open reduction of a Lisfranc injury or a talar neck fracture is a 3; primary arthrodesis of the medial tarsometatarsal joints, with three joints denuded and compressed, and a crushed multi-ray foot are a 4; a fifth metatarsal screw is a 2

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Tell the patient
The foot will be numb until some time the next day; when the feeling returns it is sore for two to three days and the tablets taken before bed are what carry you through. The trap
A swollen midfoot after a crush or a high-energy injury is the foot that develops compartment syndrome (fasciotomy in 18% of crush injuries combined with a forefoot injury in registry data), so the block that is ideal for the elective fixation at ten days is the wrong block on the night of injury: time the regional technique to the injury, not to the operation

What the surgeon is doing and why

This card covers the foot fractures between the ankle and the toes that are not the calcaneus. They share a soft-tissue envelope, a block, and a habit of being missed, and they differ in what the surgeon is trying to save.

The Lisfranc injury is a failure of the arch’s keystone. The bases of the five metatarsals lock into the cuneiforms and cuboid like the stones of a Roman arch, with the second metatarsal recessed between the cuneiforms and tied to the medial cuneiform by the Lisfranc ligament; there is no ligament between the first and second metatarsal bases. A twist on a plantarflexed foot (a fall from a horse, a football tackle) or a crush tears that ligament, and the metatarsals drift laterally and dorsally. Left displaced by even 2 mm the arch collapses and the joints become arthritic. The surgeon opens the foot through one or two dorsal incisions, reduces the second metatarsal base into its mortise first, and then chooses how to hold it:

  • Open reduction and internal fixation: screws across the first, second and third tarsometatarsal joints or, to spare the cartilage, plates bridging them; the mobile fourth and fifth joints are held with wires that come out at six weeks. The screws or plates cross joints and are usually removed at 3-6 months.
  • Primary arthrodesis: the cartilage of the medial two or three joints is removed and the joints are fused at the first operation. The pooled evidence favours this across acute Lisfranc injuries as a group, not only the purely ligamentous patterns for which surgeons most often choose it: better function and pain scores, more patients back to activity, and unplanned reoperation in 14.7% against 38.3%.

The other midfoot bones follow the same column logic. A navicular body fracture or a crushed cuboid (the “nutcracker” injury) shortens the medial or lateral column, and the surgeon restores length with a plate, graft or a small frame. Metatarsal shaft fractures are wired or plated when several are displaced; the fracture at the base of the fifth metatarsal in an athlete gets a single intramedullary screw through a stab incision.

The talus is a different problem. It has no muscle attachments, is covered mostly by cartilage, and takes its blood supply through the neck and the tarsal canal. A fracture of the neck, from forced dorsiflexion, cuts the body off from that supply in proportion to its displacement: pooled osteonecrosis rates are about 10% for an undisplaced fracture and around 50% once the body has dislocated from the subtalar and ankle joints, with subtalar arthritis in half or more. The surgeon reduces the neck anatomically through two incisions (anteromedial and anterolateral), because a neck that heals shortened or in varus locks the hindfoot, and fixes it with screws and mini plates while staying out of the sinus tarsi where the remaining vessels run. Timing of that fixation does not change the osteonecrosis rate; only a dislocated body pressing on skin or the neurovascular bundle is an emergency, and it is reduced within hours and fixed later.

The pain logic is bone under thin dorsal skin in a dependent, swollen foot: the same mechanism as an ankle fracture, with the same fast decline. A block covers it completely, so the block and its rebound dominate the plan. Primary arthrodesis moves a point up the scale because three joints are denuded and compressed (foot arthrodesis ranked among the most painful of 179 procedures for day 1 pain without blocks), and the crushed foot with several rays fixed does the same through soft-tissue injury.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Which bone, and how much energy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A footballer’s subtle Lisfranc sprain found on weight-bearing films and a foot run over by a forklift are booked under the same name. The surgeon stages treatment by energy.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Energy decides almost everything for you too. Low energy, intact skin, surgery at 7-14 days: an elective-style plan with a block. High energy or crush: other injuries to exclude, a foot at risk of compartment syndrome, staged surgery, and restraint with regional anaesthesia at the first operation. Many of these injuries are diagnosed late, and a patient presenting weeks after injury for an arthrodesis is an elective case with a chronically painful foot.

  2. 2 The first days: reduce, hold, and watch the foot

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A dislocated midfoot or talar body tents the dorsal skin and stretches the dorsalis pedis artery; it is reduced closed or open within hours and held with temporary wires or a spanning frame. Definitive surgery waits for the swelling.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    In the national trauma database, fasciotomy for foot compartment syndrome followed 18% of crush injuries combined with a forefoot injury and 14% of isolated crush injuries. Pain out of proportion and pain on passive toe movement are the warning signs, and a dense sciatic block abolishes both for a day. For the temporising operation use a general anaesthetic, surgeon’s infiltration and systemic analgesia; if a block is wanted for a difficult reduction or a patient who cannot have opioids, it is low in concentration (ropivacaine 0.2% or less), free of adjuncts, agreed with the surgeon and paired with documented checks at set intervals. The 2021 Association of Anaesthetists guideline states that single-shot or continuous peripheral nerve blocks using lower concentrations of local anaesthetic without adjuncts are not associated with delayed diagnosis provided surveillance is appropriate. That guideline addresses lower leg (tibial) trauma; applying it to the foot is extrapolation, set out in nerve injury and compartment syndrome. Record pulses and sensation before and after.

  3. 3 Definitive surgery: anaesthetic and blocks

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    At 7-14 days the dorsal skin wrinkles, the blisters have healed and the surgeon can open the foot.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway, a single-shot popliteal sciatic block (20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%) and a saphenous block in the adductor canal (10 mL), with dexamethasone 8 mg IV. The saphenous block is needed more often than expected: the dorsomedial incision over the first tarsometatarsal joint and the anteromedial approach to the talus both lie in skin that the saphenous nerve reaches in many patients, and the medial midfoot joints take saphenous articular branches. At 7-14 days, with the swelling receded and the skin wrinkling, the period of compartment syndrome risk from the injury has passed in the author’s judgement; no source defines its end, and a foot that is still tense on the day of surgery gets the plan in step 2. Where a spinal is used in a patient on prophylactic enoxaparin, the needle goes in at least 12 h after a dose and the next dose is given at least 12 h after the needle (venous thromboembolism). Cefazolin before the cuff.

  4. 4 Position, tourniquet and imaging

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Supine with the knee bent over a bolster puts the sole flat on the table, which gives true anteroposterior and oblique fluoroscopy of the midfoot without anyone holding the leg.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A thigh cuff for midfoot and talar work, set from the limb occlusion pressure (the tourniquet); the flexed knee must not rest against the cuff tubing or a bolster edge behind the fibular head. In a crushed or very swollen foot the surgeon may leave the cuff down to avoid reperfusion swelling, and the field then bleeds from cancellous bone at a rate that matters to the view and not to the haemoglobin.

  5. 5 Lisfranc: dorsal approach and the neurovascular bundle

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A dorsomedial incision between the first and second rays, with the deep peroneal nerve and dorsalis pedis artery identified and retracted with extensor hallucis brevis; a second incision over the fourth ray if the lateral joints need opening. Interposed ligament and bone fragments are cleared from the joints.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The artery dives between the first and second metatarsal bases at exactly the point the surgeon is working; in the crushed foot it may be the main inflow. Numbness in the first web space afterwards is the deep peroneal nerve in the incision. Maintain a normal pressure for the skin bridge between two dorsal incisions, which is the tissue that fails.

  6. 6 Reduction and medial column fixation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The second metatarsal base is reduced to the medial cuneiform with a pointed clamp and held with the “home run” screw along the line of the torn ligament. The first and third joints are then reduced and held with transarticular screws or dorsal bridge plates. A flexible suture-button device replaces the home run screw in some hands for ligamentous injuries.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Reduction is checked on several fluoroscopic views and by direct vision of the joint; the step is slow and needs a still foot. Tourniquet time passes 60 min here in most cases.

  7. 7 Lateral column, or conversion to primary arthrodesis

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The fourth and fifth tarsometatarsal joints must keep their motion, so they are held with percutaneous wires left protruding or buried. If the surgeon is fusing the medial joints, the cartilage and subchondral plate of the first to third joints are removed with osteotomes and a burr, the surfaces drilled, graft packed in, and the joints compressed with screws or plates.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Arthrodesis adds 30-45 min and changes the pain: three prepared joints behave like a small fusion, a point higher on the scale and a day longer. If the decision is made on the table, a catheter can no longer be placed conveniently, so extend the single shot with dexamethasone if it was not given and plan the stay and the oral regimen for a fusion. Surgeons vary on NSAIDs after arthrodesis; see ankle, subtalar and triple fusion.

  8. 8 Navicular and cuboid

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A displaced navicular body fracture is opened dorsomedially, the joint surface facing the talus is reduced and held with screws or a plate, sometimes spanning to the cuneiforms. A crushed cuboid is distracted back to length through a lateral incision, the void filled with graft, and plated.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The lateral incision is sural territory and within the popliteal block. Graft from the iliac crest or proximal tibia is a second site outside or at the edge of the blocks and needs the surgeon’s infiltration.

  9. 9 Metatarsals, including the fifth metatarsal screw

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Displaced shaft or neck fractures of several metatarsals are held with intramedullary wires through the toe or with small plates. The proximal fifth metatarsal fracture is fixed with a solid screw passed down the canal from the base through a stab incision under fluoroscopy.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    These are 30-45 min day cases with a pain score of 2. An ankle block with an ankle cuff, or a general anaesthetic with the surgeon’s infiltration, is enough; a 26-h popliteal block is more than the operation needs and puts an athlete on crutches with an insensate foot. The sural nerve lies beside the fifth metatarsal entry point.

  10. 10 Talus: approaches and reduction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The anteromedial incision runs between tibialis anterior and tibialis posterior, the anterolateral one lateral to the long extensors, and through the two the surgeon can see both sides of the neck and judge rotation and length. A body fracture that cannot be reached is exposed by a medial malleolar osteotomy, which is pre-drilled, hinged down on the deltoid ligament and screwed back at the end. A dislocated body is levered back with a pin in the calcaneus as a distractor.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A dislocated talar body can be very difficult to reduce and is the one moment in this card where the surgeon may ask for complete muscle relaxation; have it available. The posterior tibial neurovascular bundle lies against a posteromedially dislocated body. The malleolar osteotomy adds a second bone injury in saphenous territory.

  11. 11 Talus: fixation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Screws are placed across the neck from anterior or from posterolateral, with a mini plate on the comminuted side to prevent the neck collapsing into varus. Stripping is kept to the minimum that allows an anatomical reduction and the sinus tarsi is left alone.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A long fluoroscopy-guided step at the end of a case that is often at 120 min; agree whether the cuff comes down or stays. Nothing the anaesthetist does changes the osteonecrosis rate, which was set by the displacement at injury, and the patient should not hear otherwise from anyone.

  12. 12 Closure, splint, discharge and the later operation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Dorsal skin is closed without tension, sometimes leaving one of two incisions partly open; a padded backslab at neutral; elevation; non-weight-bearing for 6-12 weeks. Transarticular metalwork across a Lisfranc joint comes out at 3-6 months.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The slab goes on before emergence. First oral doses before discharge or on arrival in the ward. With intravenous dexamethasone the block lasts about a day; the ankle fracture trials that timed it found the first analgesic request at 26 h against 17 h without dexamethasone and rebound pain in 15% against 44%. The metalwork removal is a short day case with a pain score of 1, described in removal of metalwork, and the patient will arrive with a clear memory of how this block wore off.

Pain plan

The mechanism is bone under thin skin, completely within reach of a popliteal sciatic and a saphenous block, in a patient group that is young and often male or athletic. The plan is the ankle fracture plan, borrowed with acknowledgement: there is no trial of regional anaesthesia in midfoot or talar fracture surgery. The ankle trials show that popliteal and saphenous blocks give better pain control and less opioid use than spinal anaesthesia over the first 27 h, that the price is rebound when the block recedes, and that intravenous dexamethasone cut that rebound from 44% to 15% and delayed it by about 9 h. The ambulatory cohort adds that bone surgery and younger age are risk factors.

The defensible default for open reduction of a Lisfranc injury or a talar neck fracture is therefore single-shot popliteal and saphenous blocks with dexamethasone 8 mg IV under general anaesthesia, regular paracetamol and an NSAID, an oral opioid for the first two nights, and the written night plan. A catheter earns its place for a multi-joint primary arthrodesis, a crushed foot with several rays fixed, a talar body fixation with osteotomy in an inpatient, and the opioid-tolerant patient. An isolated metatarsal needs an ankle block or infiltration and simple analgesia.

The exception is timing. In the first days after a crush or high-energy injury, while the foot is still swelling, it is at risk of compartment syndrome, and a block that outlasts surgery by many hours is avoided: systemic multimodal analgesia with opioid PCA, the surgeon’s infiltration, and at most a low-concentration block without adjuncts, single-shot or by a catheter that can be turned down, with surveillance. That is consensus guidance written for the lower leg and extrapolated to the foot, and a shared decision, and it is the reason definitive fixation at ten days is the easier anaesthetic.

Escalation triggers: escalating pain with a tense foot and pain on passive toe movement in the first days after injury, or in the 24-48 h after an early operation, is compartment syndrome until the surgeon has assessed it, whether or not a block is still partly working. Pain with a cold or pale forefoot is the dorsalis pedis or the splint. Rebound after the block is treated with the opioid already supplied. Pain that persists with a foot that is shiny, stiff and sensitive to touch at 4-6 weeks raises complex regional pain syndrome and is referred early.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The missed Lisfranc. Late presentations go straight to arthrodesis; plan for a fusion, not a fracture.
  • Polytrauma. Midfoot and talar fractures are often the last injuries fixed in a patient who has had several anaesthetics; sequencing is in polytrauma sequencing.
  • Open and crush injuries. Debridement, frame, negative-pressure dressing, repeated visits; see open fractures and damage control. Each return to theatre is a decision about the block against the state of the foot.
  • Talar extrusion and open talar dislocations. Rare, dramatic, and managed by reimplantation and a frame; the anaesthetic is that of any open fracture.
  • Lateral process and posterior talar fractures. Small fragments fixed or excised through a lateral or posterior approach, the latter prone; a short case with a pain score of 2.
  • Navicular stress fracture in an athlete. A percutaneous screw with or without graft; day case.
  • Primary arthrodesis decided intra-operatively. The pain plan changes after the block has been chosen; dexamethasone and an overnight stay are the adjustments available.
  • Diabetic neuropathy. A “Lisfranc injury” without trauma in a diabetic is a Charcot foot; see diabetic foot surgery.
  • Wires through the toes. Protruding wires are deliberate; the ward should know.

Key sources

The surgical evidence is mostly observational. The comparison of primary arthrodesis with open reduction is a meta-analysis of 18 small studies, only a few of them randomised, and pooled all acute Lisfranc injuries; its conclusion is not restricted to ligamentous patterns. Talar outcome figures come from a meta-analysis of case series, and the statement that timing does not affect osteonecrosis from a review of those series. Compartment syndrome rates are fasciotomy rates from a registry and so underestimate missed cases. No trial of anaesthetic technique exists for these operations: the block advice is extrapolated from two randomised trials in ankle fracture surgery, the timing advice from consensus guidance, and the pain scores are the author’s judgement by analogy with ankle fixation and foot fusion.
