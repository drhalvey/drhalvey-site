105 Neuromuscular and early onset scoliosis: growing rods and fusion

Growth-friendly instrumentation (traditional and magnetically controlled growing rods, rib-based devices) and its repeated lengthenings in the child under 10 years, and long posterior instrumented fusion to the pelvis for the scoliosis of cerebral palsy, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, spinal muscular atrophy and myelomeningocele at 10-16 years

5Severe and prolonged

Dominant mechanism: Muscle. Fusion: paraspinal muscle stripped from 15-17 vertebrae and the iliac wings and held under retractors for 5-8 h, every facet excised, screws at each level and into the pelvis, then a collapsed trunk levered straight so that ribs, hips and abdominal wall sit at a new length. In the spastic child the trunk muscles answer with spasm. Growing rod insertion is two short exposures and a tunnelled rod, scoring 3-4; an open lengthening is one small incision and a few millimetres of distraction, scoring 2

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Block
Fusion: intrathecal morphine 5 microg/kg before incision or by the surgeon under vision, low-dose ketamine infusion (0.1-0.2 mg/kg/h), paracetamol 15 mg/kg IV 6-hourly (max 1 g), then nurse- or patient-controlled morphine in intensive care, with regular diazepam and clonidine in the spastic child. Lengthening: wound infiltration with ropivacaine 0.2% up to 1 mL/kg (max 20 mL)
Catheter: selective One or two surgeon-placed epidural catheters at closure (ropivacaine 0.1% at 0.2-0.3 mg/kg/h in total, max 0.4 mg/kg/h, started after a neurological examination or, where none is possible, after closing motor potentials are unchanged) for the child who cannot tolerate systemic opioid: severe restrictive disease heading for extubation onto non-invasive ventilation. Otherwise intrathecal morphine without a catheter
Alternatives: Bilateral erector spinae plane blocks or catheters, ropivacaine 0.2% 0.3-0.5 mL/kg per side (total within 3 mg/kg), as an opioid-sparing adjunct for growing rod insertion and for fusion, without procedure-specific evidence in this group; Methadone 0.1-0.2 mg/kg IV (max 20 mg) at induction in place of intrathecal morphine where the child will be ventilated overnight regardless; Dexmedetomidine 0.2-0.5 microg/kg/h in intensive care for sedation, spasm and opioid sparing
Position
Prone on a paediatric open frame or chest and pelvic rolls sized to the child, abdomen free, with fixed hip and knee contractures accommodated and never forced, the gastrostomy and any baclofen pump hanging clear, arms at or below 90 degrees, head in a foam holder with eyes checked on a schedule; halo-femoral or halo-gravity traction on the table for severe rigid curves

Tranexamic acid 10-30 mg/kg IV loading (max 1 g) then 5-10 mg/kg/h for fusion, the pharmacokinetic recommendation in the paediatric chapter; the Duchenne series that showed a 40% reduction in blood loss used 100 mg/kg then 10 mg/kg/h, a load well above that recommendation and in the range at which seizures have been reported, so it is not copied here. Tell the patient
This is a long operation and the first nights are spent in intensive care, often with help from a breathing machine or mask; the back is very sore for several days, and sitting is usually more comfortable and more stable afterwards.
The trap
In Duchenne muscular dystrophy the machine can kill before the surgeon starts: suxamethonium causes hyperkalaemic arrest and volatile agents cause rhabdomyolysis, so the plan is total intravenous anaesthesia on a flushed machine with the vaporisers removed, and the same applies to the 30-minute rod lengthening as to the 8-hour fusion

What the surgeon is doing and why

Two problems are joined here by the fact that the patient is a child whose spine cannot simply be fused in the way described on the adult deformity and scoliosis card, which should be read first.

Early onset scoliosis is a curve that appears before the age of 10, when the thorax and lungs are still growing. Alveolar number rises until about 8 years and thoracic volume until maturity, and a short stiff chest cannot hold normal lungs: children fused across the thoracic spine at about three years had vital capacities near 58% of predicted in adolescence. So the surgeon controls the curve without stopping growth. Anchors (screws or hooks) are placed at the top and bottom of the curve through two small incisions, and rods are tunnelled under the muscle between them without exposing or fusing the spine in between. With traditional rods the child returns about every six months for an open lengthening through a small incision over a connector. Magnetically controlled rods are lengthened in clinic with an external magnet and no anaesthetic, which removed dozens of operations in the matched comparison, at the price of a 44% complication rate and a one-in-three unplanned revision rate. Rib-based devices do the same job from the chest wall. Serial casts under anaesthesia are used in infants. At about 10-12 years most children are converted to a definitive fusion.

Neuromuscular scoliosis is a collapsing spine. Without trunk muscle control (weakness in Duchenne muscular dystrophy and spinal muscular atrophy, imbalance and spasticity in cerebral palsy, paralysis in myelomeningocele) the spine falls into a long C-shaped curve with the pelvis tilted beneath it, usually in the non-ambulant child. The child can no longer sit without propping on the arms, the ribs rest on the iliac crest, and the lungs are squeezed. Bracing does not stop it. The operation is a long posterior instrumented fusion from T2 or T3 to the sacrum and ilium, to level the pelvis and give a stable sitting platform; in Duchenne it is done early, at 12-15 years when the curve passes 20-30 degrees, because the vital capacity falls every year the surgery is delayed.

Compared with an idiopathic curve the surgery is longer (15-17 levels and the pelvis), the bone is osteopenic, the bleeding is greater, and the patient brings the comorbidity of the underlying disease.

Pain logic: the same as any long fusion, muscle stripped over the whole back plus bone plus a trunk re-tensioned into a new shape, a score of 5. Three things modify it here. Many of these children cannot report pain. The spastic ones add spasm. The weak ones cannot afford the respiratory depression of the opioid that the pain demands.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Pre-assessment: the disease decides the anaesthetic

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon has fixed the levels months ahead. Whether the child comes off the ventilator afterwards is decided by lungs and heart.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Sort by diagnosis. Duchenne (boys, 12-16 years, usually on corticosteroid): echocardiogram and ECG within the year for dilated cardiomyopathy and arrhythmia, which may be silent in a boy who cannot exercise; lung function; steroid cover. Spinal muscular atrophy: profound weakness, often already on night-time non-invasive ventilation, limited mouth opening, and a need for the surgeon to leave a lumbar window for intrathecal drug treatment. Cerebral palsy: seizures, reflux, baclofen pump, nutrition, as on the cerebral palsy card. Myelomeningocele: latex-free theatre, shunt function, renal function, insensate skin. Rett syndrome: breath-holding and a long QT interval. For all: vital capacity where the child can perform it. The Duchenne consensus statement treats a forced vital capacity under 50% of predicted as increased risk and under 30% as high risk, advising training in non-invasive ventilation and assisted cough before surgery and extubation directly onto it afterwards. Haemoglobin, platelets, coagulation, a crossmatch of at least one blood volume, weight and nutritional state (a body mass index under 17 predicts massive blood loss).

  2. 2 A trigger-free anaesthetic and the induction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Dystrophin-deficient muscle membrane is fragile. Suxamethonium depolarises it and volatile agents destabilise it; the result is potassium and myoglobin release.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    For Duchenne and Becker dystrophy: vaporisers off the machine, circuit and absorber changed, machine flushed to the manufacturer’s protocol or charcoal filters fitted, and total intravenous anaesthesia with propofol and remifentanil, which neuromonitoring requires anyway. No suxamethonium, ever. In 232 orthopaedic anaesthetics run this way there was no rhabdomyolysis or unexplained fever. Have calcium chloride 10% 0.2 mL/kg (max 10 mL), insulin with glucose and salbutamol to hand as for any hyperkalaemic arrest. Suxamethonium is also avoided in spinal muscular atrophy and other denervating conditions; volatile agents are acceptable there and in cerebral palsy. Expect a difficult laryngoscopy in the dystrophies (large tongue, stiff jaw and neck): videolaryngoscope first. If relaxant is used for intubation, a small dose of rocuronium (0.3-0.6 mg/kg) lasts far longer than usual in dystrophic muscle; reverse with sugammadex 2-4 mg/kg before baseline motor potentials. Cardiomyopathy means a slow, titrated induction and a vasopressor running early.

  3. 3 Lines, salvage and baselines

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeons will expose the back from the top of the thorax to the sacrum, and the cord is at risk at correction.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Arterial line, two large cannulae or a central line (veins in a contracted, oedematous child may dictate the latter, with ultrasound), urinary catheter, core temperature, cell salvage set up, tranexamic acid loaded before incision. A rapid infuser primed for a 30 kg child means small aliquots: packed cells 10-15 mL/kg, plasma 10-15 mL/kg, platelets 10 mL/kg, cryoprecipitate 5 mL/kg or fibrinogen concentrate 30-50 mg/kg guided by viscoelastic testing, and calcium with every aliquot. Give intrathecal morphine now if you are giving it. Baseline sensory and motor potentials are recorded supine and again prone: sensory potentials were obtainable in over 80% of neuromuscular patients, motor potentials in about 85% of those without cerebral palsy but only 39% of those with severe cerebral palsy. Agree before incision what will be done if there are no motor signals, since a wake-up test is not possible in a child who cannot follow commands. A bite block goes in for motor stimulation; active epilepsy and a shunt are discussed with the neurophysiologist, not treated as absolute bars.

  4. 4 Turning prone

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The abdomen must hang free to keep epidural venous pressure down, on a frame built for adults.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A 25 kg child with hip flexion contractures, a windswept pelvis and a gastrostomy does not fit an open frame without adaptation: use paediatric pads or rolls, let the hips and knees stay flexed on supports, keep the gastrostomy and any baclofen pump off the pads, and pad osteopenic limbs as they lie. Halo-femoral traction, if used, pulls through the skull and the distal femora for the whole case: check pin sites and the neck. Confirm tube position and ventilation pressures after the turn, and the eyes on a schedule. Recheck potentials. Cover everything that is not being operated on: these children have little fat and cool fast.

  5. 5 Growing rod insertion

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Proximal anchors at T2-T4 and distal anchors at L3-L5 or the pelvis through two midline incisions, each fused locally to give a foundation; rods contoured and tunnelled beneath the fascia between them and joined at a connector or containing a magnetic actuator; modest distraction applied.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A child of 3-9 years, 12-30 kg, for 2-3 hours prone with 5-10 mL/kg of blood loss. Motor and sensory monitoring as for fusion, so intravenous anaesthesia without relaxant after intubation. Tranexamic acid 15 mg/kg IV (max 1 g). Analgesia can be generous relative to the surgery, because the spine between the anchors is untouched: paracetamol, an NSAID if the surgeon agrees, erector spinae plane blocks or surgeon infiltration, and nurse-controlled morphine for a day or two. Many of these children have syndromes (airway, heart, cervical instability) that outweigh the operation.

  6. 6 The lengthening: the same short case every six months

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    With traditional rods, a 3-4 cm incision over the connector, the set screws loosened, 5-10 mm of distraction, and closure. Early onset scoliosis treated this way can mean ten or more anaesthetics before the final fusion.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A prone tube case of 30-45 minutes; many surgeons monitor potentials for it because distraction is the mechanism of cord injury, which commits you to the same intravenous technique. The dystrophy rules apply in full to a 30-minute case. Infiltration with ropivacaine 0.2% (up to 1 mL/kg, max 20 mL), paracetamol 15 mg/kg IV (max 1 g) and fentanyl 1-2 microg/kg IV are enough; a score of 2 and often home the same day or next morning. The cumulative burden is the issue: these children know the anaesthetic room well, so keep a record of what premedication and induction worked, protect their veins, and remember that implants become stiffer and infection-prone with each visit. Magnetically controlled rods come to theatre only for insertion, exchange and complications, and limit MRI.

  7. 7 Fusion: exposure from thorax to pelvis

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A single midline incision; paraspinal muscles are stripped subperiosteally off 15-17 vertebrae and the posterior iliac crests, and every facet is excised.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Blood loss begins here and does not stop: osteopenic bone, poor nutrition, anticonvulsants and, in Duchenne, abnormal vascular smooth muscle and platelets all contribute, and neuromuscular fusions lose about 40% more than idiopathic ones, with a third of it hidden. More than 12 levels, a low body mass index and a long operation are the predictors, which describes every patient on this card. Keep the mean arterial pressure at the lower end of normal for age during exposure where the heart allows, measure haemoglobin, gas and viscoelastic tests hourly, start transfusing early in a small child, and keep the temperature above 36 degrees C.

  8. 8 Screws and pelvic fixation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Pedicle screws at most levels, with sublaminar bands or wires where pedicles are tiny, and long iliac or sacral-alar-iliac screws to control the pelvis. The pelvic screws give the lever that levels the pelvis.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Screw stimulation thresholds and motor potentials need no relaxant. Iliac screw tracks bleed from cancellous bone. This phase takes one to two hours and is the last opportunity to catch up on volume, calcium, coagulation factors and warmth before the correction; repeat cefazolin and check the tranexamic acid infusion is running.

  9. 9 Correction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Rods are contoured, fixed to the pelvis and cantilevered up the spine, or the spine is translated to the rods level by level, with compression and distraction to level the pelvis and balance the trunk over it. Stiff curves need posterior column osteotomies.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    As on the adult card: raise the mean arterial pressure before the rods go in (to the awake value, and higher if signals fall), haemoglobin above 80-90 g/L, normothermia, no bolus of anything that depresses potentials. If signals are lost the sequence is the same: pressure up, haemoglobin and oxygenation checked, anaesthetic depth and technical causes excluded, and the surgeon releases the correction. In a child with no recordable motor potentials the sensory potentials are the only monitor and the correction is done in stages with a pause after each. A stiff ventricle in Duchenne may not tolerate the pressure target; decide the compromise with the surgeon beforehand, not during an alert.

  10. 10 Closure and loading the analgesia

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Decortication and bone graft over the whole fusion, drains, and a layered closure that takes 45 minutes. Vancomycin powder is often left in the wound.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Decortication is the final bleed. Give paracetamol, the planned adjuncts, and ask the surgeon to place epidural catheters now if that is the plan. Total the blood balance including an estimate for hidden loss, and send a full coagulation screen before leaving theatre.

  11. 11 Extubate or ventilate

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgery is over and the risk now is respiratory.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Decide on criteria, not hope. Favouring a night of ventilation: pre-operative vital capacity under 30-40% of predicted, blood loss over a blood volume, temperature under 35.5 degrees C, a face and airway swollen from 8 hours prone, cardiomyopathy on inotrope, or a child who was slow and weak before. Duchenne fusion patients in the largest series were ventilated for a mean of 19 hours, and 15% of neuromuscular fusions needed more than 72 hours, predicted only by pre-operative lung function. If extubating a weak child, do it awake, upright, straight onto the non-invasive ventilation they practised beforehand, with assisted cough available. Either way the destination is intensive care.

  12. 12 Days 1 to 7

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeons want the child sitting on day 1-2; the new trunk shape changes seating, feeding and breathing.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Morphine by nurse-controlled analgesia (bolus 10-20 microg/kg, background 0-10 microg/kg/h) with oximetry and capnography where available; ketamine 0.1-0.2 mg/kg/h for 48 h; paracetamol; an NSAID from day 1-2 if bleeding has stopped and the kidneys and the surgeon permit; diazepam 0.05-0.1 mg/kg (max 5 mg) every 6 h and baclofen continued in the spastic child. Ileus is usual for two to four days and superior mesenteric artery compression of the duodenum is a known complication in thin children after correction: restart gastrostomy feeds slowly. Chest physiotherapy and assisted cough are part of the analgesic plan, because a child who cannot cough from pain or weakness returns to the ventilator.

Pain plan

For fusion the mechanism is that of the adult card and so is the foundation: intrathecal morphine, which in children having scoliosis fusion cut 24-hour morphine use from 49 mg to 12 mg at 5 microg/kg, a low-dose ketamine infusion, paracetamol and an NSAID when allowed, with opioid by nurse- or patient-controlled pump. PROSPECT’s complex spine guideline, written for adults, supports the ketamine and NSAID and a surgeon-placed epidural, and does not recommend intrathecal opioid for want of procedure-specific data; the paediatric trial is why this card keeps it.

The neuromuscular child changes the weighting in two opposite directions. If the child will be ventilated overnight in intensive care, the respiratory depression of intrathecal morphine or of methadone 0.1-0.2 mg/kg IV is covered and the first 18-24 hours can be made comfortable without compromise. If the child is weak and is to be extubated onto non-invasive ventilation, every milligram of opioid counts against the cough, and that is the case for surgeon-placed epidural catheters with dilute local anaesthetic, or erector spinae catheters, both author’s judgement without trials in this group, plus dexmedetomidine in intensive care. The motor examination that an epidural clouds is less of a loss in a child who could not move the legs beforehand, though sensation and bladder function still matter and closing potentials should be unchanged before local anaesthetic goes in.

Spasm in cerebral palsy is treated as on that card: regular diazepam, baclofen continued, clonidine 1-2 microg/kg orally or by gastrostomy every 6-8 h. Pain is scored with an individualised observational tool agreed with the carers.

For growing rod insertion the plan is that of a two-incision spinal operation scoring 3-4: infiltration or erector spinae blocks, simple analgesics, and an opioid pump for 24-48 hours. For lengthenings: infiltration, paracetamol and ibuprofen 10 mg/kg (max 400 mg), and one or two oral opioid doses.

Rebound for fusion comes on day 2-3 when the intrathecal dose has gone, the ketamine stops and sitting begins: stagger those three events. Escalation triggers that are not pain: agitation with tachycardia and a rising carbon dioxide is respiratory failure; with a distended abdomen and bilious aspirates it is ileus or duodenal compression; with fever and a wet wound in the second week it is deep infection, which is several times commoner than after idiopathic fusion.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Volatile agents in undiagnosed dystrophy. A boy under 8 with large calves, a raised creatine kinase or delayed walking who arrests after sevoflurane and suxamethonium has hyperkalaemia from rhabdomyolysis: treat as that, with calcium, not as malignant hyperthermia.
  • Cardiomyopathy. Ejection fraction can be normal at rest and fail under haemorrhage, prone positioning and vasopressor; an arterial line and cautious fluid are the minimum, and cardiology advice on a milrinone or adrenaline plan is reasonable when the fraction is under 45%.
  • Long-term corticosteroid in Duchenne. Stress-dose hydrocortisone 2 mg/kg IV (max 100 mg) then 1 mg/kg every 6 h; fragile skin and bone; a cushingoid airway.
  • Spinal muscular atrophy on disease-modifying treatment. Intrathecal dosing needs access afterwards: the surgeon leaves an interlaminar window or a port, and the intrathecal morphine plan is agreed with that in mind.
  • Myelomeningocele. Latex-free from the door; kyphectomy variants bleed heavily and the dural sac may be in the field; a shunt that fails after correction presents as failure to wake.
  • Baclofen pumps. The catheter crosses the surgical field and is often divided and reconnected; withdrawal in the following days presents as fever, rigidity and agitation, as described on the cerebral palsy card.
  • Mitochondrial disease. Avoid prolonged high-dose propofol; a volatile-light technique with remifentanil, ketamine and dexmedetomidine is used and the neurophysiologist warned.
  • Casting for infantile curves. A tracheal tube, traction on a frame, and a plaster jacket that raises airway pressure until the abdominal window is cut; 45 minutes, repeated every two to three months.
  • Halo-gravity traction before surgery. Weeks of inpatient traction for severe curves improve lung function and nutrition; pin-site care and repeated anaesthetics for pin adjustment come with it.
  • Anterior release. Rare now; thoracoscopic or open anterior surgery adds one-lung ventilation in a child with no reserve, and is the strongest predictor of post-operative ventilation.
  • Consent conversations. Mortality and major complication rates are far above those of idiopathic surgery, and families should hear that from the anaesthetist as well as the surgeon.

Key sources

The Duchenne statements rest on a consensus document and a 232-anaesthetic retrospective series, which is as good as evidence gets for a rare disease; the vital capacity thresholds are expert opinion. Blood loss data are retrospective cohorts that agree with each other; tranexamic acid evidence in neuromuscular fusion is a non-randomised comparison using a higher dose than most units give or this card recommends. Monitoring feasibility comes from one 68-patient series. Intrathecal morphine evidence is from idiopathic scoliosis and is extrapolated; the choice between it and an epidural or plane-block technique in the weak child is the author’s judgement. The surgical and neuromonitoring detail common to all long fusions is on the adult deformity and scoliosis card and in spine essentials; the general paediatric points are in the child having orthopaedic surgery.
