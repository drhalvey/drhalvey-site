101 Clubfoot and paediatric foot surgery

Percutaneous Achilles tenotomy at the end of Ponseti casting in the infant of 6-16 weeks, tibialis anterior tendon transfer for relapse at 3-6 years, posteromedial release for the resistant or syndromic foot, and the osteotomies and coalition resections of the older child

3Moderate

Dominant mechanism: Capsule. Open clubfoot surgery (posteromedial release or tendon transfer): capsules of the ankle, subtalar and talonavicular joints opened, three or four tendons lengthened or re-routed and held at a new length by wires and a cast, with a thigh tourniquet for an hour. Sharp for two days, then quiet inside the cast. Percutaneous tenotomy alone is a 1; hindfoot and midfoot osteotomy in the school-age child is bone surgery scoring 4 with a high rebound risk

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: moderate.

Block
Open surgery: popliteal sciatic block (ropivacaine 0.2% 0.3-0.5 mL/kg, max 20 mL) plus a saphenous block (0.1-0.2 mL/kg, max 10 mL) for one foot; caudal ropivacaine 0.2% 1 mL/kg (max 20 mL) with clonidine 1 microg/kg for both feet or the child under about 10 kg. Tenotomy: local infiltration only
Catheter: selective A popliteal sciatic catheter for 48-72 h (ropivacaine 0.1-0.2% at 0.1-0.2 mL/kg/h, max 0.3 mg/kg/h) for osteotomies, triple arthrodesis and revision releases in the child over about 5 years; not for tenotomy, tendon transfer or primary release in an infant, where a single shot outlasts the worst of it
Alternatives: Caudal block as the equal alternative for unilateral surgery in infants: no difference in time to first opioid or 24 h morphine use against sciatic-based blocks in a 118-child randomised trial; Ankle block or surgeon infiltration for forefoot and toe procedures in the older child; Topical anaesthetic cream and 0.5-1 mL of lidocaine 1% (max 3 mg/kg) for tenotomy awake in clinic, with oral sucrose and a feed
Position
Supine with a bump under the buttock to bring the lateral foot up for tendon transfer and osteotomy; prone for posteromedial release through a Cincinnati incision in some hands, supine with the hip externally rotated in others; tenotomy supine with an assistant holding the knee and foot
Tourniquet
Yes. Thigh tourniquet for all open surgery, inflated to about 100 mmHg above systolic pressure (150-200 mmHg in the infant and preschool child) for 45-90 min; none for percutaneous tenotomy. The foot must be seen to perfuse in its corrected position after release, before the cast
Cement
No
Blood loss
A few drops for tenotomy; minimal under tourniquet for open surgery, with ooze into the cast after release
Duration
5 min of surgery and 15 min of casting for tenotomy; 60-90 min for tibialis anterior transfer; 90-150 min for a posteromedial release, per foot; 2-3 h for multiple osteotomies
Length of stay
Day case for tenotomy (overnight for the infant inside the post-anaesthetic apnoea window) and for tendon transfer in many units; 1-2 nights for posteromedial release and bony reconstruction. Day case: selected
Antibiotic
None for tenotomy; cefazolin 30 mg/kg IV (max 2 g) before tourniquet inflation for open surgery

Tell the patient
The heel cord is released through a tiny nick and the foot goes straight into its last plaster; babies are usually settled by a feed and simple pain relief afterwards.
The trap
The tenotomy takes two minutes and the cast takes fifteen: the anaesthetic is for the cast, which must set on a foot held in full correction with the knee at 90 degrees, and a baby lightened at the moment of the cut wakes, kicks and loses the position that eight weeks of casting produced

What the surgeon is doing and why

A clubfoot is a foot rolled in and down around the talus: cavus, forefoot adductus, heel varus and equinus, held there by short medial and posterior ligaments and tendons. It is idiopathic in about four in five and bilateral in about half. Ponseti’s insight was that an infant’s ligaments creep under gentle sustained stretch, so the deformity can be unwound in order by weekly manipulation and long-leg casts, rotating the foot outwards around the head of the talus, over four to eight weeks. The last element, equinus, does not yield to stretching because the Achilles tendon is thick and the heel is high. So when the foot abducts to 60-70 degrees but will not dorsiflex past neutral, the surgeon divides the tendon completely through a stab incision a centimetre above its insertion. The ankle drops into 15-20 degrees of dorsiflexion, the final cast holds that for three weeks while the tendon regrows across the gap at its new length, and boots on a bar keep the correction until the age of four or five. About nine feet in ten need the tenotomy.

Relapse is common and increases with every year of follow-up. The typical relapse at 3-6 years is dynamic supination: tibialis anterior, inserting on the medial side, pulls the forefoot up and in during swing. The fix is to move the pull: the tendon is detached from the medial cuneiform, passed under the skin to the lateral cuneiform and anchored through a drill hole, tied over a button on the sole, often with a repeat Achilles lengthening.

The open posteromedial release is the operation Ponseti treatment largely replaced, and it survives for the stiff syndromic foot (arthrogryposis, myelomeningocele), the neglected foot and the failed Ponseti foot. Through a posteromedial or circumferential (Cincinnati) incision the surgeon Z-lengthens the Achilles, tibialis posterior and the long toe flexors, opens the ankle, subtalar and talonavicular capsules, reduces the navicular onto the talus and holds it with one or two wires. In the school-age child and adolescent the same deformities, and flat feet, cavus feet and tarsal coalitions, are treated through bone: calcaneal and midfoot osteotomies, coalition resection, and at skeletal maturity triple fusion.

Pain logic by rung. Tenotomy: a stab wound and a divided tendon in a cast, score 1. Tendon transfer and posteromedial release: capsules opened and tendons re-tensioned, with tourniquet ischaemia, score 3 for two days, then a quiet immobilised foot. Osteotomies: bone, score 4, and the foot is where adult cohorts record some of the worst first-day pain, so the older child needs the plan of an adult hindfoot operation scaled by weight.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 The infant for tenotomy: awake or asleep

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs a still foot for the cut and a perfectly held foot for the cast. In many centres both are achieved in clinic with topical anaesthetic, a drop of local, sucrose and a feed. General anaesthesia or light sedation is chosen for the older, stronger infant (over about three months), for bilateral feet in some hands, and by institutional habit.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    If the list is yours, the patient is 6-16 weeks old and 4-7 kg. Establish gestation: a term infant under 44 weeks post-menstrual age, and an ex-premature infant under 60 weeks, needs apnoea monitoring overnight after general anaesthesia, which turns a day case into an admission and is a fair argument for the awake technique. Look for the syndromic foot: stiff extended joints and absent skin creases (arthrogryposis, with a small jaw, difficult veins and a tendency to run hot under anaesthesia), a sacral dimple or known spina bifida (latex-free environment), hypotonia with a tented upper lip (congenital myotonic dystrophy: no suxamethonium, expect prolonged apnoea). Fasting is formula 4 h, breast milk 3 h, clear fluid 1 h; put the infant first on the list.

  2. 2 Anaesthetic for tenotomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Five minutes of surgery, fifteen of casting.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Inhalational induction with sevoflurane, a cannula once asleep, and spontaneous ventilation through a face mask or a size 1 supraglottic airway. No opioid is needed. Paracetamol 15 mg/kg IV (10 mg/kg under 1 month; max 60 mg/kg/day from 1 month). The casts are cut off in the anaesthetic room or theatre: a baby with both legs free for the first time in weeks is easier to cannulate in the foot’s saphenous vein before the surgeon preps, if the hands fail.

  3. 3 The cut

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    With the foot dorsiflexed to tension the tendon, a small blade is passed medial to the tendon and turned to divide it from front to back; a palpable pop and a sudden gain in dorsiflexion confirm a complete division. The medial approach keeps the blade away from the peroneal artery and lesser saphenous vein laterally; the posterior tibial bundle is anterior and medial.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The surgeon infiltrates a small volume of local anaesthetic, deliberately small because a bleb hides the tendon: lidocaine 1% 0.5-1 mL or ropivacaine 0.2% 0.5 mL, well under the ceilings of 3 mg/kg and 2 mg/kg for this age. Brisk bleeding is uncommon and is managed by five minutes of pressure. In clubfoot the dorsalis pedis artery is often small or absent and the posterior tibial is the dominant vessel, so a white foot after the cut is taken seriously: watch the toes pink up before the cast goes on.

  4. 4 The final cast

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The cast holds 60-70 degrees of abduction and 15-20 degrees of dorsiflexion with the knee at 90 degrees, so it cannot slip, and is moulded around the heel and talar head as it sets.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Do not lighten yet. This is the step the anaesthetic exists for. Plaster takes several minutes to hold a mould and a kicking infant produces a cast with the heel riding up inside it, which is a failed treatment discovered three weeks later. Keep the infant warm (the legs are wet and exposed), then wake, feed within 30 minutes and discharge when feeding normally, or admit for monitoring by age.

  5. 5 Tendon transfer or open release: set-up and block

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs a bloodless field, a relaxed calf and 60-150 minutes.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A child of 6 months to 6 years. Supraglottic airway for a supine unilateral case; tracheal tube if prone or over two hours in an infant. Block after induction: popliteal sciatic (ropivacaine 0.2% 0.3-0.5 mL/kg, max 20 mL) with a saphenous block at the mid-thigh (0.1-0.2 mL/kg, max 10 mL), because every incision for a clubfoot is on the medial side, which is saphenous territory. For both feet, or under about 10 kg, a caudal (ropivacaine 0.2% 1 mL/kg, max 20 mL, with clonidine 1 microg/kg over 6 months of age) is simpler and stays inside the dose budget. Neither block covers the thigh tourniquet: expect a slow rise in heart rate and pressure from about 45 minutes, treated with depth and fentanyl 0.5-1 microg/kg IV, not taken as a failed block.

  6. 6 Tourniquet and exposure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The limb is exsanguinated by elevation or a soft bandage and the cuff inflated. For a tendon transfer, a medial incision exposes the tibialis anterior insertion and a second dorsolateral incision the lateral cuneiform; for a release, the neurovascular bundle is found and protected first, because every later step is done around it.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Use the lowest cuff pressure that works: about 100 mmHg above systolic, 150-200 mmHg in this age group, with the time called at 60 minutes. Exsanguinating both legs of a 9 kg infant returns perhaps 10% of blood volume to the circulation, which is tolerated, and the reverse on deflation is a brief fall in pressure and a rise in end-tidal carbon dioxide. Cefazolin 30 mg/kg IV (max 2 g) is in before inflation. General tourniquet physiology is in the tourniquet chapter.

  7. 7 Lengthening, release and reduction, or the transfer

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    In a release, the tendons are Z-lengthened, the capsules opened from behind forwards until the talus can be covered by the navicular and the heel comes out of varus, and wires are driven across the talonavicular and sometimes the subtalar joint. In a transfer, the detached tendon is tunnelled to the lateral incision, pulled through a drill hole in the cuneiform with sutures that exit the sole, and tied over a padded button with the foot dorsiflexed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Surgically quiet. A surgeon assessing the tension of a transfer or the reducibility of a foot may ask for the calf to be completely slack; the block gives that and relaxant is rarely needed. The dose of local anaesthetic the surgeon adds to the wound counts against the same 3 mg/kg of ropivacaine.

  8. 8 Tourniquet down, perfusion check, closure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The corrected foot has stretched its posteromedial skin and its dominant artery over a longer path. The surgeon deflates the cuff before closing and watches the foot: if it stays white in full correction, the position is backed off and the skin closed, or left partly open, without tension. Correction is then completed by serial casts.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Have the child warm and the pressure normal for this check, because a cold vasoconstricted infant makes a normal foot look ischaemic and an ischaemic one look acceptable. Allow five minutes. This is also the reason not to use adrenaline-containing solutions in the wound.

  9. 9 Cast and emergence

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A long-leg cast with the knee flexed, well padded and often split, for six weeks; wires and the button come out in clinic or under a brief anaesthetic.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Keep the child asleep until the cast is set. Paracetamol 15 mg/kg IV (max 1 g) and, over 3 months of age, an NSAID before waking. Emergence delirium is frequent in the 1-5 year old after sevoflurane, more so when the first sensation is a heavy numb leg in plaster: clonidine 1 microg/kg IV or dexmedetomidine 0.3-0.5 microg/kg IV near the end helps. Elevate the leg on the way to recovery.

  10. 10 Osteotomies and coalition resection in the older child

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    From about 8 years the corrections are bony: a lateral column (calcaneal) lengthening with an iliac crest or allograft wedge for the flat foot, medial cuneiform and calcaneal osteotomies for the cavovarus foot, resection of a calcaneonavicular or talocalcaneal bar with fat or muscle interposition.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is adult foot surgery in a 30-60 kg patient and the pain is of the same order. Popliteal sciatic and saphenous blocks at ropivacaine 0.2-0.375% within 3 mg/kg, dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV (max 8 mg), and a sciatic catheter for the multi-osteotomy foot. The cavovarus foot is the commonest orthopaedic presentation of Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease: document the existing sensory and motor deficit before any block, avoid suxamethonium, and expect a normal response to other agents. The reasoning for the adult equivalents is on the hindfoot fusion card.

Pain plan

Tenotomy is a score of 1: infiltration by the surgeon, paracetamol 15 mg/kg every 6 h for a day or two (max 60 mg/kg/day), and a feed. An infant who cries inconsolably that evening has a tight cast or a slipped heel, not surgical pain.

Tendon transfer and posteromedial release are capsule-and-tendon operations scoring 3. The block has two jobs, the intra-operative anaesthetic and the first night, and either a sciatic-plus-saphenous combination or a caudal does both: in the one randomised trial of 118 children having releases, time to first morphine was six to eight hours and 24-hour morphine use was identical whichever was used. The author’s default is therefore chosen on logistics. One foot in a child over 10 kg gets the peripheral blocks, which last 8-12 hours at 0.2% and leave the other leg and the bladder alone; two feet or a small infant get a caudal with clonidine. A catheter is not needed: by the time a single shot recedes the foot is immobile in plaster and the pain is 3-4 out of 10 for a day. Rebound is moderate and shows as a sudden evening of crying around 8-10 hours after the block; the first oral opioid dose (oxycodone 0.1 mg/kg, max 5 mg, or morphine 0.2 mg/kg, max 10 mg) is best given at bedtime before that happens. Regular paracetamol and ibuprofen 10 mg/kg every 8 h (max 400 mg; from 3 months of age) run for five days.

Bony reconstruction in the older child is a 4 with high rebound risk, and here a continuous sciatic technique has a real claim: a case series of 160 children reported 72 hours of good analgesia from a dilute infusion without block complications. Ropivacaine 0.1-0.2% at 0.1-0.2 mL/kg/h, to a ceiling of 0.3 mg/kg/h, for 48-72 hours, with a single-shot saphenous block for the medial incisions.

The compartment question is small but not nil: osteotomies and a tight cast on a swollen foot can produce foot compartment syndrome, and a dense sciatic block hides it. Dilute solutions, a split cast, elevation and attention to breakthrough pain or a rising opioid requirement despite a working block are the safeguards.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The syndromic infant. One clubfoot in five has a cause: arthrogryposis, myelomeningocele, congenital myotonic dystrophy, amniotic bands, chromosomal syndromes. Each brings its own airway, latex or relaxant rule and should be recognised before the 5-minute list slot.
  • Post-anaesthetic apnoea. The tenotomy age (6-16 weeks) sits inside the monitoring window for many ex-premature infants. Awake tenotomy under local avoids the question.
  • Repeat tenotomy and cast changes. Relapsed toddlers return for a second tenotomy or manipulation and casts under a 15-minute mask anaesthetic; by then they are 12-15 kg and remember.
  • Prone release. A tube, chest and pelvic rolls sized to an infant, and eyes; the caudal goes in before turning.
  • Vertical talus. The rocker-bottom foot is treated by reverse-Ponseti casting, then a talonavicular wire and Achilles tenotomy under general anaesthesia at about 3-6 months: the same anaesthetic as step 2 with a wire.
  • Wire and button removal. Usually in clinic. If under anaesthesia, a 5-minute mask case.
  • Charcot-Marie-Tooth and other neuropathies. A block is not contraindicated but the baseline must be written down, and concentration kept low.
  • Bilateral staged osteotomies. The second foot is done weeks later in a child who has learned what day 1 feels like: plan the catheter.

Key sources

Evidence here is thin and mostly descriptive. The tenotomy literature shows that awake, sedated and anaesthetised techniques all work and that practice is institutional. The only randomised analgesic trial compares caudal with sciatic-based blocks for open release and finds no difference, which is why the card treats the choice as logistics. The sciatic catheter recommendation for bony surgery rests on a large case series and on adult foot-surgery evidence, and is the author’s judgement. Apnoea-monitoring ages and drug doses are consensus tier. General paediatric points are in the child having orthopaedic surgery.
