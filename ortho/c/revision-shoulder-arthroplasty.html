70 Revision shoulder arthroplasty

Conversion of a failed anatomic or hemiarthroplasty to reverse, stem and cement extraction, one- and two-stage revision for infection

4Severe

Dominant mechanism: Bone. A fixed stem and its cement mantle dug or split out of the humerus, often through a humerotomy closed with cerclage; a loose glenoid removed and the defect reamed or grafted; all of it through scar that has to be released from the deltoid, the conjoint tendon and the axillary nerve. If the revision is to a reverse, the arm is also lengthened, which adds the deep deltoid ache of re-tensioning; and many patients arrive opioid-exposed after years of a painful shoulder

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Block
Interscalene brachial plexus catheter placed before induction and loaded for surgery, with intravenous dexamethasone, under general anaesthesia
Catheter: routine Author's judgement, not trial evidence: the randomised trial that found no benefit from a continuous infusion studied primary surgery. A revision is longer, wider and bonier, the patient is more often opioid-tolerant, and a single shot placed at the start of a four-hour case has less night left in it. A single shot with dexamethasone remains reasonable for a short isolated component exchange in an opioid-naive patient
Alternatives: Single-shot interscalene block with intravenous dexamethasone and a written night plan; Superior trunk block or catheter where the patient cannot afford a phrenic palsy; Suprascapular plus axillary nerve blocks: phrenic sparing, weaker, and poor cover for a humeral shaft opened to the deltoid insertion; General anaesthesia with opioid and low-dose ketamine where the block is declined or the surgeon wants an immediate axillary and radial nerve examination
Position
Beach chair, 45-70 degrees, head fixed, arm free on a support or holder; the whole humerus to the elbow is prepared because the incision may be extended distally, and the iliac crest is draped when structural glenoid graft is planned

No
Cement

Tell the patient
This is a longer and bigger operation than your first replacement; the arm will be numb from a fine tube in the neck for the first two days, and the shoulder will be sore for longer than it was last time.
The trap
The case is planned as two and a half hours and becomes four at the moment the stem will not come out: the humerus fractures or is split, the blood loss doubles, a cemented long stem goes into a canal with a hole in it, and all of this happens in beach chair with the head out of reach

What the surgeon is doing and why

A shoulder replacement fails in one of five ways, and the way it failed decides the operation. The cuff or subscapularis gives out under an anatomic implant and the head rides up (the commonest reason an anatomic replacement or hemiarthroplasty is revised). The glenoid component loosens or the native glenoid erodes under a hemiarthroplasty. The joint is unstable. The bone breaks around the stem. Or the implant is infected, usually quietly, with Cutibacterium acnes. In almost every one of these the answer is the same implant: a reverse, because it does not need a cuff, it can be bolted to deficient glenoid bone with screws, and its constraint deals with instability. So the usual revision is “take out what is there, convert to a reverse”, and the pain and physiology of the reverse apply on top of everything below.

What makes it a different operation from a primary is the taking out.

  1. The approach is through scar. The deltopectoral interval is no longer a plane, the conjoint tendon is stuck to the subscapularis remnant, and the axillary nerve is tethered in scar on the front of subscapularis and under the deltoid. The surgeon releases the deltoid from the humerus and finds the nerve before doing anything else. This is slow, bloody, and the source of the muscle component of the pain.
  2. The humeral component has to come out without destroying the bone that will hold the next one. A modular or convertible stem that is well fixed and well positioned may be left and converted with a new tray, which removes most of the operation. A fixed monoblock or malpositioned stem has to be extracted: osteotomes around the proximal ingrowth surface, a slap hammer, and if it will not move, a planned vertical split of the humerus (humerotomy) or a cortical window, closed afterwards with cerclage. The alternative to a planned split is an unplanned one: intraoperative humeral fracture happened in about 16% of revisions to reverse in the largest series, most often in women and after a previous hemiarthroplasty.
  3. Cement has to follow the stem out, piece by piece, with osteotomes, drills, burrs or an ultrasonic device, down a narrow canal with the radial nerve lying on the posterior cortex at the level of the cement plug. Perforation and cement extrusion are the recorded complications of this step and of the long stem that follows.
  4. The glenoid component is removed and what remains is a cavity. A contained defect is impaction-grafted behind the baseplate; an uncontained one needs a structural graft (the resected humeral head is no longer available, so iliac crest or allograft) or an augmented baseplate. A defect too large to hold a baseplate turns the plan into a hemiarthroplasty or a staged reconstruction.
  5. Then the new implant: baseplate and glenosphere, a long stem that bypasses the humerotomy or fracture by at least two cortical diameters, press-fit or cemented, and soft tissue tension set by lengthening the arm.

Infection changes the choreography. Every revision is treated as possibly infected because deep samples come back unexpectedly positive in about a quarter of shoulder revisions, three quarters of them C. acnes, an organism that takes up to two weeks to grow and produces no pus. A known infection is treated by one-stage exchange (everything out, radical debridement, new implant with antibiotic cement in the same sitting) or two stages (an antibiotic cement spacer, six or more weeks of antibiotics, then a second operation that is itself a revision). The pooled non-randomised data favour one stage, with the usual selection bias. For you the difference is that a two-stage patient has two long operations a couple of months apart and arrives at the second already on opioids. The principles are in periprosthetic joint infection.

The pain logic: bone is dominant (extraction, cement removal, humerotomy, glenoid reaming and grafting, cerclage), with a muscle component from the scar release that a primary does not have, the re-tensioning ache of the reverse, and an iliac crest harvest site in some. It is the same class as a primary reverse, a 4, with a higher day 1 and a slower fall, in a patient who is more likely to be opioid-tolerant. That last fact, more than the surgery, is why the plan differs.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Planning: why it failed and what will come out

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs to know the implant (modular or monoblock, cemented or not), the bone left on both sides, and whether the joint is infected. Inflammatory markers are often normal in C. acnes infection, so an aspirate or arthroscopic biopsy may already have been done.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Read the operation note of the previous surgery and the plan for this one, and ask one thing of the list: whether the stem is expected to stay or to come out. A retained convertible stem is a two-hour reverse; an extraction of a cemented monoblock is a half-day case with blood loss to match. Group and screen for all, crossmatch when a cemented stem is coming out or the starting haemoglobin is low: in an older series revisions were transfused in 21-27% of cases against 4% of primary anatomic replacements, and revision remains an independent predictor in a modern 3,168-patient cohort where the overall rate was 1.8%. Record the opioid history. Note the previous anaesthetic: how the last block performed and whether the diaphragm noticed.

  2. 2 Catheter, lines, induction and the chair

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants a still arm, a dry field at a safe pressure and no interruptions for four hours.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Interscalene catheter placed awake before induction, tunnelled or dressed well away from a field that extends to the base of the neck, and loaded with ropivacaine 0.5% 15-20 mL; dexamethasone 8 mg IV. Document deltoid, wrist extension and hand function first, because both the axillary and radial nerves are at risk in this operation and neither can be examined until the infusion is paused. Tracheal tube. Two reliable cannulae in the other arm, an arterial line for the long or frail case (transduced at the tragus), urinary catheter if it will run past three hours, forced-air warming over the legs. Beach chair physiology is unchanged from a primary and is set out in the anatomic TSA card: pressure is managed at the head, not at the cuff, with a vasopressor infusion and no deliberate hypotension. A long case in the chair also means heels, sacrum, the other ulnar nerve and the neck in its holder are checked on a timer.

  3. 3 Approach through scar and release of the deltoid

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The old incision is reopened and extended; the deltoid is freed from the humerus and the subdeltoid space re-created so the humerus can be delivered. The axillary nerve is found by palpation or dissection because it is no longer where anatomy says.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Slow, steady ooze from scar with no single bleeding point: this is where the first 200-300 mL goes and why the tranexamic acid is in before the knife. The deltoid twitches when diathermy comes near the axillary nerve, and the surgeon uses that twitch; the author’s practice is no further neuromuscular blocker after intubation, with depth and remifentanil providing stillness. The block will have paralysed deltoid in any case, so tell the surgeon it has been done.

  4. 4 Deep samples, then the antibiotic decision

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Five or more tissue samples from the capsule, the membrane around each component and the canal, each with a clean instrument, are the diagnostic test for a low-virulence infection and are held for 14 days.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Surgeons vary on timing. Some want the antibiotic withheld until samples are taken; others give it at induction on the grounds that a single prophylactic dose does not sterilise tissue cultures. Agree it at the briefing and have the dose drawn up either way; if it is to be held, it goes in the moment the last sample leaves the field, and a second dose is due at four hours or after 1,500 mL of blood loss.

  5. 5 Dislocation and removal of the head or humeral tray

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The modular head or tray is knocked off its taper to give room; a retained stem is assessed for fixation, height and version, and the decision to keep or extract is made here.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the fork in the case. Ask which way it has gone. If the stem stays, plan for the remaining 60-90 min of a reverse. If it comes out, send for blood if you have not, and move the arterial line and second cannula from “considered” to “done” before the humerus is opened.

  6. 6 Stem extraction and cement removal

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Osteotomes break the bond around the proximal stem, an extractor and slap hammer back it out, and the cement mantle and distal plug are removed so that the new stem can reach sound diaphysis. If the stem will not move, the surgeon splits the humerus vertically through the bicipital groove or cuts a window, and lifts the stem out of an opened bone.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The longest and least predictable step. The extensile exposure runs down the anterolateral humerus through bleeding muscle and an opened medullary canal that cannot be tamponaded; expect 300-500 mL here. A crack heard across the room is a humeral fracture, the commonest intraoperative complication: it adds cerclage or a plate, a longer stem and 45-60 min, and it puts the radial nerve in the field. Hammering and canal instrumentation produce the small embolic dips of any canal work. Ultrasonic cement removal generates heat next to the radial nerve, and the surgeon irrigates and pauses for that reason. Keep the patient warm; this is the hour in which temperature is lost.

  7. 7 Glenoid component removal and reconstruction of the defect

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The polyethylene component and its cement are removed, the cavity curetted to bleeding bone and its walls assessed. Graft is impacted into a contained defect, or a structural graft or metal augment is fixed to an uncontained one, before the baseplate is screwed through it into native scapula.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Glenoid exposure is the worst retraction of the operation, as in a primary, and is now done in a stiffer shoulder with the humerus already weakened: brachial plexus traction is longest here. If iliac crest is harvested, the surgeon needs access to the crest and you gain a second wound that the interscalene catheter does not cover; ask for the harvest site to be infiltrated with ropivacaine 0.2% 20 mL before closure and count it in the local anaesthetic budget.

  8. 8 The new humeral stem, with or without cement

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A long stem bridges the weak zone and gains fixation in the diaphysis. Press-fit when the canal will grip it; cemented when the canal is wide, thin or split. For a two-stage revision this step is replaced by an antibiotic cement spacer.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A cemented long stem in a revision humerus is the one true cementation in shoulder surgery and it is done in a sitting, vasodilated, partly depleted patient: restore volume first, have the vasopressor running, raise inspired oxygen and tell the surgeon the pressure before the cement goes in. The mechanism and the response are in bone cement implantation syndrome. Cement pressurised into a canal with a perforation, a window or a humerotomy can escape onto the radial nerve; that is a surgical problem that presents later as a wrist drop under your block. A spacer is hand-moulded and unpressurised, and its local antibiotic load (gentamicin, vancomycin) matters only in the patient with poor renal function.

  9. 9 Reduction, tension and closure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Trial reduction sets arm length and deltoid tension; revision shoulders are scarred and tend to be either too tight to reduce or unstable, so several trials are usual. Subscapularis is repaired if there is any left. A drain is more common than in a primary.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Each reduction pulls on the plexus and the axillary nerve. Give the second tranexamic acid dose, the antiemetic, and repeat the antibiotic if four hours have passed. Check a haemoglobin on the gas before the drapes come down. The sling goes on before emergence.

  10. 10 Recovery and the first two nights

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants a check radiograph, a working hand and, as soon as the block permits, proof that the axillary and radial nerves survived.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Start the catheter at a low rate (ropivacaine 0.2% at 4-6 mL/h with patient boluses) once the loading dose is receding, and agree with the surgeon when it will be paused for a nerve examination; the morning of day 1 is the usual compromise. Oral paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor (unless renal function, the antibiotic regimen or the surgeon’s view on graft healing argue against) start in recovery. The opioid-tolerant patient continues their baseline opioid from the first evening with rescue on top. Haemoglobin on day 1; the drain and the humeral canal continue to bleed overnight.

Pain plan

The mechanism is bone over released scar with a re-tensioned deltoid, in a patient who has often been taking opioids for a failed shoulder. Three things follow. The first night after a single shot is worse than after a primary because there is more bone and the block was placed four hours before the end. The pain lasts longer than 48 hours because the humerus has been opened along its length. And the oral rescue that works for a primary works less well in an opioid-tolerant patient.

The author’s practice is therefore an interscalene catheter for two nights as the default, with intravenous dexamethasone, regular paracetamol and an anti-inflammatory, the patient’s baseline opioid continued and immediate-release opioid charted for breakthrough. This is judgement. The randomised trial that found no advantage for a continuous infusion over a single shot with dexamethasone studied primary arthroplasty and cuff repair in mostly opioid-naive patients, four of its catheters fell out, and nobody has repeated it in revision surgery. If the service cannot look after a catheter overnight, a single shot with dexamethasone, low-dose ketamine during the case and a written night plan is the honest alternative, not a neglected catheter.

A superior trunk catheter gives the same cover with less phrenic block and is the choice for the patient with lung disease. Neither covers an iliac crest harvest (infiltrate it) or the lower end of a long humeral incision completely: the skin over the distal lateral arm is radial and the medial arm is intercostobrachial, and an ache there is expected, not a failed block.

For the opioid-tolerant patient, add low-dose ketamine intraoperatively and for 24-48 h, and follow the opioid-tolerant patient. Gabapentinoids have no specific support here.

Escalation: pain rising under a working catheter with a tense arm is a haematoma. New pain at the mid-humerus on movement is a fracture or a displaced humerotomy and needs a radiograph. Removing the catheter on day 2 is planned for the morning with oral opioid given an hour beforehand, because rebound from a catheter is the same phenomenon as rebound from a single shot, moved to a time when staff are present.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The retained convertible stem. The best revision is the one where nothing is extracted. Duration, blood loss and pain fall back to those of a primary reverse, and a single-shot block is then enough.
  • The infected shoulder. Long antibiotic courses through a PICC, rifampicin that induces the metabolism of opioids, anticoagulants and many other drugs, and a second-stage operation in a deconditioned patient. Renal function is measured before an NSAID is charted alongside vancomycin or gentamicin.
  • Wrist drop in recovery. The radial nerve is at risk from the humerotomy, cerclage passed around the shaft, cement extrusion and the fracture itself; an interscalene block does not usually abolish wrist extension, so a new wrist drop under a proximal block is reported to the surgeon at once and not attributed to the catheter.
  • Axillary nerve. Tethered in scar and stretched by lengthening. It cannot be examined under a working block; say so in the notes and agree the pause.
  • Phrenic palsy with a continuous block is continuous. A patient who tolerated a single shot may not tolerate 48 hours; dyspnoea on day 1 is treated by stopping the infusion, sitting the patient up and not by oxygen alone.
  • Periprosthetic fracture as the indication. An acute fracture around a stem is a trauma case with a haematoma already in the arm and often a frail patient; see periprosthetic fracture and proximal humerus fracture.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis and long-term steroid use are over-represented: cervical spine in the head holder, fragile skin under the straps, and bone that fractures during extraction.
  • Lateral or supine positioning. A few surgeons revise with the patient nearly supine for an extensile humeral approach or a plate. The pressure-at-the-head problem eases and the access problem does not.

Key sources

There is no procedure-specific analgesic trial for revision shoulder arthroplasty. The catheter recommendation is extrapolated from the mechanism and from the patient population, against a well-conducted negative trial in primary surgery, and is labelled as judgement for that reason. The surgical risks are better described: the 16% intraoperative fracture rate and the long-stem complication series come from one high-volume centre, the transfusion data are retrospective and span a 25-year change in practice, and the infection literature (unexpected positive cultures in about a quarter, one stage against two) is entirely non-randomised.
