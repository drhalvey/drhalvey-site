11 Total elbow arthroplasty and radial head replacement

Linked cemented total elbow for rheumatoid, post-traumatic and fracture indications; metallic radial head replacement for the unreconstructable radial head

3Moderate

Dominant mechanism: Bone. Distal humeral and proximal ulnar bone cuts, canal reaming and cement in both bones; the triceps reflected or split and repaired to the olecranon; the ulnar nerve mobilised and often transposed, which adds a neuropathic edge; a long tourniquet. Radial head replacement is a shorter version of the same mechanisms with a lateral ligament repair added

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: moderate.

Block
Infraclavicular or supraclavicular brachial plexus block, single shot, with intravenous dexamethasone, under general anaesthesia
Catheter: selective Single shot plus multimodal is the defensible default for a primary total elbow and for a radial head replacement; a catheter earns its place for revision, bilateral-stage or opioid-tolerant patients and for the rheumatoid patient who cannot take an NSAID, provided the surgeon has agreed that the ulnar nerve check will wait until the block recedes
Alternatives: Axillary block with a separate musculocutaneous and intercostobrachial component (covers the elbow less reliably at the posterior aspect); General anaesthesia with surgeon infiltration and no block when the surgeon insists on an immediate ulnar nerve examination; Continuous infraclavicular or retroclavicular catheter for the selected patient
Position
Supine with the arm across the chest on a padded bolster is the usual position for a total elbow; lateral decubitus with the arm hanging over a padded post is the alternative that some surgeons prefer and that the distal humerus fracture card describes. Radial head replacement is supine on an arm table
Tourniquet
Yes. Sterile tourniquet high on the arm for a total elbow, often approaching or exceeding 120 min; many surgeons release it before triceps repair and closure. A radial head replacement sits comfortably inside a single inflation
Cement
Yes. Both components of a linked total elbow are cemented, usually with antibiotic-loaded cement, into small canals that are pressurised with a gun; the humeral canal is the step with any bone cement implantation syndrome potential, and it is small. Radial head stems are press-fit, intentionally loose, or cemented depending on the design
Blood loss
200-400 mL for a total elbow, mostly after tourniquet release and into the drain; transfusion uncommon but the rheumatoid patient starts anaemic. Radial head replacement under 100 mL
Duration
120-180 min skin to skin for a primary total elbow, longer for fracture or revision; 60-120 min for a radial head replacement, longer when it is part of a terrible triad repair
Length of stay
1-3 nights for a total elbow; day case or one night for a radial head replacement. Day case: selected
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV within 60 min of incision (3 g over 120 kg); antibiotic-loaded cement is the surgeon's second measure for a joint with a 2-8% infection rate
Tranexamic acid
Tranexamic acid 1 g IV before incision; the elbow-specific evidence is thin and the practice is borrowed from the hip and shoulder
VTE
Mechanical prophylaxis; chemoprophylaxis only for the patient's own risk factors, since upper limb arthroplasty carries a low baseline risk
Antiemetic
Dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction and ondansetron 4 mg IV before emergence
Tell the patient
The arm will be numb and heavy into the night; the elbow is sore for the first two or three days and then settles, the hand may tingle on the little finger side for a few weeks because the nerve was moved, and the replaced elbow is for daily living, not for lifting.
The trap
Document ulnar nerve function before the block and agree with the surgeon beforehand whether the nerve will be examined before or after the block recedes: the nerve is mobilised in every total elbow, a dense block hides a neuropraxia until the next day, and a surgeon who expected to test it in recovery will not thank you for a perfect block

What the surgeon is doing and why

The elbow is a hinge that has to be stable through a 140 degree arc while the forearm rotates on it, and it is held together by a deep, congruent bony articulation and two collateral ligaments rather than by muscle. Rheumatoid arthritis destroys that in a particular order: synovitis thins the cartilage, erodes the bone of the trochlea and the olecranon, stretches the collateral ligaments, and leaves a painful, unstable, often flail joint in a patient with weak muscles, thin skin, long-term steroids and an immune system suppressed by design. Post-traumatic arthritis and the comminuted distal humeral fracture in an osteoporotic patient over 65 arrive at the same end point by a different route: the articular surface is either worn or in pieces that will not hold a screw, and the surgeon’s choice is between a fixation that may fail and a replacement that will work on day 1. In the one randomised trial, five of 21 patients allocated to fixation of a distal humeral fracture were converted to a replacement on the table because the fragments were too small, the replacement was half an hour quicker (108 versus 140 min), and function was better at six weeks and six months.

The modern total elbow is a linked, semiconstrained hinge: a cemented humeral stem with an anterior flange, a cemented ulnar stem, and a pin-and-bushing coupling between them that allows a few degrees of varus-valgus laxity so that the ligaments and the cement interfaces share the load rather than the bushing taking all of it. Linking the two halves means the collateral ligaments do not matter, which is the point: the surgeon can release them, excise the radial head, and reconstruct a flail or grossly deficient joint and it will not dislocate. The cost of a hinge is that every load on the hand becomes a torque on two cement mantles in small canals, which is why aseptic loosening leads the failure list (38% of revisions in the pooled 9,308 elbows, less often with linked than unlinked designs) and why the patient is told, for life, not to lift more than 2-5 kg. Unlinked resurfacing designs keep the ligaments and need them, and are used by some surgeons for the rheumatoid elbow with good bone and intact collaterals; they are less constrained, loosen less, and dislocate more.

The surgeon’s aims, in order:

  1. Get to the back of the joint through a posterior midline incision, find and protect the ulnar nerve where it runs behind the medial epicondyle, and decide whether it will be transposed anteriorly or released in situ.
  2. Get the triceps out of the way without losing it. The tendon can be reflected off the olecranon as a continuous medial-to-lateral sleeve (the Bryan-Morrey approach), split in the midline, or left attached and the joint entered on either side of it (triceps-on). Whatever is done to the triceps at this step has to be undone at the end, and the repair is the tendon-tension element of this operation.
  3. Release the capsule and the collaterals, excise the radial head if a linked design is used, cut the distal humerus to accept the stem and the flange, and ream the small, curved humeral and ulnar canals.
  4. Cement both stems with antibiotic-loaded cement, bone graft behind the anterior flange so that it integrates, and link the hinge.
  5. Repair the triceps to the olecranon through bone tunnels, decide where the ulnar nerve finally sits, close thin skin over a prominent joint, and splint the elbow in extension so that the posterior wound is not folded for the first days.

Radial head replacement is a different and smaller operation that shares the joint. A metallic radial head goes in when the native head is in too many pieces to fix (three or more fragments, Mason type III) and the elbow needs it for stability: a terrible triad (dislocation with radial head and coronoid fractures), an Essex-Lopresti injury where the interosseous membrane is torn and the forearm will shorten without a radial column, or a medial collateral ligament injury where the radial head is the secondary valgus stabiliser. The surgeon goes in laterally between anconeus and extensor carpi ulnaris, removes the fragments, sizes a monopolar or bipolar head to the native one (too large, and the capitellum is eroded and the elbow stiff: overlengthening accounts for 13-23% of revisions), seats a press-fit, loose-fit or cemented stem, and usually repairs the lateral collateral ligament complex back to the epicondyle, which is the tendon-tension step of that operation.

The pain logic for both follows from the tissues. The dominant mechanism is bone: cuts and canal preparation in two bones, with cement, which hurts hard for two or three days and then settles because nothing is loaded through a replaced elbow in the first week. Behind it sit three contributors that give this card its character. The triceps repair, or the lateral ligament repair in a radial head case, is a tendon under tension that will be protected but still loaded by every attempt to push up from a chair. The ulnar nerve has been handled, dissected over 10 cm and often moved, so a proportion of patients wake with dysaesthesia in the ring and little fingers that is neuropathic in character and lasts weeks. And the tourniquet has usually been up for the better part of two hours. Compared with distal humerus fixation (elbow fractures), there is less subperiosteal stripping and no two-column plating, so the periosteal element is smaller and the early curve a little kinder; compared with a reverse shoulder, nothing is lengthened or re-tensioned.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 The patient before the block: rheumatoid, elderly, or both

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs an elbow that can be operated on safely for two hours and a patient who can protect it for six weeks. Most elective total elbows are rheumatoid; most fracture total elbows are frail women over 70 who fell from standing.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The rheumatoid patient brings the atlantoaxial question (flexion-extension views or a documented stable neck before the head is positioned for a two-hour case with the arm across the chest), a temporomandibular joint and cricoarytenoid involvement that make the airway harder than the Mallampati suggests, long-term steroids that need a peri-operative supplement, methotrexate and a biologic that the surgeon will usually have asked to continue or pause according to their unit’s practice, anaemia of chronic disease that makes the modest blood loss matter, and skin that tears under tape. The fracture patient brings the hip-fracture problems in a different joint: dehydration, delirium risk, and a reason for the fall. Examine and document ulnar nerve sensation and intrinsic power before any block or sedation, because this is the one nerve that is handled in every total elbow and the block will remove your ability to assess it for 12-24 hours. Agree the nerve-check plan with the surgeon now, not in recovery.

  2. 2 Block, airway and position

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A posterior approach to the elbow needs the arm either across the chest on a bolster with the patient supine, or hanging over a padded post with the patient lateral. Both give the surgeon the back of the joint; the supine version is quicker to set up and keeps the airway in front of you, the lateral version gives a more stable field.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The block is an infraclavicular or supraclavicular brachial plexus block with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, placed awake or lightly sedated before induction. The two approaches performed identically for elbow surgery in the 150-patient randomised trial (supplementation 4.2% against 4.1%, conversion to general anaesthesia 4.2% against 5.5%), and the infraclavicular approach produced fewer paraesthesiae during placement (8.3% against 23.2%), which is a reasonable tie-breaker in a patient with rheumatoid neuropathy. The tourniquet site high on the arm is covered by the intercostobrachial nerve and by the depth of general anaesthesia rather than by the plexus block. General anaesthesia with a tracheal tube is the author’s default for a two-hour case with the arm across the chest and the surgeon leaning on it; a supraglottic airway is reasonable in lateral in a slim patient with a short radial head case. Pad the contralateral ulnar nerve with more care than usual, because a bilateral ulnar neuropathy after an elbow replacement is an argument nobody wins. In lateral, the dependent brachial plexus, the eye and the peroneal nerve need the usual attention and the head needs to be neutral once the surgeon has set the arm over the post.

  3. 3 Sterile tourniquet, antibiotic and tranexamic acid

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The field is the whole elbow and the proximal forearm, so the tourniquet goes high and sterile, and the surgeon will want it up for the bone cuts and cementing. Infection is the second commonest reason for revision (19% of revisions) and the skin over the olecranon is thin and poorly vascular, so antibiotic-loaded cement and a prophylactic antibiotic are both used.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cefazolin 2 g IV finished before inflation, 3 g over 120 kg; tranexamic acid 1 g IV at the same time on borrowed evidence from the hip and shoulder, since the elbow-specific trials are few and small. Note the inflation time and say it aloud at 90 and 120 min; a total elbow routinely runs to the limit that most units set, and the surgeon’s usual answer is to release before the triceps repair and close with the field bleeding. Tourniquet pain in a patient under general anaesthesia presents as a slow rise in pressure and heart rate from about 45 min that does not respond to opioid and does respond to deflation.

  4. 4 Posterior incision and the ulnar nerve

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A straight posterior incision, curved around the tip of the olecranon, gives access to both sides of the joint. The ulnar nerve is found proximally in the arcade of Struthers, released through the cubital tunnel and into the flexor carpi ulnaris, and either moved anteriorly into a subcutaneous pocket (most surgeons’ routine, in the Mayo review) or released in situ and left where it is (the practice in the 83-elbow series, with persistent symptoms in 2.6%). Either way the nerve is dissected free along most of its course at the elbow and retracted for the rest of the case.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Nothing to do, and the step is worth watching for timing: the nerve dissection is slow and the surgeon’s quietest ten minutes. Severe ulnar neuropathy after total elbow is quoted at around 5% in the Mayo review and 3-11% across systematic reviews; most is traction and devascularisation from this step and the retractors that follow, and most resolves. This is the injury your pre-operative documentation and your block agreement were for.

  5. 5 Triceps: reflect, split or spare

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The triceps inserts broadly onto the olecranon and covers the whole posterior joint. The Bryan-Morrey approach peels it off the olecranon in continuity with the forearm fascia and anconeus and reflects the whole sleeve laterally; a midline split goes straight through it; a triceps-on approach works around either side of the tendon and is favoured for fracture cases, where the condyles can be removed and the joint entered without touching the insertion. Reflecting and splitting give the best exposure and require a repair; sparing the triceps protects extension strength and makes the ulnar preparation harder.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The choice decides a third of the pain course and all of the rehabilitation. A reflected triceps repaired through drill holes is a tendon that the patient will not be allowed to extend actively against resistance for six weeks and that will be loaded by every push on the arms of a chair; triceps insufficiency (about 3%) is the failure of that repair. A triceps-on approach means an elbow that can extend from day 1. Ask which was done before writing the discharge analgesia, because the triceps-repair patient has more movement-evoked pain in week 2 than the mechanism tag suggests.

  6. 6 Releases, radial head excision and the distal humeral cut

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    With the triceps out of the way the surgeon releases the capsule and both collateral ligaments (a linked design does not need them), dislocates the joint, excises the radial head when it is arthritic or in the way, and cuts the distal humerus to a shape that accepts the stem and the anterior flange. In a fracture case the condyles come out as fragments and the cut is already half made.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Full capsular release and dislocation of an elbow is the point of maximum nociception before the bone work, and in a patient whose block is incomplete the pressure and heart rate will say so now. Bone bleeding is controlled by the tourniquet for the moment. A humerus with thin rheumatoid cortex or an osteoporotic fracture patient can fracture during dislocation or stem preparation; the surgeon’s reaction tells you the case has just lengthened by 30 min and a cerclage.

  7. 7 Canal preparation in two small bones

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The humeral canal is opened from the cut surface and reamed and broached to the stem size, then the ulnar canal is entered at the base of the coronoid or the tip of the olecranon and reamed along its curve. Both are small, both are curved, and the ulnar canal is easy to perforate; a cement restrictor goes into the humerus if the surgeon uses one.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Reaming and broaching are the bone mechanism being created. Marrow and fat vent from two canals but the volumes are small and neither is pressurised yet, so the physiological disturbance is a fraction of a femur’s. Warm the patient: a long case, a tourniquet that has shut off a limb’s worth of circulation, and an exposed joint lose heat steadily, and the cement step works better in a normothermic patient.

  8. 8 Cementing both stems and linking the hinge

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Antibiotic-loaded cement is injected into both canals with a gun and the stems are pushed home, the humeral stem with a wedge of bone graft from the excised bone trapped behind its anterior flange so the flange integrates and resists the posterior and rotational loads that would otherwise loosen the stem. The two halves are then linked with the pin and bushing, and the surgeon checks that the elbow flexes and extends through its arc without the triceps.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the only cement step of the upper limb that deserves the name, and it is a modest one: two small canals, hand pressurised, in a patient who has already been under anaesthesia for over an hour. Treat it as the hip card does in miniature: pressure supported before the cement goes in rather than after, an inspired oxygen increase, and the frail fracture patient watched for the fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide and pressure that marks a cement reaction, because that patient is the one most likely to produce it. The cure time is a fixed interval in which the surgeon does nothing to the patient; use it to plan the deflation.

  9. 9 Triceps repair, ulnar nerve placement, closure and the extension splint

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The triceps sleeve is sutured back to the olecranon through bone tunnels with heavy non-absorbable sutures, with the arm in a few degrees of flexion so the repair sits without tension. The ulnar nerve is checked for kinking at the new entry into the pocket or along its native course, the tourniquet is released, bleeding points are dealt with, a drain is placed by many surgeons, and the thin posterior skin is closed in layers. An anterior splint holds the elbow in extension so that the wound is not folded, and the arm is elevated.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Tourniquet release at this point is the usual sequence: a reperfusion dip in pressure, a rise in end-tidal carbon dioxide, and a bleeding field that the surgeon now has to deal with before closing. If the tourniquet has been up for the whole case, the metabolic washout is noticeable and the pressure takes a few minutes to recover. The repair wants a still patient and a held arm, not neuromuscular block. Give the antiemetic. The splint goes on before the patient lightens: an awake patient with a dense block and a repaired triceps will otherwise bend the elbow with the other hand.

  10. 10 Recovery, the first night and the nerve

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the wound dry and flat, the elbow in extension for the first few days, no active extension against resistance for six weeks if the triceps was reflected, and to know that the ulnar nerve is working.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The hand is examined when the block recedes, and the time of that examination has been agreed in advance (step 1). The oral regimen starts in recovery, not when the block wears off: paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor if renal function and the rheumatoid drug list allow it, and an oral opioid for the nights of the first three days. In the 972-patient ambulatory cohort half of block patients had rebound pain, with bone surgery (OR 1.82) and no intravenous dexamethasone (OR 1.78) raising the odds; this is bone surgery. A patient who wakes at 2 am with a numb little finger and a rising elbow is having the block wear off; a patient who wakes with a tense, swollen posterior wound is bleeding into a closed space over a nerve and needs the surgeon. Length of stay is one to three nights, longer in the rheumatoid patient with slow skin.

  11. 11 Radial head replacement: the lateral approach and the ligament

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A different operation on the same joint, usually in a trauma patient. Through a lateral (Kocher) interval between anconeus and extensor carpi ulnaris, the surgeon removes the radial head fragments, checks the coronoid and the ligaments, sizes the implant against the removed head, prepares the radial neck with a small reamer, and seats the stem. If the lateral collateral ligament complex has come off the epicondyle (it usually has in a dislocation) it is repaired back with anchors or bone tunnels, and in a terrible triad the coronoid is fixed first through the same wound.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Supine on an arm table with a standard tourniquet, 60-120 min, and the same infraclavicular or supraclavicular block with dexamethasone. The posterior interosseous nerve runs through supinator a few centimetres distal to the radial head and is protected by pronating the forearm; a wrist drop after a radial head replacement is retraction or an over-long reamer, and it is another reason to document before the block. The ligament repair is the tendon-tension element and is the reason the elbow is splinted and the forearm kept pronated afterwards. Sizing is the surgical trap: an over-long radial head (overstuffing, in up to about 20% of implants) erodes the capitellum and stiffens the elbow over months and is the cause of 13-23% of revisions.

  12. 12 The radial head patient and discharge

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants early protected motion within a hinged brace or a splint, because a stiff elbow after a terrible triad is the usual bad outcome, and the ligament repair protected from varus stress.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Most radial head replacements can go home the same day with a working block, oral analgesia started, and a written night plan; the isolated replacement in a fit patient is a day case, the terrible triad with a coronoid fixation and a ligament repair is one night. The pain course is a 3 that behaves like a 2 after the first night, because the bone work is small and the early motion is passive.

Pain plan

The dominant mechanism is bone with cement, which argues for a single-shot block with dexamethasone to carry the first night and an oral regimen that carries the next three days, and the two secondary mechanisms (a repaired triceps or ligament, and a handled nerve) argue for a regimen that expects movement-evoked pain in week 2 and a neuropathic flavour in the hand. There is no procedure-specific guideline for the elbow; the nearest PROSPECT evidence is for the shoulder, and this card’s plan is author’s judgement built on the mechanism and on the general ambulatory block data.

The defensible default is an infraclavicular or supraclavicular block with 20-25 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% (the two were equivalent for elbow surgery in the randomised comparison; infraclavicular gave fewer paraesthesiae on placement), intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg at induction, paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started before the block recedes, and oral opioid for the first three nights. The NSAID decision is harder than usual: rheumatoid patients are often on methotrexate, have renal function that does not tolerate a week of NSAID, and may already be on a COX-2 inhibitor. When the NSAID is off the table the opioid requirement rises and the catheter argument strengthens.

The catheter question has two parts. On pain grounds, a primary total elbow sits where the anatomic shoulder sits: a single shot with dexamethasone and a reliable oral regimen is enough for most, and a continuous infraclavicular or retroclavicular catheter (described for total elbow replacement as a technique with good analgesia, without a trial behind it) earns its place for a revision, a two-stage reconstruction, an opioid-tolerant or chronic pain patient, or the rheumatoid patient who cannot take an NSAID. On nerve grounds, a catheter means no ulnar nerve examination for two days, and that is the surgeon’s call as much as yours; most will accept it if the nerve was seen intact and released without tension, and some will not. The author’s practice is a single shot for a primary total elbow and for every radial head replacement, and a catheter only after a conversation with the surgeon about the nerve.

Escalation and what rebound looks like: the block recedes 12-24 h after placement, the elbow pain rises over an hour or two to its day 1 level, and the oral regimen that was started in recovery is the treatment. Pain above 7 with a soft wound, a warm hand and the splint on is treated with the charted opioid and a review of whether the regular doses were given. Pain out of proportion in a tense posterior wound is a haematoma over the ulnar nerve and is surgical. New ring and little finger numbness that was not there when the block wore off, or that worsens over days, is a nerve under pressure in its new bed and also surgical. Burning dysaesthesia that is present from the start and stable is traction neuritis and settles over weeks; a gabapentinoid is reasonable for it as author’s judgement, not evidence.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Triceps-reflecting versus triceps-on. The reflected sleeve gives the best exposure and the most movement-evoked pain in weeks 1-2, with a repair that fails in about 3%; the triceps-on approach is favoured for fracture cases and leaves an elbow that can extend from day 1. Ask which was done.
  • Unlinked designs. Kept for the rheumatoid elbow with good bone and intact collaterals by some surgeons: less constraint, less loosening, more instability. The operation is the same from your side, except that the collaterals are preserved and the surgeon will want the arm held still while they are balanced.
  • Fracture total elbow. The frail patient over 70 with a comminuted distal humeral fracture, often converted on the table from a planned fixation when the fragments turn out too small. Expect the position and the tourniquet of the distal humerus fixation card, a cement step in the frailest patient of the week, and the hip-fracture medical problems.
  • Rheumatoid airway and neck. Atlantoaxial instability, a stiff temporomandibular joint and cricoarytenoid arthritis; a documented stable neck before a two-hour case with the head turned and the arm across the chest, and a plan for a difficult airway that does not involve neck extension.
  • Steroids and disease-modifying drugs. A supplement for long-term steroid users; continuation of methotrexate in most units; biologics paused around surgery by most rheumatologists. Thin, steroid-damaged skin tears under tape and heals slowly over the olecranon.
  • Tourniquet time. A primary total elbow routinely approaches 120 min. The common sequence is release before the triceps repair; if the surgeon wants it up to closure, the metabolic load at release is noticeable in a frail patient.
  • Infection. 2-4% in the Mayo review, up to 8% in some series, and 19% of revisions; antibiotic-loaded cement, a prophylactic antibiotic, and a low threshold for the surgeon to be called about a red posterior wound.
  • Periprosthetic fracture. 5-30% of primary elbows over their life, and 12% of revisions; the thin humeral shaft above a long stem is the usual site, and the revision is a long case with a strut graft.
  • Ulnar nerve. Transposed by most, released in situ by some; symptoms in 3-11%, mostly transient. Document before the block, agree when the nerve will be examined, and treat a worsening deficit as surgical.
  • Radial head overstuffing. The implant is sized against the removed head, and too long by 2 mm is enough to erode the capitellum; the stiffness that follows presents months later as a revision.
  • Essex-Lopresti. A radial head fracture with a torn interosseous membrane and a distal radioulnar joint injury: the radial head must be replaced, not excised, and the wrist may be fixed in the same anaesthetic, which lengthens the case and moves the tourniquet.
  • Posterior interosseous nerve. At risk in the lateral approach to the radial head; a wrist drop in recovery needs the block ruled out first (document before) and then the surgeon.
  • Bilateral disease. Rheumatoid patients may need both elbows; the second side is a separate admission, and the contralateral elbow already replaced cannot be leaned on, which changes how the patient mobilises and what the nurses need to know.

Key sources

Total elbow arthroplasty is an uncommon operation with a complication literature far larger than its analgesia literature. The failure data (loosening, infection, fracture) come from the 9,308-elbow systematic review and the Mayo narrative review; the ulnar nerve figures from the same review and an 83-patient series that argues against routine transposition; the fracture indication from the single randomised trial of fixation against replacement in the elderly. The block recommendation rests on a randomised comparison of infraclavicular and supraclavicular approaches for ambulatory elbow surgery, which found them equivalent, and on the general rebound-pain cohort; the catheter advice is author’s judgement, and the only published catheter description for total elbow replacement is a technique report. Radial head replacement figures come from the 727-patient systematic review and the overlengthening review. The pain score and trajectory are calibrated against the elbow fracture anchor in the Part B scale rather than against procedure-specific data, which do not exist.
