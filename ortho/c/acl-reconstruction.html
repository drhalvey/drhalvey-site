32 ACL reconstruction

Hamstring, bone-patellar tendon-bone and quadriceps tendon autografts, with or without lateral extra-articular tenodesis and meniscal repair

3Moderate

Dominant mechanism: Bone. The graft harvest is the dominant early pain: a hamstring tendon stripped from its muscle up the posteromedial thigh, or a bone block sawn from the patella and tibial tubercle, or a strip of quadriceps tendon; then two tunnels drilled through femur and tibia, which is bone and the reason the peak is on day 1 and falls fast. The graft itself is tensioned but then protected, so there is no tendon-under-load mechanism; a lateral tenodesis adds an iliotibial band strip and a lateral incision, and a meniscal repair adds a capsule under suture and a brace

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Block
General anaesthesia with a single-shot adductor canal (femoral triangle) block, IV dexamethasone, and surgeon-delivered infiltration of the harvest site, portals and tunnel entries; IPACK added for selected patients
Catheter: rarely An adductor canal catheter is for the opioid-tolerant patient, the revision with bone grafting, or the reconstruction that has grown into a multiligament case; the standard day-case ACL does not earn one, and the mechanism is bone that settles in 48 h
Alternatives: Femoral nerve block: the same analgesia as an adductor canal block, a quadriceps that cannot straight-leg raise on the day, a 13% strength deficit still measurable at six weeks, and no place in a day case; IPACK added to the adductor canal block for posterior cover: the combination beat local infiltration alone for 24 h morphine in the one trial and adds nothing to quadriceps function; Spinal anaesthesia with sedation where general anaesthesia is undesirable, with the same block, at the cost of voiding and discharge time; Surgeon infiltration alone, which is the comparator the blocks have been tested against and loses on opioid consumption
Position
Supine with the thigh in a leg holder and the foot of the table dropped, or the leg flat against a lateral post; the knee is hyperflexed past 120 degrees for the femoral tunnel through the anteromedial portal and taken to full extension to set the graft tension
Tourniquet
Sometimes. Applied by most surgeons, inflated by fewer; the pooled randomised trials show pain 0.4 points higher at 24 and 48 h, about 100 mL more drain output and a thinner thigh with the cuff, and no difference in operating time without it
Cement
No
Blood loss
Minimal in the field; a haemarthrosis of 100-300 mL forms after the cuff comes down and the tunnels bleed into the joint, and a drain is unusual
Duration
60-120 min for an isolated reconstruction, depending on graft and fixation; add 20-30 min for a lateral extra-articular tenodesis and 30-45 min for a meniscal repair; revision with tunnel bone grafting is a different, longer operation
Length of stay
Day case in most units; one night for a meniscal repair with a brace, a lateral tenodesis, or a patient without support at home. Day case: yes
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV within 60 min of incision (3 g over 120 kg) and before the cuff if one is inflated; the graft, the buttons and the screws are implants, and some surgeons soak the graft in vancomycin
Tranexamic acid
Not routine; TXA 1 g IV before incision is a reasonable request from a surgeon who wants a smaller haemarthrosis, and the evidence is for comfort rather than transfusion
VTE
Mechanical and early weight-bearing; chemoprophylaxis for the patient's own risk factors (the pill, obesity, previous thrombosis) or for the weeks of restricted weight-bearing after a concurrent meniscal repair, by unit protocol
Antiemetic
Dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction, for the block as much as the nausea, and ondansetron 4 mg IV before emergence; a vomiting day-case patient does not take the night's tablets
Tell the patient
The knee is numb on the first evening and the worst of the pain is the next day, mostly where the graft was taken rather than in the knee itself; it settles fast over three or four days, the tablets taken on time are what keep it that way, and the physiotherapy starts straight away.
The trap
The 2 am rebound: a young, often female patient after bone and tendon surgery with a single-shot block is the exact phenotype in which half of ambulatory block patients go from a 2 to a 7 overnight, and the dexamethasone, the first oral doses before discharge and the written night plan are what stand between the block wearing off and the telephone ringing

What the surgeon is doing and why

The anterior cruciate ligament stops the tibia sliding forward and rotating inward under the femur. When it ruptures, usually in a non-contact pivot in a patient between fifteen and thirty, the knee gives way on cutting and twisting, and each episode of giving way tears a meniscus or shears cartilage. The ligament does not heal; it is replaced. The surgeon harvests a tendon from the same leg, drills a tunnel into the femur at the ligament’s origin and a tunnel through the tibia at its insertion, pulls the graft through both, tensions it with the knee near extension and fixes it at each end, and then waits a year for the graft to turn from a tendon into something like a ligament while the patient rebuilds the quadriceps that the injury switched off. Everything about the pain follows from where the graft came from and from the two tunnels.

The graft is the surgeon’s main choice and the main determinant of what the patient feels on day 1. Hamstring: semitendinosus with or without gracilis, taken through a 3 cm incision over the pes anserinus and stripped from its muscle belly with a tendon stripper passed up the posteromedial thigh, then folded into a four- or five-strand graft. The pain is a muscle and harvest-site pain, an ache and cramping in the back of the thigh, with the infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve often cut in the incision. Bone-patellar tendon-bone: the central third of the patellar tendon with a bone block sawn from the patella and another from the tibial tubercle. The pain is bone, at the front of the knee, and the pooled trials put donor-site morbidity (anterior knee pain or difficulty kneeling) at 47% across all grafts, with the odds 69% lower for hamstring and 88% lower for quadriceps tendon than for patellar tendon. Quadriceps tendon: a strip of the tendon above the patella, with or without a patellar bone block, through a short incision, and the least harvest pain of the three. Surgeons choose by the patient’s sport, their own training and the failure data, and the choice arrives on the booking slip.

Two things are commonly added. A lateral extra-articular tenodesis (a strip of iliotibial band passed under the lateral collateral ligament and fixed to the femur, the modified Lemaire) controls the rotational laxity that a single-bundle graft does not, and in the randomised trial of 618 high-risk patients under 25 it cut graft rupture from 11% to 4%, at the cost of a lateral incision and more pain at three and six months. A meniscal repair is done in the same sitting when the meniscus is torn, and it changes the rehabilitation more than the reconstruction does: a brace, restricted weight-bearing and the rules on the knee arthroscopy card.

The pain logic: the dominant mechanism is bone, from two tunnels and, with a patellar tendon graft, two bone blocks, which is why the worst day is day 1 and the fall is fast; underneath it is muscle and harvest-site pain in the thigh for the hamstring graft, a capsule distended by arthroscopy and a haemarthrosis from the tunnels, and the tourniquet if the surgeon inflated it. The graft itself is fixed under tension but is not loaded like a repaired rotator cuff; the surgeon’s rehabilitation protects it for months, so the tendon-tension mechanism that makes rotator cuff repair a 5 is absent. The open ligament reconstruction of the 1990s sat near the top of the 179-procedure cohort on day 1; the arthroscopic operation on this card is a 3, and the thing that makes it feel like a 5 is a block that wears off at 2 am in exactly the patient most likely to rebound. Compare the multiligament reconstruction, which is the same tunnels three or four times over with a catheter and a bed.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Pre-operative plan: the graft, the day-case default and the night

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon has already chosen the graft, decided whether a lateral tenodesis is going on, and read the MRI for the meniscus; what they want from you is a relaxed leg that can be hyperflexed, a view, and a patient who goes home that evening with a quadriceps that fires.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway, an adductor canal block placed in the femoral triangle before induction or at the end, intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, paracetamol and an NSAID before induction, and the surgeon’s infiltration. A spinal is a reasonable alternative in a unit that prefers it, with the same block and a slower discharge. The night plan is written now: this patient is young, the surgery is bone, the block is single-shot, and the rebound cohort says half of them will go from comfortable to a 7 overnight unless the oral regimen is already running. Decide the IPACK question with the surgeon’s list in front of you (see the pain plan).

  2. 2 Examination under anaesthesia, positioning and the tourniquet

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    With the patient asleep the surgeon confirms the diagnosis by Lachman and pivot-shift tests that a guarding patient would not allow, then sets the leg in a holder with the foot of the table dropped or against a lateral post, so that the knee can be taken from full extension to beyond 120 degrees of flexion.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The examination is a rotational force on an unprotected knee and the first stimulus; the leg holder is a clamp on the thigh above the cuff. The tourniquet is applied by habit and the evidence is against inflating it: in the pooled randomised trials the cuff added 0.4 points of pain at 24 and 48 h, 100 mL to the drain, and took 1.8 cm off the thigh circumference, with no saving in operating time. If it is inflated it goes up after the block and not immediately after it, and the harvest site and the tunnels bleed into the joint when it comes down.

  3. 3 Graft harvest

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Hamstring: a small oblique incision over the pes anserinus, the sartorial fascia opened, semitendinosus identified, freed from its bands to the medial gastrocnemius, and stripped off the muscle with an open or closed stripper passed up the thigh; gracilis the same if a second tendon is wanted. Patellar tendon: a longitudinal anterior incision, the central third of the tendon marked, and bone blocks cut from the patella and the tubercle with a small saw and an osteotome. Quadriceps tendon: an incision above the patella and a strip of tendon taken with a harvester, with or without a patellar plug. The graft goes to the back table to be prepared and sized while the surgeon moves inside the joint.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the mechanism the patient will describe. The stripper is a blind instrument passed up the posteromedial thigh beside the saphenous nerve and the muscle belly, and a premature amputation of the tendon (the stripper cutting it short) adds time and a second graft. The patellar saw is bone work at the front of the knee and the step with the rare patellar fracture. The infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve crosses the hamstring incision and its numbness is the commonest permanent sequel of the operation. This is also where the surgeon’s infiltration earns its place: ropivacaine into the harvest site and along the stripper’s track at the end of the case is the part of the pain the adductor canal block covers least well for a hamstring graft, and covers best for the anterior incision of a patellar or quadriceps graft.

  4. 4 Arthroscopy, notch preparation and the meniscus

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Standard portals, a diagnostic round, the torn ligament remnant debrided or preserved as a stump, the intercondylar notch cleared of soft tissue so that the femoral origin can be seen, sometimes a notchplasty. The menisci are examined and a ramp lesion behind the medial meniscus or a root tear is repaired now, before the graft is in.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Irrigation and time. A meniscal repair here changes the discharge plan to a brace and crutches and adds a capsule mechanism to the pain; it is the moment at which an IPACK or a night in hospital becomes reasonable, and it is announced from the scope, not from the booking.

  5. 5 Femoral tunnel

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The tunnel must enter the femur at the ligament’s anatomical origin on the inner wall of the lateral condyle. Through the anteromedial portal the surgeon hyperflexes the knee past 120 degrees so that the guide pin exits the lateral thigh safely away from the peroneal nerve and the vessels, then drills a socket to the graft’s diameter and a narrower channel through the far cortex for a suspensory button. Transtibial and outside-in techniques reach the same spot from different directions.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The hyperflexion is the position that matters: the leg holder or the assistant forces the knee closed, the foot comes towards the buttock, and the guide pin and drill pass out through the lateral thigh. Bone is drilled here for the first time, and the pressure and heart rate will say so in a patient whose block has not taken. The stimulus is brief.

  6. 6 Tibial tunnel

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A guide is set in the footprint of the ligament on the tibial plateau and a pin drilled from the anteromedial tibia, often through or beside the hamstring harvest incision, up into the joint; the tunnel is reamed to the graft diameter.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The second bone cut, and the one closest to the surface and to the harvest site; the tibial tunnel entry is a sore spot the patient points to at a week. The surgeon will want the knee at 90 degrees and still. Marrow and blood from the tunnel start the haemarthrosis.

  7. 7 Graft passage, tensioning and fixation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The graft is pulled through the tibial tunnel into the femoral socket and fixed on the femur, usually with a suspensory button flipped on the lateral cortex, sometimes with an interference screw. The knee is cycled through its range to settle the graft, then held near full extension with tension applied by hand or a tensioner while an interference screw or a staple fixes the tibial end. The surgeon checks that the knee reaches full extension without the graft impinging on the notch, and that the Lachman test is now firm.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The retension mechanism is created here, but it is a ligament set to a tension and then protected rather than loaded, and the brace or the crutches shield it; this is why the pain falls after day 2 rather than persisting. A graft that is too tight or impinges is the surgeon’s problem now, and an unexpected return to the scope to trim the notch means fifteen more minutes.

  8. 8 Lateral extra-articular tenodesis

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A 5 cm lateral incision over the distal iliotibial band, a 1 cm strip of the band detached proximally and left attached at Gerdy’s tubercle, passed under the lateral collateral ligament and fixed to the femur with a staple or anchor with the knee in flexion and neutral rotation. It is added for the young patient with a high-grade pivot shift, generalised laxity or a pivoting sport, and for revisions.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A second incision, a second fixation into bone and 20-30 min; the lateral side of the knee is outside the adductor canal block’s territory and inside the surgeon’s infiltration. In the randomised trial the tenodesis group had more pain at three months and on the KOOS at six, so the patient who had one and still hurts at the twelve-week review is not unusual.

  9. 9 Infiltration, closure, tourniquet release and the haemarthrosis

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon infiltrates the harvest site, the portals and the tunnel entries with ropivacaine, with or without an intra-articular dose; closes the harvest incision in layers; applies a compressive dressing and cryotherapy. A brace is a surgeon’s choice for an isolated reconstruction and a rule after a meniscal repair or a tenodesis.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Count the ropivacaine: 20 mL in the block and 20-40 mL in the infiltration is a total to know in a 50 kg athlete. If the cuff was up, release brings the haemarthrosis and the fall in pressure; the knee that is tense and painful in recovery after a bloodless case is bleeding from the tunnels and the harvest bed, and a bulky dressing and ice are the surgeon’s answer. The first oral doses go in before the patient leaves recovery.

  10. 10 Recovery, discharge and the first week

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants full extension from day 1, quadriceps activation, weight-bearing as tolerated for an isolated reconstruction, and physiotherapy that starts within days; a knee that stays bent and swollen becomes a stiff one and the cyclops lesion that brings it back for an arthroscopic release.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The adductor canal block with dexamethasone lasts 12-24 h; the trajectory on this card has pain at 2 that evening and 5 on day 1 with the oral regimen running, falling to 2 by a week. The night plan is explained to the patient and the parent or partner in words: the leg will wake up in the small hours, the first tablets have already been taken, the next dose is at the bedside, and the opioid is for the night and the morning after. A straight-leg raise before discharge is the proof that the right block was used; a patient who cannot lift the leg has had a femoral block or a block that has tracked proximally, and goes home on crutches with a locked brace and a warning. A hot, tense knee from day 3 to 10 is a septic arthritis until the aspirate says otherwise; a calf that is sore rather than tense is examined and the threshold for an ultrasound is low in a patient on the pill or on crutches.

Pain plan

The mechanism is bone plus a harvest site, with a day 1 peak and a fast fall, in a patient who is young, often female and going home with a single-shot block: a plan built for the first night and the morning after, not for a week. The block question has two honest answers that have to be held together. In the meta-analysis of ambulatory knee surgery, an adductor canal block gave no measurable benefit over placebo after ACL reconstruction and was no different from a femoral block; the authors asked for trials in the setting of infiltration and harvest-site analgesia, which is where the later trials went. In those, a femoral triangle block with IPACK cut 24 h morphine from 17 to 9.7 mg against the surgeon’s infiltration alone, and the adductor canal block was non-inferior to the femoral block for morphine and pain while preserving a quadriceps that could contract at two and a half times the strength at 45 min. The block therefore earns its place as the motor-sparing way to deliver what a femoral block delivers, on top of infiltration, not as a technique with a large effect of its own.

The femoral block argument is the one to have with a colleague who still uses it. The analgesia is the same. The cost is a quadriceps that does not fire on the day the surgeon wants it to: a 13.4% lower strength symmetry index and a worse symptom score at six weeks in the randomised comparison, a 13% rate of prolonged motor or sensory disturbance in another, and no difference by six or nine months. Add the rebound signal from the spinal trial, where a femoral block gave less pain at 6 h and more pain from day 3 to day 7, and the day-case straight-leg raise that it abolishes, and the adductor canal block wins on everything except the length of the needle. The one dissenting result is a retrospective cohort of 230 femoral and 30 adductor canal blocks that found a larger fast-extension deficit at six months in the adductor canal group; it is small, non-randomised and alone, and it does not change the plan. The saphenous nerve itself is at risk in this operation from the hamstring incision more than from the needle: in 28,196 blocked knee operations there were 18 saphenous injuries, eight after ACL reconstruction with autograft, and the block was the likely cause in five.

The defensible default, then, is a single-shot adductor canal block in the femoral triangle with 15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%, intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, the surgeon’s infiltration of the harvest site, portals and tunnel entries, paracetamol and an NSAID started before induction, and an oral opioid for two nights. The author’s practice is to add an IPACK (20 mL ropivacaine 0.2%) for the patient who has had a posterior meniscal repair, the opioid-tolerant, and the one going home a long way from help; the Lausanne trials show the combination beats infiltration for opioid use without touching quadriceps function, and that a selective tibial nerve block does the same job. A catheter is for the revision with bone grafting, the reconstruction that has become a multiligament operation, and the patient whose previous knee surgery ended in a bad week.

Rebound pain is the planning target. In the ambulatory cohort it occurred in half of all block patients; bone surgery (OR 1.82), female sex (OR 1.52) and younger age raised the odds and intravenous dexamethasone lowered them (its absence OR 1.78). This operation and this patient tick every box, and the dexamethasone, the first doses before discharge and the written plan are the three interventions that change the night. Escalation: pain above 7 on the first morning with a quadriceps that fires and a soft calf is treated with the opioid that was charted and a check that the regular doses were taken; a tense, hot knee from day 3 is infection until proven otherwise; a calf that is swollen is imaged; pain that is worst at the pes anserinus with a hamstring cramp is the harvest site and settles with heat, gentle stretching and time.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Graft choice is the pain choice. Hamstring: thigh ache and cramp, a numb patch below the knee, and the least kneeling pain; patellar tendon: anterior knee pain and a patellar fracture risk, with donor-site morbidity four times that of a quadriceps graft; quadriceps tendon: the quietest harvest. The booking slip tells you which.
  • Lateral tenodesis. A second incision outside the block’s territory, bone fixation, 20-30 min and a knee that hurts for longer at three and six months in the trial that proved its value; the infiltration has to reach it.
  • Concurrent meniscal repair. Brace, crutches, restricted weight-bearing, a night in hospital in some units, and the IPACK or a longer-acting plan; decided from the scope.
  • The tourniquet. Inflated for the whole case it adds pain at 24 and 48 h and a thinner thigh, which in an athlete whose rehabilitation is the quadriceps is the wrong direction; the conversation is had once, and the cuff goes on either way.
  • Femoral block in a day case. The same analgesia, a leg that cannot straight-leg raise, a six-week strength deficit and a fall risk on the stairs at home; the adductor canal block replaces it.
  • Paediatric and adolescent patients with open physes. Physeal-sparing or all-epiphyseal techniques, a longer operation, a parent who needs the night plan, and the same rebound phenotype.
  • Revision reconstruction. Old tunnels filled with bone graft in a first stage, allograft or a contralateral harvest, a longer and bloodier case; this is the ACL that earns a catheter or a night.
  • Infection. Rare, and more often after hamstring grafts; some surgeons soak the graft in vancomycin. A hot knee in the second week is aspirated, not reassured.
  • Arthrofibrosis and the cyclops lesion. A knee that will not straighten at six weeks is the surgeon’s complication and the manipulation card’s patient; an operation done on a swollen, stiff knee too soon after the injury is the usual cause.
  • Opioid prescribing to the young. A two-night supply with a stop date is the right prescription; the harvest site does not need a week of opioid, and the adolescent athlete is the patient in whom a leftover box does harm.

Key sources

The block evidence is contradictory on its face and consistent on inspection: the pooled ambulatory trials found no benefit from an adductor canal block over placebo after ACL reconstruction, while the trials that compared it with a femoral block found non-inferior analgesia with a working quadriceps, and the Lausanne trials found that a femoral triangle block with IPACK beat infiltration for opioid use. The reading on this card, that the block is the motor-sparing way to deliver a modest effect on top of infiltration and dexamethasone, is the author’s reconciliation. The quadriceps data are randomised and short-term; nobody has shown a lasting deficit. The graft morbidity figures are from a network meta-analysis of randomised trials, the tenodesis data from one large randomised trial, and the tourniquet data from seven small ones. The Gerbershagen ranking refers to open ligament reconstruction and is cited for perspective, not for a number on this card.
