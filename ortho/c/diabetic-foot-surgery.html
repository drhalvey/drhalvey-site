68 Diabetic foot: debridement, toe and ray amputation, transmetatarsal amputation, Charcot reconstruction

Drainage and debridement of the infected foot, digital, ray and transmetatarsal amputation with Achilles lengthening, and reconstruction of the Charcot midfoot and hindfoot, in a patient with neuropathy, vascular disease and renal impairment

2Mild to moderate

Dominant mechanism: Bone. Infected soft tissue is excised and bone is cut across at a toe, a ray or the metatarsals, in a foot whose sensory nerves are already largely gone. Nociceptive pain is muted by the neuropathy; what the patient reports is more often neuropathic burning, ischaemic rest pain or the wound being dressed. The score assumes a neuropathic foot; the ischaemic, sensate foot scores 3 and Charcot reconstruction, despite its size, rarely more than 2

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: low.

Block
Popliteal sciatic block (with a saphenous block when the surgery is medial or at the midfoot or above), ultrasound-guided, at reduced concentration and without adrenaline, as the sole anaesthetic with minimal sedation
Catheter: rarely Pain rarely justifies one, and an infected, anticoagulated, immunocompromised patient is a poor host for an indwelling catheter; considered only for the sensate ischaemic foot facing serial debridements
Alternatives: Ankle block for toe and ray surgery when the skin at the ankle is healthy; General anaesthesia when the patient is septic and unstable, cannot lie still, or refuses a block; Spinal anaesthesia where anticoagulation and sepsis allow it; No block and light sedation with surgeon's infiltration for minor debridement of a tested, insensate foot
Position
Supine, with the opposite heel, both malleoli and the sacrum padded: the other foot is as neuropathic as the one being operated on. Charcot hindfoot reconstruction may use a lateral position or a bump
Tourniquet
Sometimes. Often omitted: calcified vessels may not compress at safe pressures, a distal bypass graft or stent lies under the cuff, and the surgeon wants to see the tissue bleed at the level of amputation. Used at thigh or ankle for Charcot reconstruction and for clean elective procedures in a foot with good inflow
Cement
Sometimes. Antibiotic-loaded cement beads or a spacer are sometimes left in a debrided bone cavity; the volume is small and has no circulatory effect
Blood loss
Under 100 mL for digital and ray amputation; 100-300 mL for transmetatarsal amputation or debridement without a tourniquet in an anticoagulated patient; 200-500 mL for Charcot reconstruction
Duration
Debridement or dressing change 20-40 min; toe or ray amputation 30-45 min; transmetatarsal amputation with Achilles lengthening 60-90 min; Charcot reconstruction 150-240 min
Length of stay
Usually already an inpatient; days to weeks, set by the infection, the vascular plan and the wound, not by the anaesthetic. Day case: selected
Antibiotic
The therapeutic antibiotic course continues, with the due dose given on time; in a stable patient surgeons may ask for a new agent to be held until deep samples are taken, never in sepsis. Cefazolin 2 g IV for clean Charcot reconstruction
Tranexamic acid
Not routinely needed
VTE
Inpatient chemoprophylaxis adjusted for renal function unless the patient is already therapeutically anticoagulated
Antiemetic
Ondansetron 4 mg IV; dexamethasone is usually omitted because of its effect on glucose in a patient whose control is already disturbed by infection
Tell the patient
The leg will be numb from below the knee, usually for longer than in someone without diabetes, often into the next day; you will be awake or lightly sedated, and afterwards the numb foot and the other foot both need protecting from pressure.
The trap
The foot is the smallest problem in the room: this patient has a five-year mortality of about one in two, an eGFR, a potassium, a gastric emptying time, an anticoagulant and a glucose that each matter more than the amputation, and will be back on the list within weeks. Choose the technique that disturbs that physiology least, which is usually a block and very little else, and choose it with the next three visits in mind

What the surgeon is doing and why

Three processes destroy the diabetic foot, usually together. Neuropathy removes protective sensation, so a blister or a shoe seam becomes an ulcer without the patient noticing; its motor component claws the toes and concentrates pressure under the metatarsal heads, and its autonomic component dries and cracks the skin. Arterial disease, typically in the tibial vessels with heavy medial calcification, removes the blood supply that would heal the ulcer. Infection then travels from the ulcer along tendon sheaths and through the plantar compartments, reaches bone, and in a poorly perfused, hyperglycaemic foot becomes abscess, osteomyelitis and wet gangrene.

The surgeon’s operations are a ladder, and the patient may climb several rungs in one admission:

  1. Drainage and debridement. An infected foot with pus or necrosis is opened along the track of the infection, every pocket is drained, dead tendon and fascia are excised back to bleeding tissue, deep samples are taken, and the wound is left open, often under a negative pressure dressing. This is urgent: tissue is lost by the hour and the infection is the source of the patient’s sepsis and hyperglycaemia.
  2. Toe amputation. A gangrenous or osteomyelitic toe is removed through the proximal phalanx or at the metatarsophalangeal joint.
  3. Ray amputation. The toe and its metatarsal head or shaft are removed as a wedge, for infection that has reached the joint.
  4. Transmetatarsal amputation. All five metatarsals are divided through their shafts with a long plantar flap, when several rays are lost. The Achilles tendon is often lengthened percutaneously at the same sitting, because the shortened foot is pulled into equinus and ulcerates at its tip otherwise.
  5. Below-knee amputation, when the hindfoot cannot be saved; that operation is in amputation.

Charcot neuroarthropathy is a separate branch. In a warm, well-perfused, insensate foot the midfoot or hindfoot joints fragment and collapse after minor trauma, leaving a rocker-bottom deformity with a bony prominence under the arch that ulcerates. When bracing fails, the surgeon resects the prominence or reconstructs the arch: wedges of bone are removed, the joints are prepared as for a fusion, and the columns are held with long intramedullary bolts, plates, a hindfoot nail or a circular frame. In selected patients the pooled fusion rate is 86% and later amputation 5.5%, with technique-related complications in over a third.

The pain logic is unlike any other card. The tissue being cut is bone and inflamed soft tissue, which would score 3 in a sensate foot, but most of these feet have lost the fibres that would carry it. The pain patients do have is neuropathic burning that pre-dates the operation, ischaemic rest pain if the foot is under-perfused, and procedural pain from dressings. The plan-in-place score is 2 with a low rebound risk, and the block is chosen for what it does to the anaesthetic, not for what it does to the pain.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Infection, ischaemia or Charcot: how urgent is it

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A septic foot with pus is drained within hours, before revascularisation and before optimisation. Dry gangrene or chronic osteomyelitis is amputated on a planned list, ideally after any angioplasty or bypass so the amputation heals. Charcot reconstruction is elective and done only when ulcers are healed and infection is absent.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The urgency sets how much of the next step you get to do. For the septic foot the operation is the resuscitation: glucose, potassium, ketones, lactate and renal function are measured and treatment started, but source control is not delayed to perfect them. For the planned amputation there is time to arrange dialysis the day before, withhold a DOAC and place the patient first on the list.

  2. 2 The patient attached to the foot

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon is treating an end-organ complication; the same disease process has reached the heart, kidneys and autonomic nerves.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    In pooled follow-up after amputation for a diabetic foot ulcer, mortality was 13.6% at one year and 50.6% at five. Assess accordingly. Coronary disease is often silent and heart failure common. Renal function determines drug choice and dose, the potassium, and whether the patient has a fistula arm that must not carry a cuff or a cannula. Autonomic neuropathy shows as resting tachycardia, postural hypotension and gastroparesis: treat the stomach as full, and expect exaggerated hypotension with induction or a spinal. Glucose is driven up by the infection; a variable-rate insulin infusion with hourly measurement is appropriate for the septic patient and anyone with type 1 diabetes, and a range of 5-10 mmol/L is the conventional target. Patients on an SGLT2 inhibitor can develop ketoacidosis at a normal glucose when fasting and unwell, so ketones are measured and not inferred. GLP-1 agonists slow gastric emptying further.

  3. 3 Anticoagulants, antiplatelets and the choice of technique

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Atrial fibrillation, coronary stents and peripheral stents or grafts mean that most of these patients take an antithrombotic, and the surgeon usually wants it continued to protect a graft.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the strongest practical argument for a peripheral block. A spinal needs the anticoagulant stopped for an interval that reduced renal clearance prolongs, and urgent surgery does not wait for it: at least 12 h after a prophylactic dose of enoxaparin, longer when the creatinine clearance is below 30 mL/min, and therapeutic regimens do not fit an urgent list at all (venous thromboembolism). A popliteal or ankle block is at a superficial, compressible site, and in a two-centre series of 105 diabetic foot operations in patients on antiplatelet or anticoagulant drugs there was no block-related bleeding. Surgical ooze is the price of continuing the drugs, managed with a pressure dressing.

  4. 4 Whether the foot needs a block at all, and which

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs a patient who does not move, a foot that does not withdraw, and for anything beyond a toe, deep tissue that does not hurt.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A profoundly neuropathic forefoot can be debrided or lose a toe with no anaesthetic, and some surgeons do exactly that at the bedside. It is a poor default in theatre: the neuropathy is patchy, deep infected tissue and periosteum retain sensation after the skin has lost it, ischaemic tissue hurts, and the foot still withdraws: the reflex needs an afferent limb, and in a neuropathic foot the deep and patchy nociceptive afferents that survive are enough to drive it even when the skin feels nothing. Test the foot beforehand; if a block is omitted, agree a low threshold for the surgeon’s infiltration or conversion.

    When a block is used it is the whole anaesthetic. Popliteal sciatic is the workhorse because the needle is well proximal to cellulitis that often reaches the ankle, with a saphenous block added for medial or proximal incisions; an ankle block suits toe and ray surgery if the ankle skin is clean. Neuropathic nerves behave differently: in a prospective comparison, sensory onset after a popliteal block was 59% faster in patients with diabetic neuropathy and the time to first opioid request doubled, from 710 to 1,440 min. Animal work shows the same prolongation and a small increase in local anaesthetic neurotoxicity. The practical response is ultrasound guidance (nerve stimulation thresholds are unreliable in neuropathy), a reduced concentration such as ropivacaine 0.375% 15-20 mL, no adrenaline, and telling the ward that the block will last into the next day.

  5. 5 Position, the other heel and the tourniquet

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Supine, foot at the end of the table. The tourniquet is frequently left off: the surgeon reads viability from bleeding, calcified arteries may not occlude at a safe pressure, and a cuff over a bypass graft or stent can thrombose it.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The opposite foot is the limb you can injure. It is as insensate and as ischaemic as the operative one, and a heel resting on a table for an hour can ulcerate; float both heels on padding and protect the malleoli and sacrum. Ask about grafts and stents before any cuff is applied. Without a tourniquet in an anticoagulated patient, a debridement can lose a few hundred millilitres, which matters in a patient with renal anaemia.

  6. 6 Drainage and debridement

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Incisions follow the infection, not anatomy: plantar compartments are opened, tendon sheaths are laid open as far as pus has tracked, and tissue is excised until it bleeds. Deep tissue and bone samples go for culture.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Infection sometimes tracks proximally beyond the block’s upper limit, along the flexor tendons into the calf; sedation or conversion has to be available. Handling of infected tissue can precipitate rigors and a fall in pressure within the hour, in theatre or recovery; have a vasopressor ready and do not attribute it to the sedation. Give the due antibiotic dose on schedule. Keep the patient warm: wound washout volumes are large.

  7. 7 Toe and ray amputation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A racquet incision around the toe, extended along the metatarsal for a ray; bone is divided with a saw or cutter well proximal to the infected joint, cartilage and tendons are trimmed, and the wound is closed loosely or left open.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Thirty minutes under a block with the patient awake. Use the time: glucose and potassium repeated, insulin adjusted, the plan for the antithrombotic and the next visit agreed with the surgeon.

  8. 8 Transmetatarsal amputation and Achilles lengthening

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A dorsal incision at mid-metatarsal level and a long plantar flap; the five metatarsals are cut with a bevel and a gentle cascade in length, tendons and nerves are divided under tension so they retract, and the plantar flap is brought over the ends. The Achilles is lengthened through three percutaneous half-cuts, or the gastrocnemius is recessed, to balance the foot.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the operation on this card most likely to hurt, because five bones and five digital nerves are divided, and it is the one most likely to fail: pooled data across all partial foot amputations give later major amputation in 23%, with a reported range of 8-54% after transmetatarsal amputation itself, and re-ulceration in up to 69%. A popliteal block with a saphenous block covers both the foot and the Achilles lengthening. Phantom sensations of the toes are common afterwards and are neuropathic, not surgical.

  9. 9 Charcot reconstruction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Through medial and lateral incisions the collapsed midfoot is taken down, a wedge is removed to restore the arch, joint surfaces are prepared, and long axial screws, plates, a hindfoot nail or a circular frame hold the correction for the months that neuropathic bone takes to unite.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A 3-4 h operation with a tourniquet and blood loss after its release, in a patient with the same comorbidities and no sepsis. The block is still the logical basis, with general anaesthesia or sedation added for duration; the foot itself hurts little afterwards, so a catheter is unnecessary and the analgesic plan is simple. The risks are those of long surgery in cardiac and renal disease, pressure areas, and the wound. A frame adds weight and pin sites and makes later blocks and positioning harder.

  10. 10 Leaving the wound open, and the return visits

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Infected wounds are rarely closed at the first operation. The surgeon returns at 48-72 h to look again, debride further, change a negative pressure dressing, close, graft, or go to a higher level.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    In a prospective cohort one patient in four needed another operation within 60 days, and with three risk factors more than 80% of debridements did. Plan the series, not the case. Each visit is another fast for a patient on insulin with poor nutrition: first on the list, clear fluids until called, and insulin adjusted. A block can be repeated at each visit; repeated general anaesthetics in this population accumulate hypotension and delirium. Dressing changes on a sensate wound can often be done with a short block or modest sedation.

  11. 11 Recovery and the ward

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the foot elevated, off-loaded and perfused, and the glucose controlled so the wound can heal.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Analgesia has to respect the kidneys. Regular paracetamol; no NSAIDs with reduced renal function; opioids chosen for renal safety (fentanyl, buprenorphine or reduced-dose oxycodone, with morphine, codeine and tramadol avoided at low eGFR); the patient’s existing neuropathic agents continued at a renally adjusted dose. A block that lasts 24 h in a neuropathic limb produces a foot the patient cannot feel or move in a bed where the other foot is also insensate: heel protection and falls precautions are written orders. A block that has not worn off by 36-48 h is examined and discussed, not assumed to be the diabetes. Restart the usual diabetic regimen as eating resumes.

Pain plan

The mechanism is bone and soft tissue cut in a largely denervated limb, so the conventional question (which block gives the best analgesia) is the wrong one. The useful question is which technique gives a still foot with the least disturbance to a fragile circulation, a full stomach, a failing kidney and an anticoagulant, and the evidence consistently favours a peripheral block. A randomised trial of 109 diabetic foot operations found less hypotension, less fluid, lower pain scores for 48 h and earlier mobilisation and discharge with nerve blocks than with general anaesthesia. The blocks in that trial were combined femoral and sciatic blocks; the popliteal and saphenous blocks recommended here reach the same nerves more distally and were not what was tested, so the trial supports them by extension. Retrospective comparisons agree, and one reported less pneumonia and intensive care admission. None has shown a difference in mortality or limb outcome, so the claim is for a smoother perioperative course and no more.

The defensible default is a popliteal sciatic block, with a saphenous block when needed, at a reduced concentration without adrenaline, as the sole anaesthetic with minimal sedation. General anaesthesia is right for the unstable septic patient, the patient who cannot cooperate, and spread beyond the block. Dexamethasone is usually left out: the block is already prolonged by the neuropathy, rebound is uncommon, and the glucose does not need the push. No catheter.

Afterwards, simple analgesia covers most neuropathic feet. The exceptions should be anticipated. The ischaemic, sensate foot (rest pain before surgery, no neuropathy on testing) hurts like any forefoot amputation and scores 3. The patient with painful neuropathy has burning and allodynia that surgery does not improve and may aggravate; continue and review the neuropathic agents. Dressing changes may need their own plan.

Escalation triggers: new severe pain in a previously insensate foot is alarming in itself and means spreading infection, ischaemia or a collection, and the surgeon is called. Rising glucose and ketones with a wound that looks satisfactory means infection that has not been drained. Calf pain and swelling may be a thrombosis or infection tracking proximally. Persistent dense block beyond 48 h is assessed as a possible nerve injury along the pathway in nerve injury and compartment syndrome.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The fistula arm. No cuff, no cannula; plan access before the patient is on the table.
  • Dialysis timing. Elective surgery the day after dialysis; an urgent case on a dialysis day needs the potassium and the volume state known and the next session rescheduled.
  • Hypoglycaemia under sedation. Sedation hides it and autonomic neuropathy removes the warning; measure hourly.
  • Gastroparesis. Sedation deep enough to lose airway reflexes is a general anaesthetic without a protected airway.
  • Cellulitis at the needle site. Move proximally; do not needle through infected skin.
  • The sensate ischaemic foot. Pain is real, a block is strongly indicated, and revascularisation may be planned on the same admission; coordinate anticoagulation with the vascular team.
  • Sepsis that worsens after debridement. Expected in the first hours; arrange monitoring before the patient leaves recovery.
  • Local anaesthetic dose. Low body weight, renal and cardiac disease and repeated blocks days apart: calculate each time (the block menu).
  • Pre-existing nerve injury and the medicolegal record. Document the neuropathy before the block; these patients have deficits that will be found later.
  • Antibiotic bone cement. Beads or a spacer in a debrided cavity have no systemic effect.
  • Conversion to below-knee amputation. Sometimes decided on the table when the hindfoot is found to be destroyed; consent, block coverage (a femoral or adductor canal block for the medial stump) and blood should allow for it. See amputation and osteomyelitis and septic arthritis.

Key sources

The comparison of nerve blocks with general anaesthesia rests on one single-centre randomised trial, which used combined femoral and sciatic blocks, and several retrospective series, all showing better haemodynamic stability and early pain control and none showing a survival or limb benefit. Block behaviour in neuropathy is from one prospective human cohort and animal studies; the advice to reduce concentration is a reasonable inference and is not trial-proven. Safety of peripheral blocks under antithrombotic treatment is from an uncontrolled series. Outcome figures after amputation come from systematic reviews of heterogeneous cohorts and describe populations, not individuals. The Charcot data are from highly selected case series. The SGLT2 inhibitor meta-analysis is cited for the need to measure ketones, not for a withholding interval. Whether an insensate foot needs a block at all has not been studied; the position taken here is the author’s judgement.
