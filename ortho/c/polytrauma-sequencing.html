50 Polytrauma: sequencing, the second hit and when to operate

Early total care, damage control orthopaedics and early appropriate care; lactate and base deficit as the gate; trauma coagulopathy, tranexamic acid and massive transfusion; fat embolism; the order of operations; and the conversation before the surgeon starts

5Severe and prolonged

Dominant mechanism: Bone. Several fractured, periosteum-stripped bones at once, usually with rib fractures that hurt with every breath and soft tissue wounds that are reopened; staged operations reset the curve every few days, so the pain is the sum of the injuries and has no single peak. The score is for the patient, not for any one operation

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: moderate.

Block
Systemic multimodal analgesia built on opioid and low-dose ketamine in the first 24-48 h, while coagulation, compartments, the head and the spine are still open questions; femoral or fascia iliaca block for a femoral fracture from the emergency department; regional techniques for the chest wall and the limbs added as each of those questions closes
Catheter: selective Chest wall catheters (thoracic epidural, paravertebral, erector spinae or serratus plane) for three or more rib fractures once coagulation is normal and the spine is cleared; limb catheters for the staged reconstructions after the compartment window has closed; none on the night of injury in a coagulopathic patient
Alternatives: Thoracic epidural or paravertebral catheter for multiple rib fractures or flail chest in a patient with normal coagulation and a cleared spine; Erector spinae or serratus anterior plane catheter where neuraxial techniques are barred by coagulopathy, anticoagulation or spinal injury; Opioid patient-controlled analgesia with a ketamine infusion and no regional technique
Position
Supine on a radiolucent table with spinal precautions until the spine is cleared, both groins and the abdomen and chest prepared so that any cavity can be opened without repositioning; lateral and prone positions belong to the reconstruction phase, not the first night
Tourniquet
Sometimes. For life-threatening limb haemorrhage before theatre and for distal limb work in a stable patient; released as early as possible in a shocked patient, whose reperfusion load arrives on top of an existing acidosis
Cement
No
Blood loss
Already large and still running: 1-1.5 L for each closed femoral fracture, 500 mL for a tibia, 2-3 L or more for an unstable pelvic ring, before any incision. Damage control surgery adds little; early total care with a reamed nail or pelvic fixation adds 500-1500 mL
Duration
Damage control: fixators on each long bone in 30-45 min apiece and out of theatre within about 90 min of orthopaedic time. Early total care: 2-6 h, by agreement, with stopping points
Length of stay
Weeks; intensive care first, then two to five further operations over 5-14 days. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV (3 g over 120 kg), redosed at 4 h and after every 1.5 L of blood loss; open fractures follow their own clock, with the first dose due within an hour of injury
Tranexamic acid
Tranexamic acid 1 g IV over 10 min as early as possible and within 3 h of injury, then 1 g IV over 8 h; not started beyond 3 h, when CRASH-2 found more deaths from bleeding
VTE
Mechanical prophylaxis from arrival; low molecular weight heparin as soon as bleeding is controlled and any head or spinal cord injury permits, usually within 24-72 h; the polytrauma patient is among the highest-risk patients in the hospital
Antiemetic
Ondansetron 4 mg IV; not a priority in a patient going to intensive care ventilated
Tell the patient
The broken bones will be fixed in stages: a quick, safe hold on the first night, then the proper repairs over the following week once the body has recovered from the injury itself.
The trap
The decision that matters is made before induction, not at hour three: agree with the surgeon which class the patient is in, what the short version of the operation is, and which numbers will make you both stop, because once a nail is half way down a femur nobody volunteers to convert to a fixator

What the surgeon is doing and why

A patient with several injuries is not several patients with one injury each. The fractures bleed, the bleeding produces shock, the shock and the tissue damage switch on an inflammatory and coagulation response, and that response, not the fractures, is what kills in the first week. The orthopaedic surgeon’s problem is that fixing the bones helps this patient (a stabilised femur stops bleeding, stops shedding marrow fat, stops hurting, and lets the patient be sat up and nursed) and that the operation to fix them is itself an injury. Everything on this card is about choosing the size of that second injury.

The history of the idea runs in three steps, and each is still partly right.

Early total care. Through the 1970s long bone fractures in the multiply injured were put in traction until the patient was “well enough”, and the patients died of pneumonia, fat embolism and sepsis on their backs. Bone’s 1989 randomised trial of 178 femoral fractures settled it: in the multiply injured, delayed stabilisation brought more ARDS, fat embolism and pneumonia and longer intensive care. Fix everything within 24 h became the rule.

Damage control. The rule was then applied to patients it harmed: the shocked patient with a lung contusion who had a reamed femoral nail at 3 am and was on a ventilator for three weeks. The explanation is the second hit. The injury primes neutrophils and endothelium; a long operation with blood loss, hypothermia and marrow embolised to the lungs lands on a primed system and tips it into lung injury and organ failure. The EPOFF trial measured the signal (interleukin-6 rose after a primary nail and did not rise after a fixator or after delayed conversion), and Pape’s randomised trial of 165 patients gave it a clinical size: in the 44 “borderline” patients, primary nailing carried an adjusted odds ratio of 6.69 for acute lung injury, while in stable patients the primary nail meant less time on the ventilator. Damage control orthopaedics is the response: an external fixator in 30 minutes, intensive care, and the definitive operation days later.

Early appropriate care. Damage control was in turn over-applied, and stable patients were given two operations and a pin-site infection for no benefit. The current position asks one question: has this patient been resuscitated. In 1,443 patients with operated femoral, pelvic, acetabular or spinal fractures, chest injury was the strongest predictor of pulmonary complications, lactate was the most specific laboratory predictor, and correction of acidosis with fixation inside 24-48 h went with an uncomplicated course. The protocol built on it permits definitive fixation of those injuries within 36 h once the lactate is below 4.0 mmol/L, the pH is 7.25 or above, or the base excess is -5.5 mmol/L or better; in its 335-patient validation complications were 19.7% against 22.1% historically, and highest, at 34.3%, in patients operated on having met only one of the three.

So the surgeon sorts the patient into one of four classes, and the class, not the radiograph, chooses the operation:

The borderline patient is the one the argument is about and the one in whom your numbers decide: the patient with a femoral fracture and a chest injury, with bilateral contusions, with a pelvic ring injury and shock at the scene, with a lactate that fell from 6 to 3.5 mmol/L and then stopped.

The pain logic is cumulative rather than procedural. The mechanism is bone, several times over, plus the chest wall. Stabilisation is itself the most effective analgesic for a long bone fracture. What is left after it is the pain of rib fractures with every breath, of wounds reopened every two or three days, and of staged definitive operations that each reset the curve. The score of 5 describes a patient whose pain is severe for a week or more and whose regional options open one at a time as coagulation, the spine and the compartments are cleared.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 The trauma bay: measuring the first hit

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Before any orthopaedic decision the team needs to know how big the injury was and whether the patient is still bleeding: primary survey, pelvic binder, splints and traction, whole-body CT if the patient is stable enough to reach it, and a blood gas.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Four things are yours from the first minute. The time of injury, because the tranexamic acid clock runs from it: 1 g IV over 10 min, then 1 g over 8 h, given within 3 h. In CRASH-2 the benefit was largest within the first hour (death from bleeding RR 0.68), smaller at 1-3 h (RR 0.79), and reversed after 3 h (RR 1.44); in the Australasian PATCH trial prehospital dosing lowered 28-day mortality from 21.8% to 17.3% without changing functional survival at six months. The first lactate and base deficit, because every later decision is a comparison with them. Temperature, because a patient at 34 C on arrival will be at 33 C after CT unless someone acts. And a coagulation sample before the fluids go in: about a quarter of severely injured patients are coagulopathic on arrival, before any dilution, and those patients had four times the mortality.

  2. 2 Classify the patient

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs the patient named as stable, borderline, unstable or in extremis, because the class selects between a nail and a fixator. The classification rests on shock (pressure, lactate, base deficit, transfusion so far), coagulation, temperature and the soft tissue load (chest, abdomen, pelvis, head).

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Supply the trend, not the snapshot. A lactate of 3.5 mmol/L that was 6 an hour ago in a warm patient off vasopressors is a stable patient. The same figure, static for 2 h, in a patient on noradrenaline who has had six units is borderline at best. A normal blood pressure means little in a 25-year-old; the base deficit and the lactate are the honest measures of how much oxygen debt is outstanding. Say the class aloud and have the surgeon agree to it.

  3. 3 The conversation before the surgeon starts

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon is choosing an operation for a patient whose physiology may change during it, and needs to commit to a short version in advance.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Settle these before induction, in the room, with the general or trauma surgeon present if a cavity is involved. One: the class, and the numbers behind it. Two: the plan and the bail-out, stated as operations (“nail the femur if the lactate is under 4 after the laparotomy; otherwise a fixator and out”). Three: the stopping rules, as numbers both of you will act on: a lactate that rises on two samples, a temperature below 35 C, a pH below 7.25, a vasopressor dose that is climbing, visible non-surgical ooze, or a transfusion count passing an agreed figure. Four: the head. If there is a brain injury, whether intracranial pressure is being monitored and what mean pressure it needs; without a monitor in a patient with an abnormal scan, a long elective-style reconstruction is unsafe to judge. Five: the spine, cleared or not, and therefore how the patient may be positioned. Six: the compartments and who will watch them in a patient who cannot report. Seven: blood, meaning whether the massive transfusion pack is in the room rather than available. Eight: the bed afterwards.

  4. 4 Induction with resuscitation already running

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs the patient on the table quickly, still, and with the cervical spine protected.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Rapid sequence induction with manual in-line stabilisation and a videolaryngoscope; ketamine or a reduced dose of whatever you use best, with the vasopressor in the hand rather than in the drawer, because induction removes the sympathetic drive that was holding the pressure. Ventilation with positive pressure cuts venous return in an empty patient and converts a simple pneumothorax into a tension one; know where the chest drains are. Large-bore access above the diaphragm for abdominal and pelvic bleeding, an arterial line early, and warmed fluid from the first bag. Balanced transfusion from the start: the PROPPR trial of 680 patients found no mortality difference between 1:1:1 and 1:1:2 plasma, platelets and red cells, but fewer deaths from exsanguination in the first 24 h (9.2% against 14.6%) and more patients achieving haemostasis (86% against 78%). Give calcium chloride 10 mL of 10% IV with every few units, guided by the ionised calcium, because citrate arrives faster than a shocked liver clears it. Fibrinogen is replaced when it is low; empirical early cryoprecipitate for everyone did not change mortality in the 1,604-patient CRYOSTAT-2 trial. Permissive hypotension until bleeding is controlled is reasonable for the torso and wrong for the patient with a head or spinal cord injury.

  5. 5 Life before limb: haemorrhage control and the head

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The order of operations is fixed by what kills first. Cavity bleeding comes first: laparotomy or thoracotomy, and for the pelvis a binder, then preperitoneal packing or angioembolisation with an anterior fixator. An expanding intracranial haematoma is evacuated at the same time or next.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This phase belongs to the trauma and neurosurgical cards of other books; what matters here is its effect on the orthopaedic decision. The patient who needed a damage control laparotomy is by definition not a candidate for early total care tonight. Re-measure lactate, base deficit, temperature and coagulation at the end of the cavity surgery: those numbers, not the ones from the trauma bay, are the gate for what the orthopaedic surgeon may now do.

  6. 6 Limb-saving surgery

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Next come the injuries that cost the limb within hours: an arterial injury (a temporary shunt, then stabilisation, then the definitive repair), a compartment syndrome, a dislocation that is not reduced, and the contaminated open fracture.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    These are done whatever the class, in their shortest form. A shunt and a fixator take 45 min; a vein graft takes 3 h and belongs to a resuscitated patient. Fasciotomy and reperfusion add potassium and acid to a patient who already has both; see the compartment syndrome card. The debridement and the fixator are on the open fracture card.

  7. 7 The long bones: nail or fixator

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The femur is the fracture that matters most, because an unstabilised femur keeps bleeding, keeps embolising fat and keeps the patient flat. In a stable patient the surgeon nails it now (femoral shaft nailing); in an unstable one a fixator goes on in 30 min; in a borderline one the choice follows the post-resuscitation numbers. The tibia, the pelvis and the spine follow the same logic in that order; the upper limb, the foot and periarticular fractures are splinted and wait.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Reaming is the measurable second hit: marrow fat and debris reach the lungs with every pass, the pulmonary artery pressure rises, and in a patient with contused lungs the oxygenation that was adequate at the start is not adequate at the end. Watch the end-tidal carbon dioxide and the airway pressures during reaming and nail insertion and tell the surgeon what you see; the mechanism and the differential for a collapse at this moment are in the fat embolism chapter. In the borderline patient this is the step the stopping rules were written for. Bilateral femoral fractures are a marker of a much larger injury and nearly always a damage control case on the first night.

  8. 8 Re-measuring during the operation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon has agreed to stop if the patient deteriorates, and needs to be told in time to stop cleanly, between bones rather than in the middle of one.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Sample every 30-60 min and at every natural break: gas, lactate, ionised calcium, haemoglobin, and a viscoelastic test or coagulation screen where available. Announce the result against the agreed rule, not as a number in isolation. The natural breaks are the points at which the operation can end: after the fixator, after the first bone, before the second limb. A lactate rising across two samples, a temperature that will not come above 35 C, or diffuse ooze from wound edges and cannula sites means the next procedure on the list does not start. This is the part of the card that is the author’s practice rather than trial evidence; the trials set the thresholds for starting, not for stopping.

  9. 9 Intensive care and the days that follow

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon hands the patient to intensive care to be warmed, transfused, ventilated protectively and re-examined, with the conversion to definitive fixation planned for when the physiology allows.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Three things are easy to lose at handover. The compartments, in a sedated patient with a fixator on a tibia: pressure monitoring or a named examiner. Fat embolism syndrome, which declares itself at 12-72 h with hypoxia, confusion and petechiae in the patient with long bone fractures, and is more likely in the femur left in traction than in the one stabilised. And the timing of the return: the early appropriate care thresholds apply again at 24-36 h, and a patient who meets them goes back for definitive fixation then. The traditional teaching is that days 2-4, the peak of the inflammatory response, are the worst days for a long reconstruction and that the non-urgent definitive surgery sits better from about day 5; the resuscitation-based protocols have narrowed that advice to the patient who has not normalised, and for that patient it still holds.

  10. 10 Definitive reconstruction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Conversion of fixators to nails or plates (within about two weeks, before pin tracts colonise), fixation of the pelvis and acetabulum, then periarticular fractures once the soft tissues allow, then the upper limb and the face. Several teams often want the same anaesthetic.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    These are long elective-style operations in a patient who is anaemic, catabolic, on opioids, has a tracheostomy or a recently extubated airway and healing lungs. Limit the day: the author’s rule is that combined procedures are agreed by duration (about 6 h) and by blood loss, not by how many surgeons are waiting. This is the phase in which regional anaesthesia earns its place, because the coagulation is normal, the spine is cleared or fixed, and the compartment window has closed: catheters for the limb being reconstructed, and a chest wall technique if the ribs are still limiting ventilation.

Pain plan

No procedure-specific guideline covers a patient with five injuries. The plan follows the mechanism (several fractured bones and a chest wall, with staged surgery) and is constrained by four questions that are open on the first night and close over the first days: is the patient coagulopathic, are the compartments at risk, can the patient report (head injury, intubation), and is the spine cleared. Regional techniques are added as each question closes. This structure is the author’s.

First 24-48 h. Intravenous opioid by infusion or patient-controlled pump, paracetamol, and low-dose ketamine (0.1-0.2 mg/kg/h), which preserves pressure and airway reflexes and blunts the escalation of opioid requirement. A femoral or fascia iliaca block for a femoral fracture is reasonable from the emergency department, because the thigh is not the compartment risk the leg is and the block reduces the opioid given to a patient with a head or chest injury. No neuraxial technique, no deep block and no catheter while the coagulation is abnormal. NSAIDs wait for the kidneys and the bleeding to declare themselves, usually 48-72 h.

The chest. Three or more rib fractures, a flail segment, age over 65 or a falling vital capacity is where regional analgesia changes outcome rather than comfort, because the alternative is opioid, hypoventilation and pneumonia. A thoracic epidural or paravertebral catheter is the classical answer once coagulation is normal and the spine is cleared; an erector spinae or serratus anterior plane catheter is the answer when either condition is not met, which in polytrauma is often. Catheter decision: selective, and this is the commonest indication.

The limbs. For the staged reconstructions after day 5, the block and catheter follow the procedure card for that operation. Before that, the compartment rules of the tibial card apply to every fresh tibia and forearm, and a patient who cannot report pain has no warning to preserve but still needs an examinable limb or a pressure monitor.

Oral regimen shape once the gut works: regular paracetamol, an NSAID when cleared, a scheduled opioid with a written weaning plan because the course is weeks long, and an antineuropathic agent for nerve injury or amputation pain rather than by routine. Rebound is moderate and repeated: each staged operation ends with a block wearing off.

Escalation: pain out of proportion in a limb is a compartment syndrome until measured; a rising opioid requirement with fever and a wound is infection; new hypoxia and confusion on day 1-3 is fat embolism syndrome, not opioid toxicity, until the gas and the chest radiograph say otherwise.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The head injury. Hypotension and hypoxia during early fracture surgery are what worsen the brain, not the timing of the surgery itself. A patient with an abnormal head scan needs a mean arterial pressure chosen for cerebral perfusion, no permissive hypotension, and ideally a pressure monitor before a long operation; without one, the short version is the safe version.
  • The chest injury. The strongest predictor of pulmonary complications in the 1,443-patient model, and the injury that makes a reamed nail a risk. Bilateral contusions with a femoral fracture is the textbook borderline patient.
  • The pelvis. The bleeding is venous and from bone in most cases; a binder, a fixator or packing is damage control, and definitive fixation is a day 3-10 operation on the pelvic card.
  • The spine. An unstable thoracolumbar fracture is one of the four injuries the early appropriate care protocol wants fixed within 36 h of adequate resuscitation, because the patient cannot be sat up until it is; see spinal fracture fixation. A prone operation in a patient with a lung contusion is a decision for the gas, not the diary.
  • The elderly patient. Lower-energy mechanisms, anticoagulants, a blunted heart rate and less reserve; the lactate thresholds were derived in a younger population and the margin for a second hit is smaller.
  • Tranexamic acid after 3 h. Not started for the bleeding of the injury; a single dose at the induction of a later planned reconstruction is a different indication and reasonable.
  • Hypothermia and acidosis as surgical decisions. Every exposed limb, every litre of irrigation and every hour on the table lowers temperature. A temperature below 35 C is a reason to stop, not a nursing observation.
  • Too many surgeons. Simultaneous operating by two teams halves the time under anaesthesia and is worth arranging; sequential operating by four teams through a single night is early total care by accident.
  • The stable patient over-treated. A warm, resuscitated patient with an isolated femur and a wrist does not need a fixator and a second anaesthetic; damage control has complications of its own.

Key sources

The framework is well supported for the femur and thin elsewhere. Bone’s trial established that delay harms; EPOFF and Pape’s borderline trial established that early definitive surgery harms a subset; the early appropriate care series supply resuscitation thresholds from one centre’s prospective but non-randomised cohorts. Resuscitation is better evidenced: CRASH-2 with its timing analysis, PATCH in an Australasian system, PROPPR for ratios, and CRYOSTAT-2 as a caution against adding empirical components. The intra-operative stopping rules, the day-count for reconstruction and the staged opening of regional analgesia are the author’s practice built on those, and are labelled as such above.
