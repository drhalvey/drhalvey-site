84 Nonunion surgery and iliac crest bone graft harvest

Takedown and debridement of an ununited fracture, removal of failed metalwork, compression plating or exchange nailing, and autograft from the anterior or posterior iliac crest or by reamer-irrigator-aspirator from the femur; the induced membrane (Masquelet) variant

4Severe

Dominant mechanism: Periosteum. Two wounds. At the nonunion: a re-opened, scarred approach, metalwork levered out, fibrous tissue excised to bleeding bone, cortex chiselled into petals and a new plate applied under compression along stripped bone. At the pelvis: muscle lifted subperiosteally off the iliac crest and cancellous bone gouged from between the tables, a site pulled on by every step, cough and turn in bed. The donor site is frequently the worse of the two and is the part that becomes chronic

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Block
A regional block for the nonunion limb chosen as for the original fracture at that site, plus dedicated harvest-site analgesia: a transversus abdominis plane or quadratus lumborum block for the anterior crest, and the surgeon's long-acting infiltration with a catheter left under the repaired muscle for a large harvest
Catheter: selective A harvest-site catheter for repeated local anaesthetic boluses over 24-48 h when a large anterior or posterior crest harvest is taken, and a limb catheter when the nonunion operation itself is a long re-plating of the femur, tibia or humerus in an opioid-tolerant patient; neither for a small clavicle or forearm nonunion with a thimble of graft
Alternatives: Erector spinae plane block or surgeon's infiltration and catheter for the posterior crest; Spinal or epidural anaesthesia for a lower limb nonunion with an anterior crest harvest (covers both wounds for its duration); Reamer-irrigator-aspirator harvest from the femur, which removes the pelvic wound and replaces it with thigh ache and blood loss
Position
Supine with a bump under the ipsilateral buttock and the anterior iliac crest prepared and draped in a separate field, usually on the same side as the nonunion; prone or lateral first for a posterior crest harvest and then a turn; supine on a radiolucent or traction table for reamer harvest from the femur
Tourniquet
Sometimes. Used for tibial, forearm and distal humeral nonunions where the limb allows; released before closure so the debrided bone ends can be seen to bleed, which is the surgeon's test of an adequate debridement
Cement
Sometimes. Antibiotic-loaded cement beads or a block spacer in the first stage of an infected or defect nonunion (induced membrane technique); small volume, hand-packed, no pressurisation and no cement implantation syndrome
Blood loss
300-800 mL for a long bone nonunion with crest harvest; the reamer-irrigator-aspirator adds a mean of about 800 mL that goes into the suction canister, not the swabs; femoral nonunion with plate removal can exceed 1 L. Group and hold for all, crossmatch for the femur and for any reamer harvest
Duration
120-240 min: the removal of broken metalwork is the unpredictable part; add 30-40 min for a crest harvest and a turn for the posterior crest
Length of stay
2-5 nights; mobilisation is often limited by the donor site more than by the limb. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Withheld until the deep tissue samples have been taken where infection is suspected, then cefazolin 2 g IV (3 g over 120 kg) or the agent agreed with microbiology; given before incision as usual when infection has been excluded
Tranexamic acid
TXA 1 g IV at induction, repeated at 3 h or at closure for femoral nonunion and for reamer harvest
VTE
Low molecular weight heparin from the evening of surgery for lower limb nonunions through the period of restricted weight-bearing; mechanical only for the upper limb unless other risk factors
Antiemetic
Dexamethasone 8 mg IV (after discussion with the surgeon if infection is the working diagnosis) and ondansetron 4 mg IV
Tell the patient
You will have two sore places: the bone that has been re-fixed, and the hip where the graft was taken, and many people find the hip the worse of the two for the first few days, especially when walking, coughing or turning over.
The trap
The harvest is treated as a footnote and is often the main pain, with chronic donor-site pain in about one in seven crest harvests: a single infiltration wears off in hours, so the harvest site needs its own plan (a plane block or a catheter with repeat doses), and if the surgeon uses the reamer-irrigator-aspirator instead, the trade is pelvic pain for close to a litre of blood drawn silently into a suction canister

What the surgeon is doing and why

A fracture that has not healed has failed for one of two reasons, and the operation follows from which. In a hypertrophic nonunion the bone is alive and trying: there is abundant callus that cannot bridge because the fracture is moving. Biology is intact and mechanics are wrong, so the surgeon adds stability (a compression plate, or a larger reamed nail in exchange for the old one) and no graft is needed. In an atrophic nonunion the bone ends are thin, sclerotic and quiet, joined by fibrous tissue: the blood supply was destroyed by the injury or the first operation, or the patient smokes, is diabetic or was infected. Mechanics and biology both have to be rebuilt. An infected nonunion is its own category and is staged.

For the atrophic nonunion the surgeon reopens the old approach through scar, removes the failed metalwork (broken screws and stripped heads make this the least predictable part of the case), takes several deep samples for culture before any antibiotic is given, excises the fibrous tissue until the bone ends bleed, reopens the sealed medullary canals with a drill, and raises thin petals of cortex on either side of the fracture with a chisel so that vascular bone flaps surround it. Deformity is corrected. A new plate is applied in compression, or a nail exchanged. Then living bone is brought in: autograft, which supplies cells, growth factors and scaffold together.

The graft has to come from somewhere. The anterior iliac crest is the default because the patient is already supine: an incision behind the anterior superior iliac spine, muscle lifted subperiosteally from the inner or outer table, and cancellous bone scooped out with gouges and curettes, or a block taken with the cortex. The posterior crest yields more but requires the patient prone or lateral and then a turn. The alternative is the reamer-irrigator-aspirator: a reamer with built-in irrigation and suction is passed down the femoral canal, and the reamings are trapped in a filter. In the randomised trial it yielded as much graft as the posterior crest, in less time, with similar union and significantly less donor-site pain.

The pain logic has to account for two wounds. The nonunion site is periosteal: a long bone stripped, chiselled and plated, in a limb that has often been painful for a year, in a patient who is frequently already on opioids. The crest is muscle torn off bone at a site that cannot be rested, since the abdominal wall and the hip abductors both attach to it, so walking, coughing and rolling over all pull on it. Trials in other specialties that use crest graft make the point that donor pain is often worse than the pain of the primary operation, and it persists: in pooled comparisons chronic donor-site pain followed about 14% of crest harvests and none of the reamer harvests. Together that is a 4, with a high rebound risk because two blocks wear off on the same night.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Work-up and the plan for samples

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs to know whether the nonunion is infected, because an infected nonunion is debrided and staged, not grafted. Inflammatory markers, imaging and sometimes a biopsy inform it, and at operation five or more deep samples are taken with clean instruments before antibiotics.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Ask at the briefing whether antibiotics are to be withheld until sampling, and say so aloud at the time-out so that the routine dose is not given by reflex. Dexamethasone is a reasonable thing to discuss in the same breath if infection is the working diagnosis; a large trial in general surgical patients found no increase in wound infection with 8 mg, but some surgeons prefer to avoid it here. The patient profile matters: smoking, diabetes, long-term opioids, vitamin D deficiency and months of immobility are all common. Establish the baseline opioid dose; see the opioid-tolerant patient.

  2. 2 Anaesthetic plan by site

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The operation is two to four hours and involves two operative fields.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    General anaesthesia is the default for the upper limb, the long case and the reamer harvest. Lower limb nonunion with an anterior crest harvest can be done under spinal or combined spinal-epidural anaesthesia, which covers both wounds. Add the limb block that the original fracture would have had, from the relevant fracture card, and plan the harvest site separately (step 6). For the tibia, where a compartment syndrome is possible after a long reconstruction, follow the dilute-block rules on the tibial card. Two cannulae; an arterial line for femoral nonunion and for any reamer harvest in a patient with limited reserve. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV at induction.

  3. 3 Positioning with the crest in the field

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A bump under the buttock on the harvest side lifts the crest; both fields are prepared and draped separately so that instruments used on a possibly infected nonunion never touch the donor site. For a posterior crest harvest the patient is positioned prone or lateral first, the graft taken and the wound closed, then the patient is turned and re-draped.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The turn for a posterior harvest costs 20-30 min and all the usual prone precautions for a short period. If a transversus abdominis plane or quadratus lumborum block is planned, do it before draping, on the correct side, which is not always the side of the nonunion. Forced-air warming has less skin to work with when the torso and a limb are both prepared, so start early and warm the fluids.

  4. 4 Exposure and removal of metalwork

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The old incision is reopened and the plate or nail exposed through scar. Screws that have cold-welded, stripped or broken are drilled out with trephines or burrs; a broken nail needs hooks and extraction kits.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This phase sets the duration. Scar bleeds more than virgin tissue and a tourniquet, where one can be used, hides the loss until it is released. Metal debris from burring out screws generates heat and a lot of irrigation. Samples are taken here, from around the implant and the fibrous tissue: give the antibiotic when the surgeon confirms the last sample is off the table.

  5. 5 Debridement, canal opening and decortication

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Fibrous tissue and dead bone are removed back to cortex that shows punctate bleeding. The canals are reopened to let marrow elements reach the fracture, and the cortex on each side is raised in thin attached petals. If a tourniquet is up it is released now so the bleeding test means something.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Decortication of a long bone is the periosteal injury that drives the pain at the limb, and is done with a chisel and mallet: stimulation rises sharply even in a blocked limb if the block is patchy. Tourniquet release brings the usual metabolic load and the first honest view of blood loss.

  6. 6 Anterior iliac crest harvest

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The incision is placed over the crest at least 2-3 cm behind the anterior superior iliac spine to spare the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve and to avoid weakening the spine itself. The periosteum is incised along the crest and the muscle elevated from one table; cancellous bone is harvested with gouges through a cortical window, or a full-thickness block is cut. Bone wax or a haemostatic agent is pressed into the bed and the periosteum and muscle closed tightly over it.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the wound to plan for. The skin and the muscle attachments here are supplied by the lateral cutaneous branches of the subcostal and iliohypogastric nerves and the periosteum by local branches, which is why a posterior transversus abdominis plane or quadratus lumborum block works; two small randomised trials found lower donor-site pain and less morphine over 24 h with a plane block. Ask the surgeon to infiltrate the periosteum and muscle with long-acting local anaesthetic before closing, and for a large harvest to leave a fine catheter under the muscle repair: a trial of repeated bupivacaine boluses through the site lowered pain and morphine, whereas a trial of a single injection showed no benefit at all. The cancellous bed oozes after closure, so a haematoma, and occasionally a late haemoglobin fall, comes from here.

  7. 7 Posterior crest harvest (variant)

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Through a curved or vertical incision over the posterior superior iliac spine, staying within about 8 cm of it to protect the superior cluneal nerves and well above the sciatic notch, the outer table is opened and a larger volume of cancellous bone removed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    More graft for more pain later: in the systematic review, chronic donor-site pain and sensory disturbance were commoner after posterior than anterior harvest. The cluneal nerves are dorsal rami, so an erector spinae plane block or the surgeon’s infiltration and catheter are the options, not an abdominal wall block. Deep to the notch lie the superior gluteal artery and the sciatic nerve; a sudden brisk bleed during a posterior harvest is that artery.

  8. 8 Reamer-irrigator-aspirator harvest (variant)

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Through a small incision over the greater trochanter, or through the knee, a guidewire is passed down the femur and a single pass of an oversized reamer head, with irrigation flowing down the shaft and suction drawing the reamings back, fills an inline filter with 30-90 mL of graft.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The suction does not distinguish graft from blood. In the meta-analysis the mean blood loss was about 800 mL, the mean fall in haemoglobin 3.7 g/dL, and one patient in ten was transfused. Watch the canister, subtract the irrigation, and call the volume to the surgeon every few minutes; the remedy is to stop suction whenever the reamer is not advancing. Have blood available before starting. Reaming also sends marrow emboli, although the negative pressure reduces canal pressure compared with standard reaming. An eccentric reamer can thin or perforate the cortex and the femur can fracture then or in the following weeks, which is why the patient may be told to protect weight-bearing on the donor leg.

  9. 9 Fixation under compression and grafting

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The plate is contoured and applied with a tensioning device or eccentric screws to squeeze the bone ends together, or a larger nail is driven after reaming. The graft is packed around and into the nonunion and under the cortical petals.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The quiet end of the case. Check a haemoglobin if the femur or a reamer was involved. This is the time to make sure the limb block has been done or topped up and the harvest-site plan has actually happened, because closure of the crest often occurs earlier in the case while attention is elsewhere.

  10. 10 Induced membrane technique (variant)

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    For an infected or segmental defect the surgeon stages it. First operation: radical debridement, stabilisation and a block of antibiotic-loaded cement in the gap. Over six to eight weeks a vascular membrane forms around the cement. Second operation: the membrane is incised, the cement removed, and the cavity filled with a large volume of autograft, which is the commonest reason for a reamer harvest.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Two anaesthetics some weeks apart, the second with the big harvest. The cement is hand-moulded in an open wound and carries no cement implantation risk. Antibiotic choices between stages are the microbiologist’s, and the patient may arrive for the second stage on a long course with altered renal function.

  11. 11 Closure and the first days

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Layered closure of both wounds; drains are surgeon-dependent. Weight-bearing is restricted for the limb according to the fixation.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The trajectory on this card is 3 on the first evening with both blocks working, 6 on day 1, 5 on day 2, 4 on day 3 and 3 at a week. Chart the catheter boluses or infusion for the harvest site if one was left. Day 1 haemoglobin for femoral cases and all reamer harvests.

Pain plan

Write the plan as two columns. For the limb: the same block the fracture card recommends for a fixation at that site, single shot in most cases, with a catheter when the operation is a long femoral, tibial or humeral re-plating in a patient already on opioids. For the harvest site: assume a single infiltration will have gone by the first night, because the one trial of a single injection found no benefit, and choose something that lasts. For the anterior crest the defensible default is a posterior transversus abdominis plane or quadratus lumborum block with 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.375% before incision, plus the surgeon’s infiltration at closure, and for a harvest larger than a few cubic centimetres a catheter under the muscle repair with intermittent boluses of ropivacaine 0.2% 10 mL every 6-8 h for 24-48 h, within the total daily dose once the limb block is counted. This is supported by small randomised trials for each component and assembled by the author; there is no trial of the combination in nonunion surgery.

Systemic analgesia is constrained by the surgeon. NSAIDs are the best drug for bone pain and are the drug most nonunion surgeons forbid, on the strength of observational associations between NSAID use and failure of union; in a patient whose operation exists because a bone would not heal, the author does not argue the point. That leaves paracetamol, an opioid (continuing the baseline dose and adding to it), and adjuncts: intra-operative ketamine with a low-dose infusion for 24-48 h in the opioid-tolerant patient, and dexamethasone where the surgeon agrees. Many of these patients have had neuropathic limb pain for months and are already on a gabapentinoid or an antidepressant, which continues.

Rebound is high: a limb block and a plane block recede together at 12-18 h in a patient with reduced opioid responsiveness. Escalation triggers: donor-site pain with a swelling over the crest and a falling haemoglobin is a haematoma; burning or numbness on the outer thigh is the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve and is usually temporary; groin or thigh pain on weight-bearing after a reamer harvest is a femoral fracture until imaged; and after an anterior harvest a sudden pain at the front of the pelvis on standing is an avulsion of the anterior superior iliac spine. Donor-site pain still troublesome at six weeks should be named and treated as the start of a chronic pain problem.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Small nonunions. A clavicle, forearm or scaphoid nonunion with a small local or crest graft is a shorter operation with a score nearer 3; the scaphoid is on the wrist arthroscopy and scaphoid card.
  • Hypertrophic nonunion. Stability only, no graft: an exchange nailing is the fracture nailing over again, with the reaming, on the femoral or tibial card.
  • Infected nonunion. Staged, with antibiotics withheld until sampling, more blood loss from inflamed tissue and repeat visits; see osteomyelitis and septic arthritis.
  • Alternatives to autograft. Allograft, demineralised matrix, synthetic substitutes and bone morphogenetic protein avoid the harvest and the donor pain; the choice is the surgeon’s and depends on the defect and the biology. A case booked with a crest harvest sometimes proceeds without one, and the reverse.
  • Reamer harvest in a small or osteoporotic femur. Higher risk of perforation and fracture; some surgeons add a prophylactic nail.
  • The patient. Long-term opioids, smoking, depression and a year off work are the norm. Expect pain reports above the card’s trajectory, plan with the pain service before the day, and agree a weaning plan before discharge.
  • Compartment syndrome. Possible after long tibial or forearm reconstructions; keep limb blocks dilute where the surgeon is concerned, as the compartment syndrome card describes.
  • Wrong-side harvest block. The crest used is the surgeon’s choice on the day and may be opposite to the limb. Confirm before the block.

Key sources

The harvest literature is better than the nonunion literature: a randomised trial and two meta-analyses compare crest and reamer harvest and agree on equal union and less donor pain with the reamer, and a large meta-analysis quantifies the reamer’s blood loss. The analgesic trials for the donor site are small, come mostly from spinal and maxillofacial surgery, and are consistent in direction: repeated or plane-based local anaesthetic helps, a single infiltration does not last. The NSAID restriction rests on observational data. The pain score, the trajectory and the two-column plan are the author’s synthesis.
