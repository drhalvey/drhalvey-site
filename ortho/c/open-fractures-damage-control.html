45 Open fractures, external fixation and damage control orthopaedics

Gustilo grading, the antibiotic clock, debridement and irrigation, spanning and temporary external fixation, the second hit and the conversion decision in the polytrauma patient

Block
General anaesthesia with a rapid sequence induction and multimodal analgesia including ketamine; a dilute single-shot peripheral block for the limb (popliteal sciatic and adductor canal for the leg, brachial plexus for the arm) only after a documented compartment examination and with a surveillance plan the surgeon has agreed
Catheter: rarely Not in the first 48 h of a fresh high-energy fracture, because compartment syndrome occurs in open fractures as often as in closed ones and the catheter is the device that has hidden it; reasonable later for the flap day or the staged definitive fixation, once the window has closed and the limb is examined daily
Alternatives: Brachial plexus block for the open forearm or humerus, dilute and single shot, with the same surveillance logic; No block at all for the night-time damage control fixator in a polytrauma patient who needs serial examination of the limb or the head; Surgeon's infiltration of pin sites and wound edges with long-acting local anaesthetic
Position
Supine on a radiolucent table with the whole limb in the field and the C-arm from the opposite side; the leg on a bolster or brought over the side for a posterior wound; lateral or prone only for a posterior pelvic or scapular wound, which is rare
Tourniquet
Sometimes. Applied and left down in most hands: the surgeon judges muscle viability by colour, consistency, contractility and bleeding, and an inflated cuff removes two of the four; some surgeons inflate briefly for a bloodless look at a vessel or nerve, and never for a limb with a vascular injury
Cement
No
Blood loss
The fracture and the wound have bled before you meet them: 500 mL for a tibia, 1-1.5 L for a femur, more for a pelvis. Debridement and a fixator add 200-500 mL; a fasciotomy or a vascular repair at the same sitting adds much more. Group and hold for an isolated limb; crossmatch for a Gustilo IIIC, a femur or a polytrauma patient
Duration
60-120 min for debridement, irrigation and a fixator on one limb; 30-45 min for a damage control fixator alone; several hours when a vascular repair, a fasciotomy or a flap is done at the same sitting
Length of stay
Set by the soft tissue, not the bone: a few nights for a Gustilo I or II fracture nailed and closed at the first operation; weeks for a IIIB with a frame and a flap, with returns to theatre every 48-72 h in between. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV (3 g over 120 kg) as soon as possible after injury and within the hour, almost always already given in the emergency department and redosed for the operation; Gram-negative cover (gentamicin) added for Gustilo III and heavily contaminated wounds in most protocols; duration set by the wound and the protocol rather than by a day count, and stopped soon after soft tissue cover
Tranexamic acid
Tranexamic acid 1 g IV within 3 h of injury for the bleeding patient, then 1 g over 8 h (CRASH-2); 1 g IV at induction for the debridement is reasonable
VTE
Low molecular weight heparin from the night of injury or surgery when the bleeding risk allows; mechanical prophylaxis on the uninjured leg; a frame and a flap mean weeks of immobility and the prophylaxis runs for all of them
Antiemetic
Ondansetron 4 mg IV; dexamethasone 8 mg IV is reasonable (a single dose did not raise the surgical site infection rate in the 8,880-patient PADDI trial, which was elective surgery and not open fractures) but it prolongs any block, so it is withheld when a short examinable block is the aim
Tell the patient
The first operation cleans the wound and holds the bone with a frame on the outside; most people need two or three more trips to theatre over the next week to check the wound and cover it, and the leg hurts most in the first two days and each time it is reopened.
The trap
The antibiotic is the intervention that matters most and it is not yours to delay: in type III open tibias an antibiotic more than 66 min after injury carried nearly four times the odds of deep infection and wound cover beyond five days seven times, so the question in the anaesthetic room is when the first dose went in, not whether the patient is fasted

What the surgeon is doing and why

An open fracture is a soft tissue injury with a bone in it. The energy that broke the bone has also torn the muscle, stripped the periosteum, devascularised the skin and pushed road, soil or water into the wound; the bacteria that matter arrive at the moment of injury, not on the ward. Everything the surgeon does in the first week follows from that: the wound is treated before the fracture, the fracture is held in a way that adds no soft tissue damage, and the aim is a clean, covered, vascularised wound within 72 h so that a fracture-related infection, the complication that turns a six-week injury into a two-year one, never starts.

The Gustilo grade is the shared language and it grades the soft tissue, not the bone. Type I is a clean wound under 1 cm; type II is 1-10 cm without extensive soft tissue damage; type III is extensive soft tissue damage, heavy contamination, a segmental fracture, a farm injury or a high-velocity gunshot, split into IIIA (enough periosteum and muscle left to cover the bone), IIIB (periosteal stripping and exposed bone that needs a flap) and IIIC (an arterial injury that needs repair). The grade is assigned in theatre after debridement, because the skin wound on the trolley says little about the dead muscle beneath it. The grade predicts infection: in the original 1,025-fracture series the type III infection rate was 44% before the authors’ protocol and 9% under it, and in a 642-patient series from 2020 deep infection ran at 2.1% in type I, 7.6% in type II and 14.6% in type III.

The surgeon’s aims are in a fixed order, and the first is not surgical. Antibiotics within an hour of injury: in 137 type III open tibias, an antibiotic more than 66 min after injury carried an odds ratio of 3.78 for deep infection and wound cover beyond five days an odds ratio of 7.39; with both done early the infection rate was 2.8%, with both delayed 40.5%, and time to debridement did not predict infection at all. Then photograph, seal and splint the wound. Then debride in theatre: the six-hour rule is dead (16 cohorts, 3,539 fractures, no association between debridement timing and infection at any threshold or grade) and the BOAST standard replaced it with a stratified clock: immediately for agricultural, aquatic or sewage contamination and for vascular compromise or compartment syndrome, within 12 h for other high-energy open fractures, within 24 h for low-energy ones. Then irrigate with saline at low pressure, because the 2,447-patient FLOW trial found pressure made no difference and soap was worse than saline. Then stabilise, because a stable fracture lets the soft tissues heal and is the second antibiotic: most Gustilo I to IIIA fractures whose wound can be covered at once are fixed definitively at the first operation (the BOAST standard permits internal fixation only when cover can follow immediately), and the rest get an external fixator, pins through skin and both cortices away from the wound and joined by bars, which holds length and alignment without adding stripping, goes on in 30 min at night, can span a joint, and is converted to a nail or plate once the wound is covered. Then cover within 72 h, by delayed primary closure or a flap, as a combined orthoplastic decision, with a second look every 48-72 h until then.

The same fixator does a second job in the polytrauma patient, and that is where damage control enters. The injured patient has had a first hit: tissue destruction, shock, hypoxia and the inflammatory response they provoke. A long operation with its own blood loss, hypothermia and marrow embolisation is a second hit, and in the patient whose lungs and endothelium are already primed it is what tips them into acute lung injury and organ failure. The EPOFF trial measured it: in 35 stable polytrauma patients randomised to primary femoral nailing or a fixator, interleukin-6 rose over the 24 h after the nail and did not rise after the fixator, nor after the later conversion. Pape’s randomised trial of 165 polytrauma patients turned the marker into an outcome: in the 44 borderline patients, primary nailing carried an adjusted odds ratio of 6.69 for acute lung injury against a fixator with a nail later. Damage control orthopaedics is a fixator in minutes for the unstable or borderline patient, resuscitation in intensive care, and definitive fixation once the physiology says so; the Early Appropriate Care protocol puts numbers on “says so” (a lactate below 4.0 mmol/L, a pH of 7.25 or above, or a base excess of -5.5 mmol/L or better, with fixation within 36 h once met). The whole decision is on the polytrauma card; this card covers the fixator and the wound.

The pain logic. The mechanism is bone: a fractured, mobile long bone with its periosteum stripped by the injury, which the frame splints, so most of the fracture pain is gone when the pins are tight. What remains is the debrided wound (muscle and skin cut back to bleeding tissue and left open), the pin sites (drill holes through cortex with skin and muscle moving over them) and the swelling of a limb that bled into itself. The score is 3, with a tail that does not fall as cleanly as a closed fracture’s because the wound is reopened every two or three days and the flap day is a second operation. The compartment caveat that shapes the tibial card applies in full: an open fracture does not decompress its compartments, and the 198-fracture series found compartment syndrome in 9.1% of open tibial shafts, rising with the severity of the injury.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 The emergency department: the antibiotic, the photograph and the splint

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the antibiotic in, the wound photographed and sealed under saline gauze and film, the limb realigned and splinted with pulses and nerves documented before and after, and the vascular and compartment status settled.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    You will rarely be there, but you inherit the consequences. Find out the time of injury and of the first antibiotic and write both on the chart, because the dose is due again for the operation and the hour-long window is the one number in this card that changes outcome. Sedation or a block for the reduction is reasonable; a dense long-acting block before anyone has examined the compartments is not, and a femoral or fascia iliaca block for a femoral fracture is the exception because the thigh is not the compartment site the leg is. Tetanus (a booster for a tetanus-prone wound, immunoglobulin as well if the course is incomplete or unknown) is the department’s job and worth a question.

  2. 2 Classifying the patient, not the fracture

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Before the surgeon chooses between a fixator and a nail, the trauma team has to say whether the patient is stable, borderline or unstable: lactate and base deficit, temperature, coagulation, the chest and head injuries, the transfusion count. A borderline patient gets a fixator and a second operation in days; a stable one gets the whole operation tonight.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Have this conversation in the trauma bay with the numbers in front of both of you, because once the patient is asleep the surgeon’s default is to finish. A patient under the Early Appropriate Care lines who is warm, not coagulopathic and off vasopressors can have definitive fixation; in the 335-patient series the complication rate was 19.7% against 22.1% historically, and highest in patients who met only one of the three. A patient who meets none of them is a damage control patient whatever the wound looks like. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV within 3 h of injury if there is bleeding, then 1 g over 8 h: in the 20,211-patient CRASH-2 trial it reduced all-cause mortality from 16.0% to 14.5% and death from bleeding from 5.7% to 4.9% with no excess of thrombosis. Crossmatch for a femur, a IIIC or any patient with another injury.

  3. 3 Induction: the full stomach, the lines and the block decision

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs a still patient and a relaxed limb for the reduction; for a fixator, nothing more; for a definitive nail, the femoral or tibial card follows.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Rapid sequence induction, with the fasting clock counted from the injury rather than the last meal, because pain, opioids and shock stopped gastric emptying at the roadside. Two large cannulae; an arterial line for the polytrauma patient, the IIIC and any case that may become a vascular repair. The block decision is the one the Association of Anaesthetists wrote its 2021 guideline for: a dilute single-shot peripheral block without adjuncts, placed after a documented compartment examination and with a surveillance plan the surgeon has agreed, is not linked to delayed diagnosis when the observation is competent; a dense, long-acting block or a neuraxial technique is to be avoided; and neither of you can veto the other, which means deciding together before the drapes go on. For the night-time damage control fixator in a patient who will be examined hourly for a head injury and a leg, the default is no block and a multimodal general anaesthetic with ketamine. Redose the cefazolin if more than 4 h have passed since the emergency department dose, and add the Gram-negative cover the protocol asks for in a type III wound.

  4. 4 Positioning, the tourniquet that stays down and the preparation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The whole limb is in the field, joint above to joint below, because the pins go in well away from the wound and the extensions run along fasciotomy lines; the C-arm comes from the opposite side. The skin is prepared with the splint off and the fracture held by an assistant.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Taking the splint off is the most painful moment of the day and the first time the fragments have moved freely since the reduction; it needs depth before it happens, and the fracture bleeds again. A tourniquet is usually applied and left uninflated, because muscle viability is judged by colour, consistency, contractility and bleeding and an inflated cuff hides two of the four; a limb with a vascular injury gets no cuff at all. Warm the patient from the start: cold on arrival, exposed from hip to toe, and about to have litres of room-temperature saline poured over the limb.

  5. 5 Wound extension, debridement and irrigation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The wound is extended so that the whole zone of injury can be seen and the extension does not compromise a later flap. Dead and contaminated skin, fat, fascia and muscle are excised back to tissue that bleeds and contracts, the bone ends are delivered into the wound and cleaned, and cortical fragments with no soft tissue attachment are discarded, because dead bone is a sequestrum and a culture plate. The Gustilo grade is assigned now. Then litres of saline at low pressure wash out what the knife has loosened; high pressure drives bacteria into tissue planes and soap damages the cells trying to heal, which is why FLOW moved practice away from both.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Bleeding muscle by design: a debridement that does not bleed has not been done, and the loss from a type III leg is steady rather than dramatic over 30-60 min. The moment the periosteum turns out to be stripped over several centimetres is the moment a IIIA becomes a IIIB, the plastic surgeon is telephoned and the plan for the frame and the cover changes. Muscle contractility under cautery is part of the assessment, and a request to hold off relaxant until the muscle has been tested is reasonable. The irrigation is heat loss and nothing else.

  6. 6 Stabilisation: the fixator

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Half-pins are drilled through small incisions into the bone away from the zone of injury, two above and two below the fracture, and connected with clamps and bars; across a joint for a periarticular fracture, the frame holds the limb out to length by ligamentotaxis.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Drilling through cortical bone under imaging: quick, bloody at each pin and painful afterwards in the way a drill hole through periosteum is. A frame across a knee or ankle fixes the foot and makes the compartments harder to examine, which is how the late diagnoses in the spanning fixator series happened. A damage control fixator is 30-45 min from skin to bars and is the point at which, in the borderline patient, the operation stops; a surgeon who proposes to ream a femur in a patient with a lactate of 6 mmol/L and a chest injury is proposing the second hit the randomised trial put an odds ratio of 6.69 on, and that conversation is better had now than in intensive care.

  7. 7 The compartments

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon examines the compartments at the end of the debridement and measures pressures in a high-energy tibia or forearm; an open wound does not decompress a compartment, and a fasciotomy at this sitting beats one tomorrow.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The examination at the end of the case is the baseline for the next 48 h and the moment the surveillance starts, so write the time and the findings. A dilute single-shot block will have receded by the evening; dexamethasone and perineural adjuncts are withheld for that reason. If a fasciotomy is done, the limb moves to the compartment syndrome card, with its blood loss, its open wounds and its own pain score.

  8. 8 The vascular injury: the IIIC limb

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A pulseless limb with a fracture is revascularised within 6 h of injury or the muscle dies: a temporary intraluminal shunt by the vascular surgeon, the fixator so a mobile fracture cannot disrupt the repair, then the definitive vein graft, and a prophylactic fasciotomy in most hands because the reperfused compartments will swell.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A long case booked as a short one, with two teams, no tourniquet, a reperfusion event when the shunt opens (potassium, acid, myoglobin, a fall in pressure) and a blood loss measured in units. Heparin at the surgeon’s request with the head and the pelvis considered first; an arterial line, warmed products and a plan for the compartments. The unstable patient here is the one in whom the amputation conversation is had early; the BOAST standard puts delayed primary amputation within 72 h after a multidisciplinary assessment, and the amputation card covers what follows.

  9. 9 Dressing, the open wound and the plan for cover

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A type I or II wound is often closed at the first operation; a type III is left open or loosely closed over the bone, with the extensions closed, under a negative pressure dressing that is not disturbed on the ward. The orthoplastic plan is written: second look at 48 h, definitive cover by 72 h.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The negative pressure canister collects blood nobody counts; check it in recovery. Awake extubation after a rapid sequence induction. The first night’s analgesia is opioid-based and multimodal with ketamine, and the pain score the ward acts on is the one that rises despite it; the compartment surveillance chart is the document that matters tonight.

  10. 10 The second look, the flap and the conversion

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The patient returns at 48-72 h for a repeat debridement; doubtful muscle has declared itself, and the wound is closed, covered with a local or free flap, or dressed again. The fixator is converted to a nail or plate once the wound is covered and before the pin tracts colonise, in most hands within two weeks, and in the polytrauma patient after the inflammatory peak of the first days has passed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Each return is a short general anaesthetic in a patient who is now fasted, on opioids and often anaemic; a block is reasonable at the second look once the compartments have been examined and recorded, and a catheter for the flap day. The flap day is a different anaesthetic: 4-8 h of microsurgery, a warm, well-filled, vasodilated patient with a mean pressure the plastic surgeon has named, vasopressors avoided where possible, a haemoglobin not allowed to fall far, and a urinary catheter. The conversion to a nail in a polytrauma patient is the second-hit question asked again; in EPOFF the delayed conversion did not raise the markers the primary nail did.

Pain plan

The mechanism is a fractured, periosteum-stripped bone splinted by a frame under a debrided wound that is reopened every two or three days, so the plan is a multimodal general anaesthetic with a regional component chosen for the compartment risk rather than for the pain, and a catheter held back until the window has closed. There is no PROSPECT guideline for open fractures; the structure is the author’s, built on the Association of Anaesthetists’ 2021 guideline for lower leg trauma.

The block. For the leg, a single-shot popliteal sciatic block with an adductor canal or femoral block, dilute (ropivacaine 0.2% or equivalent), no perineural adjunct and no intravenous dexamethasone, placed after the compartment examination has been written down and with a surveillance chart the surgeon has agreed; the aim is a block that has receded by the evening examination. For the arm, a brachial plexus block on the same terms, because the forearm has compartments too. For the night-time damage control fixator in a polytrauma patient, no block: the patient needs serial examination of the limb and often the head, the operation is 40 min, and the first 48 h belong to the surveillance. This is the author’s judgement, agreed with the surgeon before the case.

The catheter. Rarely, and never in the first 48 h of a fresh high-energy fracture: the large tibial series found every missed compartment syndrome in its blocked group had a catheter, and the open fracture carries the same compartment risk as the closed one. It earns its place later, for the flap day, when the limb has been examined daily for a week, the case is long and a sympathectomised warm limb is what the plastic surgeon wants, and for the staged definitive fixation once the window has closed.

The oral regimen shape: regular paracetamol; an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor where the surgeon accepts it (many withhold NSAIDs after a high-energy tibial or femoral fracture for fear of non-union, an observational association and their call); a regular opioid for the first 48-72 h with a patient-controlled analgesia pump for the young high-energy patient; and low-dose ketamine for the unblocked and the opioid-tolerant patient. Rebound is moderate: when the dilute block recedes the frame is holding the bone and the pain is a sore wound and sore pins, severe for an hour or two until the regular regimen catches up. Each return to theatre resets the clock by a day.

Escalation: pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch of the toes or fingers, a tense compartment or a rising opioid requirement in the first 48 h is a compartment syndrome until the pressure says otherwise, and the response is the surgeon and a measurement, not a rescue block and not more morphine. Pain that rises at day 3-5 with a wound that smells, a fever and a rising C-reactive protein is a fracture-related infection and the surgeon sees it before the dressing is changed.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The isolated low-energy open fracture. Debrided, fixed and closed in one sitting; the closed fracture’s anaesthetic with an antibiotic clock, and a dilute block is reasonable.
  • The open femur. A reamed nail for most IIIA wounds in a stable patient; a fixator for the borderline patient and the contaminated or vascular wound; the femoral card for the reaming decision.
  • The open forearm. Plated at the first operation in most clean wounds, with a brachial plexus block on the same compartment terms as the leg; forearm compartment syndrome is real and under-recognised.
  • The farm, river and sewage wound. Immediate debridement whatever the hour, broader antibiotic cover per the protocol, and a return at 24 h rather than 48 h.
  • Pin site infection and the long frame. Pin infection is common and usually superficial, and is the reason a late conversion to a nail carries a higher infection rate than an early one; a circular frame for a bone defect stays on for months, with pin pain, sleep loss and opioid escalation as the course, and a pain service referral at the second week beats one at the second month.
  • The head-injured patient. Hypoxia, hypotension and raised intracranial pressure cause the secondary injury, not early fixation; the fixator over the nail in the first days.
  • Fat embolism and the fixator. A fixator reams no canal and embolises no marrow, one of its two advantages over a nail in a borderline patient; the other is time.

Key sources

The infection evidence is good and consistent: the Gustilo series, the antibiotic timing study that put an hour on the clock, the systematic review that killed the six-hour rule, FLOW for irrigation and the BOAST standard that turns them into a pathway. The damage control evidence is smaller but randomised: EPOFF for the measured second hit and Pape’s borderline trial for the clinical one, with the Early Appropriate Care series supplying the resuscitation thresholds from a prospective, non-randomised cohort. The regional anaesthesia advice is the Association of Anaesthetists’ guideline applied to open wounds, with the 1986 series as the reason an open fracture earns no exemption. There is no procedure-specific analgesia guideline; the block and catheter timing is the author’s reading of the compartment window.
