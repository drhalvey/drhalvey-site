82 Distal femur fracture fixation

Lateral locked plating, retrograde nailing, nail-plate and dual plate constructs for supracondylar and intercondylar fractures; the frail older patient as a hip fracture analogue; fractures above a knee replacement and distal femoral replacement

4Severe

Dominant mechanism: Bone. A fractured metaphysis in osteoporotic bone next to the knee joint, with a haematoma in the thigh and the suprapatellar pouch; then a long lateral plate slid under vastus lateralis or a reamed retrograde nail through the knee, and often an arthrotomy to rebuild the joint surface. Fixation removes the fracture motion, which is the worst of the pain, but the knee is swollen and stiff, the plate lies on stripped periosteum, and every transfer loads the construct

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: moderate.

Block
Femoral nerve or fascia iliaca block on arrival and repeated as a single shot for surgery, with spinal anaesthesia or general anaesthesia chosen as for a hip fracture
Catheter: selective A femoral or fascia iliaca catheter for the patient waiting more than a day for theatre, the opioid-intolerant or delirious older patient, and the complex articular reconstruction; single shot for the straightforward extra-articular fracture fixed within 36 h
Alternatives: Spinal anaesthesia as the primary anaesthetic in the frail older patient, with a block sited first to allow positioning; General anaesthesia with a single-shot block for the long articular reconstruction or the anticoagulated patient; Surgeon's periarticular and wound infiltration as a supplement
Position
Supine on a radiolucent table with a bump under the buttock and the knee flexed 30-60 degrees over a triangle or bolster to relax gastrocnemius; the other leg lowered or scissored out of the lateral image
Tourniquet
Sometimes. Usually none, because the plate or nail extends up the thigh; a sterile tourniquet is used by some surgeons for the articular reduction and released before the plate goes on, and routinely for a distal femoral replacement
Cement
Sometimes. None for fixation, apart from occasional cement augmentation of screws in very poor bone; a distal femoral replacement has cemented femoral and tibial stems, with the bone cement implantation risks of a revision knee in a frail patient
Blood loss
300-800 mL at operation on top of 500-1,000 mL already lost into the thigh and knee; more for open articular reconstruction and dual plating. Check the admission haemoglobin again before theatre and group and hold
Duration
90-150 min for a lateral plate or retrograde nail; 150-240 min for an intra-articular fracture, a nail-plate or dual plate construct, or a distal femoral replacement
Length of stay
5-10 nights in the older patient, usually followed by rehabilitation; 3-5 nights in the young patient with an isolated fracture. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV within 60 min of incision (3 g over 120 kg), repeated at 4 h; open fractures follow the open fracture plan
Tranexamic acid
TXA 1 g IV at induction; a second 1 g IV at closure for the open reconstruction, the arthroplasty and the anticoagulated patient
VTE
Low molecular weight heparin from admission, timed around neuraxial anaesthesia, and continued through the weeks of restricted mobility; mechanical prophylaxis on the other leg. This is a high-risk injury in an immobile older patient
Antiemetic
Ondansetron 4 mg IV; dexamethasone 4-8 mg IV unless the patient is delirious or has poorly controlled diabetes
Tell the patient
The break just above the knee has been held with a plate or a rod so the leg can be moved without the bones grating; the thigh and knee stay swollen and sore for the first few days, and the knee is stiff and aches when you bend it or put weight through the leg for some weeks.
The trap
In the frail older patient this is a hip fracture by another name, with the same one-year mortality, and it loses the hip fracture pathway because it has a different label: the block, the early theatre slot, the orthogeriatric review and the day 1 mobilisation have to be asked for by somebody, and that is often you

What the surgeon is doing and why

The distal femur breaks in two populations. The young patient has a high-energy injury, often open and often with other fractures, and belongs to the trauma cards. The larger group is the older woman with osteoporotic bone who falls onto a flexed knee, and increasingly the patient who falls with a knee replacement already in place, where the stiff implant concentrates stress at the thin bone just above its anterior flange. This card is mainly about that second group, because it is the group whose outcome depends on the anaesthetic pathway.

The mechanical problem is a short, soft distal fragment acted on by strong muscles. Gastrocnemius arises from the back of the condyles and tips the distal fragment into extension, so the fracture angulates apex-posterior with the popliteal vessels lying directly behind it; the adductors pull it into varus; quadriceps and hamstrings shorten the thigh. If the fracture runs into the joint, the condyles separate and the articular surface steps. The surgeon has to restore the joint surface exactly where it is involved, restore length, alignment and rotation of the shaft on the condyles, and hold it all with an implant that can get purchase in a fragment a few centimetres long made of poor bone.

Three implant strategies do this. A lateral locking plate is slid up the thigh under vastus lateralis from a small incision at the knee, bridging the fracture without stripping it, with a cluster of locking screws in the condyles acting as a fixed-angle device. A retrograde nail is passed through the knee, up the canal, and locked through the condyles; it is load-sharing and suits extra-articular fractures and many fractures above a knee replacement whose femoral component has an open box. Where the bone is very poor or the fracture very low, surgeons combine a nail with a plate, or a lateral with a medial plate, so that the construct is rigid enough to stand on. When a knee replacement above or below the fracture is loose, or the fragment is too small and soft to hold anything, the distal femur is resected and replaced with a hinged, cemented prosthesis, as on the periprosthetic fracture card.

Why it matters to you: in the cohort that first made the comparison, mortality after a native distal femur fracture in older patients was no different from a hip fracture control group, and delay to surgery beyond four days raised mortality at six months and a year. A more recent series put one-year mortality at 25%, and 38% in those with a high Nottingham Hip Fracture Score. The meta-analysis of timing is less clear, with no significant association overall, a trend toward benefit, and stronger effects in the studies with strict definitions of early. The honest reading is that this is the same patient as on the neck of femur card with a less protected pathway.

The pain logic: bone, with a swollen knee. Before fixation every movement grinds the fragments, and the pain is severe. Fixation removes that, leaving a reamed or plated metaphysis, a haemarthrosis, sometimes an arthrotomy, and a construct that is loaded with each transfer. It sits at a 4 with other periarticular knee fractures and with periprosthetic fractures, above the 3 of a nailed shaft, because the joint is involved and stiffness pain with knee movement persists for weeks.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Arrival: block, splint, bloods, pathway

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the leg splinted in slight flexion, the distal pulses and the skin checked (the fracture apex points at the popliteal artery), radiographs and usually a CT to see the joint and any existing implant, and the patient made ready for theatre.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Treat this as a hip fracture from the door. A femoral nerve or fascia iliaca block on arrival makes splinting, imaging and nursing tolerable and reduced opioid use across the admission in the retrospective series. Check the haemoglobin, renal function, anticoagulant and antiplatelet history, and request the orthogeriatric review. The thigh and knee can hide a litre, so an anaemic patient on arrival will be more anaemic by the morning. A cool foot or absent pulses with this fracture is a popliteal artery problem and an emergency in its own right. Press for a theatre slot within 36-48 h; nobody audits this fracture the way they audit a hip.

  2. 2 The anaesthetic choice

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs a still patient, a relaxed thigh and one and a half to four hours.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The choice between spinal and general anaesthesia is made on the same grounds as for a hip fracture, where the large randomised trials cited on the neck of femur card found no difference in survival, delirium or return to walking, so it is decided by anticoagulation, the expected duration and the patient. A spinal of plain or hyperbaric bupivacaine covers a 90-150 min plating or nailing and relaxes the thigh well; a block sited first lets the patient sit or turn for it. For an articular reconstruction, a dual plate construct or a distal femoral replacement running beyond three hours, general anaesthesia with a block is more dependable. An arterial line is reasonable for the frail patient with cardiac disease and essential for the cemented arthroplasty.

  3. 3 Positioning and closed reduction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The knee is flexed over a triangle or a stack of towels: flexing the knee relaxes gastrocnemius and corrects the apex-posterior angulation, and the bolster height is the surgeon’s main reduction tool. A bump under the buttock brings the patella to face the ceiling for rotation. The other leg is dropped or raised out of the lateral X-ray beam.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Muscle tone is the enemy of the reduction: a dense spinal or a relaxant makes the surgeon’s job much easier, and a request for more relaxation during reduction is a real one. Osteoporotic skin and bone need padding at the heels, sacrum and the opposite leg, and the arms on boards with shoulders under 90 degrees. Warm the patient from the start; the whole leg and half the torso are exposed and the patient is old and thin.

  4. 4 Approach

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    For an extra-articular fracture, a 6-8 cm lateral incision over the condyle and a tunnel developed under vastus lateralis. For an intra-articular fracture, a lateral parapatellar arthrotomy that lets the patella be displaced medially and the whole joint surface be seen. For a nail, a short incision through or beside the patellar tendon with the knee flexed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The arthrotomy releases the haemarthrosis and is the start of measurable blood loss. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV should already be in. Antibiotic before the incision.

  5. 5 Rebuilding the joint surface

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    In an intercondylar fracture the condyles are reduced to each other with clamps under direct vision and held with lag screws placed where the plate will not need to go; a coronal (Hoffa) fragment needs screws from front to back. The joint is converted from a three-part problem to a two-part one before the shaft is addressed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A quiet, meticulous phase of 30-60 min. If a sterile tourniquet is used it is for this step; note the time and expect the reperfusion effects in a frail patient to be larger than the short inflation suggests.

  6. 6 Reducing the shaft and passing the plate

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    With length, alignment and rotation restored by traction, the bolster and percutaneous clamps or a temporary external fixator, the plate is slid proximally along the lateral femur under the muscle, checked on two views, fixed to the condyles with locking screws and to the shaft through stab incisions. The fracture zone is bridged and left undisturbed so its blood supply and haematoma are kept.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Repeated imaging and repeated pulls on the leg. Blood loss is concealed in the thigh and the drapes; reassess at the end of this step and check a haemoglobin in the anaemic or the long case. The perforating arteries are at risk from the proximal screws and can bleed late into the thigh.

  7. 7 Retrograde nailing (alternative)

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A guidewire is placed in the intercondylar notch just in front of the posterior cruciate ligament origin, the entry is reamed through articular cartilage, the canal is reamed and the nail passed and locked through the condyles, sometimes with a blade or multiple distal screws for hold.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Reaming pressurises the canal and embolises marrow fat, as described in femoral shaft nailing. In the older patient with limited cardiopulmonary reserve the transient fall in saturation and blood pressure during reaming is more often seen than in the young; watch end-tidal carbon dioxide and pressure and tell the surgeon. See fat embolism.

  8. 8 Nail-plate and dual plate constructs

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    In very osteoporotic or very distal fractures a single lateral plate can fail by varus collapse. Adding a nail to a plate, or a medial plate to a lateral one, makes the construct stiff enough for immediate weight-bearing, which is the surgeon’s real goal in a patient who cannot hop.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Another 45-60 min, a second approach on the medial side close to the femoral vessels in the adductor canal, and more bleeding. This is the variant most likely to outlast a spinal.

  9. 9 Distal femoral replacement (variant)

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    For a fracture above a loose knee replacement, or a fragment that cannot be fixed, the surgeon excises the distal femur, prepares the femoral and tibial canals and cements a hinged prosthesis. The patient can bear full weight the next day.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A different operation: tourniquet, canal preparation, and cemented stems with the risk of bone cement implantation syndrome in the patient least able to tolerate it. Have an arterial line, a vasopressor running or drawn up, a well-filled patient and an agreed warning before cementing; see cement and BCIS and revision knee arthroplasty. In the systematic review, mortality and reoperation after replacement and after fixation did not differ.

  10. 10 Closure and the first day

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The arthrotomy and the iliotibial band are closed and the knee is put through a range to confirm stable fixation. Weight-bearing instructions vary: historically six to twelve weeks of touch weight-bearing after plating, with a move toward immediate weight-bearing as tolerated in the older patient, supported by a meta-analysis that found no excess of complications with early loading.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The trajectory on this card is 3 on the first evening with the block working, 5 on days 1 and 2, 4 on day 3 and 3 at a week. Ask the surgeon directly whether the patient may stand on the leg tomorrow, because that single instruction decides whether this patient mobilises or lies in bed for six weeks, and write the answer where the ward will see it. Haemoglobin on day 1. Delirium screening, bowel care and nutrition as for a hip fracture.

Pain plan

The femoral nerve supplies the anterior femur, its periosteum and the anterior knee capsule; the sciatic and obturator nerves supply the back of the knee. A femoral or fascia iliaca block therefore covers most but not all of this fracture, and that matches clinical experience: it transforms the pre-operative period and leaves some posterior knee ache. There is no procedure-specific guideline. The PROSPECT recommendations for hip fracture (a pre-operative single-shot femoral or fascia iliaca block, paracetamol, an NSAID where safe, and catheters reserved for expected delay) transfer by analogy and are the basis of the defensible default: a block on arrival, a repeat single shot for surgery if more than 12 h have passed, spinal or general anaesthesia, paracetamol regularly, an NSAID only where renal function and age allow (in this population, usually not), and a low-dose oral opioid with a laxative.

The two retrospective studies specific to this fracture point in different directions and both deserve to be believed for what they measured. Emergency department fascia iliaca blocks were associated with roughly 40% less opioid across the admission. Peri-operative regional anaesthesia in a larger cohort was associated with slightly more opioid at 24-48 h and more outpatient opioid demand to 90 days. A single shot is an early intervention with a rebound when it recedes; it does not shorten the weeks of knee pain. That is the argument for a catheter in selected patients: the one waiting more than a day for theatre, the delirious patient in whom every opioid dose is costly, and the complex reconstruction. A femoral catheter weakens the quadriceps, which matters less here than after a knee replacement because the limit on mobilisation is the fracture and the surgeon’s instruction, though the falls precautions still apply. An adductor canal catheter is too distal for a plate that runs up the thigh.

Rebound is moderate. Escalation triggers: a tense, painful thigh with a falling haemoglobin is a perforator bleed; calf pain or a swollen leg is a deep vein thrombosis until scanned; a cold or pulseless foot at any stage is vascular; new confusion is delirium first and uncontrolled pain second, and the two feed each other.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The label. Hip fracture standards (time to theatre, orthogeriatric care, nerve block on arrival, day 1 mobilisation) do not formally apply to this fracture in most systems, so they are applied only if someone insists.
  • Anticoagulants and antiplatelets. Common in this group, and the usual reason for both delay and a general anaesthetic. Reverse or wait by drug and renal function, as for a hip fracture, and do not let an unnecessary wait for a spinal cost the patient a day.
  • Fracture above a knee replacement. A previous knee replacement predicted shorter survival in the mortality cohort. Whether the surgeon nails, plates or replaces depends on the implant design and whether it is loose.
  • Fracture below a hip stem or between two implants. A long plate overlapping the stem with cables, a longer incision and more blood; see the periprosthetic card.
  • The young high-energy fracture. Often open, comminuted and associated with a tibial fracture (a floating knee), a vascular injury or a compartment syndrome of the leg; temporary spanning external fixation first, as on the open fracture card. Block decisions there follow the compartment rules.
  • Popliteal artery. Tethered behind the fracture; an injury is uncommon and limb-threatening. Document pulses before and after reduction.
  • Stiffness and nonunion. The reasons these patients return: manipulation under anaesthesia, plate revision and bone grafting, as on the nonunion card.
  • Non-operative care. Reserved for the non-ambulant patient with an undisplaced fracture or the patient at the end of life, where a block and a splint are the treatment.

Key sources

The mortality comparison with hip fracture is a single prognostic cohort, later supported by registry-style series; the timing meta-analysis is low-certainty and its headline result is null. The weight-bearing meta-analysis pools retrospective studies. The analgesic evidence specific to this fracture is two retrospective cohorts with opposite signals, and the recommendation is built by analogy with the hip fracture guideline, which is stated as analogy and not as evidence. The pain score and trajectory are the author’s calibration against the periprosthetic and femoral shaft cards.
