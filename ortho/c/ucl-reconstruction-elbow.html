78 Elbow ligament reconstruction

Medial ulnar collateral ligament reconstruction and repair in the throwing athlete; lateral ulnar collateral ligament reconstruction for posterolateral rotatory instability

3Moderate

Dominant mechanism: Bone. Drill tunnels in the ulna and the humeral epicondyle, a free tendon graft harvested from the forearm or the thigh and tensioned through them, and a muscle-splitting approach through the flexor-pronator mass or the extensor origin. The graft is tensioned and then protected in a brace, so, as with a cruciate reconstruction, the course is bone-shaped: a sharp day 1 that falls quickly. The ulnar nerve lies in the field of the medial operation and adds a neuropathic risk, not a routine pain

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Block
General anaesthesia with surgeon infiltration of the wound and graft site, then a single-shot infraclavicular or axillary block placed in recovery once ulnar nerve function has been examined and recorded; the same block before surgery, with intravenous dexamethasone, where the surgeon has agreed to a delayed nerve examination
Catheter: rarely A day-case operation in a young patient with a 48-hour bone-pattern course does not earn a catheter; the exception is a revision or combined reconstruction in an opioid-tolerant patient, with the catheter left unloaded until the nerve has been examined
Alternatives: Pre-operative plexus block with a short-acting agent so the ulnar nerve can be examined the same evening; General anaesthesia with infiltration and oral multimodal analgesia, no block; Supraclavicular block, accepting a phrenic palsy in a minority; Adductor canal block or surgeon infiltration for a gracilis harvest site in the leg

What the surgeon is doing and why

The elbow is a hinge whose bony fit gives most of its stability near full extension and full flexion. In between, two ligaments do the work, and they fail in different patients for different reasons.

The medial ulnar collateral ligament, specifically its anterior bundle from the underside of the medial epicondyle to the sublime tubercle of the ulna, resists valgus. Nothing in ordinary life loads it to failure, but overhead throwing does on every pitch: in late cocking and early acceleration the valgus torque at the elbow approaches the ligament’s breaking strength, and with repetition it attenuates and tears. The patient is a baseball pitcher, javelin thrower or gymnast, young, fit and intent on returning to sport. The injured ligament is usually too degenerate to sew, so it is replaced with a free tendon graft passed through bone tunnels at its two attachments. Acute avulsions from one end in good tissue can instead be repaired and reinforced with a suture tape.

The lateral ulnar collateral ligament runs from the lateral epicondyle to the supinator crest of the ulna and stops the radial head and ulna rotating out from under the humerus. It fails after an elbow dislocation that did not heal, after a fracture-dislocation, or after surgery or repeated steroid injection at the lateral epicondyle, and produces posterolateral rotatory instability: clunking, giving way when pushing up from a chair. This is a general orthopaedic population, older and more varied. Reconstruction again uses a free graft through tunnels in the ulna and the humerus.

The medial operation, in the surgeon’s order:

  1. Harvest the graft. Ipsilateral palmaris longus through two or three small transverse incisions at the wrist and forearm is the first choice; it is absent in a minority of people, and the surgeon confirms it before the day. Gracilis from the leg is the common alternative, then a toe extensor, a strip of triceps, or allograft.
  2. Approach. A medial incision centred on the epicondyle, protecting the branches of the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve. The flexor-pronator mass is split in line with its fibres (the modern, less morbid route) or lifted off the epicondyle, exposing the ligament. The ulnar nerve lies immediately behind, in the cubital tunnel.
  3. Tunnels. Two converging holes at the sublime tubercle of the ulna with a bone bridge between; on the humerus either a single socket with two small exit holes (the docking technique) or a Y of tunnels for a figure-of-eight weave. Biomechanically the two are equivalent.
  4. Pass and tension the graft with the elbow reduced in varus at about 30 degrees of flexion, tie over the humeral bridge, and sew the native ligament remnant to the graft.
  5. The ulnar nerve. The original operation transposed it routinely and paid for that with neuropathy. Current practice is to leave it in its bed unless the patient had ulnar symptoms beforehand or the nerve subluxes, in which case it is decompressed or moved anteriorly under a fascial sling.

The lateral operation is the mirror image through the interval between anconeus and extensor carpi ulnaris, with the posterior interosseous nerve protected by pronating the forearm. Pooled data put an ulnar nerve event as its commonest complication too, at 2.3%, from positioning, swelling and bracing.

The pain logic: tunnels in dense metaphyseal bone, a muscle split and a harvest site, with the graft protected in a hinged brace. As in ACL reconstruction, that is bone pain with a harvest-site contribution: a 3, high on day 1 and falling fast, with the population (young, athletic, day case) at high risk of rebound after a block. The nerve is the thing that can change the course.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Pre-operative: the graft, the nerve and the plan for examining it

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon has tested for palmaris longus (opposing thumb and little finger with the wrist flexed) and chosen the graft, and has recorded any ulnar nerve symptoms, which decide whether the nerve will be transposed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Record ulnar nerve function yourself: sensation over the little finger pulp, finger abduction and crossing. Many throwers have pre-existing paraesthesia, and that is the comparison for everything afterwards. Then settle the block question with the surgeon using the three workable answers from the block menu: block after the surgeon has examined the patient in recovery; a short-acting block before surgery and an examination that evening; or a catheter placed before surgery and left unloaded until the examination is done, which is rarely worth it for a day case. A fourth answer, outside the menu and acceptable only by explicit agreement written in the notes, is a long-acting block before surgery with the examination deferred to the next day. Find out which leg, if gracilis is the graft, and keep the cannula out of the operative arm.

  2. 2 Induction and positioning

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The medial approach needs the shoulder externally rotated and abducted with the elbow on the table; the lateral approach needs the arm across the chest or the patient on their side.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway for supine cases, a tracheal tube for lateral decubitus. External rotation of the shoulder under anaesthesia in a thrower is well tolerated; in the older lateral-ligament patient do not force it. Pad the opposite elbow. Antibiotic, then the tourniquet. No further neuromuscular blocker is needed, and the surgeon may stimulate around the ulnar nerve.

  3. 3 Graft harvest

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Palmaris longus is delivered through a small wrist crease incision and stripped proximally to give 15-20 cm of tendon. Gracilis is taken through a short incision over the pes anserinus with a closed stripper, as for a cruciate graft.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The palmaris tendon lies directly over the median nerve at the wrist; taking the wrong structure is a recorded disaster of this harvest and the reason the surgeon looks twice. A leg harvest is a second site outside any arm block: ask for the harvest wound and the stripper track to be infiltrated with ropivacaine 0.2% 20 mL. The saphenous nerve crosses the gracilis tendon, and a numb patch on the medial shin is the common donor complaint.

  4. 4 Exposure through the flexor-pronator split, or the lateral interval

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The muscle is split over the sublime tubercle to show the torn anterior bundle; the joint is opened along the ligament to confirm the diagnosis. Laterally, the capsule and ligament remnant are exposed over the radial head and supinator crest.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Under tourniquet and unremarkable. Retractors on the posterior edge of the medial split sit on the ulnar nerve for the next hour.

  5. 5 Ulnar and humeral tunnels

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Converging drill holes are made at each attachment with a guide, leaving a bone bridge of about 1 cm that the graft loops around. Tunnel position decides whether the graft is isometric.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the bone-pain generator: four or five drill holes in dense bone. The posterior ulnar tunnel exits close to the ulnar nerve, and the humeral exit holes are close to it proximally; this is where an intraoperative nerve injury happens, and nothing in your monitoring will show it.

  6. 6 Graft passage, tensioning and fixation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The graft is passed through the ulnar tunnel, both limbs are taken up to the humerus, and it is tensioned with the joint held reduced and the forearm supinated (medial) or pronated (lateral) before being tied or fixed with a button or interference screw.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The surgeon wants the arm held still in a specific position for several minutes. Keep depth steady; a cough or movement at the moment of tensioning loses the tension.

  7. 7 The ulnar nerve decision

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    If transposition is indicated, the nerve is released from the arcade proximally to the two heads of flexor carpi ulnaris distally and moved in front of the epicondyle under a fascial sling. Otherwise it is inspected and left.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Ask what was done to the nerve and write it on the anaesthetic record and the recovery handover: “nerve not exposed”, “retracted only” or “transposed”. A transposed nerve commonly gives a few days of little-finger paraesthesia; a dense new motor deficit does not belong to that picture. The tourniquet has by now been up 60-90 min.

  8. 8 Arthroscopy or osteophyte excision, when added

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Throwers often have posteromedial olecranon osteophytes or loose bodies from valgus extension overload, dealt with arthroscopically before the ligament or through the same incision.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Arthroscopy first means a position change (lateral or prone to supine) and 20-30 min more; fluid extravasation into the forearm is usual and settles. See elbow arthroscopy.

  9. 9 Closure, tourniquet release and brace

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The muscle split is closed, the medial antebrachial cutaneous branches checked, skin closed, and the elbow placed in a splint at 60-90 degrees, changed to a hinged brace within a week.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Surgeon infiltration of the skin and harvest sites with ropivacaine, kept away from the cubital tunnel so that the nerve can be examined. Dexamethasone and ondansetron are in. Wake the patient with the splint on.

  10. 10 Recovery: examine, then block

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon or a delegated member of the team tests ulnar motor and sensory function as soon as the patient can cooperate.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Give intravenous opioid in small doses to bridge the examination; it takes two minutes and should be done within the first 15. Once function is recorded, place the infraclavicular or axillary block (ropivacaine 0.375-0.5% 20 mL) if that was the plan, and start oral paracetamol and an NSAID. If the nerve is not working, do not block: the surgeon needs to follow it. Discharge with a sling, written advice on the numb arm and the time the block should wear off.

Pain plan

The mechanism is bone tunnels with a muscle split and a harvest site, in a young day-case patient: a block-responsive first night and a short course. The plan is a single plexus block timed around the nerve examination, intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, paracetamol and an NSAID for five to seven days, and oral opioid for two or three. Surgeons vary on NSAIDs after ligament reconstruction; the author’s judgement is that a week’s course does not threaten graft incorporation, and the author’s practice is to give one unless asked not to.

The timing of the block is the only decision specific to this operation, and it is judgement, not evidence. The author’s default is block in recovery after the examination: it costs 10-15 min of recovery time and a short period of opioid-covered pain, and it leaves no ambiguity. A pre-operative block is smoother and allows a lighter anaesthetic; it is the better choice for the lateral operation, where the ulnar nerve is not in the field (accepting that pooled data still record occasional ulnar events), and for the medial operation when the surgeon is content to examine the next day. A short-acting pre-operative block is the compromise when same-evening examination is wanted. The infraclavicular site covers the medial arm and forearm skin, which the medial incision needs; an axillary block needs the medial cutaneous nerves included.

Rebound: this is the population most at risk in the ambulatory cohort (young, bone surgery). Dexamethasone, oral dosing before the block recedes, and a specific instruction about the small hours are the measures.

No catheter for a primary. For a gracilis harvest, infiltration is enough; the leg pain is minor beside the elbow.

Escalation: increasing ulnar paraesthesia or new intrinsic weakness once the block has gone is reported the same day, since a haematoma in the cubital tunnel or a tight brace strap is correctable. Burning pain along the medial forearm is a medial antebrachial cutaneous neuroma in the making. A tense, very painful forearm after a long tourniquet and a muscle split is rare and is compartment syndrome until examined.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Repair with suture-tape augmentation. A shorter operation without a graft or a harvest site, with an anchor at each end; pain a notch lower, and the same nerve considerations.
  • Revision reconstruction. Scar around the ulnar nerve, a contralateral or leg graft, a longer tourniquet. The nerve is at more risk and the case for examining before blocking is stronger.
  • Lateral reconstruction after a fracture-dislocation. Often combined with radial head replacement or coronoid fixation (total elbow and radial head, elbow fracture fixation); the pain moves up and a pre-operative block or catheter is more easily justified.
  • Position for the lateral operation. Lateral decubitus brings the airway, the dependent arm and the neck into play for a one-hour day case.
  • Adolescents. The medial reconstruction is done in skeletally mature teenagers; weight-based local anaesthetic limits matter when the surgeon also infiltrates.
  • Expectations. Return to throwing takes about a year. Opioids prescribed to a young athlete after this operation are a recognised first exposure; supply a short, counted course.
  • Pre-existing ulnar neuritis. Document it, because it both predicts transposition and confuses the post-operative examination.
  • The acutely dislocated elbow that needs ligament repair in the first week is trauma surgery in a swollen arm and follows elbow fracture fixation.

Key sources

The outcome literature is large, almost entirely non-comparative series in baseball players, pooled in systematic reviews that report return to sport and complications. Nothing in it addresses anaesthesia or analgesia. The block-timing advice is reasoning from the surgical risk to the ulnar nerve and from the general upper limb and ambulatory rebound evidence, and is the author’s practice; the pain score is set by analogy with other tunnel-and-graft reconstructions.
