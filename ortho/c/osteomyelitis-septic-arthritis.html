48 Osteomyelitis debridement and septic arthritis washout

Excision of chronic osteomyelitis with dead space management and staged reconstruction; arthroscopic and open washout of the native septic joint

3Moderate

Dominant mechanism: Bone. Dead and infected bone cut back to a bleeding bed with a burr, a saw and a reamer, leaving a cavity packed with a carrier; for the septic joint, a capsule that was distended under pressure and is decompressed, then a synovium stripped by a shaver. Both operations remove the pain generator, so the post-operative pain is bone and capsule, short, and smaller than the pain the patient arrived with

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: moderate.

Block
General anaesthesia with multimodal analgesia; a single-shot block placed through clean tissue away from the infected field where the site allows (adductor canal for a knee washout, popliteal sciatic and saphenous for a tibial debridement), never through cellulitis or an abscess; spinal anaesthesia for the lower limb in the well host with chronic infection and no bacteraemia
Catheter: no No catheter: a perineural catheter near an infected field, in a bacteraemic patient, in a limb that may need serial examination and a second washout within 48 h, has nothing to offer that a single shot and the oral regimen do not, and the mechanism does not demand it
Alternatives: Spinal anaesthesia for the well host having a lower limb debridement, with the tourniquet time in mind; Surgeon infiltration of the wound edges and portals with long-acting local anaesthetic; Low-dose ketamine infusion for the chronic osteomyelitis patient on long-term opioids
Position
Supine for most sites, with the limb on a bolster or a radiolucent board and a tourniquet in reach; lateral for the hip and the posterior tibia, prone for the calcaneus and the posterior elbow; a long case with the C-arm in and out
Tourniquet
Sometimes. Common for a tibial, foot or forearm debridement so the surgeon can see dead bone against living, and deflated before the end so the bleeding bed can be judged; not used for the proximal femur, the pelvis or the spine, and avoided over cellulitis
Cement
Sometimes. Antibiotic-loaded polymethylmethacrylate as beads or a spacer, or an absorbable calcium sulphate or calcium sulphate and hydroxyapatite carrier, packed into the cavity; a cement spacer in the induced membrane (Masquelet) technique that is removed at the second stage; none of it is pressurised into a canal and none of it is a cement implantation syndrome risk
Blood loss
100-300 mL for a joint washout; 300-800 mL for a long bone debridement with reaming and saucerisation, most of it after the tourniquet comes down from bone deliberately cut back to a bleeding bed; more with a flap or a resection with a fixator
Duration
30-60 min for an arthroscopic washout; 60-90 min for an open arthrotomy; 90-180 min for a long bone debridement with a carrier and closure; half a day when a free flap or a resection and frame are added
Length of stay
A washout 3-7 nights, set by the sepsis and the antibiotic plan; a chronic osteomyelitis debridement 5-10 nights, with weeks of outpatient antibiotics afterwards and a second stage months later. Day case: no
Antibiotic
For chronic osteomyelitis the patient has usually been off antibiotics for two weeks and the surgeon wants deep samples before the first dose; the randomised evidence from prosthetic joint infection is that a dose of prophylaxis before the incision does not change the culture yield, so whether cefazolin 2 g IV goes in before the knife or after the samples is agreed with the surgeon rather than assumed. For septic arthritis the antibiotics started after the blood cultures and the aspirate, in the emergency department, and are never withheld for theatre
Tranexamic acid
Tranexamic acid 1 g IV for a long bone debridement with reaming and a bleeding bed; not needed for a washout
VTE
Low molecular weight heparin from the evening of surgery; infection, immobility and a swollen limb are a prothrombotic set, and a patient on weeks of antibiotics with a frame is at risk for the whole course
Antiemetic
Ondansetron 4 mg IV; dexamethasone 4 mg IV for the well host having an elective debridement, and omitted in the frankly septic patient, by the author's practice rather than by evidence
Tell the patient
Most of the pain you came in with is from the infection and should be better once the dead bone or the pus is out; the wound and the bone are sore for a few days, and the antibiotics run for weeks afterwards whatever the wound feels like.
The trap
Two patients share this card and the mistake is to treat one like the other: the chronic osteomyelitis patient is a well host whose antibiotics have been deliberately stopped so the samples mean something, and the septic arthritis patient is a sick one whose antibiotics must never wait for theatre; the sampling plan and the antibiotic timing are agreed with the surgeon before the first dose, not discovered at the drug cupboard

What the surgeon is doing and why

Two operations share this card because the same surgeon does both and the same anaesthetist is called, but they are opposite problems. Chronic osteomyelitis is a slow disease in a well patient; septic arthritis is a fast disease in a sick one. The first is a planned excision of dead bone that will fail if the surgeon leaves any behind. The second is a drainage procedure on a time clock that will fail if anyone waits.

Chronic osteomyelitis is bone that has died with bacteria living in it. The organisms, most often Staphylococcus aureus, sit in a biofilm on dead bone (the sequestrum) and on any implant, where they are a thousand times less susceptible to antibiotics than in broth, and the body walls them off with new bone (the involucrum) and drains them through a sinus. Antibiotics alone suppress and do not cure. The disease is classified on two axes that decide the operation: the anatomy (medullary, where the canal is infected after a nail; superficial, where exposed bone sits under a wound; localised, a full-thickness cortical segment that can be excised without destabilising the bone; and diffuse, a through-and-through infection where excision leaves the bone unstable or absent), and the host (a normal host, a compromised one with diabetes, smoking, vascular disease, renal failure or malnutrition, and one so compromised that the treatment is worse than the disease). Fracture-related infection after a plate or a nail is the commonest adult form; the infected arthroplasty has its own card.

The surgeon’s aims run in a fixed order. Take deep samples before any antibiotic muddies them, five or more from separate sites with clean instruments, for culture and histology. Excise every piece of dead and infected bone and soft tissue back to a bleeding margin: the sinus tract in its entirety, the sequestrum, the involucrum where it is involved, the infected canal reamed out, and the cortex burred or sawn back to the point where it bleeds from every surface. Then manage what the excision has created: a dead space that will fill with haematoma and re-infect unless it is filled with something, a bone that may now be unstable, and a soft tissue envelope that may not close. The dead space is packed with a local antibiotic carrier: antibiotic-loaded cement beads or a spacer that must be removed later, or an absorbable calcium sulphate or biocomposite carrier loaded with gentamicin or vancomycin that dissolves over weeks and releases concentrations systemic dosing cannot reach. In the 100-patient prospective single-stage series from a specialist unit, debridement, multiple sampling, culture-specific antibiotics, stabilisation, filling with the biocomposite and primary closure eradicated infection with one operation in 96, with three fractures and six wound leaks, and the result did not depend on the host class or the organism. Where the excision leaves a segmental defect, the induced membrane technique puts a cement spacer in the gap, waits six to eight weeks for a vascular membrane to form around it, and then replaces the cement with bone graft at a second stage; where the bone is unstable, an external fixator or a plate goes on at the same sitting. Soft tissue cover is a local or free flap in many cases.

Septic arthritis is a different disease. Bacteria reach a joint by the blood, a wound, an injection or an adjacent osteomyelitis, and inside a closed synovial cavity the neutrophil enzymes, the pressure of the effusion and the organism destroy cartilage within days. Incidence is rising with an ageing, more diabetic population, and in the French national series of 109,160 episodes the mean age was 62, staphylococci accounted for half, rheumatoid arthritis, complicated diabetes, dialysis and drug addiction each quadrupled the risk, and mortality was 3.2% at a month and 11.4% at a year. The surgeon’s aims are drainage, decompression and debridement: get the pus out, wash the joint with litres of fluid, strip the inflamed synovium so that it stops producing enzyme, and do it today. The knee is done arthroscopically by most surgeons now, with a shaver synovectomy and a drain; the meta-analyses of retrospective comparisons favour arthroscopy over arthrotomy for re-infection and complications in the knee and shoulder, with no difference in mortality, and the 80-patient series that looked for predictors found MRSA, not the approach, behind the failed single washout. A patient who does not improve in 48 h goes back for another look, and a quarter do.

The pain logic is the same for both and it runs backwards from most of the book: the operation removes the pain generator. The chronic osteomyelitis patient has had an aching, discharging limb for months or years and is often on opioids; the septic arthritis patient has a joint distended under pressure that hurts to move a degree. Afterwards the mechanisms are bone (cortex, canal and cancellous bone cut back to a bleeding bed, which behaves like an osteotomy, worst for two days and NSAID-responsive) and capsule (a decompressed joint whose synovium has been shaved, sharp with movement and settling in days). Neither mechanism is loaded, tensioned or lengthened, so the pain falls quickly: a 3, with opioids needed for two or three days and the shape of the curve set by the pre-operative opioid state rather than by the operation. The frame, the flap and the second stage each bring their own pain.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Which patient this is, and the antibiotic-free interval

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    For chronic osteomyelitis the surgeon has stopped antibiotics for two weeks so that the deep samples grow the organism, has staged the disease from the MRI and the host from the clinic letter, and has a plan for the dead space, the stability and the cover. For septic arthritis the surgeon has a patient diagnosed by aspiration in the emergency department whose joint needs washing out within hours.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The chronic patient is a well host or a compromised one, not a septic one: the assessment is the diabetes, the smoking, the renal function, the nutrition, the months of opioids and the plan for them from the opioid-tolerant chapter. The septic arthritis patient is assessed as a septic patient: lactate, blood cultures, the renal function, the anticoagulant for the atrial fibrillation, the rheumatoid drugs, and the fasting status, which is irrelevant to the timing because the joint will not wait. Look at the limb yourself: cellulitis over the block site rules the block out.

  2. 2 The antibiotic timing conversation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants an organism and a sensitivity, because weeks of empirical therapy against a biofilm is guessing. For the chronic patient the first antibiotic dose is timed against the sampling; for the septic patient the dose has already been given and the samples will be taken anyway.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The one randomised trial, in prosthetic joint infection, gave prophylaxis either before the incision or after three sets of operative cultures and found the operative cultures matched the pre-operative organism in 82% and 81%, which is equivalence; the authors concluded that prophylaxis should not be withheld for the sake of the cultures. Many bone infection surgeons still ask for the first dose after the samples; in a well host with no implant that is a reasonable request, in a patient with a fresh implant going in it is not, and the trial is the argument. Agree it before the patient is asleep. For septic arthritis the antibiotics (empirical anti-staphylococcal cover, broadened for the immunosuppressed, the dialysis patient and the injecting drug user) went in after the blood cultures and the aspirate and are continued through the operation; the dose is not held because the patient is going to theatre.

  3. 3 Anaesthetic technique, lines and the tourniquet

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A debridement needs a bloodless field to tell dead bone from living, then a perfused one to judge the bleeding margin, so the tourniquet goes up for the excision and comes down before the end. A washout needs a still joint and, for arthroscopy, a pump.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    General anaesthesia for the septic patient and for any case with a flap; spinal anaesthesia is reasonable for a lower limb debridement in the well host without bacteraemia, with the tourniquet time in mind. A single-shot block through clean tissue away from the field (adductor canal for a knee washout, popliteal sciatic and saphenous for a tibial debridement) is the author’s practice where it can be placed cleanly; a needle through cellulitis or into a limb with a tracking abscess is not placed. No catheter. An arterial line for the septic patient and for a long case. Cefazolin 2 g IV at the agreed moment, and tranexamic acid 1 g IV for the long bone case because the bleeding bed is the point of the operation.

  4. 4 Exposure, sinus excision and sampling

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The sinus is excised in continuity with the skin ellipse around it, the bone is exposed through the old scar or the old plate incision, any implant is removed and sent for sonication, and five or more deep samples are taken from separate sites with a fresh instrument each time, for culture and for histology.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the step the antibiotic timing was about, and if the dose was held for the samples it goes in now, once the surgeon says the last one is taken. Removal of old metalwork is slower than it looks and bleeds from the screw holes; the removal of metalwork card applies to this part.

  5. 5 Debridement to bleeding bone

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Sequestrum is lifted out, the involucrum opened, the canal reamed in a medullary infection, and the cortex burred or sawn back until every surface bleeds, the surgeon’s sign that living bone has been reached. In a localised infection a cortical window or a full-thickness segment comes out; in a diffuse one the bone is resected and the stability decision follows.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The tourniquet comes down for the judgement, and the blood loss of the operation starts then: a reamed canal and a saucerised tibia bleed steadily from cancellous bone and do not stop until the carrier is in and the wound closed. Reaming a chronically infected canal is a marrow embolisation step with an inoculum attached; a rise in temperature and a fall in pressure over the next hour is bacteraemia from the reaming, and the antibiotic cover should be running when it happens.

  6. 6 Dead space management, carriers and stabilisation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The cavity is filled so that it cannot fill with haematoma: antibiotic-loaded cement beads or a spacer that will be removed in weeks, or an absorbable calcium sulphate or biocomposite carrier that dissolves and needs no second operation. A segmental defect gets a cement spacer for the induced membrane technique, and an unstable bone gets a fixator or a plate.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The carrier is pressed into a cavity by hand and is not a cement implantation syndrome risk; the volume of gentamicin or vancomycin in it is large by systemic standards, and in a dialysis patient with a large cavity the levels are checked afterwards because the carrier elutes into a kidney that cannot clear it. Calcium sulphate leaks serous fluid through the wound for a week or two in a proportion of patients, six of 100 in the specialist series, which is expected and not an infection. A frame adds an hour and the pin sites.

  7. 7 Soft tissue cover and closure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Dead bone was often dead because the soft tissue over it had failed; the excision leaves exposed bone or a carrier that must be covered with vascular tissue, so a local muscle flap, a fasciocutaneous flap or a free flap is raised, and primary closure is the aim in every case because an open wound re-infects.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A free flap is the plastic surgeon’s day and the usual flap rules apply: warm, filled, a pressure the surgeon is content with, no vasopressor boluses into a fresh anastomosis, and a bed in a unit that will monitor the flap. A local flap adds 30-60 min and a second sore site.

  8. 8 Septic arthritis: the approach and the washout

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The knee is entered through standard arthroscopic portals, the joint is washed with several litres of saline through a pump, the fibrin and the loculations are broken down, the synovium is shaved, and a drain is left; an arthrotomy does the same through a parapatellar incision with a hand and a sucker. The hip is scoped or opened according to the surgeon, the shoulder is scoped, and the small joints are opened. The pooled retrospective evidence favours arthroscopy for the knee and shoulder on re-infection and complications, with no difference in mortality.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A pump and litres of room-temperature saline through a joint in a septic patient is a cooling system, and the patient arrived cold; warm the fluid or the patient. The washout mobilises organisms: a rise in temperature, a fall in pressure and a rising heart rate during the lavage are bacteraemia and are treated as the sepsis they are, with the antibiotic already in and a vasopressor rather than a litre of crystalloid in a patient whose heart is the comorbidity.

  9. 9 The septic patient through the operation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon’s operation is short; the patient’s physiology is the long part. A quarter to a third of septic knee patients in the national surgical registry suffered a serious adverse event within 30 days, which is the population, not the operation.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Treat the patient as a septic laparotomy with a smaller incision: an arterial line, a lactate before and after, noradrenaline rather than fluid once the first litre is in, glucose in the diabetic, steroid cover for the rheumatoid patient on prednisolone, and a bed decided before induction for the patient on a vasopressor. The dialysis patient, the injecting drug user with endocarditis until proven otherwise and the immunosuppressed patient are the ones who die.

  10. 10 Recovery, the ward and the second look

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the limb elevated and moved early (a washed-out joint stiffens if it is rested, and the physiotherapy is the treatment), the drain watched, the inflammatory markers falling, and a low threshold for a second washout at 48 h if they are not. The chronic osteomyelitis patient starts culture-specific antibiotics, usually intravenous at first and then oral for weeks, and goes home with an outpatient antibiotic plan and a return date for the second stage.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The regular paracetamol and the NSAID (where the kidneys and the surgeon permit) start in recovery, the opioid is charted for 48 h, and the chronic patient’s baseline opioid continues unchanged. Pain that rises on day 2 with a hot, swollen joint and markers that have not fallen is a failed washout, not a rebound, and the second operation is the same anaesthetic in a patient who is now more septic. A wet dressing over a calcium sulphate carrier is the carrier, not the infection.

Pain plan

The mechanism is bone and capsule with the pain generator removed, so the plan is multimodal and short, built around a single-shot block where one can be placed cleanly and the oral regimen from recovery. There is no PROSPECT guideline for either operation; the plan is standard practice and the author’s judgement, and the card says so.

The block decision follows the field. A block is placed through clean tissue away from the infection, never through cellulitis or lymphangitis and never into a limb with a tracking collection, and it is a single shot. The mechanism does not need a catheter: the bone pain is short, the joint pain is short, and the patient who is septic, who may need a serial examination or a second washout in 48 h, and whose limb may have a frame or a flap on it, is a poor candidate for a line that stays in. Surgeon infiltration of the wound and the portals is asked for in every case.

The oral regimen: regular paracetamol; an NSAID for the well host, withheld for the dialysis patient and the septic one with a rising creatinine; an immediate-release opioid for 48-72 h and then as required; the baseline opioid continued in the chronic patient, with a low-dose ketamine infusion through the case and the first night for the one on a large dose, which is the author’s practice. A frame brings pin-site pain that outlasts the bone pain and responds to the same regimen; a flap brings a donor site that needs its own infiltration.

Rebound is moderate because the mechanism is small: when the block recedes the patient finds a sore bone that was far more sore the week before. Escalation: pain rising on day 1-2 with a hot joint, a fever and markers that are not falling is failed source control and a surgical conversation, not an analgesic one; pain in the calf of a limb with a frame or after a long knee lavage is a compartment syndrome until the compartments have been felt; pain that is unchanged at a week in a patient who was on opioids for a year is the chronic state, and the pain service rather than the ward is the right owner.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The antibiotic-free interval that nobody told you about. The chronic patient’s antibiotics were stopped on purpose; restarting them on admission for a sore red leg wrecks the samples. The decision about the first dose is the surgeon’s and the conversation is before induction.
  • Fracture-related infection with metal in place. The implant is removed, sonicated and often replaced with a frame; the open fracture card covers the fixator and the removal of metalwork card the extraction, which is the slow part of the day.
  • The induced membrane technique. Two operations separated by six to eight weeks: a cement spacer and a frame or plate at the first, graft from the iliac crest or a reamer-irrigator-aspirator harvest from the femur at the second. The second stage bleeds more than the first, and the crest or the reamed femur is the sorest site.
  • The diabetic foot. Osteomyelitis of the forefoot in a neuropathic, vascular, renal patient: a small operation on the sickest host on the card, often a toe or ray amputation, and a prelude to the amputation card in a proportion.
  • The hip in the adult. A deep joint, a lateral position and an open or arthroscopic washout in a patient who is usually old and septic; the native hip that fails washout becomes a resection arthroplasty, and the arthroplasty that follows months later is in a scarred hip.
  • The injecting drug user. Unusual organisms, a sternoclavicular or sacroiliac joint, endocarditis until an echocardiogram says otherwise, difficult venous access and an opioid tolerance that the ward will under-treat; central access and the pain service on day 1.
  • The rheumatoid patient. The highest risk group in the national data, on immunosuppression and often steroids, with a joint already destroyed, a cervical spine for the airway, and a presentation that mimics a flare and is diagnosed late.
  • Crystal arthropathy. The septic joint that turns out to be gout is washed out anyway, because the aspirate takes 48 h to say which it was.
  • The patient who needs an amputation. A diffuse infection in a host who cannot tolerate reconstruction, an infected non-union after years of frames, a diabetic foot that has reached the ankle; the conversation belongs to the surgeon and the patient, and the anaesthetic is on the amputation card.

Key sources

The osteomyelitis evidence is specialist-unit series and reviews rather than trials: the single-stage biocomposite series and the carrier review are the best description of modern practice, and the pathophysiology review supplies the classification and the biofilm logic. The antibiotic timing argument rests on one randomised trial from prosthetic joint infection, extrapolated here. The septic arthritis comparisons are retrospective, and the two meta-analyses that favour arthroscopy in the knee say so themselves; the registry study finding no difference in 30-day complications is the counterweight. The French national database supplies the incidence, the mortality and the risk factors. The analgesic plan is practice, not evidence.

    25 studies, 21,917 patients, one randomised: arthroscopic lavage had higher clinical success than arthrotomy (OR 2.52) and fewer complications (OR 0.69); repeated aspiration carried more re-intervention (OR 3.35) than arthroscopy; no difference in stay or mortality; the authors propose arthroscopy as the reference standard in the immunocompetent adult

    168 arthrotomies and 216 arthroscopies for the septic knee: no difference in adverse events, return to theatre or readmission at 30 days after adjustment; serious adverse events occurred in a quarter to a third of both groups, a measure of how sick the population is
  8. Jaffe D, Costales T, Greenwell P, Christian M, Henn RF. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infection is a risk factor for unplanned return to the operating room in the surgical treatment of a septic knee. J Knee Surg 2017;30:872-878
    80 adult septic knees: a single washout succeeded in 75.8% of arthroscopic and 80.9% of open cases, not different; MRSA was the only independent predictor of an unplanned second washout, with a higher synovial white cell count also associated after arthroscopy
  9. Couderc M, Gomez S, Mathieu S, et al. Longitudinal incidence of native septic arthritis in France: a 13-year analysis of the National Health Data System database. Rheumatology (Oxford) 2026;65(5)
