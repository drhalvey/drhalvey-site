26 Periacetabular osteotomy

Bernese (Ganz) periacetabular osteotomy for acetabular dysplasia and retroversion in the adolescent and young adult hip

What the surgeon is doing and why

Acetabular dysplasia is a socket that is too shallow and faces the wrong way. The femoral head is covered laterally and anteriorly by labrum instead of bone, the load that should spread over a hemisphere is carried on the edge, and the labrum hypertrophies, tears and the cartilage beside it delaminates. Left alone, the hip arrives at arthroplasty in its forties. The periacetabular osteotomy cuts the acetabulum free from the pelvis as a single fragment, rotates it so that bone sits over the head, pushes it medially so that the joint reaction force falls, and fixes it with screws until it unites. It is a reconstruction of the pelvis in a patient who is usually a woman in her late teens or twenties: the multicentre complication cohort had a mean age of 25 and was 70% female. The same osteotomy rotated the other way treats acetabular retroversion.

The Bernese design keeps the posterior column intact, and that one decision explains the operation. Because the ischial and retroacetabular cuts stop short of the posterior column, the pelvic ring stays in one piece, the fragment is held by screws rather than an external fixator, the patient can sit and bear partial weight from the first day, and the birth canal is unchanged. It also explains the two cuts the surgeon cannot see: the ischial cut is made under the capsule by feel and fluoroscopy, and the retroacetabular cut runs down the quadrilateral plate with the sciatic notch a centimetre behind it.

The sequence is fixed. Get onto the pelvis through a modified Smith-Petersen approach, taking the anterior superior iliac spine or the sartorius origin, lifting iliacus off the inner table and working under rectus femoris and iliocapsularis to the capsule. Make four cuts: ischium below the socket, superior pubic ramus medial to the iliopectineal eminence, the supra-acetabular ilium towards the pelvic brim, and the retroacetabular cut down the quadrilateral surface to meet the ischial cut. Mobilise the fragment on a Schanz screw, reorient it under the image intensifier, fix it with three or four screws from the iliac crest, graft the gap, open the joint if the labrum or the head-neck junction needs attention, repair and close. A hip arthroscopy may precede the osteotomy under the same anaesthetic; it adds its own time, not to the osteotomy’s.

The pain logic follows from a free piece of pelvis held by screws. The dominant mechanism is bone, but not the bone of a single cut that is then rested: four cancellous surfaces across the ilium, pubis and ischium, with the fragment loaded through the hip by every protected step for six to eight weeks until it unites, and a pubic ramus that unites slowly or not at all. Beneath that is muscle, from iliacus and the abductors stripped from both tables of the ilium and the rectus origin taken down, so every hip flexion and every transfer hurts; a capsule re-tensioned by the reorientation (in cadaveric testing the osteotomy reduced abduction and internal rotation and increased adduction and external rotation); and nerve, because the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve runs through the wound. The early peak with a working continuous technique is a 3 or 4; the 5 is earned afterwards. In the largest analgesic cohort, patients on a periarticular injection pathway left hospital with a pain score of 4.8, and when patients describe dissatisfaction with this operation, half of it is pain. Compare hip arthroscopy, which treats the labral consequences of the same dysplasia through portals and sits at 2.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Pre-operative plan: blood, the continuous technique, and chemoprophylaxis timing

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon’s requirements are a still patient on a radiolucent table for two to three hours, a field dry enough to see osteotomes going into bone they cannot see, and a patient who can get out of bed with crutches the next morning.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Three decisions are made in clinic, not on the day. Blood: the calculated loss is 900-1,500 mL in the series that measured it, 11% more for every hour of surgery, and the haematocrit nadir settles at about 0.69 of the pre-operative value, so a 20-year-old woman with a borderline haemoglobin needs iron and a crossmatch, not a group and hold. The analgesic technique: a continuous technique for the first 48 h is the author’s default for a score of 5, and the choice between a lumbar epidural and a peripheral catheter or single-shot block with infiltration is set by the unit’s pathway (see the pain plan), because the epidural changes the length of stay, the bladder catheter and the timing of every anticoagulant dose. Chemoprophylaxis: three of 205 patients in the multicentre cohort had a thromboembolic event, so youth does not exempt this operation from LMWH or aspirin.

  2. 2 Induction, lines, position and the regional technique

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The patient is supine on a radiolucent table with the image intensifier on the operative side, and the leg is draped free so that the hip can be flexed and adducted for the ischial cut and ranged at the end for impingement. Fluoroscopy is used for every cut and for the correction.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A tracheal tube for a two to three hour supine case with a litre of loss and a C-arm over the abdomen; two large cannulae; an arterial line is the author’s practice because the losses come in pulses at the cuts and the pressure needs to be readable while the surgeon is asking for it to be low. Cefazolin 2 g IV and tranexamic acid 1 g IV before the knife, dexamethasone 8 mg IV for the block as much as for nausea. If an epidural is the plan it goes in before induction at L2-3 or L3-4 and is tested; a combined spinal-epidural lets the spinal carry the exposure and the epidural start from the first cut. A suprainguinal fascia iliaca block takes 30-40 mL of ropivacaine 0.375% and six minutes. Warm the patient from the start and pad the contralateral leg with care: one of the nine reoperations in the multicentre cohort was a decompression of the other leg’s peroneal nerve.

  3. 3 Approach: the spine, the iliacus and the rectus origin

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The incision runs along the anterior iliac crest and down towards the thigh. The anterior superior iliac spine is osteotomised with the sartorius and inguinal ligament attached, or the sartorius is released; iliacus is lifted subperiosteally off the inner table down to the pelvic brim; the direct head of rectus femoris is taken down or, in the rectus-sparing modification, retracted; iliocapsularis is elevated off the capsule to expose the front of the joint and the infracotyloid groove. The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve crosses the field under the inguinal ligament, is protected, and still produces dysaesthesia in a proportion of patients.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The first bleeding is from the inner table and the nutrient vessels of the ilium as iliacus comes off, steady rather than dramatic, and the cell salvage should be collecting from now. Retractors on iliacus lie on the femoral nerve. Muscle relaxation makes the inner table reachable; a light patient with a tense iliopsoas is an unworkable field. The muscle mechanism of the pain is created here, and it is the part a fascia iliaca block covers well.

  4. 4 The ischial cut

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The first and blindest cut. A curved osteotome is passed under the capsule along the infracotyloid groove to the ischium below the acetabulum and driven through the medial two-thirds of the ischium, stopping short of the posterior cortex so that the posterior column survives. The surgeon works by feel and by the lateral and anteroposterior images; the sciatic nerve lies posterolateral to the osteotome’s tip, and the hip is flexed and adducted to relax the capsule and the nerve.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The surgeon will ask for the leg in flexion and adduction and for stillness; neuromuscular block is the right answer rather than depth. Nothing bleeds much here. A sciatic palsy after this operation originates at this osteotome or from a posterior column fracture, and the reviews find it more often with the ilioinguinal and two-incision approaches than with this one; it declares itself when the block recedes, so the foot is examined the morning after, not only in recovery.

  5. 5 The pubic cut

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The superior pubic ramus is divided medial to the iliopectineal eminence. Retractors are placed around the ramus to protect the obturator neurovascular bundle beneath it and the femoral vessels in front of it; a corona mortis, the anastomosis between obturator and external iliac systems that crosses the ramus in a proportion of pelves, is the vessel that bleeds here.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Brisk venous bleeding from behind the ramus is the obturator plexus or a corona mortis and is controlled with packing and patience; the arterial bleed that needed embolisation in the adolescent series and the external iliac vein injury in the Stoppa series are the rare versions. A sudden pressure fall here is surgical, not anaesthetic, and the cell salvage, the second cannula and the crossmatched blood are what you brought for it. The retractor on the front of the ramus compresses the femoral vessels: a leg that is pale and pulseless at the end of the case was clamped here.

  6. 6 The supra-acetabular and retroacetabular cuts

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The iliac cut runs from the outer table below the anterior inferior iliac spine towards the pelvic brim and stops a centimetre short of the iliopectineal line. From its end the retroacetabular cut turns down the quadrilateral surface at roughly 120 degrees, behind the socket and in front of the posterior column, to meet the ischial cut from above. The surgeon has the inner table exposed and the outer table only felt; the greater sciatic notch, the superior gluteal vessels and the sciatic nerve are behind the osteotome.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The largest bleed of the operation: cancellous bone on both surfaces of a thick iliac cut, the venous plexus on the quadrilateral plate and the nutrient vessels of the ilium, with the superior gluteal artery as the thing that must not be hit. The request for a lower pressure arrives here. Hold a mean of 60-65 mmHg in a healthy 25-year-old for the minutes it takes, and not lower, because the dysplastic hip is the only abnormal organ in this patient. A cut that strays into the posterior column converts the operation: the pelvic ring is broken, the fragment is unstable, plate fixation and a longer, bloodier case follow, and three of 715 patients in the large single-surgeon series were reoperated for exactly that.

  7. 7 Fragment mobilisation and reorientation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    With all four cuts complete the fragment is levered free on a Schanz screw and rotated: lateral and anterior coverage are increased, the fragment is medialised to shorten the lever arm of body weight, and version is set so that the socket is neither retroverted (which impinges) nor excessively anteverted. The surgeon checks the correction on a true anteroposterior pelvis, measuring the lateral centre-edge angle, the roof angle and the crossover sign, and adjusts until the numbers are right.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Moving a fragment still attached by soft tissue moves the whole pelvis, and the table and the patient move with it; a deep, relaxed patient and an arterial line watched rather than charted. Fluoroscopy time accumulates over thirty or more exposures. The retension mechanism of the pain is set here: the capsule, the psoas tendon over the front of the joint and the abductors are all at a new length when the fragment is fixed.

  8. 8 Fixation and bone graft

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Three or four long cortical screws from the iliac crest into the fragment, sometimes a transverse screw from the anterior inferior iliac spine, hold the correction. The wedge that opened at the iliac cut is grafted with the bone removed from the anterior spine or the osteotomy. Stability is checked and the posterior column examined on the lateral image.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Drilling and screws bleed little. If the correction has been large the iliac wedge is wide, and the volume of bleeding cancellous surface left under the closure is the volume that drains over the first night.

  9. 9 Arthrotomy, femoral osteochondroplasty and the impingement check

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Over half of these patients have a cam deformity or a labral tear as well, and the surgeon either opens the capsule to resect the head-neck junction and inspect the labrum, or has already dealt with it arthroscopically at the start. The hip is then flexed and internally rotated under the image to make sure the new roof does not impinge, because an over-corrected or retroverted fragment creates the disease the operation was meant to prevent.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A capsulotomy adds the capsule mechanism to the pain and a bleeding femoral neck to the losses; an arthroscopy done first adds irrigation fluid tracking into the iliopsoas and the retroperitoneum, which can make the exposure wet, and the abdomen is felt before the osteotomy begins. The range-of-motion check is a reminder that the leg is free and the patient is about to be moved hard.

  10. 10 Repair and closure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The anterior spine is reattached with a screw or sutures, rectus is repaired, the abdominal wall muscles are closed to the crest, a drain is often left on the inner table, and the skin closed. Nothing is under tension and nothing is protected by a splint; the protection is the crutches.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Tranexamic acid 1 g IV. Return the salvaged blood, check a haemoglobin and a gas, and count the losses honestly: the suction, the swabs and the cell saver reservoir add up to what the surgeon will describe as moderate. Infiltration of the wound and the periarticular tissues with ropivacaine by the surgeon is the posterior and capsular cover a fascia iliaca block does not provide and is worth asking for; on a periarticular injection pathway it is the whole regional technique. A patient with an epidural is extubated with the infusion running; a patient with a single-shot block is extubated with the first oral doses already given.

  11. 11 Recovery, the first 48 h and the day the catheter comes out

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants protected weight-bearing from day 1 (toe-touch to partial, with crutches, for six to eight weeks until the osteotomies unite), hip flexion limited to protect the rectus repair, and an early radiograph. Nonunion of the pubic ramus is common and usually silent; loss of fixation and migration of the fragment is the reoperation they fear.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Haemoglobin on the evening of surgery and on days 1 and 2, because the fall continues as the haematoma equilibrates; 71% of one large cohort reached a haematocrit below 26. The trajectory on this card has a working continuous technique for 48 h: 3 on the first evening, 4 on day 1, and the 5 on day 2, which is the day the epidural or catheter stops and the patient walks further. Regular paracetamol and an NSAID from the first evening (the surgeon’s view on NSAIDs and union is a conversation to have once per surgeon; most accept a short course), oral opioid for the nights of the first week, and a written plan for day 2. On a block-and-infiltration pathway the figures for days 0 and 1 are about a point higher and the patient goes home a day earlier with the same day 7. Examine the lateral thigh (lateral femoral cutaneous nerve), the quadriceps (femoral nerve, retractor) and the foot (sciatic and peroneal) when the block has receded, against what you recorded before it. Chemoprophylaxis starts on the evening of surgery or day 1 by the epidural rules.

Pain plan

The mechanism is four pelvic osteotomies loaded through the hip for weeks, a large muscle elevation, a re-tensioned capsule and a nerve in the wound, so the plan has to cover lumbar and sacral plexus territory for 48 h and then hand over to an oral regimen that runs for two to three weeks. There is no PROSPECT guideline for this operation and the systematic review of its analgesia found six studies. What exists is a set of institutional cohorts comparing the lumbar epidural that was standard for thirty years against what replaced it, and they agree with each other.

The epidural covers everything: ilium, pubis, ischium, iliacus and the abductors, the capsule, and the posterior cuts that no anterior block reaches. In the 724-patient cohort it gave a pain score of 2.9 over the first 48 h against 4.7 for periarticular injection, and the price was a stay of 3.4 days against 2.2, a bladder catheter, hypotension and legs that do not walk on day 1. The erector spinae cohort measured the side effects directly: weakness in 16% of epidural patients against 4%, numbness in 40% against 10%, and symptomatic hypotension in 11% against 1%, for one point more pain on day 0 and less opioid with the plane block. The suprainguinal fascia iliaca cohorts found stays of 2.9 days against 4.1 and 4.4, earlier physiotherapy milestones, lower daily opioid use and no difference in pain scores at physiotherapy. Removing the epidural on day 1 rather than day 2 shortened stay in one of the six studies.

The defensible default, then, is a continuous technique for the first 48 h, and the author’s judgement is that which technique is a decision about the pathway, not about the evidence. Where the unit runs a three to four night stay with a bladder catheter and a physiotherapist who works around an epidural, a low-concentration lumbar epidural (ropivacaine 0.2% or less with fentanyl) started before induction gives the best analgesia on record for this operation and is stopped on day 1 or 2 rather than left to run. Where the pathway is two nights and walking on day 0 or 1, the choice is a suprainguinal fascia iliaca block with dexamethasone (single shot, or a catheter where the unit can run one), surgeon periarticular infiltration to cover the capsule and the posterior cuts, and an oral regimen started before the block recedes; an erector spinae catheter is the third option, with one comparison behind it. In every version: intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, paracetamol and an NSAID from the first evening, oral opioid for the first week’s nights, and tranexamic acid twice.

Rebound pain is the planning target for the block pathway. The ambulatory cohort found it in half of all block patients, with bone surgery (OR 1.82), female sex (OR 1.52) and younger age raising the odds and intravenous dexamethasone lowering them; this patient has all three risk factors and a bone that will be loaded the next morning. The written plan for the night the block recedes, and the opioid charted before it does, are the difference between the 2.9-day stay in the cohorts and a 4 am call.

Escalation: pain above 7 on day 1 or 2 with a soft thigh, a warm foot and a working quadriceps is treated with the charted rescue opioid and a check that the regular doses were given; a tense groin and thigh with a haemoglobin falling faster than the drain explains is a haematoma or a vessel, and the surgeon is called before the second unit is given; new quadriceps weakness or a foot drop after the block has receded is a retractor, an osteotome or a posterior column fracture and is the surgeon’s to assess that day; calf pain or breathlessness in the first week is a thromboembolic event until proven otherwise. Pain at the pubis at six to eight weeks is a ramus nonunion and usually settles; pain at the iliac crest or the screws is the commonest reason for a second small operation.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Blood. The losses are proportional to time and to the size of the correction; a surgeon early in their experience, a large correction, a previous pelvic operation or a bilateral staged programme in one admission all push the figure past a litre and a half. Two doses of tranexamic acid, cell salvage and a crossmatch are the floor.
  • The request for hypotension. Cancellous bone bleeds at any pressure; a mean of 60-65 mmHg for the minutes of the iliac and retroacetabular cuts is reasonable in a healthy young patient, lower is not, and the arterial line is what makes the number honest.
  • Concomitant arthroscopy or open osteochondroplasty. Adds 45-90 min, irrigation fluid in the iliopsoas and a capsule mechanism to the pain; it did not raise the complication rate in either large series and does not lengthen the osteotomy itself.
  • Posterior column fracture or an intra-articular cut. A fragment that will not stabilise, a plate, an hour and a litre more, and sometimes a change to non-weight-bearing; it presents to you as a surgeon who has gone quiet and a pressure that is falling.
  • Nerve injury. Lateral femoral cutaneous dysaesthesia is common and sometimes lasting (four neurolyses in 715 patients); femoral palsy is a retractor; sciatic palsy is the ischial osteotome or a posterior column problem, commoner with other approaches. A dense epidural or fascia iliaca block hides all three until day 1 or 2; the pre-block examination is the baseline.
  • Vascular injury. Rare, but the obturator plexus, a corona mortis, the external iliac vein and the superior gluteal artery are all within reach of the osteotomes and retractors, and embolisation has been needed.
  • Nonunion and heterotopic ossification. The pubic ramus commonly fails to unite and is usually silent; heterotopic bone was the commonest minor complication in the multicentre cohort. NSAIDs are a surgeon-by-surgeon conversation, and most accept a short course.
  • Venous thromboembolism. Three of 205 in the multicentre cohort despite a young population; chemoprophylaxis is routine and the epidural sets the timing.
  • The adolescent. The same operation at 14 with open growth plates, often for a neuromuscular or syndromic dysplasia, carries more minor complications and the same blood loss; a concomitant femoral osteotomy doubles the bone and the bleeding and was the predictor of major complications in the adolescent series.
  • Retroversion and the reverse osteotomy. Same cuts, the fragment rotated the other way; same pain, same blood, and more complications when dysplasia and retroversion are corrected in one fragment.
  • Smoking. Raised the odds of a complication by about 70% in the large series.

Key sources

The analgesic evidence for this operation is a set of institutional cohorts, one of them prospective in its block arm, comparing a lumbar epidural with what replaced it: fascia iliaca blocks, an erector spinae catheter and periarticular injection. They agree that the epidural gives the lowest pain scores over the first 48 h and the longest stay, and that the alternatives trade about a point of pain for a day in hospital and legs that work; none is randomised and the systematic review found six studies in total. The blood management evidence is better: a double-blind randomised trial of tranexamic acid and a before-and-after series agree on about 300 mL less loss and a halving of transfusion. The complication figures come from a prospective multicentre cohort of experienced surgeons and a 715-patient single-surgeon series. The pain score and trajectory are the author’s calibration of those cohorts against the Part B scale; the operation is not in the Gerbershagen cohort, and no number on this card is borrowed from it.

  12. Ali M, Malviya A. Complications and outcome after periacetabular osteotomy: influence of surgical approach. Hip Int 2020;30:4-15
    40 studies, 4,070 hips: overall complication rate about 7%; lateral femoral cutaneous and sciatic nerve injuries more frequent with the ilioinguinal and two-incision approaches than with the modified Smith-Petersen and minimally invasive approaches
  13. Shamrock AG, Westermann RW, Gulbrandsen TR, Khazi ZM, Carender CN, Willey MC. Hip arthroscopy prior to periacetabular osteotomy does not increase operative time or complications: a single center experience. Iowa Orthop J 2021;41:127-131
    93 PAOs, 72% preceded by arthroscopy under the same anaesthetic: PAO time 128 vs 125 min, so the arthroscopy is additional time, not a longer osteotomy
  14. Barry GS, Bailey JG, Sardinha J, Brousseau P, Uppal V. Factors associated with rebound pain after peripheral nerve block for ambulatory surgery. Br J Anaesth 2021;126:862-871
    972 ambulatory block patients: rebound pain in 49.6%; bone surgery OR 1.82, female OR 1.52, younger age; absence of IV dexamethasone OR 1.78. The PAO patient carries all three risk factors
