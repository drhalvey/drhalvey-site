100 Developmental dysplasia of the hip: closed and open reduction, femoral and pelvic osteotomy

Examination under anaesthesia, arthrogram, adductor tenotomy and closed reduction in the infant of 6-18 months; anterior or medial open reduction; femoral shortening and Salter, Pemberton or Dega pelvic osteotomy in the child of 18 months to about 8 years; all finishing in a hip spica

4Severe

Dominant mechanism: Bone. Open reduction with osteotomy: the ilium cut across or hinged open and held with a wedge of iliac crest, the femur shortened and plated, the capsule opened, trimmed and double-breasted tight, psoas and the adductors divided, and the iliac apophysis split. Bone leads, capsule and muscle spasm follow, and the spica that stops movement is what ends it early. Closed reduction with tenotomy alone is a 1-2

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Block
Open reduction with osteotomy: epidural analgesia for 24-48 h (caudal-threaded or lumbar catheter, ropivacaine 0.1-0.2% at up to 0.4 mg/kg/h over 1 year of age, 0.3 mg/kg/h at 6-12 months, 0.2 mg/kg/h under 6 months), or a single-shot caudal of ropivacaine 0.2% 1 mL/kg (max 20 mL) with clonidine 1-2 microg/kg backed by nurse-controlled morphine. Closed reduction: single-shot caudal 1 mL/kg
Catheter: selective A catheter for any combination that includes a pelvic osteotomy, bilateral surgery, or a child over about 4 years in whom spasm is fierce; a single-shot caudal with clonidine or preservative-free morphine for open reduction alone and for the unit that cannot nurse an epidural inside a spica. Never for closed reduction
Alternatives: Lumbar plexus (psoas compartment) block, ropivacaine 0.2% 0.5 mL/kg (max 20 mL), single shot or catheter, for unilateral surgery; better than suprainguinal fascia iliaca in the one randomised comparison; Suprainguinal fascia iliaca or quadratus lumborum or transversalis fascia plane block, ropivacaine 0.2% 0.5 mL/kg, where neuraxial block is declined or contraindicated; Caudal preservative-free morphine 30-50 microg/kg with the local anaesthetic, over 12 months of age, with 24 h of respiratory monitoring; Surgeon infiltration of the iliac crest and wound, within a total of ropivacaine 3 mg/kg
Position
Supine at the edge of a radiolucent table with a bump under the operative hemipelvis and the whole leg and flank prepared free; moved to a spica table at the end. Arthrogram and closed reduction are done supine on the flat table, then the spica table

Tell the patient
Your child will wake in a plaster cast from the chest to the ankles; a numbing medicine near the nerves keeps the hip comfortable for the first day or two, and the cast itself is what makes the following weeks manageable.
The trap
A 10 kg child has 800 mL of blood: 150 mL in the swabs and under the drapes is a fifth of it, lost quietly from cut ilium over two hours while everyone watches the radiograph. Weigh the swabs, know the starting haemoglobin, have blood available before the pelvic osteotomy starts, and remember that the finished spica hides the wound, the abdomen and the caudal site

What the surgeon is doing and why

A hip that is not concentrically located in infancy does not form a socket. The acetabulum is cartilage that ossifies into the shape of whatever presses on it: a seated femoral head moulds a deep cup, an empty socket stays shallow and fills with fat (pulvinar), and the capsule, psoas tendon and labrum reshape themselves around the dislocated head and become obstacles to putting it back. Treatment is therefore a ladder, and each rung exists because the child has outgrown the one below:

age             problem                          operation                    your case
0-6 months      lax, reducible                   harness                      none
6-18 months     reducible but tight adductors    arthrogram, tenotomy,        45-60 min
                                                 closed reduction, spica
6-24 months     blocked: psoas, limbus, capsule  open reduction, spica        90-120 min
over 18 months  socket too shallow to hold       + pelvic osteotomy           2.5-4 h
over 2-3 years  head too high, tight when down   + femoral shortening         2.5-4 h

Closed reduction is done under anaesthesia because the reduction must be gentle and the surgeon must feel its stability: the arc between the angle at which the hip redislocates in adduction and the limit of comfortable abduction is the “safe zone”, widened by dividing adductor longus through a stab incision. The arthrogram shows whether the cartilaginous head is deep in the socket or sitting on an inverted labrum with a pool of dye medial to it. The cast then holds 90-100 degrees of flexion and no more than 45-55 degrees of abduction, because forced abduction compresses the vessels to the head: avascular necrosis is the complication of treatment, not of the disease.

Open reduction removes what blocks the head. Through an anterior groin-crease incision the surgeon splits the iliac apophysis, exposes the capsule, divides psoas at the brim, opens the capsule in a T, removes the ligamentum teres and pulvinar, divides the transverse acetabular ligament, everts the labrum, seats the head and closes the capsule tightly (capsulorrhaphy) to hold it. A medial approach through the adductors is used by some surgeons under 12 months. In the walking child the socket is redirected or reshaped at the same sitting. A Salter osteotomy cuts straight across the ilium above the acetabulum and tips the whole socket forwards and outwards on the pubic symphysis, held by a wedge of iliac crest and two wires. Pemberton and Dega osteotomies are incomplete cuts that hinge the roof down on the triradiate cartilage or the posterior column, reducing the volume of a capacious socket. If the head only reduces under pressure, 1-2 cm of femur is removed and plated, usually with derotation, to protect the head.

Pain logic: for the top of the ladder this is a pelvic and femoral osteotomy with capsular tensioning and muscle division in a toddler, which is a 4, with bone as the lead mechanism, and spasm of the divided and stretched muscles as the pain parents remember. It behaves differently from the adult periacetabular osteotomy, a 5, because the spica abolishes movement-evoked pain and a child’s bone is quiet in three or four days.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Examination under anaesthesia and arthrogram

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon assesses reducibility and stability with the muscles relaxed, then injects 1-2 mL of contrast into the joint by a medial or anterior needle under screening to outline the cartilage head, the labrum and any medial pooling.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    An infant of 6-18 months and 7-12 kg, usually well, usually a first-born girl. Fasting: formula 4 h up to 12 months of age and 6 h after it, breast milk 3 h, clear fluid until 1 h. Inhalational induction, cannula, and a supraglottic airway or tube according to what follows: a closed reduction can become an open one on the arthrogram, so ask the surgeon’s estimate before choosing, and intubate if the spica table is in the plan and the child is under about 10 kg. Do the caudal now (ropivacaine 0.2% 1 mL/kg, max 20 mL): adductor relaxation helps the examination, and once the cast is on the sacrum is out of reach.

  2. 2 Adductor tenotomy and closed reduction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Dividing adductor longus percutaneously at the groin widens the safe zone so the hip stays in with less abduction, which protects the blood supply of the head. The hip is then reduced by flexion and gentle abduction with a lift on the trochanter.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A stab incision in the groin, covered by the caudal. The tenotomy bleeds briefly from the wound and is pressed on, which is a second reason for the caudal to be done first. No relaxant is needed and the surgeon wants to feel native tone, so avoid one where possible.

  3. 3 Spica in the human position

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The cast is applied on a spica table with hips flexed to about 100 degrees and abducted about 45 degrees, moulded behind the greater trochanters to keep the head forward in the socket.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The spica table routine is described on the femoral fracture card: warm theatre (24-26 degrees C), abdominal towel under the cast, tube and circuit supported, no forced-air warming possible while the plaster goes on, and no lightening until the cast is hard and the check radiograph is done. Many units then confirm reduction by MRI or limited CT in the cast; if that is to happen under the same anaesthetic, plan the transfer, the monitoring and the MRI-safe equipment before induction.

  4. 4 Setting up for open reduction with osteotomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs the hemipelvis tilted up, the leg free, the image intensifier, and two to four hours.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is now major surgery in a small child. Tracheal tube, two cannulae (one away from the leg and the cast), a urinary catheter if an epidural infusion is planned, temperature probe, underbody warming, and warmed glucose-containing isotonic maintenance fluid in the infant. Haemoglobin and a group and screen before the day; crossmatch for bilateral surgery, a combined pelvic and femoral procedure, or a starting haemoglobin under 110 g/L. Calculate the allowable loss on the whiteboard: blood volume 75-80 mL/kg, so a 12 kg child with a haematocrit of 0.35 reaches 0.25 after about 250 mL. Tranexamic acid 15 mg/kg IV (max 1 g) and cefazolin 30 mg/kg IV (max 2 g) before incision. Place the epidural or caudal catheter, or the lumbar plexus block, after induction and before positioning.

  5. 5 Anterior approach, apophysis split and psoas tenotomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A bikini incision below the iliac crest; the interval between sartorius and tensor is opened, the cartilaginous iliac apophysis is split and the muscles peeled off both tables of the ilium subperiosteally, rectus femoris is detached, and psoas tendon is divided over the pelvic brim.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Stripping the ilium is where the steady ooze begins, from periosteum and nutrient vessels that cannot be cauterised. The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve is in the wound and the femoral nerve lies on psoas, one retractor away. Keep the mean arterial pressure at the lower end of normal for age (about 50-55 mmHg in the infant, 55-65 mmHg in the preschool child) without chasing hypotension, and start counting swabs.

  6. 6 Capsulotomy and clearing the socket

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The capsule is opened along the acetabular rim and down the neck, the ligamentum teres is followed to the floor of the socket and excised with the pulvinar, the transverse ligament is cut, and the head is seated under vision to judge how much pressure it is under.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The quiet part. The surgeon’s assessment here decides whether a femoral shortening is added, which is another 45 minutes and another 3-5 mL/kg; ask.

  7. 7 Femoral shortening and derotation osteotomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A second, lateral incision; the subtrochanteric femur is cut, 1-2 cm is removed, excessive anteversion is corrected, and a small plate is applied. This takes the pressure off the reduced head in the older child whose soft tissues have shortened.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Marrow and muscle bleeding from vastus lateralis. The femoral component is why a lumbar plexus or epidural technique is preferred to an iliac-crest-only block: the pain map now runs from T12 at the crest to L3-4 at the femur. Cefazolin is repeated at 4 h.

  8. 8 Pelvic osteotomy: Salter, Pemberton or Dega

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    For a Salter osteotomy a wire saw is passed through the sciatic notch and the ilium is cut across to the anterior inferior spine; the lower fragment is levered forwards and outwards with a towel clip, a triangular graft from the crest is wedged in, and two or three wires are driven across into the supra-acetabular bone. Pemberton and Dega cuts are made with curved osteotomes under screening and stop short of the notch.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The bloodiest ten minutes, from cancellous iliac bone, and the moment of risk for the sciatic nerve and superior gluteal artery in the notch: a sudden brisk bleed here is arterial and needs blood in the room, not ordered. Reassess total loss now: at about 15-20% of blood volume lost with osteotomies still to close, measure the haemoglobin, and in the stable child transfuse packed cells 10-15 mL/kg at 70 g/L, sooner if the bleeding is brisk or the child is unstable, because a further unmeasured loss continues under the cast. Levering the acetabular fragment can provoke a vagal bradycardia in an infant; atropine 20 microg/kg IV should be drawn up.

  9. 9 Capsulorrhaphy and closure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Redundant capsule is excised and the remainder is sewn tight with the hip in flexion, abduction and internal rotation, so the capsule itself holds the head in. The apophysis is closed over the crest, and a radiograph confirms the position.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The leg is held by an assistant in the position of stability from now until the cast is hard; nothing you do should require it to be moved. Send a haemoglobin or blood gas before leaving the flat table, since the next opportunity is after 20 minutes on the spica table and with the child covered in plaster. Load the epidural, or top up the caudal through the catheter, before the back disappears.

  10. 10 Spica and emergence

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The cast holds the hip in the position the capsule was closed in, typically 30 degrees of flexion, 30 degrees of abduction and internal rotation after an anterior open reduction, for six weeks, often followed by a second cast.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Secure the epidural catheter up the back and out over the shoulder or flank with the filter outside the cast, and confirm the dressing is not going to be plastered over; a caudal-threaded catheter sits in the nappy area and is usually limited to 48 h for that reason. Paracetamol 15 mg/kg IV (max 1 g) before the end. The child will wake unable to move the legs, in a hard shell, with strangers: clonidine 1 microg/kg IV or dexmedetomidine 0.5 microg/kg IV over 10 minutes before emergence, an awake extubation once the cast is set, and a parent in recovery early. Tachypnoea or desaturation in a new cast is a tight abdomen until proven otherwise.

  11. 11 The ward and the next cast

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants warm, moving toes, a clean perineal opening and a family who can lift. At six weeks the cast is changed, with an examination and sometimes an arthrogram, under a short anaesthetic.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Charted regularly: paracetamol 15 mg/kg every 6 h (max 60 mg/kg/day) and ibuprofen 10 mg/kg every 8 h from 3 months of age; diazepam 0.05-0.1 mg/kg orally (max 5 mg) every 6-8 h for spasm; oral morphine 0.1-0.2 mg/kg or oxycodone 0.1 mg/kg every 4 h as required once the epidural stops. The change of spica is 30-40 minutes with a supraglottic airway and no block; it is also when wires are removed.

Pain plan

The mechanism for open reduction with osteotomy is bone at two sites (ilium T12-L2, proximal femur L2-4), a tensioned capsule and divided muscle, score 4, peaking on the first night and day 1 and largely gone by day 4 inside the cast. Rebound is high because the standard single-shot caudal ends 4-6 hours after it was placed, which for a morning case is early evening, with spasm arriving at the same time.

The author’s default follows from that. For anything including a pelvic osteotomy, and for bilateral surgery, an epidural infusion for 24-48 hours: ropivacaine 0.1-0.2% at 0.2-0.4 mg/kg/h over 1 year of age (0.3 mg/kg/h maximum at 6-12 months, 0.2 mg/kg/h under 6 months), with clonidine 1 microg/kg added to the loading dose or 0.1 microg/kg/h to the infusion for spasm. Where an epidural cannot be nursed in a spica, a single-shot caudal of ropivacaine 0.2% 1 mL/kg (max 20 mL, which is the caudal ceiling of 2 mg/kg) with clonidine 1-2 microg/kg extends analgesia to 8-12 hours, and preservative-free morphine 30-50 microg/kg in the caudal carries it to 12-24 hours in the child over 12 months at the cost of itch, nausea, retention and a requirement for respiratory monitoring overnight. Either is backed by nurse-controlled morphine (bolus 10-20 microg/kg, lockout 15-20 minutes, no background under 12 months without high-dependency observation).

For unilateral surgery a lumbar plexus block or catheter is a legitimate alternative that spares the other leg and the bladder; it outperformed suprainguinal fascia iliaca block in a 60-child trial, and plane blocks (quadratus lumborum, transversalis fascia) beat no block in another. The only comparison of neuraxial against plexus techniques is retrospective and favours the caudal epidural by about two points of pain at 24 hours with no opioid difference. That is thin, and the choice between a neuraxial and a plexus technique is the author’s judgement, weighted by the fact that many of these operations are bilateral or staged within weeks. Compartment syndrome is not a realistic concern in elective hip surgery; a pressure area under the cast at the heel or sacrum is, and a child with a dense block cannot report it, so the infusion should permit some movement of the toes.

Closed reduction with tenotomy needs only the caudal, paracetamol and ibuprofen. Escalation: screaming with rhythmic jerks of the legs inside the cast is spasm, treated with diazepam and clonidine, not with opioid. A child who is pale, tachycardic and irritable on the evening of an osteotomy has bled into the pelvis until a haemoglobin says otherwise.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Underestimated loss. Transfusion was needed in 11.8% of a 721-operation series, predicted by a low starting haemoglobin, a small child, bilateral surgery and the number of osteotomies. The Dega osteotomy in particular adds loss that never reaches the suction.
  • Hypothermia. A 10 kg child, a wide preparation from nipples to toes, a long case and then wet plaster: underbody warming from the start and a warm room at both ends.
  • The medial approach. Shorter, less bloody, no iliac crest; a caudal suffices. Its avascular necrosis rate was 20% in the pooled series, higher under 12 months.
  • Bilateral dislocations. Staged two to six weeks apart by most surgeons; the second anaesthetic is in a child with one hip in plaster, and a caudal or epidural must be sited before the first cast or through a window.
  • Teratological dislocation. Arthrogryposis, myelomeningocele (latex precautions), Larsen syndrome (cervical spine instability) and other syndromes: stiff, high dislocations needing the full ladder, with the anaesthetic problems of the syndrome.
  • Older children (4-8 years). Triple or double pelvic osteotomies approach the adolescent periacetabular osteotomy in blood loss and pain; plan a catheter.
  • Redislocation in the cast. Found on the post-operative scan and returned to theatre within days; the cast comes off for the revision, so the block options are those of steps 1 and 4.
  • Epidural in a spica. The insertion site cannot be inspected; fever with back pain or leg weakness means the cast is windowed.
  • Residual dysplasia surgery years later is an isolated pelvic osteotomy in a school-age child: the same pain plan without the capsulorrhaphy.

Key sources

The surgical ladder is consensus practice with observational support. The transfusion figures come from one large retrospective cohort; evidence for tranexamic acid in children’s hip osteotomy is extrapolated from spine and adult practice and from small mixed studies in neuromuscular hips (see the cerebral palsy card). Regional technique rests on two small randomised trials of peripheral blocks and one retrospective comparison with caudal epidural analgesia; no trial has compared an epidural infusion with single-shot caudal plus adjuncts in this operation. Doses are standard paediatric formulary values. See also the child having orthopaedic surgery.
