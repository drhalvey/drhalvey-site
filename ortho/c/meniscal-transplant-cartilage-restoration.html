87 Meniscal transplant and staged cartilage restoration

Meniscal allograft transplantation with bone plug, bone trough or soft tissue fixation; two-stage matrix-induced autologous chondrocyte implantation; fresh osteochondral allograft; and the osteotomy or ligament reconstruction that often accompanies them

3Moderate

Dominant mechanism: Bone. Meniscal transplant: a bone trough or tunnels cut in the tibial plateau, a meniscal rim rasped to bleeding tissue, and a row of sutures through the posterior and peripheral capsule tied over it through a separate incision. Second-stage chondrocyte implantation and osteochondral allograft: an arthrotomy and a defect cut back to stable walls or reamed to a bony socket. Bone and capsule together, in a knee that is then kept off full weight and moved passively. The first-stage biopsy is surface work only and is a 1

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: moderate.

Tell the patient
The knee will be sore and swollen for the first few days, most on the day after the operation when the numbing wears off; the longer task is protecting the new tissue, which means crutches and limited weight for six weeks or more and a brace or a motion machine to move the knee gently.
The trap
The graft sets the terms of the day: a size-matched meniscus or a fresh osteochondral allograft with a short shelf life, or the patient's own cultured cells with an expiry time, has been committed before the patient arrives, so an avoidable cancellation wastes something irreplaceable, and afterwards the tissue is protected by weight-bearing limits that a dense motor block and a careless first stand can break

What the surgeon is doing and why

These are operations for a young knee that is missing something: a meniscus removed years earlier, or a patch of articular cartilage that has sheared off. Neither tissue regenerates. Without a meniscus the contact area in that compartment falls and the peak pressure on the cartilage rises, and the compartment aches and then wears. A full-thickness cartilage defect leaves bone to bear load through a hole with unsupported edges that enlarge. The patient is typically between 18 and 45, too young and too active for an arthroplasty, and the surgeon’s aim is to replace the missing tissue with living tissue and buy a decade or more. The simpler cartilage procedures (chondroplasty, microfracture, small osteochondral autograft plugs) are on the knee arthroscopy card; this card covers the larger reconstructions.

Meniscal allograft transplantation puts a donor meniscus, size-matched from radiographs or MRI, into the empty compartment. The surgeon debrides the remnant to a thin bleeding rim for the graft to heal to, then fixes the graft at its two horns, which is what makes it bear load: for the lateral side usually as a bone bridge carrying both horns that slots into a trough cut in the plateau, for the medial side as two bone plugs in tunnels, or in either case by sutures through tunnels without bone. The graft is drawn into the joint through an enlarged portal or a small arthrotomy and its periphery is sutured to the capsule with a row of vertical mattress sutures, many passed from inside the joint and retrieved through a posteromedial or posterolateral incision. No fixation method has shown a clear advantage. In the pooled long-term studies most series reported ten-year graft survival of 73% or better, with a wide range, and in athletes nearly one in four needed a revision or had a complication.

Matrix-induced autologous chondrocyte implantation is done in two operations. At the first, a short arthroscopy, the surgeon maps the defect and takes a few hundred milligrams of healthy cartilage from a non-weight-bearing margin. The cells are expanded in a laboratory over several weeks and seeded onto a collagen membrane. At the second, through a small arthrotomy, the defect is cut back to stable vertical walls and its base cleared to subchondral bone without making it bleed, the membrane is cut to a template and fixed with fibrin glue, and the knee is cycled to confirm it stays put. In the randomised trial of defects of 3 cm2 or more, pain and function at two years improved significantly more than after microfracture, and the difference held at five years.

A fresh osteochondral allograft replaces cartilage and its underlying bone together and is used for large or deep lesions and failed earlier repairs. The surgeon reams the defect to a cylindrical socket a few millimetres into bone, cuts a matching dowel from the same site on a donor condyle, washes the marrow out of it, and presses it in flush. Pooled survival is about 87% at five years and 79% at ten, with reoperation in three in ten.

None of these works in a knee that is crooked or unstable, so a realignment osteotomy or an ACL reconstruction is often done in the same sitting or as a stage, and when it is, that operation sets the anaesthetic and the pain.

The pain logic: bone and capsule. The trough, tunnels or reamed socket are small, well-defined bone injuries; the arthrotomy and the peripheral and posterior capsular sutures hurt with movement; nothing is under tension and the knee is deliberately unloaded. That gives a 3, peaking on day 1 when a single-shot block recedes, with a rehabilitation that is long but not especially painful. The first-stage biopsy is a diagnostic arthroscopy with a 1.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Planning, staging and the graft

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon confirms alignment, stability and the size of the defect, orders a size-matched meniscal allograft or accepts a fresh osteochondral graft offer, or books the second stage for the date the cultured cells are released. Fresh osteochondral grafts and cultured chondrocytes both have a limited usable life.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The patients are young and usually fit, so the anaesthetic assessment is short; its purpose is to find anything that could cancel the case on the day (an untreated respiratory infection, a pregnancy test, an unmanaged anticoagulant) early enough to fix. If the case cannot proceed, a meniscus that has not yet been thawed can be kept, but a fresh osteochondral graft or a cell batch may be lost. Ask what else is being done in the same sitting: an osteotomy changes the block, the stay and the pain score. Ask about previous operations on the knee, which are the rule, and about how the patient coped.

  2. 2 First-stage biopsy arthroscopy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Two portals, a systematic look, measurement of the defect, and a cartilage biopsy with a curette or gouge from the edge of the notch or the trochlea. Twenty to thirty minutes.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A day-case general anaesthetic with a supraglottic airway, portal infiltration by the surgeon, paracetamol and an NSAID, and no nerve block. It behaves exactly as a diagnostic arthroscopy on the knee arthroscopy card, and the patient walks out fully weight-bearing. The value of this visit to you is that it previews the second: note the airway, the nausea and the analgesic response.

  3. 3 Anaesthetic and block for the second stage or the transplant

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs a still leg for one to three hours, access to the whole knee including its posterior corner, and a patient who can go home that day or the next on crutches.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway suits most; a spinal is an alternative for the 60-90 minute second stage. Where chemoprophylaxis is planned after a spinal, the first prophylactic dose of enoxaparin is given at least 12 h after the needle and a direct oral anticoagulant no sooner than 6 h after it; see venous thromboembolism prophylaxis. An adductor canal block with 15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% before incision covers the anterior and medial knee, the arthrotomy and the tibial bone work while leaving the quadriceps able to control the leg on crutches. Record foot dorsiflexion and sensation before any block for a lateral meniscal transplant, because the common peroneal nerve lies in the suture-retrieval field. Dexamethasone 8 mg IV and the antibiotic before the tourniquet.

  4. 4 Positioning and tourniquet

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The knee has to move through its whole range and be stressed into varus and valgus to open each compartment; the surgeon uses a post or a leg holder and drops the foot of the table or hangs the leg.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Pad the other leg and keep its hip and knee slightly flexed if it hangs. A leg holder high on the thigh sits near the tourniquet and both compress the thigh: check that neither traps skin and that the holder is not directly on the cuff. Tourniquet times run longer than for routine arthroscopy; note the time and warn at 90 min. See the tourniquet.

  5. 5 Arthroscopy and preparation of the bed

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    For a transplant, the meniscal remnant is trimmed to a 1-2 mm vascular rim and rasped, and the horn attachment sites are cleared. For a cartilage procedure, the defect is inspected and measured again before the knee is opened; the plan can change if the lesion has progressed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Low stimulation, pumped irrigation and a cool patient if the case is long: fluid warming and forced air from the start for a three-hour transplant. The allograft is thawed or the cell membrane opened only once the surgeon has confirmed the knee is suitable, so there may be a pause.

  6. 6 Meniscal transplant: trough or tunnels, and graft passage

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A slot is burred and chiselled across the plateau in line with the horns (lateral), or two tunnels are drilled from the front of the tibia to the horn footprints (medial). The graft, prepared on the back table with traction sutures, is pulled into the joint through an enlarged portal or small arthrotomy and its bone seated in the slot or tunnels and secured.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the bone step and the main source of the day 1 pain: tibial plateau bone, covered by the adductor canal block only in part, since the posterior and lateral plateau belong to the sciatic nerve. Getting the graft through the portal and under the condyle needs the compartment opened hard; the surgeon may ask for relaxation, and some release part of the collateral ligament to gain room.

  7. 7 Meniscal suturing and the posterior incision

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The posterior horn and body are sutured to the capsule with inside-out needles retrieved through a posteromedial or posterolateral incision, the anterior horn with outside-in sutures. On the lateral side a retractor is placed in front of the lateral head of gastrocnemius and the incision kept anterior to biceps femoris, to shield the common peroneal nerve; on the medial side the saphenous nerve and vein are at risk under the retractor.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Long flexible needles cross the joint and emerge at the back of the knee; any movement or cough is a hazard to the surgeon’s fingers and to the nerve. Keep the patient deep and still. A new foot drop in recovery after a lateral transplant is a peroneal nerve caught in a suture until proven otherwise, which is why a sciatic or popliteal block is not used here. Numbness over the medial shin after a medial transplant is the saphenous nerve and is usually temporary; it is also what your adductor canal block produces, and a block of ropivacaine 0.5% with intravenous dexamethasone can last beyond 24 h, so the threshold is tied to the block: the patient reports medial shin numbness that is still present at 48 h, or that remains after feeling has returned to the rest of the knee.

  8. 8 Second-stage chondrocyte implantation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Through a 5-8 cm parapatellar arthrotomy the defect is debrided with a scalpel and curette to healthy vertical walls, the calcified layer removed while keeping the subchondral plate intact and dry, a template cut, and the cell-seeded membrane trimmed, placed cell side down and sealed with fibrin glue. The knee is flexed and extended several times; if a tourniquet was inflated it is released and the surgeon watches for bleeding under the membrane.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A dry bed matters more here than anywhere else in knee surgery, because blood beneath the membrane lifts it. Avoid a hypertensive surge at tourniquet release and during closure. The surgeon will not want a suction drain pulling on the graft and will not want local anaesthetic poured into the joint over living cells; infiltration goes into the capsule, fat and skin only. The laboratory product has an expiry time on the day, so this case should not be moved down the list.

  9. 9 Osteochondral allograft

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Through an arthrotomy a guide pin is placed perpendicular to the lesion, the recipient site is reamed to healthy bleeding bone 6-8 mm deep, the depth is measured at four points, and a matching dowel cut from the donor condyle is trimmed, irrigated with pulsed lavage to remove marrow elements, and pressed in by hand with minimal impaction, since hammering kills chondrocytes.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Reaming subchondral bone is brief and stimulating. The graft is fresh donor tissue: check the allergy and consent paperwork with the team as for any allograft. Stability comes from the press fit, so the first weeks are protected exactly as for the other procedures.

  10. 10 Concomitant osteotomy or ligament reconstruction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A varus knee overloads a medial transplant or a medial condyle repair, so the surgeon corrects it with a tibial or femoral osteotomy; an ACL-deficient knee shears a new meniscus, so the ligament is reconstructed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Read the relevant card and apply its plan in full: an osteotomy is a 4 with a case for a catheter, a tourniquet time approaching two hours when combined, tranexamic acid and an inpatient stay. Combined cases run four hours or more, long enough to justify a tracheal tube, active warming and a urinary catheter in some patients.

  11. 11 Closure, brace and emergence

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The arthrotomy and incisions are closed in layers, a compressive dressing and cold therapy applied, and a hinged brace fitted, locked in extension for walking after a transplant, with a set flexion limit.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Wake the patient with the brace on and the leg supported; an agitated emergence with the knee flexing hard against fresh meniscal sutures or a glued membrane is what the brace is for. Check ankle dorsiflexion after a lateral transplant before anything else. Start oral analgesia in recovery while the block is working.

  12. 12 Rehabilitation and weight-bearing

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    After a meniscal transplant most surgeons prescribe touch or partial weight-bearing in extension for about six weeks and limit flexion to 90 degrees, to keep load off the posterior horn fixation. After chondrocyte implantation or an osteochondral allograft, a graded return to full weight-bearing over six to eight weeks with continuous passive motion; in the one randomised trial, of 37 knees, a six-week return gave clinical and MRI outcomes no different from eight weeks at 5 and at 10 years, with an overall failure rate of 8.1% at 5 years and 11.8% at 10. Patellofemoral grafts can bear weight in extension early but have flexion restricted.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The trajectory on this card is 2 on the first evening, 5 on day 1 as the block recedes, 4 on day 2, 3 on day 3 and 2 at a week. The physiotherapist needs to know which block was used before the first stand: the restriction is partial weight on a knee that must not buckle or twist, and a patient who does not feel the leg may load it fully. Day-case discharge needs safe crutch use within the limit, the brace understood, and a written night plan.

Pain plan

The mechanism is modest bone work and capsular suturing in the anterior and medial knee, with a posterior capsular component after a transplant, in a young ambulatory patient who must control the leg on crutches. The adductor canal block fits that: sensory cover of the saphenous territory and the medial and anterior capsule with little quadriceps loss. There is no trial of regional analgesia in meniscal transplantation or cartilage restoration. The nearest evidence is the meta-analysis of adductor canal block in ambulatory knee surgery, which found a modest benefit after minor arthroscopy (lower pain scores to 8 h and about 7 mg less oral morphine equivalent over 24 h) and none after ACL reconstruction, so the block here is the author’s judgement that these procedures sit between the two and that the bone and arthrotomy component justifies it. The first-stage biopsy does not need one.

The defensible default for the second stage, the osteochondral allograft and the isolated transplant: a single-shot adductor canal block with dexamethasone 8 mg IV, the surgeon’s extra-articular infiltration of portals, arthrotomy and posterior incision, paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started before the block recedes and continued for one to two weeks, and an oral opioid for the first two to four nights. Some surgeons restrict NSAIDs after a transplant or an allograft out of concern for graft-to-bone and meniscus-to-capsule healing, on laboratory and observational grounds; if so, paracetamol and the opioid carry more of the load and the night plan matters more. Cold compression is effective and universally liked. Continuous passive motion is usually comfortable within the set range.

After a transplant with many posterior sutures, posterior knee pain on day 1 is expected and is outside the adductor canal block. An infiltration between the popliteal artery and the capsule covers it without motor block and is an option once peroneal function has been confirmed; the author does not use a sciatic block, for the reason in step 7. Intra-articular local anaesthetic is avoided over implanted cells and fresh cartilage; the chondrotoxicity evidence is summarised on the knee arthroscopy card.

Rebound is moderate: young patients having bone surgery are the group with the highest odds in the 972-patient ambulatory cohort, and dexamethasone lowers them. Escalation triggers: a tense, hot knee with fever in the first two weeks is infection, which with an allograft in place is urgent; a locked knee or a sudden mechanical catch is a displaced graft or a torn suture; calf pain on restricted weight-bearing is a thrombosis until scanned; and foot drop or persistent numbness beyond the block’s duration goes to the surgeon the same day.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Combined procedures. The commonest reason the card’s score is wrong for the patient in front of you. Transplant with osteotomy, or cartilage repair with a tibial tubercle osteotomy for a patellar lesion as on the patellofemoral card, is a 4 with an overnight stay.
  • Patellofemoral cartilage lesions. The arthrotomy is larger, the patella is everted or tilted, and quadriceps inhibition afterwards is marked; a femoral block is especially unhelpful.
  • Medial against lateral transplant. Medial: saphenous nerve at the posteromedial incision, bone plugs and tunnels. Lateral: common peroneal nerve at the posterolateral incision, a bone trough, and the popliteal artery not far behind the posterior horn.
  • Graft logistics. A meniscal allograft is ordered to size and stored frozen; fresh osteochondral grafts are offered at short notice with a short usable window; cultured cells have a release date and time. Each makes the list order and the start time less flexible than usual.
  • Arthrofibrosis. Stiffness after an arthrotomy and weeks in a brace sometimes needs a manipulation or arthroscopic release, as on the manipulation card.
  • Reoperation. Three in ten after osteochondral allograft and close to one in four after transplant in athletes: second-look arthroscopy, suture or implant removal and revision are part of the pathway, and the patient will be back.
  • Disease transmission and infection. Allograft carries a very small risk of transmitted infection and the usual risk of bacterial contamination; a hot knee after an allograft is treated as infected until the aspirate is clear.
  • The patient on opioids before surgery. Uncommon in this group but important when present, since they have often had three or four knee operations already.

Key sources

The surgical evidence is strongest for chondrocyte implantation, which has a randomised trial against microfracture with five-year follow-up and a small randomised trial of rehabilitation speed (37 knees) with ten-year follow-up. Meniscal transplantation and osteochondral allografting rest on systematic reviews of case series with wide ranges of graft survival. No analgesic trial exists for any of the three; the block recommendation is extrapolated from ambulatory knee surgery and from the anatomy, and the pain score and trajectory are the author’s calibration between knee arthroscopy and ACL reconstruction.
