94 Limb salvage resection and endoprosthetic reconstruction

Wide resection of a primary bone sarcoma with modular megaprosthesis: distal femoral, proximal tibial, proximal femoral, total femoral and proximal humeral replacement

4Severe

Dominant mechanism: Muscle. A long incision that ellipses the biopsy track, with whole muscles or parts of them taken with the tumour, the rest detached and sutured back onto a metal implant or a mesh sleeve; a diaphyseal osteotomy, a reamed canal and a long stem; major nerves dissected free over 15-20 cm and sometimes one sacrificed; in a patient weeks out of chemotherapy and often already on opioid

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: high.

Block
Perineural catheters laid by the surgeon under direct vision beside the nerves already exposed in the dissection (sciatic for the distal femur and proximal tibia, with a femoral or adductor canal catheter for the anterior wound), bolused only after the neurovascular examination in recovery, then run for three to five days; general anaesthesia with a ketamine infusion for the operation
Catheter: routine The dissection is large, the pain lasts beyond 72 h and physiotherapy starts early, so a continuous technique is the default; it is started late on purpose, because the surgeon must know that the foot moves and is perfused before anything is blocked
Alternatives: Lumbar epidural for lower limb resections when the platelet count and coagulation allow, run dilute so that foot movement can still be checked; Interscalene or supraclavicular catheter for proximal humeral replacement, placed away from the tumour and its biopsy track; Fascia iliaca or lumbar plexus catheter for proximal femoral replacement; Opioid patient-controlled analgesia with ketamine and no catheter when counts, infection risk or the surgical field rule one out
Position
Supine with a bump under the buttock and the whole limb free-draped to the iliac crest for the knee and femur; lateral for some proximal femoral resections; beach chair for the proximal humerus; the contralateral leg padded and accessible for a vein graft if a vascular reconstruction is possible
Tourniquet
Sometimes. A sterile thigh tourniquet is used by some surgeons for proximal tibial and distal femoral resections during the dissection, inflated after elevation without exsanguination and released before the implant goes in; proximal femoral, total femoral and humeral resections have none
Cement
Sometimes. Stems are cemented or press-fit according to the surgeon, the bone and the planned radiotherapy; a cemented diaphyseal stem is long, pressurised into a reamed canal and a true cement step, and a knee reconstruction has two of them, femoral and tibial
Blood loss
500-1,500 mL for a distal femoral or proximal tibial replacement; 1,000-3,000 mL for proximal and total femoral replacement or after a pathological fracture; less with a tourniquet; the patient usually starts anaemic from chemotherapy, so crossmatch 2-4 units
Duration
180-300 min for a distal femoral replacement; 240-420 min for proximal tibia with a gastrocnemius flap, total femur, or any case with vascular reconstruction
Length of stay
5-10 nights, with a night in high-dependency care after the longer resections; chemotherapy restarts two to three weeks later if the wound allows. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV within 60 min of incision (3 g over 120 kg), repeated every 4 h and after 1.5 L of blood loss, continued for 24 h; the randomised trial of five days against one found no fewer infections and more antibiotic complications with the longer course
Tranexamic acid
Tranexamic acid 1 g IV before incision and 1 g at 3 h or at closure; malignancy raises the baseline thrombotic risk and the pooled surgical data show no excess of events
VTE
High risk: malignancy, a long operation, vessels dissected and retracted, chemotherapy. Mechanical prophylaxis from induction and low molecular weight heparin from the first evening or day 1 once the drains are quiet, timed around the catheter rules and the platelet count
Antiemetic
Dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction and ondansetron 4 mg IV; these patients have been made sick by chemotherapy for months and anticipatory nausea is common, so add a third agent early and not on request
Tell the patient
The tumour is removed with the bone around it and replaced with a metal implant; you will wake with thin tubes beside the nerves that give local anaesthetic for several days, and we will ask you to move your foot before we start them.
The trap
The patient looks young and fit and is neither: weeks after doxorubicin, cisplatin and methotrexate the heart, kidneys, marrow and peripheral nerves have all been dosed, so read the last echocardiogram, creatinine, magnesium and blood count as carefully as you would in a patient of 80, and document the pre-existing neuropathy before anyone places a catheter

What the surgeon is doing and why

A primary bone sarcoma (osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma in the young, chondrosarcoma in the middle-aged) is cured by removing it with a cuff of normal tissue on every side, a wide margin, and by chemotherapy for the first two. Amputation achieves that margin easily. Limb salvage achieves it while keeping the limb, on the condition that the main neurovascular bundle can be dissected free of the tumour and that enough muscle remains to cover and move a reconstruction. The knee is the commonest site for these tumours, so the common operations are a distal femoral or proximal tibial replacement; the proximal femur and proximal humerus account for most of the rest.

The operation has two halves with different surgical mindsets.

The resection is oncological and unhurried. The incision is long and includes the biopsy track as an ellipse. The surgeon never sees the tumour: dissection stays one muscle layer away from it throughout, so parts of vastus, or the whole of vastus intermedius, come out with the femur. The vessels and nerves are found in normal tissue above and below, traced through the adductor hiatus or the popliteal fossa, and every branch going to the tumour is tied. The joint is entered or, if the tumour has breached it, resected closed. The bone is then cut at a level measured from the pre-chemotherapy scan plus 2-3 cm, a marrow sample from the remaining canal goes for frozen section, and the specimen leaves in one piece.

The reconstruction is arthroplasty at scale. The remaining canal is reamed and a stem, cemented or press-fit, is fixed in it. A modular body rebuilds the length of bone that was removed, and at the knee a rotating hinge replaces the ligaments that went with the specimen, with a second stem in the tibia. Then the soft tissue: the remaining muscles are sutured to each other and to the implant, the patellar tendon is reattached to a proximal tibial prosthesis and covered with a rotated medial gastrocnemius flap, and the wound is closed over drains with no dead space, because a haematoma around a metal implant in a patient about to restart chemotherapy is how infection starts. Infection is the commonest way these implants fail (it was the leading failure mode in a five-centre series of 2,174), and in the randomised antibiotic trial about one patient in six had a surgical site infection within a year.

The pain logic: the dominant mechanism is muscle, a large soft-tissue dissection with detachment and reattachment, more than any arthroplasty. Bone contributes an osteotomy and reamed canals. Nerve contributes twice: the sciatic, peroneal or femoral nerve has been handled over a long distance and is neurapraxic in a share of patients, and where a nerve is sacrificed for the margin the patient has a deafferentation injury of the kind that produces phantom-type pain. The patient is typically young, which predicts more pain, and may be taking opioid for tumour pain already. That combination earns a 4 with a high rebound risk and a routine catheter.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Assessment after neoadjuvant chemotherapy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Surgery is scheduled about three weeks after the last pre-operative cycle, in the window when the marrow has recovered and before the next cycle is due.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Read the chemotherapy chart, not just the diagnosis. Doxorubicin causes a dose-dependent cardiomyopathy that can be silent at rest in a teenager: find the most recent echocardiogram and treat a reduced or falling ejection fraction as you would in an older patient with heart failure. Cisplatin leaves renal impairment, magnesium and potassium wasting and a sensory neuropathy; high-dose methotrexate adds renal and hepatic injury and mucositis; ifosfamide a tubular leak; vincristine a peripheral neuropathy with foot drop. Check the full blood count, electrolytes including magnesium, and creatinine on the day or the day before. Document the neuropathy in the notes before any regional technique. Ask about the central line: it has been used for months, is the patient’s lifeline for the rest of treatment, and is accessed with full asepsis or left alone.

  2. 2 Lines, monitoring and warming

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A four- to six-hour operation with steady blood loss from muscle and bone, most of which cannot be put under a tourniquet.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Arterial line, two large peripheral cannulae in addition to the existing central line, urinary catheter, temperature probe, forced-air warming over the upper body and warmed fluids. General anaesthesia with a tracheal tube. If an epidural is the chosen technique it is placed awake before induction with the platelet count and clotting known; in practice, borderline counts and the wish to examine the foot make surgeon-placed perineural catheters the more reliable plan. Antibiotic and tranexamic acid before the knife. Cell salvage in tumour surgery is a matter of unit policy; assume allogeneic blood.

  3. 3 Incision and the soft-tissue margin

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The biopsy track is excised in continuity and the dissection proceeds through normal muscle around the tumour’s reactive zone.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Blood loss is continuous and unimpressive at any one moment: it is the sum of cut muscle over a 30-40 cm wound for two hours. Weigh swabs, watch the suction, and check haemoglobin and clotting hourly. If a sterile tourniquet is in use it was inflated without exsanguination, and the usual rules apply (see the tourniquet). Keep the mean pressure normal; hypotensive anaesthesia to dry the field risks the kidneys that cisplatin has already injured.

  4. 4 Neurovascular dissection

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The femoral or popliteal vessels and the sciatic nerve with its tibial and peroneal divisions are freed from the back of the tumour. If the tumour encases the vessels they are resected and reconstructed with a reversed vein or a prosthetic graft; if it encases a major nerve, the nerve is taken.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Traction on the sciatic or femoral nerve can produce brief bradycardia or hypertension. A planned vascular reconstruction means systemic heparin at the surgeon’s request, a clamp time, and reperfusion; it also removes the epidural from consideration that day. An unplanned vascular injury is sudden arterial bleeding in a field with no tourniquet. If a nerve is sacrificed, note which one: the post-operative examination, the catheter plan and the pain trajectory all change, and the patient has in effect had a partial amputation of sensation.

  5. 5 Osteotomy and delivery of the specimen

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The bone is divided at the planned level, the last attachments are cut and the tumour comes out. The surgeon waits for the frozen section of the marrow margin before committing to a stem.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The open medullary canal bleeds freely and cannot be tamponaded until the stem is in. The wait for pathology is 20-30 minutes of ooze: a good moment to catch up on blood, calcium and temperature, and to tell the surgeon the running loss. A positive margin means a higher cut and a longer implant.

  6. 6 Canal preparation and stem fixation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The diaphysis is reamed and a stem 12-15 cm long is fixed. With cement, the canal is plugged, washed, dried and filled with a gun, and the stem pushed in to pressurise it.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is a cemented stem in a tight young diaphysis: high intramedullary pressure with no vent, and the mechanism of bone cement implantation syndrome complete. Echocardiographic work on intramedullary procedures found that almost all the severe pulmonary responses occurred during reaming of pathological bone or cementation. The healthy adolescent tolerates it; the patient with an anthracycline-impaired right ventricle, an older patient with a chondrosarcoma, or anyone hypovolaemic at that moment may not. Apply the hip fracture bundle: ask for the moment to be named, have the patient filled and the pressure supported beforehand, increase the oxygen, and watch the capnograph and the arterial trace for five minutes afterwards. At the knee there is a second cementation in the tibia; if a tourniquet is up, the load arrives at deflation instead.

  7. 7 Assembly, trial reduction and limb length

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Modular segments are built to match the resected length; the surgeon reduces the joint and checks rotation, patellar tracking and the tension in the vessels and nerves.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A limb restored to full length after weeks of tumour and fracture shortening stretches the sciatic and peroneal nerves and can kink a mobilised artery. The surgeon will feel for a pulse or use a Doppler probe with the knee straight; a warm, normotensive patient makes that examination meaningful. Vasopressor in high dose and a cold patient both make a healthy foot look ischaemic.

  8. 8 Soft-tissue reconstruction and closure

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Muscle is closed over the implant in layers. At the proximal tibia the medial gastrocnemius is rotated forward to cover the prosthesis and anchor the patellar tendon; at the proximal femur the abductors are fixed to the implant; at the shoulder the remaining cuff and deltoid are sutured to a mesh sleeve. Drains, then skin under no tension.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This takes an hour and is when the catheters go in: ask the surgeon to lay a catheter alongside the sciatic nerve in the proximal wound and bring it out through separate clean skin, and a second beside the femoral nerve or in the adductor canal if the anterior wound allows. Leave them unprimed with local anaesthetic. Flap and skin-edge perfusion depend on a warm, filled patient; correct the haemoglobin and temperature before emergence.

  9. 9 Emergence and the neurovascular check

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs to see the foot or hand move and to confirm pulses before the patient leaves theatre or recovery.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Wake the patient on systemic analgesia: the ketamine infusion continuing, a longer-acting opioid titrated in during closure. Once dorsiflexion, plantar flexion and sensation have been documented by the surgical team, bolus the catheters (10-15 mL of ropivacaine 0.2% each) and start the infusions. If a foot drop is present on waking, the catheter stays off until the surgeon has decided whether it is stretch, a tight dressing or a haematoma.

  10. 10 The first week

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Drains stay until output falls; the knee is rested in extension for a proximal tibial reconstruction and moved early for a distal femoral one; oncology wants the wound healed for chemotherapy.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    High-dependency care on the first night for the longer cases. Drain losses of several hundred millilitres are usual and the haemoglobin falls again on day 1-2. Low molecular weight heparin timing has to fit the catheters and the platelet count, which may still be recovering. Catheter sites in a patient who will be neutropenic again within weeks are inspected daily and the catheters removed at three to five days or at the first sign of redness.

Pain plan

The mechanism is a large muscle dissection with nerve handling, lasting well beyond 72 hours, so the plan is continuous regional analgesia on a systemic base. The defensible default for the lower limb is surgeon-placed perineural catheters started after the neurovascular check, running ropivacaine 0.2% at 5-8 mL/h each within a safe total dose, with regular paracetamol, a ketamine infusion at 0.1-0.2 mg/kg/h for 48 hours, and opioid patient-controlled analgesia. NSAIDs are withheld where cisplatin, methotrexate or ifosfamide have reduced renal function, which is most of the osteosarcoma population, and otherwise used. A lumbar epidural gives more complete analgesia and is a good choice when counts and coagulation are normal and the surgeon accepts a dilute solution; it conflicts with intra-operative heparin, with early thromboprophylaxis and with a falling platelet count. For the proximal humerus an interscalene catheter is placed before induction through skin and muscle clear of the tumour.

None of this rests on trials in sarcoma surgery. It is author’s judgement built from the mechanism, from catheter evidence in other large limb operations, and from the constraint that the nerves must be examined first. The delayed start is the price: the first hour in recovery is covered by opioid and ketamine, and the patient should be told so.

Three patients break the plan. The opioid-tolerant patient with months of tumour pain needs the baseline continued and the adjuncts from the start (see the opioid-tolerant patient). The patient with a sacrificed nerve has neuropathic pain in that territory within days and is started on a gabapentinoid early, as after amputation. And the patient whose counts or infection risk preclude any catheter gets ketamine, lidocaine or both by infusion and a higher opioid ceiling.

Escalation: pain that rises on a working catheter, with a tense thigh or calf, is haematoma or compartment syndrome, and the dilute infusion should not be blamed for hiding it before the limb has been examined. A cool or pale foot is the vascular repair until proved otherwise.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Proximal tibia. The hardest soft-tissue cover and the highest infection and extensor mechanism failure rates; the peroneal nerve lies on the fibular neck in the resection; the knee is splinted in extension for weeks.
  • Proximal and total femur. No tourniquet, the largest blood loss, abductors detached and a real dislocation risk; these are the cases for an arterial line and blood in theatre from the start.
  • Proximal humerus. Beach chair, the axillary nerve often sacrificed with the deltoid insertion, and a functional result that is a stable spacer for the hand; the pressure-at-the-head rule from anatomic TSA applies.
  • Children and growing prostheses. Weight-based everything, a smaller blood volume against the same wound, and repeat anaesthetics for lengthening unless the implant is magnetically driven.
  • Pathological fracture before resection. Haematoma has contaminated more tissue, the resection is wider and bloodier, and the patient arrives on more opioid.
  • Chondrosarcoma. No chemotherapy, older patients with ordinary comorbidity: the cement step is the risk, not the anthracycline.
  • Conversion to amputation. If the bundle cannot be freed with a margin, the consent usually allows the surgeon to amputate; the pain plan then moves to the amputation card with the catheters already in the right place.
  • Pre-operative radiotherapy. Used for Ewing sarcoma and some soft tissue sarcomas: fibrosed planes, more bleeding, poorer wound healing.

Key sources

The infection figures come from a large international randomised trial and a five-centre failure registry and are robust. The cement and embolic statements are extrapolated from arthroplasty and from echocardiographic studies of intramedullary procedures that included pathological bone; no cohort has measured bone cement implantation syndrome in sarcoma endoprostheses specifically. The chemotherapy toxicities are standard pharmacology. The analgesic plan has no procedure-specific trial evidence and is labelled as judgement throughout.
