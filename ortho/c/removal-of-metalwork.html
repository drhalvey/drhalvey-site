49 Removal of metalwork

Elective removal of plates, screws, wires and intramedullary nails after fracture healing: the short case that overruns, stripped and cold-welded screws, broken hardware, and what the antibiotic trial showed

2Mild to moderate

Dominant mechanism: Periosteum. An old scar reopened and the implant dug out of the bone and scar that have grown over it, with subperiosteal dissection along a plate or a re-entry through the patellar tendon or the gluteal muscles for a nail; empty screw holes and no fracture. A single screw or wire through a stab incision is a 1; a long locking plate or a nail is a 2, and a 3 on the day the screws strip and the surgeon is burring metal for an hour

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: low.

Block
Surgeon's infiltration of the wound with long-acting local anaesthetic under general anaesthesia; a regional block as the sole anaesthetic where it suits the site (axillary or supraclavicular for forearm and hand, popliteal and saphenous for ankle and foot) or a short-acting spinal for the lower limb
Catheter: no No indication: there is no fracture, no osteotomy and nothing under tension; the pain is incisional and falls within two or three days
Alternatives: Single-shot peripheral nerve block as the anaesthetic for forearm, hand, ankle and foot metalwork, chosen to outlast a tourniquet and a doubled operating time; Short-acting spinal (prilocaine or chloroprocaine) for lower limb metalwork in a day-case patient, accepting that it may not outlast a difficult extraction; General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway and infiltration alone
Position
Supine for most; whatever position put the metal in is the position that takes it out: lateral for a posterior distal humeral or lateral femoral plate, a traction table or a flexed knee over a triangle for a nail, prone for a posterior malleolar plate or spinal instrumentation
Tourniquet
Yes. Routine for limb metalwork distal to the mid-thigh or mid-arm; the cuff clock keeps running through a difficult extraction, and a stripped screw at 90 min means deflation before the metal is out; not possible for clavicle, proximal humerus, proximal femur or pelvis
Cement
No
Blood loss
Minimal under tourniquet; 100-300 mL for a femoral or tibial nail or a plate above the cuff; more when bone has to be removed to free the implant
Duration
15-30 min for a syndesmotic screw or wires; 30-60 min for a plate or nail as booked; allow double, because in one locking plate series the mean was 71 min, the range 28-180 min and five of 36 cases passed two hours
Length of stay
Day case. Day case: yes
Antibiotic
The one randomised trial (cefazolin 1 g IV against saline before removal of metalwork below the knee) showed no reduction in surgical site infection; many units still give cefazolin 2 g IV, and it is clearly indicated when metal is being replaced, the case is long, or infection is suspected (in which case it is withheld until samples are taken)
Tranexamic acid
Not needed

Antiemetic
Dexamethasone 4-8 mg IV and ondansetron 4 mg IV, as for any day case
Tell the patient
This is usually a small operation and you go home the same day; it is sore for a few days rather than weeks, and the bone needs protecting from heavy loads for about six weeks while the screw holes fill in.
The trap
The booking says 30 minutes and the metal has not been told: a locking screw that has cold-welded to its plate or a hexagonal head that strips turns a day-case removal into a two-hour operation with a burr, a tourniquet at its limit and a block or spinal that is running out

What the surgeon is doing and why

Fracture implants are scaffolding: once the bone has united they carry no load that the bone cannot, and most stay in for life. A minority come out, and the reason matters to you because it sets the operation. Symptomatic metal is the commonest: a plate under thin skin (clavicle, olecranon, distal fibula, medial malleolus, the subcutaneous tibia), a nail whose entry point hurts the knee, a screw tip that catches a tendon. Planned removal is the second: a syndesmotic screw, wires through a growth plate, elastic nails in a child, a bridging plate across a joint. Then come the removals that are steps in another operation (metal out so an arthroplasty can go in), the patient’s own wish, and the suspected infection or non-union, which is a different operation that happens to start the same way and belongs on the osteomyelitis or revision cards.

The indication is softer than the theatre list suggests, and the surgical literature has been walking it back. The syndesmotic screw is the clearest example: in the RODEO trial of 152 patients, leaving the screw in unless it caused trouble was noninferior to routine removal for ankle function at a year and at four years, and the routine-removal arm had 12 complications in 73 patients against one in 79. Removal has a cost, and the cost is mostly wound.

What the surgeon has to do sounds trivial and is not always. The old scar is reopened, and the plane beneath it is no longer anatomy: nerves that were found and protected at the first operation are now embedded in scar on top of the plate (the superficial radial nerve over a radial plate, the radial nerve over a posterior humeral plate, the superficial peroneal nerve over a fibular plate). Bone has grown over the plate edges and into the empty holes and has to be cleared with an osteotome before a screwdriver will seat. Then each screw has to turn. Two things stop it. The first is a stripped head: the hexagonal or star recess is soft, especially in titanium, it has usually been torqued once already, and if the driver is not fully seated in a recess full of fibrous tissue it rounds off on the first attempt. The second is cold welding: a locking screw threads into the plate as well as the bone, and titanium on titanium under load fuses at the thread, so the screw and plate become one piece. Titanium also osseointegrates, so the screw shank is held by bone along its length. In a series of 36 locking plate removals from the femur and tibia, 10.6% of 349 screws and 38.9% of cases were difficult, the mean operating time was 71 min with a range of 28-180 min, and nothing about the patient, the plate or the time since surgery predicted which case it would be.

When a screw will not turn, the surgeon climbs a ladder: a new driver and an impact to seat it; a conical reverse-threaded extraction screw; a hollow trephine drilled over the shank; cutting the plate between the holes so each fragment can be rotated out with its screw; a carbide or diamond burr to grind the screw head off, after which the plate lifts away and the headless shank is either unscrewed with a chuck, over-drilled, or left in the bone. Nails have their own version: the end cap is overgrown and has to be found under imaging, the extraction bolt has to thread into a nail whose top is full of bone, the nail may have been bent by a later injury, and a broken nail needs hooks, stacked guidewires or a window cut in the cortex. Each rung adds time, heat, metal debris and bone loss, and the surgeon will not know which rung is needed until the first screw is tried.

The pain logic is simple and favourable. There is no fracture, no osteotomy and nothing repaired under tension. The dominant mechanism is periosteum: a scar and a strip of bone surface re-dissected, with screw holes that behave like small drill holes. That is a score of 1 for a single screw or wire through a stab incision and 2 for a long plate or a nail, a day or two of incisional pain that responds to paracetamol and an NSAID. The only thing that moves it is the difficult extraction, which adds an hour of bone removal and tourniquet and gives the patient a 3 for the first two days.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 The booking: what is in there and what will get it out

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Implants are manufacturer-specific. The surgeon needs the original operation note or the implant record to know the make, the screw head (hexagonal, star, cruciate, proprietary), whether the screws are locking, and whether the metal is titanium or steel, and then needs the matching driver and a broken-screw set on the table. A radiograph shows broken screws, bone overgrowth and whether the fracture has united.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Read the same radiograph. A short steel plate with non-locking screws on a fibula is the 30 min case the list promises. A long titanium locking plate on a distal femur or proximal tibia, metal that has been in for years, a broken screw visible on the film, or a nail with no visible end cap are each a reason to plan for 2 h: a technique that can be extended, a second patient who is not fasting against a fixed clock, and a conversation with the surgeon about where they will stop. Metal put in at another hospital with no record is the highest-risk booking of all.

  2. 2 Choosing the anaesthetic for a case of unknown length

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs a still limb and, for most sites, a tourniquet. Nothing about the operation needs muscle relaxation.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The choice is driven by how the technique fails if the case doubles. General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway and wound infiltration fails gracefully and is the author’s default for anything with locking screws. A peripheral block as the sole anaesthetic suits the forearm, hand, ankle and foot and tolerates a long case, but must cover the tourniquet; an axillary or supraclavicular block does for an arm cuff, and a popliteal and saphenous block with a calf cuff avoids the thigh tourniquet that an awake patient will not tolerate beyond 45-60 min. A short-acting day-case spinal (prilocaine or chloroprocaine) is elegant for the 30 min ankle and is the technique most exposed by a stripped screw at 70 min; if the radiograph suggests difficulty, choose something else. Children’s metalwork is general anaesthesia with infiltration or a block placed asleep.

  3. 3 The antibiotic decision

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Removal of metalwork is classed as clean surgery, yet its infection rate is not that of clean surgery: old scar, a poorly vascular subcutaneous site, dead space where the plate was, and often a biofilm on the implant that was never clinically apparent.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The WIFI trial is the evidence and it is negative. In 477 patients having metalwork removed from below the knee, cefazolin 1 g IV gave an infection rate of 13.2% against 14.9% with saline, a difference of 1.7% with a confidence interval from 8.0% fewer to 4.6% more. The striking number is the baseline: 14% of these wounds became infected with or without the antibiotic. A second trial of cefazolin 2 g IV was designed because the first may have under-dosed; its protocol is published and no result has been reported. Practice therefore varies and both positions are defensible: no prophylaxis for a simple removal below the knee on the trial evidence, or cefazolin 2 g IV on the grounds that the dose tested was low, the drug is cheap and the wound is high-risk. Give it without debate when new metal is going in, when the case becomes long, or for an immunosuppressed patient, all of whom the trial excluded. Withhold it until deep samples are taken when the reason for removal is suspected infection or non-union.

  4. 4 Position, tourniquet and the clock

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A bloodless field lets the surgeon see nerves in scar and seat the driver cleanly in the screw head. The position is that of the original operation.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Note the inflation time and say it aloud at 60 and 90 min. A difficult extraction is the commonest reason for a tourniquet to reach 120 min in a minor case, and the surgeon who is one screw from finishing will not volunteer to deflate. Deflation with the wound open means bleeding from every screw hole and a worse view for the hardest part of the operation, so the earlier warning is the useful one. For a prone or lateral case the airway decision is the position’s, not the operation’s: a tracheal tube for a prone posterior malleolar plate even though the removal takes 20 min.

  5. 5 Reopening the scar and finding the metal

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The incision follows the old one. The surgeon dissects through scar to the implant, identifies any nerve at risk before it is cut, and clears fibrous tissue and bone from the plate surface and from every screw recess, because a driver seated in a recess full of tissue is how a head strips.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The stimulating part of a simple removal is here and at closure; the rest is turning screws. If you are relying on a block, this is where a missed cutaneous territory shows (the intercostobrachial and medial cutaneous nerves for a medial elbow incision, the saphenous for a medial malleolus). Avoid relaxant where a motor nerve lies in the scar: a radial nerve over a humeral plate is found partly by its twitch.

  6. 6 Turning the screws

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Each screw is backed out by hand. Non-locking steel screws usually come easily. Titanium locking screws are the problem: cold-welded into the plate at the head, osseointegrated along the shank, with a soft recess.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Count with the surgeon. If the first three screws turn, the case will run to time. The words to listen for are “it’s stripped” and a request for the extraction set, and the moment you hear them is the moment to revise everything downstream: tell the recovery unit, re-time the next patient, check how much of the tourniquet, the spinal or the block is left, and convert early rather than late. Converting an awake patient to general anaesthesia at 90 min with the cuff up and a burr running is harder than doing it at 60 min.

  7. 7 The extraction ladder: extractors, trephines and the burr

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A stripped screw is tried with a reverse-threaded conical extractor; if that fails, the head is ground off with a carbide or diamond burr so the plate can be lifted, or the plate is cut between holes; the remaining shank is gripped and unscrewed, over-drilled with a hollow trephine that removes a cylinder of bone with it, or left in place. Burring metal produces heat and a slurry of metal particles that the surgeon irrigates continuously to protect the skin and the wound.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is slow, noisy and unpleasant for an awake patient, who can feel vibration through bone outside a block and can smell it. Trephining removes real bone, turns a screw hole into a 6-8 mm defect, and is where the post-operative pain steps up from a 2 to a 3; give the intravenous NSAID and a longer-acting opioid now rather than in recovery. The decision to leave a headless shank in the bone is a good surgical decision, not a failure, and the patient needs to be told it was deliberate.

  8. 8 Lifting the plate or pulling the nail

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The plate is levered off the bone, sometimes with bone that has grown over its edges and sometimes, as in six of the 36 cases in the locking plate series, with screws still attached. For a nail the locking screws come out first, the proximal end is found (through the patellar tendon or a suprapatellar portal for a tibia, through the gluteal muscles for a femur), bone is cleared from its top, an extraction bolt is threaded in and the nail is driven out backwards with a slotted hammer.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Nail extraction is the only part of this operation with real blood loss, from the re-opened entry point and the empty canal, and the only part done without a tourniquet in the femur; 100-300 mL is usual. It is also impact loading on a supraglottic airway and a head that moves with every blow, so check the airway and the pressure areas afterwards. Fat embolism from an empty, unreamed canal is not a practical concern. A nail that will not move after the bolt is seated usually means a locking screw has been missed or a broken tip is retained; expect imaging and a pause.

  9. 9 Closure, tourniquet down and the empty holes

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The wound is irrigated of metal debris and closed over the dead space the implant leaves. Screw holes are stress risers: the cortex is weaker until they fill in over six to eight weeks, and the forearm, where both bones were plated, is the classic site of refracture.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Infiltration of the wound and the periosteal bed with long-acting local anaesthetic is the whole regional plan for most patients and worth asking for by name. Tourniquet deflation after a long inflation in a day-case patient brings the usual transient fall in pressure and a wound that oozes from every hole; a compression dressing follows. Paracetamol 1 g IV and an NSAID before the end.

  10. 10 Recovery and discharge

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the patient home, mobile and protected from heavy loading: no contact sport, heavy lifting or impact for about six weeks, with weight-bearing as comfort allows.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Standard day-case discharge. The patient who had a block as the anaesthetic goes home with a numb limb and written instructions; rebound is mild here because there is no fresh bone injury, unless the case was a difficult one with trephined bone, in which case the bone-surgery odds ratio from the rebound pain study applies and the oral opioid is worth supplying. Tell the patient if any metal was left behind and that it was a decision.

Pain plan

The mechanism is a re-dissected scar and periosteum with empty screw holes, so the plan is the simplest in the trauma section: wound infiltration, paracetamol and an NSAID, with an oral opioid offered for the first two nights after a plate or nail and not at all after a single screw. There is no procedure-specific guideline and no need for one.

Block choice follows the anaesthetic, not the pain. Where a block is the anaesthetic (forearm, hand, ankle, foot) it will also cover the first evening; where the case is done under general anaesthesia, the surgeon’s infiltration does the same job and a block adds a numb limb to a day-case discharge for little gain. The catheter decision is no, without qualification. The exceptions that deserve a single-shot block under general anaesthesia are the author’s judgement: the tibial or femoral nail in a young patient (adductor canal or femoral, for the knee entry), the clavicle plate (superficial cervical plexus or surgeon’s infiltration), and the patient with chronic pain or opioid tolerance in whom a minor operation is not minor.

Oral regimen shape: regular paracetamol and an NSAID for three to five days (there is no healing fracture to argue about, so the NSAID objection that surgeons raise in acute trauma does not apply); a small supply of immediate-release opioid for a plate or nail; nothing else.

Escalation: pain at day 3-7 that is rising, with a red, discharging wound, is the surgical site infection that one in seven of the trial’s below-knee patients developed, and it needs the surgeon. Sudden pain with a deformity after a trivial load in the first six weeks is a refracture through a screw hole.

Pitfalls and variants

  • The syndesmotic screw. A 15 min operation through a stab incision, often under local anaesthesia with sedation; a 1 on the scale. The randomised evidence now favours leaving it unless it causes symptoms, so the routine removal list is shrinking.
  • Wires and tension bands. Olecranon and patellar wires back out and prominent ends are the reason for removal; short, superficial, a 1, and suited to infiltration with or without sedation.
  • Elastic nails and plates in children. Planned removal at 6-12 months; general anaesthesia; the nail end may be buried in bone and a 15 min case becomes 45 min.
  • Tibial and femoral nails. The largest elective removals: a re-entry through the knee or the hip abductors, 100-300 mL of blood loss, anterior knee pain that does not reliably improve after the nail comes out, and a patient who may stay overnight.
  • Removal as the first stage of an arthroplasty. Old hip screws or a plate out before a hip or knee replacement at the same sitting: the case is the arthroplasty with an hour added and an unpredictable start, and the antibiotic is given.
  • Suspected infection or non-union. Not this card: antibiotics are held for samples, the bone is debrided, the wound may be left open, and the pain score and length of stay belong to the osteomyelitis card.
  • Broken implants. A broken plate or nail means the fracture did not unite; the removal is the first step of a revision fixation with bone graft, blood loss and a pain score two points higher.
  • Nerves in scar. Transient palsy after removal is the complication the surgeon fears most (the radial nerve at the humerus, the superficial radial and posterior interosseous nerves at the forearm, the peroneal nerve at the proximal fibula). A block makes the post-operative examination uninformative for hours; where the nerve was at risk, say so on the chart, or use infiltration instead.
  • The awake patient and the burr. Sedation depth that was right for turning screws is not right for 40 min of metal cutting; convert to general anaesthesia early.
  • Spinal instrumentation. Removal of pedicle screws and rods is a prone spinal operation with a muscle-stripping exposure and belongs with the fusion card for position and pain, at about a 3.

Key sources

The antibiotic question has one good randomised trial, negative at a 1 g dose, with a 2 g successor that has not reported. The difficulty of locking plate removal rests on a single 36-case series and a technique review, which match every trauma surgeon’s experience. The syndesmotic screw evidence is randomised with four-year follow-up. Nothing has been published on analgesia for this operation specifically, and the plan above is a deduction from the mechanism.
