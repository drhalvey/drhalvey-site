7 Frozen shoulder: MUA, hydrodilatation, capsular release

Manipulation under anaesthesia, hydrodilatation under block, and arthroscopic capsular release for adhesive capsulitis

2Mild to moderate

Dominant mechanism: Capsule. A contracted, fibrotic capsule torn (MUA), ruptured by fluid (hydrodilatation) or cut (arthroscopic release); sharp pain with movement that is deliberately provoked by physiotherapy from day 0 because the released capsule re-scars within days if the range is not used; settles over a week in most, but the pre-operative pain and the diabetic phenotype can run longer

The trap

What the surgeon is doing and why

A frozen shoulder is a capsule that has become inflamed, then fibrotic, then contracted. The rotator interval and the coracohumeral ligament thicken first, which takes away external rotation; the anterior and inferior capsule follow, which takes away abduction and elevation; the axillary pouch, which should let the head drop into it on abduction, is obliterated. The joint volume falls from 15-20 mL to a few millilitres. The disease is self-limiting over one to three years in most people, which is the fact every treatment has to be measured against, and it runs longer and harder in diabetics, who make up a third of the surgical population. Nothing about the humeral head, the glenoid or the cuff is wrong; the whole problem is a few millimetres of contracted collagen, and all three procedures on this card exist to tear, burst or cut it.

Manipulation under anaesthesia tears it. With the patient relaxed and insensate, the surgeon holds the scapula down with one hand and takes the humerus through forward elevation, then abduction, then external and internal rotation, using the forearm as the lever and the elbow as the grip, feeling and hearing the capsule give. The inferior capsule tears on abduction and the anterior capsule on external rotation. It takes five minutes. Most surgeons inject steroid into the joint at the same time. The costs are a fracture of the humeral shaft or surgical neck through a long lever in osteoporotic bone, a glenoid rim avulsion, a dislocation, a cuff tear or a plexus traction injury, each uncommon and each reported; the two recent series that counted found a glenoid avulsion in one of 68 and one of 110 manipulations, and a plexus injury in the second.

Hydrodilatation bursts it. Under image guidance, often by a radiologist rather than a surgeon, 20-40 mL of saline with local anaesthetic and steroid is injected into the joint until the capsule ruptures, usually into the subscapularis bursa, and the arm is then moved. It is done awake with local anaesthetic in many units, under a block in some, and is the least invasive of the three; the pooled trials find a small effect on pain and range over steroid injection alone that may not be clinically important, and the UK FROST team named it as the comparator their trial lacked.

Arthroscopic capsular release cuts it. Through a posterior portal in a joint with almost no room, the surgeon divides the rotator interval and coracohumeral ligament with a radiofrequency device, then the anterior capsule down to the subscapularis, then, in most hands, the inferior capsule round the axillary pouch, with the axillary nerve a few millimetres below it, and sometimes the posterior capsule. The arm is then manipulated through the released range, which is now easy. Many surgeons add a subacromial decompression. In UK FROST, 503 patients were randomised between release, MUA and structured physiotherapy with steroid injection, and at 12 months the release beat MUA by two Oxford Shoulder Score points and physiotherapy by three, which the trial had pre-defined as below clinical importance; MUA did not beat physiotherapy; serious adverse events were commoner after release (eight against two); and MUA was the most cost-effective arm while the release was not cost-effective at all. The honest summary is that all three treatments work, the release works slightly more and costs and risks more, and the physiotherapy afterwards is doing much of the work in every arm.

The pain logic. The mechanism is capsule: a torn, burst or cut fibrotic capsule that is sharply painful on movement and settles over days, with no bone cut, nothing repaired and nothing under tension. That would make a 1 if the patient were allowed to rest. They are not. The released capsule re-scars within days unless the new range is used, so the physiotherapy starts on the day of the procedure and is deliberately painful, and the patient’s pain in the first week is physiotherapy-evoked capsule pain on a background of the pre-existing inflamed joint. The diabetic shoulder is stiffer, bleeds more into the joint and re-scars faster, and the patient who comes to a release after a failed MUA has had two insults. That is why the card is a 2 rather than a 1, why the trajectory has a day 1 peak that is the physiotherapy rather than the rebound, and why the block on this card is as much a physiotherapy tool as an analgesic.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 The patient, the procedure and the block as the anaesthetic

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon has usually chosen between MUA, hydrodilatation and release on the duration of symptoms, the diabetic status, the previous treatment and their own habit; a post-surgical or post-traumatic stiff shoulder is more likely to get a release because the adhesions are extracapsular and a manipulation alone will not free them.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    For MUA and hydrodilatation the block is the anaesthetic. An interscalene block with 15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% and intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg gives a flail, insensate arm within 20 minutes and lasts into the evening; the surgeon manipulates with the patient awake or lightly sedated, which has the advantage that the patient sees the arm move through a range they have not had for a year, and the disadvantage that they hear the capsule tear. Units that do MUA under general anaesthesia alone are running the historical technique and leave the first night and the day 1 physiotherapy uncovered. For the arthroscopic release the block is the analgesic and a general anaesthetic goes on top because the beach chair and the camera make an awake patient hard work; the catheter decision is made now (see the pain plan). This population is in its fifties, a third diabetic, and the diabetic frozen shoulder patient is the one whose glucose the steroid and the dexamethasone will disturb for days; the ward and the patient need to know. A supraglottic airway is adequate for a release in a slim patient; a tube for the obese diabetic with reflux. Awake baseline pressure before any drug if a chair is involved.

  2. 2 Positioning for MUA and hydrodilatation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    MUA is done on the trolley, supine or semi-sitting, with the surgeon standing on the affected side holding the scapula down with one hand and the arm with the other; hydrodilatation is done supine or seated under ultrasound or fluoroscopy, usually in the radiology department.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    No chair, no head holder, no pressure problem. The block itself is the risk: a seated or semi-sitting patient with a fresh interscalene block has a vagal reflex waiting (two of 68 patients in one series had a vasovagal episode during the block, and one a panic attack), and the hoarseness and Horner’s syndrome that follow a high-volume interscalene are worth warning about before they are noticed. Monitoring and a cannula for the whole of the procedure, because the manipulation that follows is not pleasant to watch and the patient’s pressure will tell you how good the block is.

  3. 3 Examination under anaesthesia and the manipulation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon measures the range with the patient relaxed, then holds the scapula and takes the arm through forward elevation first (the safest direction, tearing the inferior capsule), then abduction, then external rotation at the side and in abduction, then internal rotation and cross-body adduction, feeling and hearing the capsule give. The elbow is the grip and the forearm the lever, kept short to limit the torque on the humerus. Each direction is held until the give is felt. Steroid is then injected into the joint by most surgeons.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the step the trap is about. The torque that tears a fibrotic capsule is transmitted through the humeral shaft, and in a thin-boned diabetic woman the shaft or the surgical neck can go first; glenoid rim avulsions and dislocations are the other reported failures. The anaesthetised or blocked patient gives no warning. Your contribution is a patient who is fully relaxed (a working block provides it; a general anaesthetic needs enough depth and, in some surgeons’ hands, a relaxant for the few seconds of the manipulation, because muscle tone resisting the lever is what transfers the force to bone) and a surgeon who is not fighting. A loud crack with a sudden loss of resistance is the capsule; a crack followed by a change in the shape of the arm or crepitus on the next movement is bone, and the procedure stops for a radiograph. A plexus traction injury from a forceful manipulation in a blocked patient presents the next day as a block that did not wear off; document the pre-block neurology for exactly that reason.

  4. 4 Hydrodilatation

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Under ultrasound or fluoroscopy, the joint is entered posteriorly or through the rotator interval, position is confirmed with contrast or a visible distension, and local anaesthetic, saline and steroid are injected under pressure in increments until the capsule ruptures, which the operator sees as a sudden loss of resistance and leakage into the subscapularis bursa; 20-40 mL usually does it and some operators stop short of rupture. The arm is then moved through the range.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Done awake with local anaesthetic in many units, under a suprascapular or interscalene block in some, and under sedation in a few. The injection against a contracted capsule is intensely painful without a block, and a patient who moves at the moment of rupture can lose the needle position. Under a block, the procedure is quiet and the physiotherapy that follows can start at once. Nothing else to do; the steroid is the surgeon’s and the glucose consequence is yours to warn about.

  5. 5 Beach chair and portals for the arthroscopic release

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Beach chair or lateral decubitus, as for any shoulder arthroscopy. The joint has almost no volume, so the posterior portal is placed by feel into a tight space, the capsule is distended with the pump as far as it will go, and the surgeon works in a few millilitres of room until the first releases open it up.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The usual beach chair arithmetic: the cuff overstates brain perfusion by about 1 mmHg per 1.25 cm between cuff and ear, a vasopressor infusion started early, no deliberate hypotension. The tight joint means a high pump pressure from the start, which with a long case means fluid in the deltoid; the release itself is short. Cefazolin 2 g IV before the knife in most units, though there is no implant.

  6. 6 Rotator interval, coracohumeral ligament and anterior release

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The rotator interval between supraspinatus and subscapularis is opened with radiofrequency and the coracohumeral ligament divided at the base of the coracoid, which restores external rotation; the anterior capsule is then divided from the superior glenohumeral ligament down to the top of the subscapularis, with the tendon itself preserved. Each cut gives a visible gain in range and more room to work.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Radiofrequency in a small, bleeding, fibrotic joint; the view is poor and the surgeon may ask for the pressure to come down. The answer is the same as on every beach chair card. In the diabetic the capsule is thicker, more vascular and bleeds more.

  7. 7 Inferior and posterior release

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The inferior capsule round the axillary pouch is released from the glenoid side, where the axillary nerve lies a few millimetres below the capsule as it crosses under the joint; surgeons vary in how far round they go, and some release the posterior capsule for lost internal rotation. The arm is then manipulated through the full range, which is now easy and quiet. Some surgeons add a subacromial decompression, which adds a raw bone surface and changes the pain mechanism (see subacromial decompression).

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The axillary nerve is the structure at risk from the inferior release and the block hides a deficit until tomorrow; document regimental badge sensation and deltoid power before the block. The manipulation at the end of a release is gentle, because the capsule has been cut rather than torn, and carries far less fracture risk than a primary MUA. A decompression added at the end adds bone to the mechanism and a point to the day 1 figure.

  8. 8 Closure, the physiotherapist and the first session inside the block

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Portals are closed; no sling, or a sling for comfort only, and the surgeon’s instruction is movement from today. A release that is not taken through its full range in the first days re-scars, and the whole rationale of the operation is lost.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is the step that separates frozen shoulder from every other shoulder card: the surgeon wants the arm moved, hard, while the block is working. The first physiotherapy session is timed for the recovery room or the ward on day 0, inside the block, and the patient is shown the range they now have. For MUA and hydrodilatation the session happens the same afternoon. If a catheter has been placed, the day 1 and day 2 sessions happen with a top-up or a running infusion; if not, the day 1 session is timed for the morning after the regular analgesia and a rescue dose, and the patient is told in plain words that the session will hurt and that the hurt is the point.

  9. 9 Discharge, the diabetic and the following week

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Day case for all three procedures. The surgeon wants daily physiotherapy for two weeks and home exercises several times a day, and the steroid in the joint is doing its anti-inflammatory work over that time.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The block lasts 12-24 hours with dexamethasone. Regular paracetamol and an NSAID start in recovery, oral opioid is dispensed for two or three nights and, unlike the other shoulder cards, the rescue dose is timed before the physiotherapy session rather than waited for. The diabetic has had intra-articular steroid and intravenous dexamethasone on the same day and will see the glucose rise for several days; insulin-treated patients need to know what to do with it. A dense block is not a reason to delay discharge; a catheter patient goes home with an ambulatory pump if the unit runs one and stays a night if it does not. Hoarseness and a droopy eyelid after the block are expected and resolve with it.

Pain plan

The mechanism is capsule, provoked deliberately by physiotherapy from day 0, in a population with a painful, inflamed joint before the procedure and a third of it diabetic. The plan therefore has two purposes that the other shoulder cards do not combine: to make the procedure painless, and to make the physiotherapy possible. There is no PROSPECT guideline for any of the three procedures; the rotator cuff repair guideline is the nearest and its skeleton (interscalene first, intravenous dexamethasone preferred over perineural, paracetamol and NSAID, suprascapular block as the phrenic-sparing alternative) applies.

For MUA and hydrodilatation the defensible default is an interscalene block with 15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% and intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg as the sole anaesthetic, with sedation to taste, a physiotherapy session the same afternoon inside the block, regular paracetamol and an NSAID started before the block recedes, and two nights of oral opioid timed before the sessions. A superior trunk block gives the same anaesthetic quality with far less phrenic palsy. A suprascapular plus axillary combination is adequate for hydrodilatation and a gentle manipulation but is a weaker anaesthetic for a forceful MUA, where the patient needs to be insensate and flail.

For the arthroscopic release the catheter question is different from every other card in this chapter, because the argument for it is not the pain intensity, which is modest, but the physiotherapy. A release that is not moved through its range on days 0-3 re-scars, and the sessions on those days are the difference between the operation working and not. The one series that ran a continuous interscalene-type catheter with an ambulatory pump for two to three weeks after 123 releases, with physiotherapy from day 1, reported no post-operative opioid use and transient Horner’s and recurrent laryngeal palsies that settled with dose reduction; it is uncontrolled and the duration is longer than most units would accept, but the logic is sound. The author’s practice is a catheter for the release in the diabetic or post-surgical stiff shoulder and after a failed MUA, with the infusion run into day 2 or 3 and the physiotherapy timed to it, and a single shot with the day 1 session timed inside the regular analgesia for the uncomplicated primary release. In a unit that cannot run an ambulatory infusion, a single shot with a written plan and a daily rescue dose before physiotherapy is the honest fallback.

Rebound is less of a problem here than on the bone cards, because there is no bone, but the pre-existing joint pain returns when the block recedes and in a female population in its fifties two of the four risk factors in the ambulatory cohort are present. Escalation: pain above 7 that stops the physiotherapy is a failure of the plan and needs a rescue dose and a re-timed session, not a cancelled one; new severe pain in the arm rather than the shoulder after an MUA is a fracture until the radiograph says otherwise; a block that has not worn off by the next morning after a forceful manipulation is a plexus traction injury until proven otherwise and is documented and the surgeon told.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Primary versus secondary stiffness. A primary frozen shoulder is capsule only and a manipulation frees it; a post-surgical or post-traumatic stiff shoulder has extracapsular adhesions and retained metal, needs a release, bleeds more and re-scars faster. The catheter is for the second group.
  • The diabetic. A third of the surgical population: stiffer capsule, more bleeding, faster re-scarring, a worse outcome in every series, and a glucose that the intra-articular steroid plus intravenous dexamethasone will push up for days. Warn the patient and write it down.
  • MUA fracture. Roughly one bony injury per 70-110 manipulations in the series that count, mostly glenoid avulsions, with humeral fractures reported as cases; thin bone, a long lever, a forceful surgeon and muscle tone resisting the lever are the contributors. The blocked patient gives no warning. A short lever, full relaxation and a surgeon who stops at the first unexpected sound are the defences.
  • Awake MUA under block. Some surgeons prefer the patient to see the arm move; some patients cannot bear the sound of the capsule tearing. Sedation to the patient’s taste, and a conversation beforehand about what they will hear.
  • Hydrodilatation in radiology. Often done without an anaesthetist, under local anaesthetic, and tolerated poorly by some; where an anaesthetist is involved a suprascapular or interscalene block converts a miserable procedure into a quiet one and lets the physiotherapy start at once.
  • Release with decompression. Adds a raw acromial surface and a bone mechanism, a point on the day 1 figure and the rebound odds of a bone operation.
  • Phrenic palsy. Near-universal after interscalene block; the superior trunk block gives the same anaesthetic for a manipulation with complete paresis in 5% rather than 73%, and this is the one card where the block is often the whole anaesthetic in an awake patient who will notice the dyspnoea.
  • Horner’s syndrome and hoarseness. Common after a 20 mL interscalene block, frightening to an awake patient who has not been warned, and gone by the evening.
  • Bilateral disease. Common, especially in diabetics; one side at a time, because a bilateral interscalene block is a bilateral phrenic palsy.
  • Thyroid disease and Dupuytren’s. Associations rather than anaesthetic problems, but a patient with a frozen shoulder and an untreated thyroid is worth a thyroid function test.

Key sources

The surgical evidence is unusually good: UK FROST randomised 503 patients between all three treatments and found no clinically important difference, with MUA the most cost-effective and the release the riskiest and most expensive, and the hydrodilatation meta-analyses find a small effect of uncertain clinical importance. The anaesthetic evidence is thin: the fracture and nerve injury figures come from case series of awake manipulation under block, and the only study of a continuous block after capsular release is a single-centre uncontrolled series with a two-to-three-week infusion. The pain score, the trajectory, the physiotherapy-on-day-0 logic for the catheter and the preference for the block as the sole anaesthetic for MUA are the author’s judgement from the mechanism, not measured outcomes.
