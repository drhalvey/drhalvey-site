23 Hip resurfacing

Metal-on-metal surface replacement in the young, active, large-headed man; cemented femoral cap, press-fit cup, posterior approach

2Mild to moderate

Dominant mechanism: Bone. Acetabular reaming as for a total hip, plus cylindrical reaming of the femoral head to take a cemented cap; a wider posterior exposure with a complete circumferential capsulotomy to deliver a head that is never cut off, and a full short rotator release; the femoral canal is never opened, which removes the broaching and most of the embolic load

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: low.

What the surgeon is doing and why

Hip resurfacing is a total hip replacement that refuses to cut the femoral neck. The arthritic head is reamed down to a cylinder and capped with a cobalt-chromium shell cemented onto it; the acetabulum is reamed and fitted with a thin press-fit cobalt-chromium cup with no polyethylene liner; and the two metal surfaces articulate at a diameter close to the native head, around 46-54 mm. The surgeon’s argument is bone preservation and stability: the femoral canal is untouched, so a later revision to a conventional stem is a primary operation on the femoral side; the head is large, so the hip almost never dislocates and the patient can run, squat and play sport; and the metal bearing wears less by volume than polyethylene did. The argument against it is the bearing and the neck. Metal-on-metal articulation releases cobalt and chromium ions, and in a minority of patients (women, small components, and some designs far more than others) that produces soft-tissue reactions, pseudotumours and a revision that is harder than the one the operation was meant to avoid. And the femoral neck, left in place and loaded under a cap, is the implant’s weak point: it fractures in about 1.5% of the first-generation Australian series at a mean of 15 weeks, twice as often in women, and the cause in most cases was a notch in the superior neck or a cap seated in varus.

The field has contracted accordingly. The regulators withdrew the worst-performing designs and placed every woman with a resurfacing and every man with a component of 48 mm or less under annual blood ion and imaging surveillance. The operation is now offered by a small number of high-volume surgeons to a narrow phenotype: a man under about 60, with a large femoral head, good bone density, osteoarthritis rather than dysplasia or avascular necrosis, and the wish to return to impact sport. In that patient the randomised trials show function equal to or marginally better than a total hip, a similar revision rate at 6-9 years, higher activity scores, and metal ion levels that are not different from a metal-on-metal total hip. In anyone outside that phenotype the surgeon who still offers the operation is in a minority.

The surgical sequence is the posterior total hip with three changes that matter to you:

  1. The exposure is larger. The head is never cut off, so to prepare it the whole head and neck have to be delivered out of the wound and held there on a jig. That needs a complete circumferential capsulotomy, a full short rotator release (and often quadratus femoris), and a leg brought through a wider arc with more force. Most surgeons use the posterior approach for this reason; the few who resurface through an anterior approach accept a lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury rate of about 90%.
  2. The acetabulum is done first and then the femur is prepared with the cup already in, because the femoral cap size is dictated by the cup. The head is reamed cylindrically around a guide wire placed in slight valgus, the top of the head is cut flat, cysts are curetted and grafted, and the cap is cemented on with a thin mantle and impacted. Every one of these steps is where the neck gets notched, and the surgeon will not be hurried through them.
  3. The femoral canal is never opened. There is no broaching, no pressurised cement column and no stem; the cement goes onto a closed head. That removes most of the embolic load and most of the bone cement implantation syndrome risk, which, together with a young fit population, is why the pooled randomised trials found less blood loss after resurfacing than after total hip arthroplasty despite a longer operation.

The pain logic is the posterior total hip’s with a larger soft-tissue bill. The mechanisms are bone (two reamed surfaces, one of them the femoral head itself) and capsule (a complete capsulotomy, repaired or not). Nothing is left under tension, the patient is young and recovers fast, and the pre-operative pain generator is gone. A 2 on the Part B scale, at the top of the band, with a spinal and infiltration in place. The patient’s youth cuts both ways: faster recovery and higher expectations, and in the ambulatory block literature younger age is one of the predictors of rebound pain, so the oral regimen needs to be in before the spinal recedes just as it does in a 75-year-old.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 Spinal or general anaesthesia in a young, fit, anxious patient

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon needs 90-150 minutes of a motionless, relaxed patient in lateral decubitus, and a hip that can be brought through a wide arc without the abductors fighting.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The neuraxial-versus-general evidence that supports a spinal for hip arthroplasty is drawn from an older population and its mortality signal does not transfer to a 50-year-old marathon runner; what transfers is the blood loss, the quality of the surgical field, and the smoothness of the day-of-surgery stand. A spinal with sedation is the defensible default, with the caveat that this is the arthroplasty population most likely to decline one, and a general anaesthetic with a PENG or supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block and infiltration is a reasonable second. The case is long for a spinal: size the dose for two hours and have a plan for a block that is thinning at the femoral step, which is the last and most demanding one.

  2. 2 Lateral decubitus and the wider arc

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The posterior approach is the usual one because the head has to be delivered whole. The pelvis is clamped as for a total hip, and the leg will be flexed, adducted and internally rotated harder and for longer than in a total hip, because the surgeon needs the entire head and neck out of the wound and held on a jig.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Everything on the posterior total hip card applies: dependent arm, axilla, eye, ear, peroneal nerve, pelvic clamps and the abdominal compression they bring. The additional point is the force and duration of the leg position. The sciatic nerve is stretched more during the delivery of a whole head than during the delivery of a cut neck, and the contralateral leg takes the counter-pressure; check both at the end. A young muscular patient with a stiff hip needs more relaxation here than an older one, and under a spinal that is a dense block and adequate sedation rather than anything you can add later.

  3. 3 Antibiotic, tranexamic acid and the pressure baseline

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Two large metal implants and a long operation.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Cefazolin 2 g IV before the knife (3 g over 120 kg), tranexamic acid 1 g IV before incision, and a recorded baseline pressure for the cement step later. The blood loss is a total hip’s or less, so a group and screen rather than a crossmatch is the usual preparation in this population, and cell salvage is not routine.

  4. 4 Extended posterior exposure and circumferential capsulotomy

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Gluteus maximus split, the short rotators taken down as a flap (often including quadratus femoris, which a total hip surgeon would spare), and then a capsulotomy carried all the way round the neck, including the anterior and inferior capsule that a total hip leaves alone, so that the head can be dislocated and rotated freely on the jig. The medial femoral circumflex artery, which supplies the head that is about to be capped and must stay alive, runs along the posterior neck and is protected by keeping the capsulotomy off the neck itself.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is a bigger soft-tissue dissection than the total hip and the blood loss from it is correspondingly more, from the circumflex vessels and the capsule, though it is still venous and controllable. The capsule mechanism of the Part B table is at its largest here: a complete capsulotomy, sometimes repaired at the end and sometimes not, is what the patient feels on movement for the first week. The surgeon’s concern for the head’s blood supply is the reason the dissection is slow and deliberate at this step and not a reason to intervene.

  5. 5 Dislocation and delivery of the whole head

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    With the capsule released the head is dislocated posteriorly and the leg positioned so that the entire head and neck can be brought out of the wound and held. The surgeon measures the head and chooses the cap and cup sizes.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The forcible dislocation of a whole head out of a wide capsulotomy is the moment of maximal leg force and sciatic stretch in the case, and the pressure drops with it. In the young patient it recovers quickly. The femoral vein is kinked from here until reduction, which is the mechanical half of the thromboembolic signal in the one randomised trial that found more events after resurfacing than after total hip.

  6. 6 Acetabular reaming and cup impaction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The acetabulum is reamed to a bleeding hemisphere and a thin monobloc cobalt-chromium cup is press-fit; there is no liner to insert and no screws can be used, so the fit and the orientation have to be exact. Cup inclination matters more in a metal-on-metal bearing than in any other, because a steep cup produces edge loading, high wear and the metal ion problem, so the surgeon spends longer on this than a total hip surgeon would.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The largest single bleed of the case, as for a total hip, and the first bone pain mechanism. The impaction of a monobloc cup is hard mallet work transmitted through the pelvis. Reaming hazards are the total hip’s: a medial wall breach is rare and announced.

  7. 7 Femoral head preparation on the jig

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A guide wire is placed down the centre of the neck in slight valgus, checked with a jig against the neck’s axis, and the head is reamed cylindrically to the size of the cap, with the surgeon watching the superior neck for a notch. The top of the head is cut flat, a central hole is drilled for the stem of the cap, cysts are curetted and the bed is washed. This is the longest step of the case and the one on which the implant’s survival depends: a notch or a varus wire is the mechanism of the neck fracture that presents at 15 weeks.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Nothing to do and much to not do. This is the step the surgeon will not be hurried through, and a spinal that is thinning or a sedated patient who is beginning to move is the thing most likely to spoil it. If the case is running long, say so now rather than at the cement. The femoral head is the second bone pain mechanism, and the reaming is of cancellous head rather than cortical neck, so it is not a worse pain generator than the acetabulum.

  8. 8 Cementing the femoral cap

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Low-viscosity cement is poured into the cap, or applied to the head, and the cap is pushed onto the prepared head and held or gently impacted until it sets. The mantle is thin and the pressure low; the surgeon is trying to get cement into the cancellous bone of the head without filling the central hole so full that the stem of the cap hydraulically pressurises the neck.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    This is a cement step without a canal. The cement goes onto a closed head rather than down a pressurised femur, so the embolic load and the bone cement implantation syndrome risk are a fraction of a cemented stem’s; the Donaldson grades still apply and the pressure is still watched, and the patient is young and has reserve. A proportionate version of the hip fracture cement precautions is the author’s practice: the surgeon says when the cement is going on, the pressure is at baseline, and a vasopressor is to hand. There is no case for an arterial line or a vasopressor infusion on cement grounds alone in this operation. Note the time; the cap has to set before reduction, and nothing happens for several minutes.

  9. 9 Reduction, stability and the repair

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Once the cement has set the hip is reduced, stability tested (a large head rarely dislocates, and the surgeon is checking for impingement of the neck against the cup rim rather than for instability), and the capsule and rotators repaired as the surgeon’s habit dictates. Heterotopic ossification is more frequent after resurfacing than after total hip in the pooled comparisons, which is a reason surgeons wash the field thoroughly and ask for an NSAID to be continued.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The reduction is the hard pull against the clamps that any hip reduction is, and the second embolic moment is smaller here than after a stem. Give the antiemetic. The leg is the same length it was, because nothing was cut off, so the sciatic lengthening injury of a total hip does not arise, though the sciatic stretch from the wider arc does.

  10. 10 Recovery, the first week and the fracture window

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the patient standing the same day and walking without precautions once the capsule has healed, with the caveat that the neck under the cap is remodelling for months and impact activity waits for a season. The dangerous period is weeks 6-20: new groin pain or a limp in that window is a femoral neck stress fracture, and its only treatment is a revision to a conventional stem.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The posterior total hip’s recovery and the same oral regimen. Younger age is a predictor of rebound pain in the ambulatory block data, so the first paracetamol and NSAID doses go in before the spinal recedes, as for anyone else. Chemoprophylaxis for venous thromboembolism is not skipped on the grounds that the patient is young: the one UK randomised trial found more thromboembolic events after resurfacing than after total hip. The patient is told, in words, what a femoral neck fracture feels like and when it would happen, because it presents to a general practitioner or an emergency department as a hip that was fine and then was not.

Pain plan

The mechanism is the posterior total hip’s two bone surfaces plus a larger capsulotomy, in a young fit patient whose pre-operative pain is gone, so the plan is the posterior card’s: PROSPECT’s basic regimen of paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started before or during surgery, a single intravenous dexamethasone dose up to 10 mg, and opioids for rescue; a spinal where the patient accepts one; surgeon-delivered infiltration with a dilute long-acting local anaesthetic; and a single-shot PENG or supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block where infiltration is not offered or the patient is having a general anaesthetic. There is no procedure-specific analgesic evidence for resurfacing and the extrapolation from total hip arthroplasty is the honest basis for the plan.

The larger capsulotomy earns a note rather than a change. Movement pain in the first week is a little worse than after a total hip, the NSAID is continued for longer (the surgeon will want it for heterotopic ossification in any case), and the patient’s expectations are managed in advance: a man who chose resurfacing to run again will be the one who reports the first week as worse than he was told. No catheter, because nothing in the mechanism asks for one and the stand on the day of surgery is what the operation is for.

Rebound is low: the spinal recedes over an afternoon with the infiltration behind it. Escalation triggers are the posterior card’s, with one addition that belongs to the weeks rather than the day: new groin pain or a limp between weeks 6 and 20 is a femoral neck fracture until a radiograph says otherwise.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Patient selection is the whole risk profile. Women, small heads (under 48 mm), poor bone density, dysplasia, avascular necrosis with a large necrotic segment and inflammatory arthritis are the phenotypes where the neck fractures, the cap loosens and the ions rise; a surgeon operating outside the narrow phenotype is operating outside the evidence, and the anaesthetist’s only lever is to know it.
  • Metal ions and the revision that follows. Cobalt and chromium are released from every metal-on-metal bearing; in a minority they cause adverse reactions to metal debris, pseudotumours and tissue necrosis. The patient is under annual surveillance for life. A revision for a pseudotumour is a soft-tissue reconstruction as much as an implant exchange and belongs on the revision card; a patient presenting for unrelated surgery with a known high ion level and cardiomyopathy or hypothyroidism is the systemic cobalt toxicity case report, rare and real.
  • The anterior approach. Supine, with a lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury rate of about 90% in the series that measured it and a femoral exposure that is harder than in a total hip; the anaesthetic implications are those of the anterior approach card.
  • Conversion to a total hip on the table. If the head is found to be cystic or the neck is notched, the surgeon converts to a conventional stem; the case lengthens, the femoral canal is opened and broached, and a cemented stem with its full BCIS step may appear in a case that was planned without one. Have the posterior card’s cement precautions ready for every resurfacing.
  • Revision of a failed resurfacing. Femoral-side revision for neck fracture is a conventional stem into an untouched canal, which is the operation’s selling point; acetabular-side revision for a pseudotumour or a loose cup is a large soft-tissue and bone problem with major blood loss, and it is a revision in every sense.
  • Heterotopic ossification. More frequent than after total hip; the surgeon’s NSAID request is for this as much as for pain, and it fits the plan.
  • The young patient’s expectations. Faster recovery, higher activity, and a lower tolerance for the first week; the counselling before the spinal is as important as the block choice.

Key sources

The comparative evidence is better than for most operations in this book: 18 randomised trials pooled in 2023, two long-term randomised trials, a 46-study meta-analysis of revision and complications, and a national review of neck fractures. The finding that surprises most anaesthetists, lower blood loss than a total hip despite a longer operation, is a pooled randomised result and reflects the unopened femoral canal; the mechanism is sound and the effect size is modest. There is no procedure-specific analgesic guideline and the pain plan is extrapolated from PROSPECT’s total hip recommendations, which is the honest basis and is labelled as such. The surveillance and selection advice is regulatory rather than trial-based.
