55 Posterior cervical decompression and fusion

Cervical laminectomy, laminoplasty, posterior foraminotomy, and posterior instrumented fusion with lateral mass or pedicle screws, including occipitocervical and atlantoaxial fixation

4Severe

Dominant mechanism: Muscle. The posterior neck muscles stripped off the spinous processes and laminae on both sides over three to six levels and held under retractors for hours, with laminae removed or hinged, lateral masses drilled and screwed, and facets decorticated; the nuchal muscles then have to hold the head up on day 1

Rebound risk when a single-shot block wears off: moderate.

Block
No regional block; intra-operative low-dose ketamine infusion, regular paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor where the surgeon permits it, surgeon infiltration of the wound edges, and oral opioid on days 0-3
Catheter: rarely A surgeon-placed epidural or wound catheter is PROSPECT's lumbar recommendation and has no established place in the cervical spine; the author's default is ketamine and the oral regimen, with a wound catheter only for the opioid-tolerant multilevel patient and only in units that have used one here before
Alternatives: Bilateral cervical erector spinae plane or interfascial plane blocks by the surgeon under direct vision before closure, an adjunct with case series behind it rather than trials; Intravenous lidocaine infusion for the opioid-tolerant where ketamine is contraindicated; Intrathecal morphine is not used here: the surgeon is not in the dura and a lumbar injection for a cervical wound is not the author's practice
Position
Prone with the head fixed in a three-pin skull clamp attached to the table, neck in neutral or slight flexion to open the interlaminar spaces, reverse Trendelenburg to lower venous pressure in the neck, arms tucked at the sides and the shoulders taped down for the lateral image; the face is free of all pressure and the eyes can be inspected
Tourniquet
No
Cement
No
Blood loss
200-500 mL for a laminoplasty or a two- or three-level fusion; 500-1000 mL for a long construct, an occipitocervical fusion or a revision, from the muscle and the epidural venous plexus
Duration
120-180 min for a laminoplasty or a short fusion; 180-300 min for a long construct or occipitocervical fixation; a single-level foraminotomy 60-90 min
Length of stay
2-4 nights; a posterior foraminotomy one night or a selected day case. Day case: no
Antibiotic
Cefazolin 2 g IV within 60 min of incision (3 g over 120 kg), repeated at 4 h; a long case with implants in a wound that sits in a sweaty crease and has the highest infection rate of the elective spine
Tranexamic acid
Tranexamic acid 1 g IV before incision, repeated at 3 h or infused, for any fusion; not needed for a foraminotomy
VTE
Mechanical from induction; chemoprophylaxis from day 1 or 2 once the surgeon is content the epidural space is dry, because a haematoma under a cervical laminectomy is quadriplegia
Antiemetic
Dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction and ondansetron 4 mg IV before emergence; vomiting after a cervical fusion loads the construct and the wound
Tell the patient
The back of the neck will be very sore and stiff for the first few days and the muscles will feel too weak to hold your head up; that improves day by day, and the hands and walking improve slowly over months if they improve at all.
The trap
The anaesthetic for this operation is decided by the cord, not the airway: in a myelopathic or unstable neck the intubation, the turn and the pinning are three separate opportunities to extend or move a spine that has no room, and each needs the neck held where the surgeon wants it and a check that the patient can still move their feet before the drapes go on

What the surgeon is doing and why

The posterior approach to the cervical spine is chosen when the compression is behind the cord or at many levels, when the canal is congenitally narrow, when the ligament behind the vertebral bodies has ossified over several segments (OPLL), or when the spine is unstable and needs screws. A laminectomy takes the laminae and the ligamentum flavum off the back of the canal so the cord can drift backwards away from whatever is in front of it; a laminoplasty hinges the laminae open on one side like a door and props them with small plates, which enlarges the canal while keeping a roof on it and some of the muscle attachments. A posterior foraminotomy drills a keyhole through the facet to free one root and is the small operation on this card. An instrumented fusion puts screws into the lateral masses (C3-C6), the pedicles (C7 and the upper thoracic spine) or C2, connects them with rods, decorticates the facets and lays graft, and is added to a laminectomy whenever enough bone has been removed to make the spine unstable, or when it was unstable already. At the top, atlantoaxial and occipitocervical fusions fix C1 to C2 or the skull to the upper cervical spine for rheumatoid instability, odontoid fractures and tumours.

The sequence of a laminectomy and fusion is the card’s spine. A midline incision from the occiput to the upper thoracic spine, bilateral subperiosteal stripping of the posterior neck muscles off the spinous processes, laminae and lateral masses, and self-retaining retractors that stay in for hours. Lateral mass screws placed by landmarks and fluoroscopy, each angled up and out away from the vertebral artery in front and the exiting root below. Laminectomy with a high-speed burr and punches, with the dura directly beneath. Decortication of the facets and the laminar remnants, rods contoured and locked, graft laid down, a drain and a layered closure. The laminoplasty replaces the screws and the laminectomy with a hinge and plates and leaves one side of the muscle more intact. The surgeon’s first and last concern is the cord: a myelopathic cord has no room and no reserve, and every step from the intubation onwards can injure it.

The pain logic. The dominant mechanism is muscle, and in the neck it is worse than in the lumbar spine for a reason the lumbar cards do not have: the stripped muscles are the ones that have to hold the head up when the patient sits on day 1. Bilateral stripping of the semispinalis, splenius and multifidus over four to six levels, hours under a retractor, and then the load of a head on a neck that cannot brace; the ache is deep, position-dependent, worse sitting than lying and relieved by a collar that many surgeons do not want worn. Under it is bone (lateral masses drilled, facets decorticated, laminae burred), nerve (a decompressed cord and roots, and the C5 root that may be tethered as the cord shifts back), and retension where a kyphotic neck has been pulled into lordosis across the construct. The nuchal muscle pain is the reason this card’s trajectory stays above the ACDF’s at every point and why it is a 4 while the anterior operation on the same disease is a 3. In the 179-procedure cohort the spinal fusions occupy three of the top six places for worst pain on day 1; the cervical fusion is not separated out, but its mechanism puts it among them. Compare the ACDF, where no muscle is touched and the complaint is the swallow.

Steps: what happens and what it means for you

  1. 1 The cord, the airway and the plan for three dangerous moments

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon has a myelopathic, stenotic or unstable neck on the imaging and wants it to arrive on the table without having been extended, flexed or rotated beyond what the canal will tolerate; the baseline neurology is written down because any change afterwards will be attributed to the operation.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Examine and record the neurology yourself: hand function, gait, reflexes, the dermatomal level, and whether the deltoid (C5) is already weak. Then decide the airway from the imaging rather than from the Mallampati score. A stable neck with mild myelopathy is intubated asleep with a videolaryngoscope and manual in-line stabilisation, which limits extension, and the randomised evidence in collared patients shows videolaryngoscopy beats flexible bronchoscopy on first-attempt success and time with the same complication rate. An unstable neck (atlantoaxial subluxation, a fracture, a rheumatoid pannus) or a cord with no room on the sagittal image is intubated awake with a flexible scope or a videolaryngoscope under topical anaesthesia and sedation, so the neurology can be checked after the tube is in and again after the turn. The evidence that awake intubation protects the cord is physiological and observational, and the decision is a judgement made with the surgeon, who may want the neck in a collar until the pins are on. Ask what traction or reduction the surgeon intends, because a halo or tongs change the airway plan entirely. Rheumatoid patients bring a stiff jaw, a cricoarytenoid joint and an atlantoaxial subluxation to the same airway.

  2. 2 Induction, lines, neuromonitoring and the baseline before the turn

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The monitoring team wants baseline somatosensory and motor evoked potentials with the patient supine and the neck in a neutral position, so that a change after the turn or the pinning can be attributed to position and reversed.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    A reinforced tube, secured on the side and padded where the face will rest, with a bite block if motor evoked potentials are planned. An arterial line for every fusion, because the cord’s perfusion pressure is the one variable you control and the mean pressure is kept at the patient’s baseline from now until the drapes come off; two cannulae; a urinary catheter. Propofol and remifentanil, no neuromuscular block after the intubating dose, and the baseline signals recorded before anyone moves the patient. In an awake intubation the neurology is checked with the tube in and the patient still awake; induction follows only when the feet still move.

  3. 3 The skull clamp and the turn

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The head is fixed in a three-pin clamp so the surgeon can set the neck’s position exactly and hold it motionless for hours, with the face free; the clamp is attached to the table after the patient is turned.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Pinning is a brief, intense stimulus that produces hypertension and tachycardia, which is bad for a cord with no reserve and a canal full of veins; a bolus of opioid or a deepening before the pins go in, and local anaesthetic at the pin sites, blunt it. The turn is a log roll onto the chest with the head held by the surgeon or the most experienced person in the room, the neck kept in the agreed position, and the tube held by you. Once prone, the surgeon sets flexion or neutral and the clamp is locked; then the checks: eyes free and visible, nose and chin clear, no pressure on the face at all, neck neutral or as agreed, shoulders below 90 degrees if the arms are up (they are usually tucked for a cervical case), elbows padded, abdomen free, breasts and genitals clear, knees padded. Reverse Trendelenburg lowers venous pressure in the neck and is the surgeon’s friend; the head above the heart raises the question of air embolism if a vein is opened, which is a real if rare event in the sitting position and a theoretical one here. The neuromonitoring team repeats the signals once the position is set: a loss after the turn is the position, and the position is changed before anything else.

  4. 4 Exposure: bilateral stripping of the nuchal muscles

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The posterior neck muscles are taken subperiosteally off the spinous processes, laminae and lateral masses from the midline outwards on both sides, over the levels to be decompressed and instrumented, and retractors hold them apart.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Antibiotic and tranexamic acid finished before the knife. This is the pain mechanism and the main bleeding; the muscle bleeds steadily and the surgeon controls it with cautery. The stimulus is sustained, and in an unrelaxed patient the response is a lighter plane rather than movement, which the monitoring team sees as a change in signals before you see it on the pressure. The ketamine infusion is running from now. The C2 spinous process carries the attachment of the semispinalis cervicis, which surgeons try to preserve or reattach because its loss is the axial neck pain that persists for months after a laminectomy that included C2.

  5. 5 Lateral mass or pedicle screw placement

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    Screws go into the lateral masses from C3 to C6, into the pedicles at C7 and below, and into the pars or pedicle of C2 or the lateral masses of C1, each trajectory chosen to avoid the vertebral artery in front and the exiting root below and the facet above. Fluoroscopy or navigation confirms them.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The screw that goes wrong goes into the vertebral artery or the root, and the first is a sudden deep arterial bleed that the surgeon controls with the screw itself and packing, followed by an angiogram; the second is a radiculopathy in recovery. A C1-2 construct puts the vertebral artery at risk on both sides and the screws are placed with the artery’s course mapped on the pre-operative CT angiogram. Neuromonitoring is watched during the drilling, and a drop in signals in one arm is a root.

  6. 6 Laminectomy or laminoplasty: the decompression

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The laminae are thinned with a burr and lifted off the dura, or the laminae are cut through on one side and hinged on the other and propped open with plates; the ligamentum flavum is removed; the cord, which has been flattened for months, is seen to pulsate and drift backwards.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    The burr is on the dura; this is the moment for a motionless patient and a steady mean pressure, with no rise to drive bleeding and no fall to starve a cord that has just been released. The epidural veins bleed as the flavum comes off and are controlled with haemostatic agents and venous pressure, not arterial. A durotomy here is in the cervical dura with the cord beneath; the repair is fiddly and the Valsalva is requested; a cerebrospinal fluid leak into the wound in the neck is a pseudomeningocele or a fistula. The backward drift of the cord is the mechanism of the C5 palsy: the C5 root, the shortest and most horizontal, is tethered as the cord moves and is stretched, which appears as a deltoid and biceps weakness between two days and six weeks after surgery, in about 5% after laminoplasty, 7% after decompression and fusion and 8% after laminectomy in the pooled data, with permanent weakness in about 1%. Nothing anaesthetic prevents it, and documenting the pre-operative deltoid is the one contribution.

  7. 7 Decortication, rod placement, graft and the correction

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The facets and laminar remnants are decorticated to bleeding bone, rods are contoured into lordosis and locked to the screws, and in a kyphotic neck the construct is used to pull the spine into alignment; graft is laid along the construct.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Decortication is the last bone bleeding. A correction across the construct is a change in the shape of a canal with a cord in it; the monitoring team checks the signals after the rods are locked, and a loss here is a call for a higher mean pressure, a haemoglobin, warmth and a surgeon who loosens the rod. An occipitocervical construct fixes the skull to the neck in a position that the patient will live in: a head fixed in flexion cannot see the horizon, and a head fixed in extension cannot swallow, and the surgeon will spend time on the lateral image at this point for exactly that reason.

  8. 8 Closure, drain, the release from the pins and the turn back

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    A drain, a layered closure of a thick muscular wound, and the skull clamp released after the dressings are on.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Give the second antiemetic. The head comes out of the pins with the neck supported, and the turn back is a log roll with the head held; a collar goes on if the surgeon wants one before the patient wakes. Inspect the face and the eyes, and the pin sites, which bleed. Extubation is the fourth dangerous moment: a patient who has been prone for four hours with the head below the heart during any part of it, or who has had a long upper cervical or occipitocervical case, has a swollen tongue, pharynx and larynx, and a tube that comes out of an oedematous airway cannot always go back in. The honest test is a cuff leak and a look at the tongue, and the honest decision for a long upper cervical case is often a night in intensive care with the tube in. Extubate a patient who is awake and moves both feet and both hands to order; one who does not is a surgical conversation before they leave the table.

  9. 9 Recovery and the first night: the cord, the airway and the neck

    What the surgeon is doing and why

    The surgeon wants the neurology confirmed and rechecked, the drain output watched, the wound dry, and the patient sitting up and walking on day 1 with or without a collar.

    What it means for anaesthesia

    Neurology against the baseline in recovery and then at intervals overnight: a haematoma under a cervical laminectomy presents as rising neck pain and then arm and leg weakness over an hour or two, and the window for evacuation before a permanent cord injury is short. The deltoid is tested on both sides daily for the first week because the C5 palsy is a delayed presentation. The regular analgesia starts in recovery and the opioid is charted for the night and before the first sit-up, which is the moment the nuchal muscles are first loaded. A patient who cannot hold their head up on day 1 is not weak from the cord; they are sore from the muscle, and the distinction is the one the physiotherapist needs to hear.

Pain plan

The mechanism is bilateral stripping of the muscles that hold the head up, over a bone-and-nerve operation, so the plan is multimodal and systemic rather than regional, because there is no block that covers the posterior neck reliably without reaching the cord’s own territory. PROSPECT’s complex spine guideline was written for the thoracolumbar spine and its systemic recommendations transfer: paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor where the surgeon allows it after a fusion (a laminoplasty or a foraminotomy has no fusion to protect), an intra-operative low-dose ketamine infusion (grade A, and the one intervention that most changes the first two days in the author’s judgement), opioids as rescue and no gabapentinoids. Its regional recommendation, a surgeon-placed epidural catheter, has no established place in the cervical spine and the author does not use one here. Intrathecal morphine is not part of the plan because the surgeon is not in the dura and a lumbar injection for a cervical wound is an extrapolation the author is not willing to make.

The defensible default is ketamine at induction and by infusion to closure, surgeon infiltration of the wound edges and the pin sites with long-acting local anaesthetic, dexamethasone at induction, the oral regimen from recovery, and an oral opioid charted for the first three days with a dose timed before the first sit-up and before physiotherapy. Bilateral cervical erector spinae or interfascial plane blocks placed by the surgeon under direct vision before closure are an adjunct with case series behind them and are a reasonable addition in a multilevel or opioid-tolerant patient; the evidence does not yet justify calling them standard. A wound catheter is for the opioid-tolerant multilevel patient in a unit that has used one here, and is otherwise not placed.

The opioid-tolerant patient is common on this list, particularly the rheumatoid patient with years of neck pain, and the plan is the one in the opioid-tolerant chapter: the baseline opioid continued, ketamine, and a pain service involved before the second night.

Escalation: rising neck pain with any new arm or leg weakness in the first 24 h is a haematoma and the surgeon is called before any analgesic change. Shoulder abduction weakness appearing between day 2 and week 6 is a C5 palsy and is explained, not treated. Pain that is worst on sitting and relieved by lying is the nuchal muscle and the expected course, and it is treated with the oral regimen, heat and a soft collar if the surgeon permits one. Pain still 6 or more at a week with a dry wound is the rheumatoid or chronic pain patient’s baseline reasserting itself.

Pitfalls and variants

  • Laminoplasty versus laminectomy and fusion. The laminoplasty keeps a roof and one side’s muscle and has no screws and no non-union to protect, so the NSAID objection falls away and the pain is a notch lower; the fusion is for the unstable or kyphotic neck and sits at the top of this card’s range.
  • The posterior foraminotomy. A single-level muscle-splitting or tubular keyhole through the facet for one root: 60-90 min, little blood, one night or a day case in a fit patient, and a pain score of 2. The airway and positioning rules still apply because the patient is still prone in pins.
  • The myelopathic cord and the mean pressure. A compressed cord has lost its autoregulatory reserve; the arterial line is there so the mean pressure can be held at the patient’s own baseline for the whole case, and a vasopressor infusion started early is better than boluses chasing a dip during the stripping.
  • Awake versus asleep intubation. The collar trials say videolaryngoscopy is the faster, more reliable tool in an immobilised neck; the unstable or room-less canal is still intubated awake in most hands so the cord can be examined with the tube in and after the turn, and the decision is made with the surgeon and the imaging.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis. Atlantoaxial and subaxial instability, a pannus behind the dens, cricoarytenoid stiffness, a small mouth, steroid cover, a tendency to bleed, and a long occipitocervical construct; the orthopaedic patient chapter carries the detail.
  • Ankylosing spondylitis. A fixed, brittle neck that fractures through the ankylosed segment with the slightest extension, including during a laryngoscopy; the neck is positioned exactly where it was and no further, and the pins are placed with that in mind.
  • The upper cervical and occipitocervical construct. Longer, bloodier, the vertebral artery at risk on both sides, a head position the patient must live with, airway oedema from the prone time and the proximity to the pharynx, and the strongest case on the card for leaving the tube in overnight.
  • Postoperative visual loss. A long prone case with blood loss and the head in pins carries the described risk profile, and the pins at least keep the face off the pillow; the head above the heart, a normal haemoglobin and no sustained hypotension are the modifiable parts (see spine essentials).
  • Infection. The posterior neck wound has the highest infection rate of the elective spine; the antibiotic is redosed at 4 h and many surgeons add vancomycin powder.
  • The patient who cannot lift their head on day 1. Muscle, not cord, in a patient whose neurology is otherwise unchanged; the physiotherapist needs to hear it from you.

Key sources

No procedure-specific pain guideline exists for the posterior cervical spine, and the card’s plan is PROSPECT’s lumbar backbone with the regional recommendations stripped out and the reasons given. The C5 palsy figures come from a 155-study meta-analysis and a single-centre laminoplasty series that discusses the mechanism; the airway evidence is a randomised comparison in collared volunteers and patients that says nothing about myelopathy, and the awake intubation advice is physiology and judgement, which the card says. The nuchal muscle mechanism and the day 1 head-holding problem are clinical observation rather than measured outcomes.
