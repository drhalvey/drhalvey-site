Fat embolism

Fat embolism is marrow fat in the venous blood and it happens in almost every long bone fracture and in almost every reamed nail; fat embolism syndrome is the small fraction of patients in whom that fat produces lung injury, brain injury and a petechial rash 12-72 h later. Two mechanisms run in series: a mechanical obstruction of the pulmonary bed by fat and marrow at the moment of fracture or reaming, then a chemical injury as lipase turns the trapped neutral fat into free fatty acids that strip the capillary endothelium. The diagnosis is clinical, the Gurd, Schonfeld and Lindeque criteria are three unvalidated ways of saying so, and the treatment is supportive. The one intervention with consistent evidence is early fixation of the femur, within 24 h where the patient's physiology allows it, with external fixation as the damage-control alternative when it does not. The collapse during nailing is a different animal from the syndrome on the ward: it is acute right heart failure from an embolic shower, and the differential at that moment is blood loss, tension pneumothorax, anaphylaxis and a paradoxical embolus through a foramen ovale.

Fat embolism and fat embolism syndrome are two different things

Fat embolism is a laboratory and echocardiographic fact: marrow fat in the venous blood, in the right heart and in the pulmonary vascular bed. It happens in almost every long bone fracture and in almost every operation that instruments a medullary canal. Transoesophageal echocardiography during 111 intramedullary procedures showed embolic material crossing the right heart in 97 of them; right atrial blood sampled during knee replacement contained fat in 46% of unilateral and 65% of bilateral cases; and post-mortem lung sections from trauma patients show fat in the pulmonary capillaries far more often than anyone was diagnosed with anything. Most of it is clinically silent.

Fat embolism syndrome is what happens when that fat injures the lung, the brain and the skin. It is a clinical diagnosis, usually declared 12-72 h after the fracture or the operation, in a patient who was fine when they left recovery. The incidence depends entirely on how hard you look and what you call it: 1-10% of long bone fractures in the older literature, under 1% in retrospective series, 0.03% in a coded trauma registry of 1.25 million isolated femoral and tibial fractures (which measures coding rather than disease), and 4.3% in a recent prospective single-centre series of 3,475 diaphyseal fractures that looked for it. The systematic review of femoral fractures shows the incidence falling across the decades, from 7.9% before 1980 to 4.8% in the next twenty years and 1.7% since 2000, which tracks the move from traction and delayed surgery to early nailing more closely than it tracks any drug.

Keeping the two apart matters in both directions. The fat embolism you see on the monitor during reaming is not the syndrome and does not predict it reliably; the syndrome you are asked about on the ward two days later is not an acute embolic event and will not respond to being treated as one.

Mechanism: an embolus, then a chemical burn

Two theories were argued over for fifty years and the honest reading is that they describe two phases of the same process.

The mechanical phase is quick. A fracture, or a reamer, breaches the venous sinusoids of the marrow while the intramedullary pressure is raised by haematoma, by the fracture ends being moved, by a reamer head advancing down a closed tube or by a nail being driven into one. Marrow fat, marrow cells and bone debris are forced into the metaphyseal veins, travel to the right heart and lodge in the pulmonary arterioles and capillaries. The load is proportional to the pressure and the volume of marrow disturbed: a femur produces more than a tibia, a pathological femur full of tumour more than either, and bilateral femoral fractures more than one. The immediate consequence is the same right-heart physiology described for cement in the cement chapter: a rise in pulmonary vascular resistance, a fall in end-tidal CO2 and in saturation, and in a large shower a dilated right ventricle and a falling systemic pressure. In the echocardiographic series the pulmonary response was proportional to the embolic load, and 24 of the 25 severe responses occurred during reaming of pathological lesions or cemented hemiarthroplasty, which is where the marrow is loosest and the pressure highest. Marrow is also thrombogenic: right atrial blood sampled during reaming showed activation of the clotting cascade, and in 12 of the 111 patients large coagulative masses were seen trapped in the heart.

Fat that reaches the pulmonary capillaries does not all stay there. Droplets small enough pass through the pulmonary bed, and a patent foramen ovale, present in about a quarter of adults and almost never known about, lets the larger ones cross directly. In the Edinburgh series paradoxical embolisation was seen in four patients, all with pathological femoral lesions, and two died; a separate case report with echocardiography documented a fulminant fatal syndrome from paradoxical embolism through a foramen ovale during nailing. Systemic fat was found at post-mortem in one patient without a foraminal defect, so transpulmonary passage is real as well. This is the route to the brain, the kidney, the retina and the skin.

The biochemical phase is slow, and it explains the latency. Neutral marrow fat is relatively inert. Over the following 24-72 h, tissue and plasma lipase hydrolyse it to free fatty acids, which are directly toxic to pneumocytes and capillary endothelium; the capillaries leak, the alveoli fill with protein-rich fluid and haemorrhage, platelets and fibrin deposit around the droplets, and the picture is one of acute lung injury rather than obstruction. Complement activation was found in every patient with the syndrome in the Schonfeld trial, but also in many without it, which is the usual finding for an inflammatory marker in trauma; the steroid trials that showed an effect were built on this phase, and the one that measured free fatty acids found that methylprednisolone stabilised them. The two phases together give the shape of the illness: a mechanical shower that is usually silent, then a chemical injury that declares itself a day or two later in the organs the fat reached.

Who gets it

The risk factors sort into the dose of marrow released and the time it is left to be released.

  • A femoral fracture, which carried an adjusted odds ratio of 4.63 in the Bogota series; tibial and humeral fractures carry a smaller risk, pelvic fractures some, and closed fractures more than open ones in some series because the haematoma stays under pressure.
  • Multiple fractures and bilateral femoral fractures: 4.6% against 2.9% for a unilateral high-energy fracture in the systematic review, and in the German trauma registry bilateral femoral shaft fractures were an independent risk factor for pulmonary failure (34.7% against 20.6%).
  • Pathological fractures and tumour-filled canals, which produced the most severe embolic responses on echocardiography.
  • Young age. The syndrome is a disease of the young adult with a high-energy fracture: in 274 isolated femoral fractures there were no cases over the age of 35, and younger age was an independent predictor in the national registry. The usual explanation is that young marrow is more cellular and more fatty-fluid, and young patients sustain the high-energy injuries.
  • Obesity (adjusted odds ratio 2.63 in one series, independent in the registry) and diabetes.
  • Delay to fixation, which has its own section below.
  • Elective surgery that instruments the canal: cemented long-stem arthroplasty, long-stem revision stems, intramedullary alignment rods and bilateral knee replacement (marrow cells in right atrial blood in 12% of bilateral and 4% of unilateral cases, with neurological signs in six of the sixteen positive patients) all produce it in smaller amounts than a reamed fracture nail.

The clinical picture and the three sets of criteria

The classic triad is respiratory, neurological and cutaneous, in that order of frequency, appearing 12-72 h after the insult. Respiratory signs are near universal: tachypnoea, hypoxaemia, then bilateral infiltrates on the chest radiograph, from mild desaturation on the ward to full acute respiratory distress syndrome needing ventilation; in the Bogota series 94% of patients needed advanced ventilatory support. Neurological signs follow in most: restlessness, confusion and drowsiness that are easily mistaken for opioids, then focal deficits, seizures or coma; the MRI shows scattered diffusion-restricting lesions in the white matter, the so-called starfield pattern. The petechial rash is the least common and the most specific: a crop of petechiae on the conjunctivae, the oral mucosa, the neck, the axillae and the upper anterior chest, often lasting only a day or two, and in the Schonfeld trial the only finding that reliably predicted the syndrome. Fever, tachycardia, a falling haemoglobin and platelet count out of proportion to the bleeding, retinal exudates and fat in the urine round out the picture.

Three sets of criteria exist and none has been validated, because there is no gold standard to validate them against. Gurd’s criteria, from 1970 and revised with Wilson in 1974, are the most quoted:

  • Major: petechial rash; respiratory symptoms with bilateral radiographic changes; cerebral signs unrelated to head injury or other cause.
  • Minor: tachycardia above 110 per minute; fever above 38.5 degrees; retinal changes (fat or petechiae); jaundice; renal signs (anuria or oliguria); a sudden fall in haemoglobin or platelet count; a raised ESR; fat macroglobulinaemia.
  • Diagnosis: at least one major and four minor features.

Schonfeld’s score, written for a steroid trial, weights the findings: petechiae 5 points; diffuse alveolar infiltrates on the chest radiograph 4; hypoxaemia (PaO2 below 70 mmHg on room air) 3; and one point each for confusion, fever above 38 degrees, heart rate above 120 and respiratory rate above 30, with a score of 5 or more making the diagnosis. Lindeque’s criteria, from a femoral and tibial fracture trial whose authors judged Gurd’s criteria inadequate for early diagnosis, are respiratory only: a sustained PaO2 below 60 mmHg (8 kPa); a PaCO2 above 55 mmHg (7.3 kPa) or a pH below 7.3; a sustained respiratory rate above 35 despite sedation; or increased work of breathing shown by dyspnoea, accessory muscle use, tachycardia and anxiety. Any one of these in a patient with a long bone fracture satisfies the Lindeque definition.

The practical use of the three is as a checklist, not a test. Gurd’s list reminds you to look at the conjunctivae and the axillae, to send a platelet count and to think about the brain; Lindeque’s list reminds you that hypoxaemia on day two in a young man with a femoral fracture is the syndrome until proved otherwise. Investigations exclude the alternatives rather than confirm the diagnosis: a chest radiograph or CT, a CT pulmonary angiogram when thromboembolism is plausible, an MRI brain for the confused patient (CT is usually normal), bloods for the cytopenias, and an echocardiogram if the right heart is in question. Fat in the urine, sputum or lavage fluid is neither sensitive nor specific.

Reaming and nailing as the trigger

Reamed intramedullary nailing is the operation that produces the largest embolic load, and the anaesthetist’s interest is in three questions: whether reaming makes the lung injury worse, whether the timing matters, and what to do when a patient collapses on the table.

The echocardiographic evidence is clear that reaming embolises. In 24 reamed nailings of tibial and femoral fractures, 14 patients showed minimal emboli, six showed copious showers of small emboli, and four showed multiple emboli over 10 mm long; three of those four developed the syndrome and one died, and earlier nailing was associated with a smaller load. The showers come with each pass of the reamer and with nail insertion, and they are larger in a fresh fracture with a haematoma under pressure, in a pathological bone, and when the reamer is driven fast down a tight canal. The surgeon has levers: sharp reamers with deep flutes, advancing slowly, venting the canal, and the reaming systems with irrigation and suction that remove marrow as they cut and are designed to keep the intramedullary pressure down; the surgeon who is told the patient has a bad chest or a known shunt will usually use them.

Whether reaming makes the clinical outcome worse is a different question, and the answer from the randomised trials is that it does not. The meta-analyses of reamed against unreamed femoral nailing (eight trials, about 1,100 patients) show no difference in ARDS or mortality, with reaming producing faster union and fewer secondary operations; the sheep work that measured intramedullary pressure found it higher with an unreamed nail driven into an unprepared canal than with reaming, because an unreamed nail is a tight piston. Unreamed nailing to protect the lung is not supported, and the surgeon’s choice of a reamed nail should stand.

Early fixation: the one intervention that works

The femoral fracture left in traction keeps releasing marrow with every movement, and the intramedullary pressure stays raised by the haematoma. Fixation stops both. The randomised trial of 178 adults with femoral fractures, stabilised within 24 h or after 48 h, found that in the multiply injured, delay produced more pulmonary complications (adult respiratory distress syndrome, fat embolism and pneumonia), longer intensive care and hospital stays, and higher cost. The observational data point the same way with larger numbers: in 274 isolated femoral shaft fractures there were no cases of the syndrome among the 60 nailed within 10 h and 11 among the 109 nailed later; in the Japanese trauma registry, fixation itself was not an independent risk factor but delay to operation was (odds ratio 2.21); in the American registry fixation within 48 h was protective; and in the Bogota series fixation within 24 h carried an adjusted odds ratio of 0.42. Early fixation also produces the only reliable fall in incidence in the systematic review across sixty years.

The limit on early fixation is the second hit. In the severely injured patient, a reamed femoral nail at hour six is a second major insult laid on top of the first: the EPOFF randomised study measured a perioperative rise in interleukin-6 after primary nailing that did not occur after external fixation, and the larger observational series found a smaller and shorter systemic inflammatory response after damage-control external fixation despite worse injuries in that group. This is the basis of damage control orthopaedics: early stabilisation of the femur within 24 h in every patient, by definitive nail in the physiologically stable patient and by external fixation in the patient who is under-resuscitated, coagulopathic, hypothermic, acidotic, or has a severe chest or head injury, with conversion to a nail once the physiology has recovered, usually between day 3 and day 10. The decision is made with the trauma surgeon and the intensivist on the physiology in front of you, not on the fracture; the polytrauma card sets out the markers and the sequence, and the open fracture and damage control card the external fixation itself.

Bilateral femoral fractures are the case that most often forces the question. The embolic load is doubled and the pulmonary failure rate is higher, but leaving both femurs in traction is worse. The defensible default is to fix both early, the second only after the physiology has been reassessed following the first, and with external fixation of one or both if the patient is marginal.

Management of the syndrome

There is no specific treatment. The syndrome is self-limiting over three to seven days in most patients, and the management is the management of acute lung injury and encephalopathy from any cause.

  • Oxygen titrated to saturation, then continuous positive airway pressure or high-flow nasal oxygen, then intubation and lung-protective ventilation when the work of breathing or the gas exchange demands it. Around a third of patients in the older series needed ventilation; in the recent prospective series most did.
  • Fluid balance aimed at euvolaemia. Hypovolaemia worsens the right heart and the cerebral perfusion; overload worsens the leaky lung. A fracture patient is usually behind, and the first move is usually to fill, measured against the lung.
  • Vasopressors for the hypotension of the right-heart phase, as in the cement chapter.
  • Neuroprotection as for any encephalopathy: normoxia, normocapnia, normoglycaemia, seizure control, and an MRI to confirm the diagnosis and grade it. In a series of 34 patients with cerebral fat embolism, every death had onset within 24 h and a severe MRI grade, and 26 of the 27 survivors recovered completely over a mean of 4.7 weeks. The confused patient on day two who is otherwise stable will almost always recover; the comatose patient within hours of a pathological fracture may not.
  • Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation for the refractory case, now reported in enough series to be a real option in a young patient with an otherwise survivable injury.
  • Corticosteroids are the only drug with trial evidence, all of it for prophylaxis and most of it old. The meta-analysis pooled seven trials and 389 patients: steroid prophylaxis reduced the syndrome by 78% and reduced hypoxia, with no effect on mortality and no excess of infection; the authors judged the trials poor and the confirmatory trial has not been done. Steroids given after the syndrome is established have no evidence. The author’s practice is not to use them; a unit that gives them prophylactically to the young patient with bilateral femoral fractures is not acting against the evidence.
  • Heparin, dextran, alcohol and aspirin have all been tried and none has evidence.
  • Fix the fracture if it has not been fixed. The syndrome is not a reason to delay stabilisation, which removes the source; it is a reason to do it with an arterial line, a plan for the right heart, and possibly an external fixator rather than a reamed nail.

Collapse during nailing: the differential

The patient who deteriorates on the table during reaming or nail insertion is a different problem from the ward syndrome. The event is acute, it is the embolic phase, and it looks exactly like cement implantation syndrome: a fall in end-tidal CO2, then saturation, then blood pressure, with a right ventricle that dilates on echocardiography if a probe is in. Fulminant fat embolism with collapse on the table is rare; the collapse during nailing more often has another cause, and the differential has to be run in seconds.

The discriminators are the capnograph, the airway pressure, the skin and the timeline. An embolic event under general anaesthesia announces itself as a fall in end-tidal CO2 with unchanged ventilation, because pulmonary blood flow has fallen before the pressure does; haemorrhage produces a tachycardia and a falling pressure with the end-tidal CO2 following rather than leading; a tension pneumothorax produces a rising airway pressure and a falling saturation before the pressure; anaphylaxis produces bronchospasm and a rash. In the trauma patient more than one can be true.

What to do for the embolic case:

  1. Say it. The surgeon stops reaming and withdraws the reamer; a half-inserted nail stays where it is until the patient is stable.
  2. 100% oxygen, confirm the airway and the ventilation, and read the capnograph as the cardiac output monitor it now is.
  3. Fill the right ventricle with a measured bolus, then vasopressor to restore the systemic pressure that perfuses it; adrenaline in 10-50 mcg increments if the pressure does not respond or the rate falls, and an infusion if it is needed twice.
  4. Chest compressions at once if the pulse is lost; the rhythm is usually pulseless electrical activity and compressions move blood through a transiently obstructed pulmonary bed.
  5. Echocardiography if it can be had: a dilated right ventricle with a small, underfilled left ventricle confirms the diagnosis, and a transoesophageal probe shows the material crossing the atrium and a shunt if there is one.
  6. When stable, decide with the surgeon whether to finish. A nail that is nearly home should usually be completed, slowly, with the canal vented; a reaming that had barely begun in a patient who has just arrested should stop, and an external fixator applied. The second femur in a bilateral case waits.
  7. Intensive care, ventilated, with an arterial line, an echocardiogram and the expectation that the chemical phase follows in 24-72 h.

The preparation that makes this manageable is the same as for cement: the at-risk patient named at the brief, an arterial line before the reaming starts, normovolaemia, a vasopressor running in the frail or the under-resuscitated, and the anaesthetist at the head of the bed during the reaming rather than at the chart. The femoral nailing and tibial nailing cards carry the step at which this happens; the metastasis card and the periprosthetic fracture card cover the pathological and long-stem canals where the load is largest.

The rule set

  1. Fat embolism is near universal after a long bone fracture and during reaming; the syndrome is uncommon, delayed by 12-72 h, and diagnosed clinically. Do not treat the shower on the monitor as the syndrome, or the syndrome on the ward as an embolus.
  2. Name the high-risk patient: young, femoral fracture, bilateral or multiple fractures, pathological bone, obese, delayed to theatre, chest injury, known right-to-left shunt.
  3. Fix the femur within 24 h: a reamed nail in the physiologically stable patient, external fixation and later conversion in the under-resuscitated, coagulopathic, hypothermic or chest-injured one. Reaming itself does not worsen the lung in the trials; the decision is about the patient, not the reamer.
  4. For the nailing: arterial line in the at-risk patient, normovolaemia, vasopressor ready, anaesthetist present for the reaming, surgeon asked to ream slowly and vent in the pathological or bilateral case.
  5. Collapse during reaming is acute right heart failure until proved otherwise: stop, oxygen, measured fluid, vasopressor then adrenaline, early compressions, echocardiography, with haemorrhage, tension pneumothorax, anaphylaxis and paradoxical embolus run in parallel.
  6. On the ward, hypoxaemia, confusion or petechiae in the first three days after a long bone fracture is the syndrome until excluded. Use Gurd’s list as a checklist and image to exclude the alternatives.
  7. Treatment is supportive: oxygen and ventilation, euvolaemia, vasopressors for the right heart, neuroprotection, intensive care early, ECMO for the refractory young patient. Steroids have poor-quality prophylactic evidence and no treatment evidence; nothing else has any.
  8. Expect recovery in most. The patient who dies does so early, with a pathological or bilateral femur, a severe MRI grade, or a foramen ovale.
