Nerve injury and compartment syndrome under regional anaesthesia

A new deficit after orthopaedic surgery is far more often caused by the operation, the tourniquet or the position than by the block: nerve injury follows about 0.7-0.8% of hip and knee replacements and 2.2% of shoulder replacements whether or not a block was used, while block-related injury in a prospective Australasian audit was 0.4 per 1,000. The block's real hazard is different: it can hide a deficit that has a time-critical remedy, so the rule is that any deficit outside the block's territory or outlasting its expected duration is surgical or compressive until shown otherwise. Compartment syndrome is the sharpest version of the same problem, and the evidence, all case reports, does not show that regional analgesia delays the diagnosis when surveillance is adequate; ischaemic pain broke through a working epidural in most reported cases. What the consensus guidance asks for is a block that is low in concentration, free of duration-prolonging adjuncts and no longer than it needs to be, a patient watched on a schedule by people who know what they are looking for, and a decision shared with the surgeon and written down.

How nerves are injured around an operation

A peripheral nerve fails for one of four reasons, and around orthopaedic surgery all four are in play at once.

Stretch. Nerves tolerate only a small elongation before intraneural blood flow stops. Limb lengthening at hip replacement stretches the sciatic nerve; correcting a valgus, flexed knee stretches the common peroneal nerve around the fibular neck; a retractor on the anterior acetabular wall stretches the femoral nerve; arm traction in shoulder arthroscopy stretches the plexus.

Compression. A tourniquet shears the nerve at the cuff edges (the tourniquet); a haematoma in a closed space, a tight cast, a perineal post or a badly padded elbow press on it (positioning).

Direct trauma. Scalpel, drill, screw, suture, cement heat, and the block needle. A needle that enters a fascicle and injects there causes the block-related injuries that are permanent.

Ischaemia and toxicity. Hypotension, vasoconstrictors, a compartment under pressure, and local anaesthetic itself, which is neurotoxic in proportion to concentration and exposure time.

The patient supplies the susceptibility. A nerve with a pre-existing lesion (diabetic or chemotherapy neuropathy, a compressed root in a stenotic canal, an entrapment at the elbow or wrist) has less reserve and fails at an insult a healthy nerve would shrug off; this is the “double crush” idea, and it is why the cohort data link perioperative nerve injury to diabetes, hypertension and smoking.

Severity sets the prognosis. Neurapraxia (conduction block with the axon intact) recovers in days to weeks. Axonotmesis (axon lost, sheath intact) recovers at about 1 mm a day from the site of injury, so months for a proximal lesion. Neurotmesis does not recover without repair.

Three conclusions. First, the operation carries a baseline nerve injury rate of roughly 1 in 130 for hip and knee replacement and 1 in 45 for shoulder replacement that does not change when a block is added. Second, when a patient with a block wakes with a deficit, the block is the cause in a small minority: in the Australasian audit, 3 of the 30 patients referred. Third, there is one uncomfortable signal: in the knee replacement cohort, injured patients who had also had a block were less likely to recover completely. Whether that is a second insult to an already injured nerve or confounding is not known, and it is a reason to respect the pre-existing deficit, not to abandon blocks.

Transient symptoms after a block (a patch of numbness, tingling that fades over two to four weeks) are common enough to mention in consent; the pooled older data put them at up to 3% after interscalene block. Lasting injury is rare.

The pre-existing deficit

Find it and write it down before anything else is done. A patient with a fractured humeral neck who cannot contract deltoid before the interscalene block has an axillary nerve injury from the fracture; the same finding discovered at 48 hours is an unanswerable question. The pre-block record needs the relevant motor and sensory findings for the limb, any neuropathic symptoms, and known neuropathy or spinal disease.

The ASRA advisory’s position on patients with pre-existing neurological disease is that regional anaesthesia is not contraindicated but the margin is smaller. The practical adjustments are: have a clear reason for the block; use ultrasound and keep the needle outside the nerve; use the lowest effective concentration and volume; leave out adrenaline; and consider avoiding a block of the specific nerve that is already diseased when an alternative exists (a patient with an ulnar neuropathy at the elbow and an infraclavicular block is a foreseeable dispute). For neuraxial techniques, severe spinal stenosis is associated in case series with new deficits after spinal or epidural anaesthesia; the association may be the disease and the surgery rather than the needle, and it is a reason to discuss, not to refuse.

The same advisory holds that warning symptoms are unreliable, that ultrasound has not been shown to reduce the incidence of injury, and that blocks in adults should not routinely be placed under general anaesthesia or deep sedation, because a patient who can report pain on injection provides one more imperfect safeguard.

The dense block that masks a surgical injury

Several orthopaedic nerve injuries have a remedy that works only if applied early.

A sciatic palsy after hip replacement from a haematoma or over-lengthening is treated by evacuating the haematoma or revising the construct. A peroneal palsy after correcting a valgus knee is treated by flexing the knee and loosening the dressings, which takes the stretch off the nerve. An epidural haematoma is treated by decompression within hours. A compartment syndrome is treated by fasciotomy.

Each is discovered by examining a limb that should be working, and a block that paralyses that limb overnight removes the examination. The defences are chosen before the block:

  1. Match the block’s duration to the need. A spinal that has worn off in two to three hours lets the sciatic nerve be tested in recovery after a difficult hip. Long-acting intrathecal bupivacaine with a sciatic block does not.
  2. Prefer motor-sparing blocks where the surgical nerve at risk is a motor nerve: adductor canal block and infiltration for the knee, not femoral and sciatic.
  3. Let the surgeon examine first when the operation threatens a specific nerve, then block in recovery.
  4. Run catheters at low concentration, and stop the infusion to allow an examination whenever there is doubt.
  5. Write the expected duration on the chart. “Interscalene block 14:00, ropivacaine, expected offset by 08:00” gives the ward a time after which a dead arm is abnormal.

The working rule: a deficit outside the territory of the block, a deficit that outlasts the block’s expected duration, or a block that is getting denser when it should be receding is a surgical or compressive lesion until proven otherwise.

Electrophysiology has two uses at two times. Done in the first few days, before Wallerian degeneration has reached the muscle, an abnormal study reveals a lesion that pre-dated the operation. Done at two to three weeks, it localises the lesion (root, plexus, nerve; at the block site or the surgical site or the tourniquet) and grades it. Most deficits are neurapraxias and resolve; the patient needs to be told that, given a named follow-up, and referred early for hand therapy or a foot-drop splint if function is affected. Disclosure and a contemporaneous record of the examination are part of the treatment.

Compartment syndrome: the mechanism and who gets it

A muscle compartment is a closed fascial envelope. Bleeding and oedema after injury or reperfusion raise the pressure inside it; when the pressure approaches the diastolic pressure the capillaries close, muscle and nerve become ischaemic, ischaemic tissue swells further, and the cycle runs to necrosis within hours. The perfusion pressure of the compartment is the diastolic pressure minus the compartment pressure, which is why a hypotensive patient develops the syndrome at a lower compartment pressure, and why an elevated limb fares worse than one at heart level.

The symptom is ischaemic muscle pain: severe, escalating, out of proportion to the injury, and provoked by passively stretching the muscles of the compartment. Paraesthesia follows. Paralysis and pulselessness are late and mean the muscle is already lost.

The patient at risk is a young man with a tibial fracture. In 164 cases, 69% had a fracture and about half of those were tibial shaft; most patients were men under 35, whose muscle bulk fills the compartment; high-energy distal radius and forearm fractures were the upper limb equivalent; soft-tissue injury without fracture was the second commonest cause; and one tenth of patients were anticoagulated or had a bleeding disorder. In orthopaedic lists the risk therefore sits with tibial nailing and plateau fixation, open and high-energy fractures, forearm and high-energy distal radius fractures, crush injury, revascularised limbs, calcaneal and Lisfranc injuries in the foot, and the uninjured leg held in hemilithotomy on a traction table. Elective surgery is not immune: tibial osteotomy and prolonged tourniquet cases produce occasional cases. The operation itself is described in compartment syndrome and fasciotomy.

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by pressure measurement. In 116 tibial fractures monitored continuously, a differential pressure below 30 mmHg as the threshold for fasciotomy missed no case, while an absolute pressure threshold of 30 mmHg would have sent 43% of patients for an unnecessary fasciotomy.

Does regional anaesthesia hide it: the actual evidence

There are no randomised trials and there will not be. The evidence is case reports, and a careful reading of them does not support the belief that blocks hide compartment syndrome.

The systematic review found 28 reports, 23 of them about epidurals. In 32 of 35 patients the classic symptoms and signs were present despite the epidural, and 18 had documented breakthrough pain. The delays in the reports were mostly failures to act on findings that were there. The reviewers concluded that there is no convincing evidence that patient-controlled opioid analgesia or regional analgesia delays diagnosis provided patients are adequately monitored, and made the point that is usually left out of the debate: pain is an unreliable sign with any analgesic technique, including an opioid PCA, and examination is unreliable too.

The physiological explanation for breakthrough is that ischaemic pain is an intense, summating stimulus that a low-concentration sensory block does not abolish. A patient whose block or epidural was working and who now has escalating pain, or rising opioid demand, has announced the diagnosis. That is the most useful sign available, and it is lost only if the block is dense enough to produce surgical anaesthesia of the whole limb for many hours, or if the response to the pain is a top-up.

The Association of Anaesthetists guideline for lower leg trauma, written with orthopaedic surgeons, turns this into practice. Avoid neuraxial or peripheral techniques that give dense blocks lasting well beyond the surgery. Single-shot or continuous peripheral blocks with lower concentrations of local anaesthetic and without adjuncts are not associated with delayed diagnosis provided surveillance is appropriate. Observations are made at set intervals by staff trained to recognise the syndrome, using an objective scoring chart. The patient is told the options and consents. And neither the surgeon nor the anaesthetist has a veto over a treatment recommended by the other: the decision is joint.

How to block when the risk exists

  1. Name the risk aloud at the briefing and agree the plan with the surgeon. Record the discussion and the patient’s consent.
  2. For the anaesthetic, use a general anaesthetic or a spinal that will have gone within two to three hours. Avoid long-acting intrathecal doses and avoid an epidural infusion at concentrations that give a motor block.
  3. For analgesia, use a sensory-weighted, low-concentration block: ropivacaine 0.2% or less. Leave out perineural adjuncts, and consider omitting intravenous dexamethasone on the day if its only purpose is to prolong the block; do not add dexmedetomidine or clonidine. A catheter that can be turned down or off is safer than a single shot that cannot be reversed.
  4. Choose the territory with the compartments in mind. For a tibial plateau or shaft fracture an adductor canal block treats the anterior knee and medial leg and leaves the deep and anterior compartments reporting; a dense sciatic block does not. For forearm fractures a low-dose block with early offset is preferred to a long, dense plexus block.
  5. Start scheduled surveillance in recovery and continue for 24-48 hours: pain score at rest and on passive stretch of the toes or fingers, analgesic consumption, compartment tenseness, sensation and movement, hourly at first, on a chart with a stated escalation trigger.
  6. Treat breakthrough pain as a diagnosis, not a failure of analgesia. Nobody boluses the catheter or increases the PCA until the limb has been examined by someone who can act.
  7. Split casts and dressings to skin at the first concern, keep the limb at heart level, and keep the blood pressure normal.
  8. Use continuous compartment pressure monitoring where it is available for the patient who cannot report: the obtunded, the intubated polytrauma patient, the child, and by extension the patient whose block is denser than intended.

Once a fasciotomy has been done and the post-release neurological examination is recorded, the masking argument is over; the fasciotomy card sets out when a block or catheter is added.

Edge cases

The intubated polytrauma patient. No block is hiding anything, and nobody is examining either. Pressure monitoring or scheduled clinical checks by the surgical team are the only surveillance; see polytrauma.

The anticoagulated patient with a minor injury. Bleeding into a compartment after a trivial fracture or a contusion is over-represented in the series. A low threshold for suspicion applies whatever the analgesia.

The elective osteotomy. The risk is low but real, and the peroneal nerve is also at risk. An adductor canal block and infiltration, no sciatic block, and a documented foot examination in recovery covers both.

The block that will not wear off. A single-shot block persisting beyond 24-36 hours with dexamethasone, or 48 hours at the outside, is investigated as a nerve injury, starting with the pathway above. Reassurance without examination is the error.

The patient who already has a deficit and needs a block. Document it in detail, explain the smaller margin, reduce concentration and volume, and proceed if the analgesic benefit is real. A carefully recorded pre-existing deficit protects the patient’s follow-up as well as the anaesthetist.
