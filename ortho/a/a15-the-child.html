The child having orthopaedic surgery

Most children's orthopaedics is a fit child over two years old with a fracture, and an anaesthetist who mostly looks after adults can do it safely with three habits: a measured weight and doses written per kilogram before induction, a block placed after induction as the registries support, and clear fluids until an hour before. The injured child is the exception to the fasting rule, because trauma and opioid stop the stomach emptying and no interval makes it safe. Neonates and young infants need local anaesthetic and paracetamol doses cut, clear amide local anaesthetics slowly and account for most of the toxicity in the registries. Cerebral palsy, the muscular dystrophies, the spica cast and the unexplained fracture each change the plan in a predictable way. Neonates, former premature babies, infants, and children with heart, airway or neuromuscular disease belong in a paediatric centre unless the limb cannot wait.

What is different, and why

A child differs from an adult in four ways that matter on an orthopaedic list. Oxygen consumption per kilogram is roughly double while the functional residual capacity is small, so apnoea desaturates in seconds and laryngospasm is the defining complication. Every dose is a calculation, so every dose is a chance for a tenfold error. The surface area is large for the mass, so heat leaves quickly. And the child will not lie still for a block, so the regional plan is built around a block placed after induction.

The operations are mostly kind. A supracondylar or forearm fracture is 30 minutes of closed reduction and wires, and pain after fracture fixation in a child falls away within two or three days. The hard cases are those where the surgeon cuts and redirects bone in a small body (pelvic and femoral osteotomy for hip dysplasia, multilevel surgery in cerebral palsy, neuromuscular scoliosis), where blood loss is a meaningful fraction of a small blood volume and the patient brings the comorbidity.

Weight and the dose sheet

Weigh the child. The estimate is for the trauma call when the weight is missing, and it was missing in four of five seriously ill children in a large emergency department series. The traditional formula of twice (age plus four) underestimated weight by a third in 93,827 modern children; three times age plus seven was within 7% and holds from 1 to 13 years.

The neonate and young infant columns are lower for one reason: clearance. The hepatic pathways that clear paracetamol, morphine and the amide local anaesthetics are immature for the first months of life, and alpha-1 acid glycoprotein, which binds bupivacaine and ropivacaine, is low, so the free fraction is higher. A single dose is tolerated; an infusion accumulates. That is why the infusion ceiling under six months is half the older child’s, and why severe local anaesthetic toxicity in the 100,000-block registry clustered in infants. The single-dose and infusion ceilings are those of the joint dosing advisory, which caps a caudal at 2.5 mg/kg of bupivacaine or levobupivacaine and 2 mg/kg of ropivacaine and steps the epidural infusion from 0.2 to 0.3 to 0.4 mg/kg/h with age; the lowest band is extended here from three months to six. The same network’s caudal series took 2 mg/kg of bupivacaine equivalent as its caution line and found a quarter of children above it, so work out the ceiling in millilitres before the syringe is filled. An adolescent dosed as an adult takes the adult ceilings in the block menu. Lipid emulsion 20% is 1.5 mL/kg as a bolus at any age.

Three other rules. Ibuprofen is withheld under three months because renal prostaglandin dependence is high. Codeine has no place because ultra-rapid metabolisers convert it to morphine unpredictably, and tramadol is kept for adolescents for the same reason. Tranexamic acid earns its place in osteotomy and spinal surgery; the range in the table is the pharmacokinetic recommendation for major surgery.

The emergency doses also go on the sheet: suxamethonium 2 mg/kg IV (4 mg/kg intramuscular), atropine 20 microg/kg, rocuronium 1 mg/kg, propofol 1 mg/kg for laryngospasm, adrenaline 10 microg/kg in arrest, and a fluid bolus of 10 mL/kg.

Fasting

The one-hour rule exists because the two-hour rule failed in practice: a written two-hour clear fluid fast produced actual fasts of 6-7 hours, and up to 15 hours in some audits, with no reduction in aspiration. The 2018 joint statement of three European paediatric anaesthesia societies moved to clear fluids until one hour before anaesthesia, and the 2022 European guideline confirmed it and shortened breast milk to three hours.

The Australian and New Zealand college takes the same position: children up to 16 years are encouraged to drink clear liquids, up to 3 mL/kg per hour, until one hour before anaesthesia; solids stop at six hours; breast milk stops at three hours at any age; formula stops at four hours up to 12 months of age and counts as a solid after that. Some Australian children’s hospitals allow sips until the child is called to theatre; on a mixed list the child drinks until called and goes first.

The unfasted trauma child

Fasting times do not apply to the injured child, because the stomach stops when the bone breaks. Pain, fear and opioid all delay gastric emptying, and in 110 injured children half of those starved for more than eight hours still had a gastric aspirate above 0.4 mL/kg. The interval between the last meal and the injury mattered more than the fast, and even that did not predict an empty stomach.

The decision is therefore about urgency, not about the clock. A pulseless or poorly perfused hand after a supracondylar fracture, an open fracture and a septic hip go when the surgeon is ready. A closed, neurovascularly intact fracture can wait for a daytime list, which the supracondylar evidence supports.

When the stomach is assumed full, the adult rapid sequence is the wrong tool: a child left apnoeic for a minute desaturates before the relaxant works, and cricoid pressure distorts a small airway. The controlled paediatric version is an adequate intravenous induction dose, rocuronium 1 mg/kg, gentle mask ventilation at low pressure until the relaxant has worked, and a cuffed tube. The stomach is still full at the end, so the child is extubated awake and on the side.

Blocks under general anaesthesia

In adults the awake patient is the nerve injury monitor. In children the registry data removed that argument. In the first 50,000 blocks of the Pediatric Regional Anesthesia Network, postoperative neurological symptoms occurred in 0.93 per 1,000 placed under general anaesthesia and 6.82 per 1,000 placed sedated or awake, and the only deficit lasting beyond six months followed a block under sedation. At 100,000 blocks there was no permanent neurological deficit (the upper confidence limit is 0.4 in 10,000), severe local anaesthetic toxicity ran at 0.76 in 10,000, and placing the block under general anaesthesia added no risk. The European and American regional societies’ joint advisory calls blocks under general anaesthesia the standard of care in children.

A competent adult regional anaesthetist can place a femoral, fascia iliaca, popliteal sciatic or supraclavicular block in an anaesthetised 20 kg child; the nerves are shallower and the image is better.

The joint dosing advisory gives 0.5-1.5 mg/kg of bupivacaine or ropivacaine for ultrasound-guided limb blocks. The caudal volumes are the scheme it endorses, under its caudal ceilings: 1 mL/kg of either standard solution is the ceiling dose, so the low thoracic volume is given only diluted, and under six months levobupivacaine is 0.2% at 1 mL/kg.

The caudal is the block an adult anaesthetist is least current with and it has forgiving anatomy in a child under about six: the sacral hiatus is superficial, the epidural fat is loose, and the solution spreads predictably. In 18,650 registry caudals the complication rate was 1.9%, almost all failed block, blood aspiration or intravascular injection, with no sequelae. The traps are intravascular injection (aspirate, inject in increments, watch the T wave and heart rate), the dural sac ending lower in the infant, and the sacral dimple or known dysraphism that sends the plan elsewhere. Preservative-free clonidine 1-2 microg/kg extends a caudal from about four hours to eight or more; it is avoided in the young infant because of apnoea.

Catheters are used less than in adults; a peripheral infusion is ropivacaine 0.2% at 0.05-0.15 mL/kg/h, which is the advisory’s 0.1-0.3 mg/kg/h. In 2,074 paediatric peripheral catheters the adverse event rate was 12%, nearly all catheter malfunction or block failure, with no systemic toxicity or lasting nerve problem, and infection tracked with duration beyond about three days. The epidural keeps its place after pelvic osteotomy and bilateral lower limb surgery in cerebral palsy, where it also treats spasm.

The compartment syndrome question is answered the same way as in adults (see nerve injury and compartment syndrome). The joint advisory found no evidence that regional anaesthesia delays the diagnosis in children, and a review of the paediatric epidural cases identified the warning signs as rising analgesic demand and breakthrough pain through a working block. For the at-risk injuries (tibial shaft, a forearm nailed with flexible rods, a supracondylar fracture with a forearm fracture in the same limb) the defensible default is a dilute block or none.

Emergence delirium

Emergence delirium is a dissociated state in the first 10 to 30 minutes after a volatile anaesthetic: a preschool child who thrashes, does not recognise the parent, cannot be consoled and pulls at the cannula, cast and catheter. It is commonest between two and five years and after sevoflurane. The validated scale scores eye contact, purposeful actions, awareness of surroundings, restlessness and inconsolability; the first three separate it from pain, because a child in pain makes eye contact and is consoled.

It matters in orthopaedics because the child has wires, a wet plaster or a catheter to dislodge. In a meta-analysis of 37 trials propofol, ketamine, alpha-2 agonists, fentanyl and effective peri-operative analgesia each cut the odds of emergence agitation by two-thirds or more (odds ratios 0.15-0.31), while midazolam did not. The practical recipe is a working block so that pain is not in the differential, then propofol 1 mg/kg at the end of the case or dexmedetomidine 0.3-0.5 microg/kg. Treatment is the same drugs, a quiet bay and the parent.

Spica casts

A hip spica is applied after closed or open reduction of a dislocated hip and for a femoral shaft fracture in a child under about five. The surgeon needs the pelvis and both legs free in the air, so the anaesthetised child is lifted onto a spica frame (a narrow perineal post and a chest support) with the airway at one end and the anaesthetist displaced from it by the plaster team.

Four consequences follow. The airway is secured for a move: a tube in the infant, or a well-seated supraglottic airway in the older child, taped as for a prone turn. Casting is not stimulating, so depth is lightened, but the child is not woken until the cast is rigid, because the mould holds the reduction. Wet plaster on a small trunk is an evaporative cooler and forced-air warming cannot be used while it goes on, so the theatre is at 24-26 degrees C and a radiant heater or warm mattress does the work. And the block is placed before the back and groin are under plaster: a caudal with an adjunct for hip surgery, a fascia iliaca block for the fractured femur, with a caudal for the infant.

Afterwards, a folded towel placed under the cast over the abdomen is removed once it sets, to leave room for the stomach; a tight abdominal segment causes vomiting and, rarely, duodenal compression. Pain that rises inside a spica is cast pressure, loss of reduction or compartment syndrome until shown otherwise, and it is not treated by adding opioid.

Cerebral palsy and neuromuscular disease

Cerebral palsy is a fixed brain injury with a progressive musculoskeletal consequence: spastic muscle does not grow with bone, so contractures, hip subluxation and scoliosis develop and the surgeon lengthens tendons and redirects bone, often at several levels in one sitting. The anaesthetic problems scale with the motor severity. The walking child with diplegia is a near-normal anaesthetic with more postoperative spasm than expected. The child who uses a wheelchair and is fed by gastrostomy has reflux, chronic aspiration, a weak cough, seizures, low body fat and contractures that will not sit on a standard table.

The adjustments: anticonvulsants are given on the morning of surgery. Volatile requirement is lower (halothane MAC was 0.62-0.71% against 0.90% in controls), so the standard age-adjusted dose is an overdose and emergence is slow. Suxamethonium is not contraindicated, because the muscle is not denervated, but is rarely needed. An intrathecal baclofen pump is never stopped; abrupt withdrawal produces rebound spasticity, hyperthermia and rhabdomyolysis. Postoperatively half the pain is spasm, which is why an epidural and regular diazepam do better than opioid alone, and pain is scored with a tool for non-verbal children and the carer’s reading of the child.

The muscular dystrophies are a different problem. In Duchenne and Becker dystrophy the absence of dystrophin leaves a fragile muscle membrane, and two drugs injure it. Suxamethonium produces acute rhabdomyolysis and hyperkalaemic cardiac arrest and is absolutely contraindicated; the undiagnosed boy of three with large calves looks healthy, which is the argument against its routine use in any child. Volatile agents can produce the same rhabdomyolysis more slowly, sometimes in recovery. A systematic analysis found no true increase in malignant hyperthermia susceptibility, so the treatment is that of hyperkalaemia, not dantrolene. The anaesthetic is total intravenous on a flushed machine, with a reduced dose of non-depolarising relaxant.

The muscle disease is also a cardiomyopathy and a restrictive lung defect. An echocardiogram and a forced vital capacity belong in the work-up of any boy with Duchenne dystrophy: the chest physicians’ consensus statement treats a vital capacity below 50% of predicted as increased risk and below 30% as high risk, and recommends training in non-invasive ventilation and assisted cough before surgery so that extubation is onto a familiar mask.

Safeguarding

A fracture is sometimes the presenting sign of abuse, and the anaesthetist sees the whole undressed child under good light. In a systematic review of 32 studies abusive fractures were commonest in infants and toddlers and were often multiple. With major trauma excluded, the probability of abuse was 0.71 for rib fractures, about one-half for humeral fractures and between 0.28 and 0.43 for femoral fractures, with the developmental stage the discriminator: a femoral shaft fracture in a child who cannot yet walk needs an explanation. Supracondylar fractures were less likely to be inflicted. No fracture pattern is diagnostic.

The anaesthetist contributes observation and a record: bruising in a child who is not yet mobile, bruises on the ears, neck, trunk or buttocks, patterned marks, burns, injuries of different ages, or a history that does not fit the child’s development. The findings are written down factually and passed to the treating consultant and the hospital’s child protection service the same day; the reporting duty varies by jurisdiction and the anaesthetist is not the investigator.

Blood volume, transfusion and temperature

Bleeding is judged against the child’s blood volume, not against the adult’s instinct for what a swab weighs.

Blood volume           neonate 85-90 mL/kg; infant 80; child 70-75
Allowable loss         volume x (start Hb - lowest Hb) / start Hb
Red cells              10 mL/kg raises Hb by about 20 g/L
Plasma, platelets      10-15 mL/kg each;  cryoprecipitate 5 mL/kg
Example                15 kg child, Hb 120: volume 1,100 mL;
                       a loss of 450 mL takes Hb to about 70

A 15 kg child having a femoral and pelvic osteotomy can lose a third of the blood volume into a suction canister that looks trivial, so swabs are weighed and a haemoglobin is measured at the bedside. A loss of 15-20% of the blood volume is the prompt to measure, not the trigger to transfuse. For the haemodynamically stable child without cardiac disease the trigger is a haemoglobin of 70 g/L, the restrictive threshold of the paediatric critical care consensus, which was written for intensive care and is applied to theatre by extension. It sets separate thresholds for cardiac disease, and for haemorrhagic shock it recommends red cells, plasma and platelets in ratio without waiting for a number. Red cells are prescribed in mL/kg and warmed, and calcium is given early in massive transfusion.

Heat loss follows from geometry: an infant’s surface area to mass ratio is two to three times the adult’s, and anaesthesia abolishes the vasoconstriction and non-shivering thermogenesis that defend core temperature. The defaults are a theatre warmed for the induction, forced air under the child from the start, warmed fluids for anything over 20 mL/kg, and a temperature probe in every case over 30 minutes.

When the case goes to a paediatric centre

The college guideline states what general training covers: children over two years of age without significant comorbidity having surgery of moderate complexity. It asks hospitals to consider higher-volume centres for neonates, former premature babies and infants, and for children who are ASA 3 or above or have congenital heart disease, and it asks that emergency surgery happen where there are paediatric inpatient facilities. The European APRICOT study gives the reason: severe critical events occurred in 5.2% of 31,127 paediatric anaesthetics, the risk rose with younger age and comorbidity, and what protected was the years of experience of the most senior anaesthetist, not the kind of hospital.

Translated to an orthopaedic list:

Stays local Goes to the paediatric centre
Fit child over 2 years: forearm, supracondylar, tibial or ankle fracture Any neonate, former premature infant, or infant for elective surgery
Removal of wires and plates; simple tendon and foot procedures Hip dysplasia osteotomy, multilevel surgery, scoliosis, tumour, limb lengthening
Stable slipped epiphysis in an adolescent without sleep apnoea Duchenne or other muscle disease, for any anaesthetic
Septic joint washout in a well child Congenital heart disease, syndromic or difficult airway, ASA 3 or above
Time-critical limb (pulseless hand, open fracture) when transfer would cost perfusion Polytrauma, or suspected abuse with other injuries

The last row of the left column is the judgement call. The guideline says local referral triggers must weigh delay in time-critical treatment, and a white hand after a supracondylar fracture is better served by reduction now in a hospital with a competent surgeon and anaesthetist than by a two-hour transfer. The condition is that the theatre has what the guideline lists: an assistant with paediatric skills for the whole case, paediatric equipment, one-to-one recovery nursing, and a parent at induction and in recovery.

    36 children having orthopaedic surgery: halothane MAC 0.90% in healthy children against 0.62% with cerebral palsy on anticonvulsants and 0.71% without
  24. Gurnaney H, Brown A, Litman RS. Malignant hyperthermia and muscular dystrophies. Anesth Analg 2009;109:1043-1048
    Systematic analysis: no increased malignant hyperthermia susceptibility in Duchenne or Becker dystrophy, but volatile agents can cause rhabdomyolysis, sometimes postoperatively, and suxamethonium causes life-threatening hyperkalaemia and should be avoided
  25. Hayes J, Veyckemans F, Bissonnette B. Duchenne muscular dystrophy: an old anesthesia problem revisited. Paediatr Anaesth 2008;18:100-106
    Reviews the reported reactions to inhalational agents in dystrophinopathy that resemble malignant hyperthermia and proposes recommendations for safe anaesthetic management
  26. Birnkrant DJ. The American College of Chest Physicians consensus statement on the respiratory and related management of patients with Duchenne muscular dystrophy undergoing anesthesia or sedation. Pediatrics 2009;123 Suppl 4:S242-S244
    Summary of the consensus statement on pre-operative respiratory assessment, non-invasive ventilation and assisted cough around anaesthesia in Duchenne dystrophy
  27. Kemp AM, Dunstan F, Harrison S, et al. Patterns of skeletal fractures in child abuse: systematic review. BMJ 2008;337:a1518
    32 studies: abusive fractures commonest in infants and toddlers and often multiple; probability of abuse 0.71 for rib fractures once major trauma was excluded, 0.48-0.54 for humeral and 0.28-0.43 for femoral fractures; supracondylar humeral fractures less likely to be inflicted; no fracture on its own distinguishes abuse
  28. Valentine SL, Bembea MM, Muszynski JA, et al. Consensus recommendations for RBC transfusion practice in critically ill children from the Pediatric Critical Care Transfusion and Anemia Expertise Initiative. Pediatr Crit Care Med 2018;19:884-898
    102 consensus recommendations and a decision tree for red cell transfusion in critically ill children, built on a restrictive haemoglobin threshold in the stable child
  29. Australian and New Zealand College of Anaesthetists. PG29(A) Guideline for the provision of anaesthesia care to children, 2020
    Training equips graduates to manage children over two years without significant comorbidity having surgery of moderate complexity; neonates, former premature babies, infants, ASA 3-5 and congenital heart disease do better in higher-volume centres; emergency surgery where there are paediatric inpatient facilities; local referral triggers must weigh delay in time-critical treatment
  30. Habre W, Disma N, Virag K, et al. Incidence of severe critical events in paediatric anaesthesia (APRICOT): a prospective multicentre observational study in 261 hospitals in Europe. Lancet Respir Med 2017;5:412-425
    31,127 anaesthetics: severe critical events in 5.2% (respiratory 3.1%, cardiovascular 1.9%); younger age, medical history and physical condition were the risks; years of experience of the most senior anaesthetist was protective, the type of institution was not
