Neuraxial versus general anaesthesia for arthroplasty

The large registry associations favour neuraxial anaesthesia for hip and knee arthroplasty, and the two big randomised hip fracture trials (REGAIN and RAGA) found no difference in survival, walking or delirium. Both are true and they answer different questions. Neuraxial reliably changes blood loss, the early pain curve, urinary retention and the shape of recovery on the day; it does not reliably change mortality or delirium once a modern general anaesthetic is the comparator. The author's default is a low-dose spinal for arthroplasty and for hip fracture in a patient who can lie still, a general anaesthetic for the patient who cannot, and a refusal to let either technique be a reason to delay a hip fracture.

What the two techniques actually change

Strip the argument back to physiology before touching a trial. A spinal anaesthetic produces a dense, bilateral sympathetic, sensory and motor block from roughly T10 down for two to three hours, with a fall in venous return and arterial tone that is proportional to the dose and the height of the block. A general anaesthetic produces a dose-dependent fall in sympathetic tone, positive pressure ventilation, an airway instrumented in a patient who may be old and frail, and opioid given systemically to cover a stimulus that a spinal would have covered completely. From those two descriptions you can predict most of what the literature shows.

The spinal lowers blood loss, because venous pressure in the operative field falls and the patient breathes spontaneously without positive intrathoracic pressure. It abolishes nociception at the cord for the duration of the block, so the first hours after surgery are pain free and opioid free, and the intraoperative stress response is smaller. It leaves the airway alone. It causes urinary retention, because the sacral parasympathetic outflow is blocked for longer than the sensory block, and it causes hypotension that is worst in the first thirty minutes, dose related, and treated with vasopressors rather than fluid. It does nothing to the brain directly, which is why the hope that it would prevent delirium was always a hope about avoiding volatile agents and opioids rather than a mechanism of its own.

The general anaesthetic avoids the hypotension of a high block, avoids the retention, avoids the technical failure in a kyphotic or previously fused spine, and lets the surgeon take as long as they like. It costs an airway, systemic opioid, more bleeding, and a wake-up in which the patient is in pain unless a block or infiltration has been added. A modern general anaesthetic with a peripheral nerve block and surgeon infiltration is not the comparator the older registry studies were describing, and that is the single most important thing to hold on to when reading them.

The registry associations

The observational data are consistent and large. In 382,236 primary hip and knee arthroplasties from about 400 American hospitals between 2006 and 2010, 30-day mortality was 0.10% with neuraxial, 0.10% with combined neuraxial and general, and 0.18% with general anaesthesia, and neuraxial carried the most favourable profile for prolonged stay, cost and in-hospital complications; for knee arthroplasty the adjusted odds of death with general anaesthesia were 1.83. The ICAROS consensus group pooled 94 studies, nearly all observational, and found neuraxial associated with lower odds or no difference in essentially every complication they looked at: mortality after hip arthroplasty OR 0.67, pulmonary complications OR 0.65, acute renal failure OR 0.69, deep venous thrombosis OR 0.52, infection OR 0.73, transfusion OR 0.85, with urinary retention as the one outcome that went the other way. Their recommendation is neuraxial for hip arthroplasty (strong, on moderate-to-low evidence) and for knee arthroplasty (weak, on low evidence).

Two things limit what this can tell you. The first is that technique is not randomly assigned: the patient with a fused lumbar spine, the patient on clopidogrel, the patient with aortic stenosis the surgeon does not want hypotensive, and the patient who refuses to be awake are selected into the general anaesthesia group, and those are not random patients. The second is that the general anaesthetic of 2006 to 2010 in a large American sample was often volatile, opioid-based and blockless, so the comparison is partly a comparison of eras. Neither limitation means the association is wrong. The blood loss and transfusion signal in particular is biologically plausible and reproduces in randomised data. But an odds ratio of 0.67 for death in a registry is a reason to prefer a technique when nothing else decides it, not a reason to force it on a patient in whom it is difficult.

The randomised hip fracture evidence

The Cochrane review of 31 trials and 3,231 participants, nearly all older trials in older patients, found no difference in one-month mortality (RR 0.78, with a confidence interval that crossed one), no difference in pneumonia, myocardial infarction, stroke or acute confusion, and a reduction in deep venous thrombosis only in trials where no potent thromboprophylaxis was given, which is to say in a world that no longer exists. The quality of evidence was rated very low throughout, and the authors asked for large pragmatic trials. Two then arrived.

REGAIN randomised 1,600 patients aged 50 and over having hip fracture surgery across 46 North American hospitals to spinal or general anaesthesia. The primary outcome was death or inability to walk about 3 m independently or with a frame at 60 days: 18.5% with spinal and 18.0% with general, relative risk 1.03. Death within 60 days was 3.9% versus 4.1%. Delirium was 20.5% versus 19.7%. Sixteen per cent of the patients assigned to spinal received a general anaesthetic in the end, which is close to the real-world conversion rate and part of what a pragmatic trial is for. The preplanned pain analysis is the part most readers skip: 73.5% of all patients reported severe pain in the first 24 hours whichever technique they had, worst pain in the first 24 hours was 0.4 points higher after spinal, and 25% of the spinal group versus 18.8% of the general group were still on prescription analgesics at 60 days. The honest reading is that neither technique made the first day after a hip fracture comfortable, and that the pain plan is a separate problem from the anaesthetic choice.

RAGA randomised 950 patients aged 65 and over in nine Chinese teaching hospitals to regional anaesthesia without any sedation or to general anaesthesia, with delirium in the first seven days as the primary outcome: 6.2% versus 5.1%, no difference. The delirium rate is a third of REGAIN’s, which reflects the population and the assessment, but the absence of any signal in a trial designed to find one is the point. A 2022 meta-analysis of contemporary randomised trials using a core outcome set put the delirium relative risk at 1.07 (CI 0.90-1.29), mortality no different in hospital, at 30 days or at 90 days, and acute kidney injury lower with spinal (RR 0.59), which is the one finding that fits the registry story.

So the randomised evidence says: for the hip fracture patient, the anaesthetic technique does not decide whether they live, walk or become delirious. What decides those is the fracture, the patient, the orthogeriatric care, the time to theatre and the blood pressure in theatre. The ASAP-2 analysis of 11,085 British hip fracture operations found no association between technique and death but a clear one between intraoperative hypotension and death, with each 5 mmHg fall in systolic pressure raising the odds, and the fall in pressure after a spinal correlating with the volume of bupivacaine injected. That is the finding to carry into the room: the technique matters less than the dose, and the dose matters because of the pressure.

Sedation during a spinal

The STRIDE trial answered the obvious next question. Two hundred patients aged 65 and over having hip fracture repair under spinal with propofol sedation were randomised to heavier (modified observer’s assessment score 0-2) or lighter (3-5) sedation. Delirium occurred in 39% with heavier and 34% with lighter sedation, not different; a prespecified analysis found the benefit of lighter sedation confined to patients with a low comorbidity burden. The practical consequence is that there is no reason to sedate a spinal patient to the point where they have, in effect, an unprotected general anaesthetic in the lateral position with a difficult airway behind a drape, and no strong reason to deny a frightened patient enough to lie still. The RAGA regional arm, with no sedation at all and a delirium rate of 6.2%, shows that the fully awake spinal is achievable in a different culture of expectation; whether it is achievable in yours depends on the patient in front of you and on how long the surgeon takes.

The author’s practice is a spinal with propofol target-controlled sedation at the lowest level that keeps the patient still and comfortable, supplemental oxygen, capnography on the mask, and a deliberate decision not to escalate sedation when the surgery runs long but to add local, change the plan or convert cleanly. A patient who needs deep sedation to tolerate a spinal is a patient who should have had a general anaesthetic with a block, and the time to make that decision is before the needle, not at the second top-up of ketamine.

Dose, duration and the day-case spinal

For elective arthroplasty the spinal is now chosen for its recovery profile rather than its depth, because the surgeon finishes a primary hip or knee in 60 to 90 minutes and the pathway wants the patient standing by the afternoon. A traditional 3 mL dose of hyperbaric bupivacaine 0.5% (15 mg) gives three to four hours of motor block, a reliable bladder problem, and the hypotension the ASAP-2 data link to harm. The alternatives are a smaller dose of bupivacaine, or a shorter-acting agent.

Low-dose bupivacaine (7.5-10 mg, isobaric or hyperbaric, with or without a small dose of intrathecal fentanyl) covers a primary joint in experienced hands but has a failure tail when the surgeon is slow; the surgeon’s actual skin-to-skin time, not the booked time, is what sets the dose. Mepivacaine is the agent with the best evidence for a faster exit. In a randomised trial of 163 knee arthroplasties, mepivacaine 60 mg against isobaric bupivacaine 10 mg returned motor function at 210 versus 229 minutes and reduced urinary retention from 57% to 36%, at the price of higher pain in recovery (32 versus 10 mm on a 100 mm scale) with no difference by six hours, no difference in opioid use, and no difference in same-day discharge. A meta-analysis of 13 studies and 4,167 patients found mepivacaine gave motor recovery 31 minutes sooner, walking 52 minutes sooner and a facility stay 4.4 hours shorter. Chloroprocaine is shorter again: in a 65-patient series under a fixed 50 mg dose, anaesthesia lasted 87 minutes for a mean 52 minutes of surgery, everyone mobilised the same day, and up to 45% needed intravenous morphine titration in recovery because the block was gone before the oral analgesia had started. Prilocaine sits between the two. Lidocaine is not used intrathecally because of transient neurological symptoms, which the Cochrane network meta-analysis attributes to lidocaine above the other agents.

The trade is always the same: the shorter the block, the earlier the patient walks and voids and the earlier they hurt. A short-acting spinal is only safe in a pathway where the block that will carry the patient (adductor canal, PENG, fascia iliaca, surgeon infiltration) and the oral regimen are in place before the spinal recedes. None of the short agents is available or licensed for intrathecal use everywhere; where mepivacaine and chloroprocaine are not on the shelf, low-dose bupivacaine with a careful eye on the clock is the realistic version of the same idea. Intrathecal morphine lengthens the analgesia and shortens nothing else; PROSPECT allows up to 100 micrograms for inpatients (as an option for the hip, and for the knee only when adductor canal block and infiltration are both impossible) and notes that the monitoring it needs limits it in ambulatory pathways. The ERAS consensus goes further and recommends against spinal opioids for routine use, on the grounds that they delay the recovery the pathway is built around.

The anticoagulated patient

The decision tree is simple to describe and tedious to apply. The question is not whether to do a spinal but whether the interval since the last dose, the renal function and the planned catheter together put the risk of an epidural haematoma above the benefit of avoiding a general anaesthetic. The ASRA fifth edition (2025) is the current reference, and its authors say plainly that they keep a conservative stance because a complication this rare cannot be studied by trial. The intervals that decide most cases (from the BJA Education summary of the same body of guidance): prophylactic low molecular weight heparin 12 hours before puncture and the next dose no sooner than 4 hours after; treatment-dose low molecular weight heparin 24 hours, with an anti-Xa level if the clearance is in doubt; rivaroxaban and apixaban 72 hours, or earlier with a measured anti-Xa level; dabigatran 72 hours with normal renal function and up to 120 hours as creatinine clearance falls below 50 mL/min; clopidogrel and ticagrelor 5 to 7 days; prasugrel 7 to 10 days; warfarin stopped 5 days before with a normalised INR; aspirin alone no restriction; subcutaneous unfractionated heparin at prophylactic doses 4 to 6 hours. A catheter adds a second set of intervals at removal and it is the catheter, not the single-shot spinal, that generates most of the reported haematomas.

For the elective arthroplasty patient this is a pre-admission clinic problem, and the answer is a written stop date and a bridging decision made with whoever prescribes the anticoagulant. For the hip fracture patient it is a daily problem, and the trap is letting it become a reason for delay. A patient who took rivaroxaban this morning is not spinal-eligible for three days; they are general-anaesthetic-eligible tonight. The hip fracture guideline from the Association of Anaesthetists and the Australian clinical care standard both treat anticoagulation as a reason to choose the technique, not to postpone the operation, and the data on delay beyond 36 to 48 hours are worse than any plausible difference between the two anaesthetics. The one exception is the patient on a direct oral anticoagulant with poor renal function and a surgical reason to want low bleeding, where a day’s delay to let the level fall is defensible; it should be a surgical decision, documented, not an anaesthetic default.

Antiplatelet drugs deserve a separate sentence. Aspirin is not a contraindication to a spinal. Clopidogrel within 5 days is, and a hip fracture patient on dual antiplatelet therapy after a recent stent has a general anaesthetic with a fascia iliaca block and the cardiologist’s view on the stent, in that order.

The author’s position

For elective primary hip and knee arthroplasty the defensible default is a spinal, chosen for the blood loss, the clean first hours and the recovery profile, and dosed for the surgeon’s real operating time rather than the traditional 15 mg. The patient who should have a general anaesthetic instead is the one in whom the spinal is technically unreliable (a long fusion, severe kyphosis, prior failed attempts), the one who cannot lie still or flat (severe cardiac failure, dementia with agitation, a painful contralateral joint), the one in whom the anticoagulant interval has not elapsed, and the one who, properly informed, declines. A general anaesthetic with a peripheral nerve block and surgeon infiltration is a good anaesthetic for a primary joint and the registry mortality signal is not a reason to tell a reluctant patient otherwise.

For hip fracture the trials have settled the outcome argument and left the practical one. A spinal in a frail patient should be low dose (5-7.5 mg of bupivacaine is enough in a small, old patient), placed in the lateral position after a fascia iliaca or femoral block has made positioning tolerable, with a vasopressor infusion ready before the injection rather than after the pressure falls, because ASAP-2 says the pressure is what matters. A general anaesthetic is the right call when the patient is agitated, when the surgery is expected to be long (a complex nail in a subtrochanteric fracture is not a spinal case), when the anticoagulant interval has not elapsed, and when the anaesthetist’s honest assessment is that they will end up sedating the patient to the point of a general anaesthetic anyway. In either case the block before surgery, the oral analgesia written up before the patient leaves recovery and the blood pressure kept near baseline are the interventions with the evidence behind them.

The error to avoid in both directions is the dogmatic one. A spinal forced into a patient on clopidogrel because the department prefers spinals, or a hip fracture delayed a day for the anticoagulant interval, is a harm the registry odds ratios do not begin to offset. A general anaesthetic given to a cooperative 80-year-old with a straightforward hemiarthroplasty because it is quicker to set up is a missed opportunity to avoid an airway, a litre of blood and a morphine infusion. The procedure cards (neck of femur fracture, posterior approach THA, primary TKA) carry the operation-specific version of this chapter; this is the reasoning they lean on.

Edge cases

  • Aortic stenosis. A low-dose spinal with a vasopressor infusion from the start is acceptable in moderate stenosis; the severe, symptomatic valve favours a general anaesthetic with an arterial line, because the hypotension of a spinal is unpredictable in timing and a fixed-output ventricle has no reserve for it. Either way the aim is the same: a pressure within 20% of baseline throughout.
  • The previously fused lumbar spine. A spinal through a fusion mass is often possible above or below the instrumented levels and is often patchy; an ultrasound look at the interspaces before committing is worth the two minutes, and a general anaesthetic should be the plan if the first two attempts fail rather than the fifth.
  • Obesity. The spinal is harder to place and the block height less predictable because of the smaller cerebrospinal fluid volume; the airway argument favours the spinal, the technical argument favours the general. A sitting position, a long needle and ultrasound for the midline tilt the balance back.
  • Bilateral arthroplasty. The surgical time exceeds a single-shot spinal; a combined spinal-epidural or a general anaesthetic with blocks are the options, and the epidural is the one that does not run out.
  • The day-case hip fracture does not exist, but the day-of-surgery mobilisation does, and a 15 mg spinal in a frail patient at 16:00 guarantees they will not stand until the next morning.
  • Conversion. A failed or inadequate spinal in the lateral position with a draped hip is a predictable scenario; the airway kit, the drug and the plan for turning the patient are decided before the surgeon scrubs.

Key sources

The two trials that changed this chapter are REGAIN (hip fracture, 1,600 patients, no difference in death or walking at 60 days, no difference in delirium) and RAGA (hip fracture, 950 patients, no difference in delirium with a wholly unsedated regional technique). The registry data from Memtsoudis and the ICAROS consensus describe a real association between neuraxial anaesthesia and better outcomes in elective arthroplasty that the hip fracture trials neither confirm nor refute, because the populations and the comparators differ. The spinal dose and agent evidence is small, recent and consistent: shorter blocks mean earlier walking and earlier pain. The anticoagulation intervals are consensus on a rare complication and will stay that way. Where the chapter says “the author’s practice”, it is exactly that.
