Spine essentials: neuromonitoring, prone physiology, blood loss and vision loss

Spinal surgery adds four things to an orthopaedic anaesthetic that the limb operations do not have: a patient turned face down for hours, a cord whose function is being watched electrically, a bleeding surface that scales with the number of levels, and an airway that the operation itself can swell. Prone positioning lowers cardiac index through reduced venous return and the size of the fall depends on the frame; a free abdomen is both haemodynamic and haemostatic. Ischaemic optic neuropathy is the visual loss of long, bloody prone operations, not of eye pressure: in the registry 96% of cases had six hours of anaesthesia or a litre of blood loss, and the independent risk factors are male sex, obesity, the Wilson frame, duration, blood loss and a low proportion of colloid. Motor evoked potentials require a propofol-opioid anaesthetic with no relaxant after intubation, and a signal change is managed by a rehearsed checklist that starts with pressure, haemoglobin, temperature and the anaesthetic before it reaches the implants. Tranexamic acid reduces blood loss in multilevel surgery; after anterior cervical surgery the airway fails at about 36 hours from pharyngeal oedema, not in recovery; and intrathecal morphine buys the first 24 hours after fusion at the price of itch and monitoring.

What turning prone does

Three things change when an anaesthetised patient is rolled from the trolley onto a spinal frame.

Venous return falls. Blood pools in the dependent legs, and any pressure on the abdomen narrows the inferior vena cava. Intrathoracic pressure rises where the chest rests on its supports, which reduces ventricular filling and compliance. The net effect is a fall in stroke volume and cardiac index with a compensatory rise in systemic resistance, so the blood pressure may look acceptable while the output has dropped by a fifth or more. The size of the fall depends on the support. In an echocardiographic comparison of five systems, cardiac index and stroke volume fell on the Andrews, Wilson and Siemens frames and changed least on the Jackson table and on longitudinal bolsters, and patients whose fluid deficit had been corrected before the turn had less hypotension. The practical reading: fill before turning, have the vasopressor running, and for the patient with limited cardiac reserve ask for the Jackson table.

The abdomen decides the bleeding. If the belly hangs free, caval pressure is low and so is the pressure in the epidural venous plexus, which drains to the cava through valveless veins. If the belly is compressed, caval blood diverts through the vertebral veins and the surgeon operates in a field that wells up from the epidural space. Checking that the abdomen is free is a haemostatic act. A free abdomen also lets the diaphragm descend: functional residual capacity and oxygenation in the prone anaesthetised patient are as good as or better than supine when the supports are on the chest and pelvis, and airway pressure rises when they are not.

The head becomes dependent or level. Venous pressure in the head and orbit rises with any head-down tilt and with abdominal compression, the face and airway accumulate oedema in proportion to time and crystalloid, and intraocular pressure rises. This is the background to the visual loss question below. The positioning checklist (eyes, tube, arms, pressure points) is in positioning.

Postoperative visual loss

Two different injuries share the name.

Central retinal artery occlusion is caused by pressure on the globe from a headrest, or occasionally by embolus. It is unilateral, the eye is often red, swollen or bruised, and it is prevented by keeping everything off the eye and checking that it stays off.

Ischaemic optic neuropathy is the one that matters after long spinal operations. In the ASA registry it accounted for 83 of 93 cases. The patients were not old or vasculopathic: mean age 50 and mostly healthy. The operations were long and bloody: mean anaesthetic duration 9.8 hours, median blood loss 2 L, and 96% had either six hours of anaesthesia or a litre of blood loss. It was bilateral in 55 of the 83, and 16 had the head held in pins with nothing touching the face, which rules out eye pressure as the cause. The patient wakes with painless visual loss, often complete, and there is no effective treatment.

The case-control study that followed, 80 cases against 315 matched spinal fusion controls, identified six independent risk factors:

Risk factor Odds ratio
Wilson frame 4.30
Obesity 2.83
Male sex 2.53
Anaesthesia duration, per hour 1.39
Estimated blood loss, per litre 1.34
Colloid as a share of non-blood fluid, per 5% 0.67 (protective)

The list points to a mechanism. The Wilson frame puts the head below the heart; obesity raises abdominal and therefore venous pressure when prone; long duration and large crystalloid volumes with little colloid produce interstitial oedema. The leading hypothesis is that raised venous pressure and oedema in the optic nerve, a structure in a tight sheath with a marginal blood supply, produce something like a compartment syndrome. Hypotension and anaemia, the factors most people would guess, did not emerge as independent predictors, though no one concludes from that that they are safe.

The ASA practice advisory converts this into practice, and it is consensus built on these observational data:

  1. Identify the high-risk case beforehand: prone surgery expected to be prolonged, with substantial blood loss, or both. Tell the patient that there is a small risk of visual loss. This is one of the few rare complications that belongs in every consent for long prone surgery.
  2. Position with the head neutral and level with or above the heart: slight reverse Trendelenburg, and a frame that does not drop the head. No pressure on the globe, checked and documented.
  3. Monitor blood pressure continuously in high-risk cases and avoid deliberate hypotension unless there is a specific reason for it.
  4. Use colloid along with crystalloid when replacing large losses, and limit crystalloid.
  5. Monitor haemoglobin periodically; no transfusion threshold has been shown to prevent the injury.
  6. Consider staging very long procedures.
  7. Check vision as soon as the patient is alert. If there is any loss, obtain urgent ophthalmology review, correct haemoglobin, pressure and oxygenation, and image to exclude other causes.

The anaesthetist’s leverage is on four of the six risk factors: the frame, the head height, the fluid, and (through staging and efficiency) the duration. The relevant operations are adult deformity correction, multilevel posterior lumbar fusion, posterior cervical fusion and long tumour and trauma cases.

Neuromonitoring

Monitoring exists because the surgeon cannot see cord function. Distraction, derotation, an osteotomy closing, a misplaced screw or a fall in perfusion can each stop conduction while the anatomy looks fine, and for a period of minutes the change is reversible.

Modality Pathway tested Sensitivity to anaesthesia What it detects
SSEP Peripheral nerve, dorsal columns, sensory cortex Volatile agents depress cortical responses in a dose-dependent way; relaxants help Dorsal cord, global perfusion, and limb positioning
Transcranial MEP Motor cortex, corticospinal tract, anterior horn, muscle Very sensitive to volatile agents and abolished by neuromuscular blockade Anterior cord and motor pathway; the modality that predicts motor outcome
Free-running EMG Nerve root to muscle Needs no relaxant Root irritation in real time
Triggered EMG Pedicle screw to root to muscle Needs no relaxant A screw that has breached the pedicle wall

SSEPs are averaged over hundreds of stimuli, so they report with a delay of a minute or more and test only the sensory half of the cord; a pure anterior cord injury can leave them intact. MEPs test the motor pathway directly and respond within seconds, and they are the modality the anaesthetic most easily destroys.

The anaesthetic that permits it

Responses that cross synapses are the vulnerable ones: the cortical SSEP and the muscle MEP both depend on synaptic transmission that inhalational agents suppress. The ASNM position statement is that intravenous anaesthesia with propofol and an opioid is optimal for muscle MEPs. The working recipe:

  1. Propofol and remifentanil by infusion, with processed EEG to keep the propofol dose no higher than it needs to be, because very deep propofol anaesthesia also suppresses MEPs.
  2. No volatile agent, or at most a low, constant fraction (under 0.5 MAC) agreed with the neurophysiologist where baseline signals are strong. No nitrous oxide.
  3. One intubating dose of relaxant and no more. Baselines are recorded once the train-of-four has returned; reversal with sugammadex is reasonable if the surgeon is ready before the relaxant has worn off.
  4. A soft bite block between the molars on both sides before the first stimulus. Transcranial stimulation activates the jaw muscles directly and tongue laceration is the commonest injury of the technique.
  5. Steady state. The neurophysiologist is comparing each response with a baseline, so a bolus of propofol, a change in infusion rate or a new drug is announced before it is given. Ketamine, lidocaine and low-dose dexmedetomidine infusions are compatible if started early and kept constant.
  6. A stable physiological milieu. Evoked potentials deteriorate with hypotension, hypothermia, anaemia and hypocapnia, so mean pressure, temperature, haemoglobin and carbon dioxide are kept where they were at baseline.

For lateral lumbar fusion, where the retractor passes through psoas among the roots of the lumbar plexus, EMG is the essential modality and any residual relaxant defeats it.

Responding to a signal change

A loss of MEPs, or a fall in SSEP amplitude of about half, is a shared emergency with a short window. The consensus checklist developed for deformity surgery organises the response under five headings, and the anaesthetist owns the second.

 1. GAIN CONTROL OF THE ROOM
    stop the operation, announce the change, quiet, call senior help

 2. ANAESTHETIC AND SYSTEMIC          <-- yours
    raise mean arterial pressure (above 80-90 mmHg)
    check haemoglobin, transfuse if low
    normalise temperature, CO2 and pH
    review the last 10 minutes: bolus, rate change, volatile, relaxant
    check train-of-four

 3. TECHNICAL
    electrodes, connections, stimulus; neck and limb position

 4. SURGICAL
    what was done just before; release distraction or correction,
    remove the rod, check screws, look for compression, irrigate warm

 5. IF NO RECOVERY
    wake-up test, further release, imaging, stage the operation

Raising the pressure is the single most effective anaesthetic intervention, because a cord under traction has a higher perfusion requirement than a cord at rest; it is done at once and not after the technical checks. An isolated upper limb SSEP change is a positioning problem until proved otherwise: in 1,000 monitored operations 92% of such changes reversed when the arm was moved.

The wake-up test remains the definitive check when signals do not return: the infusions are stopped, the patient is asked to move the feet, and anaesthesia is resumed. A propofol-remifentanil technique makes it possible within minutes. Mention it to the patient beforehand in any deformity case.

Blood loss and tranexamic acid

Spinal bleeding comes from decorticated bone, epidural veins and stripped muscle, and it scales with the number of levels and with osteotomies. A single-level decompression loses very little; a one- or two-level fusion a few hundred millilitres; a long deformity correction with a three-column osteotomy one to three litres or more; a vascular metastasis an unpredictable amount.

The measures, in order of effect: a free abdomen and a level or head-up table; tranexamic acid; normothermia; cell salvage for long constructs; and early attention to fibrinogen when the loss passes a blood volume. Deliberate hypotension is not on the list. It reduces arterial bleeding in a field where the bleeding is venous and osseous, and it does so at the expense of cord and optic nerve perfusion in the two situations where those are most at risk.

Tranexamic acid has consistent evidence in spine surgery. The meta-analysis of 11 trials found about 200 mL less total blood loss and a third fewer patients transfused. The multicentre placebo-controlled trial in fusions of more than three segments found significantly less intraoperative and total bleeding, with transfusion avoided in 48% against 33% and a mean of 0.85 against 1.42 units, differences that sat on the edge of significance in a trial of 95 patients. No thrombotic signal has appeared. Published regimens for multilevel surgery use a loading dose of 10-30 mg/kg followed by an infusion of 1-10 mg/kg/h; a moderate regimen (10-15 mg/kg then 1-2 mg/kg/h) is the defensible default, and a single 1 g IV dose covers one- and two-level surgery. The wider picture is in blood management.

Anterior lumbar exposure is a different risk: the loss is small until an iliac vein tears, and then it is very fast. Large-bore access and blood available are the preparation for anterior lumbar fusion, and a pulse oximeter on the left foot shows when the retractor is occluding the left iliac artery.

The airway in cervical disease

Before the operation

The patient for ACDF or posterior cervical surgery may have a cord that is already compressed, and extension narrows the canal further. Three findings decide the plan: whether the spine is unstable (trauma, rheumatoid atlanto-axial disease, tumour); whether there is myelopathy, and whether symptoms appear when the patient extends or flexes the neck in clinic; and whether the airway is otherwise difficult (a fixed flexed neck, previous fusion, a halo).

No trial shows that one intubation technique gives better neurological outcomes than another. What the techniques share when they are done well is what matters: the neck is not moved beyond the range the patient tolerated awake, and the blood pressure does not fall. For most patients with stenosis and a stable spine, videolaryngoscopy with the head neutral and manual in-line stabilisation is appropriate. Awake tracheal intubation is the choice when instability is combined with a difficult airway, when symptoms are provoked by small movements, or when the surgeon wants a neurological examination after intubation and positioning. In severe myelopathy cord autoregulation is impaired, so induction hypotension is treated as seriously as neck movement, and a mean pressure at or above the patient’s baseline is maintained from induction to decompression. Rheumatoid and ankylosing spondylitis necks are covered in the orthopaedic patient.

After anterior cervical surgery

The surgeon retracts the trachea, oesophagus and recurrent laryngeal nerve to one side for the length of the operation, and the tissues respond like any tissue under a retractor. In 311 anterior cervical operations, 6.1% had an airway complication and 1.9% needed reintubation, with one death. The timing is the lesson: symptoms developed on average 36 hours after surgery, and all but two cases were due to pharyngeal oedema, not haematoma. The associations were exposure of more than three vertebral bodies, exposure involving C2, C3 or C4, blood loss over 300 mL and operating time over five hours.

So there are two different airway emergencies. Haematoma is early, within the first hours, with a tense, swelling neck: the wound is opened at the bedside to release it before or while the airway is secured, and the tray and the instruction to do so are at the bedside in recovery. Oedema is late, on the first or second night, with a changing voice, difficulty swallowing saliva and a patient who wants to sit forward; it is managed by early senior review, nebulised adrenaline and steroid as temporising measures, and reintubation before obstruction is complete, by the most experienced person available with a plan for a front-of-neck airway.

For the long, multilevel or high cervical case, and for combined anterior and posterior surgery, the defensible default is to leave the tube in until a cuff leak is present and the patient is awake, extubate over an exchange catheter, and nurse in a monitored bed for the first night. Dexamethasone is commonly given; evidence that it prevents airway compromise is limited. Hoarseness after surgery is usually a recurrent laryngeal neurapraxia; reducing the cuff pressure after the retractors are placed is a simple measure many units use, and a patient with previous surgery on the opposite side needs the cords examined beforehand.

Analgesia after fusion and intrathecal morphine

Fusion is a pain score 4 operation and deformity correction a 5: decorticated bone, stripped muscle over many segments, and patients who are frequently opioid-tolerant before they start. PROSPECT for complex spine surgery recommends paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, intraoperative ketamine and epidural analgesia, with opioids as rescue. It found only limited evidence for intrathecal opioid, wound infiltration, erector spinae and thoracolumbar interfascial plane blocks, lidocaine, dexmedetomidine and gabapentinoids, and did not recommend methadone despite trial evidence in its favour, because the comparator was a short-acting opioid and the safety margin is narrow.

Intrathecal morphine is therefore an option and not a guideline recommendation, and it is widely used because it is simple: the dura is in the field, and the surgeon can inject under direct vision before closure, or the anaesthetist can inject before induction. In the meta-analysis of eight trials it reduced opioid consumption and pain scores for the first 24 hours, with more pruritus and no difference in nausea, sedation or length of stay; respiratory depression occurred only in the morphine groups, too rarely to reach significance. The reading: a real benefit confined to day 0 and the first night.

Practical points. Doses in the trials varied widely; 100-200 micrograms is the range in current adult practice, with the lower end for the elderly and for sleep apnoea. It does not interfere with neuromonitoring. It requires sedation and respiratory monitoring for 24 hours and no concurrent sedatives or long-acting opioid beyond the patient’s established baseline. And it stops at about 24 hours, so the rest of the plan has to be running by then: regular paracetamol, an NSAID where the surgeon accepts one (PROSPECT recommends them; some surgeons restrict them over concern about fusion), and ketamine, which in opioid-dependent patients having lumbar surgery reduced morphine use at 24 hours, 48 hours and six weeks. The opioid-tolerant fusion patient is planned as in opioid-tolerant and chronic pain patients.

Edge cases

Acute spinal cord injury. Cord perfusion depends on pressure; maintain a mean of 85-90 mmHg through fracture fixation as far as bleeding allows, expect neurogenic hypotension and bradycardia with lesions above T6, and avoid suxamethonium from about 48-72 hours after injury because of hyperkalaemia.

Vertebroplasty and cemented screws. Cement can leak into the vertebral veins and the canal; the embolic physiology is that of bone cement implantation syndrome, and a new deficit on waking needs immediate imaging.

The stimulator or pump implant. Spinal cord stimulator paddle placement may need the patient to report paraesthesia coverage, which sets the depth of sedation.

Monitoring that never had baselines. In severe myelopathy, neuropathy or obesity, signals may be absent from the start. Establish that before the incision so that their absence later is not misread, and agree whether a wake-up test will be the check.

The prone cardiac arrest. Compressions can begin prone over the thoracic spine while the wound is covered and a trolley is brought. Defibrillation pads placed before draping in the high-risk patient save minutes.
