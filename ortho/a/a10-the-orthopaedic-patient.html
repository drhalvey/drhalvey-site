The orthopaedic patient: frailty, hip fracture pathways, rheumatoid and ankylosing spondylitis, obesity

Five patient phenotypes recur across the orthopaedic list and each breaks the standard plan in a predictable place. The frail older patient dies or loses independence according to frailty, delirium and time to theatre, not anaesthetic technique, so the pathway (block in the emergency department, surgery within 36 hours, orthogeriatric care, day 1 mobilisation) is the treatment. Rheumatoid arthritis is an airway and a cervical spine problem before it is a joint problem. Ankylosing spondylitis is a fixed neck and a difficult neuraxial space. Obesity changes positioning, dosing, block technique and the day-case question. The young trauma patient is the one in whom compartment syndrome and fat embolism are real.

The frail older patient

Frailty is the loss of physiological reserve across several systems at once, so that a stressor of fixed size (a fracture, an anaesthetic, a night of hypotension) produces a disproportionate and often irreversible decline. It is not age, and it is not comorbidity, although both track with it. The Clinical Frailty Scale was built to make it measurable in a clinic in under a minute: a 9-point picture scale from very fit to terminally ill, in which each one-category increase raised the medium-term risk of death by about a fifth and of institutional care by about a quarter in the original cohort, independent of age, and outperformed measures of cognition, function and comorbidity. In hip fracture it predicts what the anaesthetist is asked to predict: in a meta-analysis of 11 studies and 45,979 patients, frailty carried an odds ratio of 1.46 for complications, 1.68 for inpatient death, 1.46 for death at six months and 2.24 for death at a year or more.

The scale is worth using because it changes behaviour. A CFS of 2 having a hemiarthroplasty is a 70-year-old who will walk out; a CFS of 7 having the same operation is someone for whom the operation is analgesia and nursing, the goal is comfort and transfer, and the family conversation about what the next year looks like belongs before theatre, not after the delirium. The Australian hip fracture standard asks for frailty, cognition, delirium risk and nutrition to be screened at presentation, and the reason is that these four, not the ASA grade, are what the discharge destination depends on.

Delirium is the complication the anaesthetist is blamed for and can least control. The pooled prevalence after hip fracture is about 23%; dementia (OR 3.42), ASA 4 (OR 4.19), age over 80 and diabetes are the risks that matter, and the anaesthetic technique is not one of them (REGAIN: 20.5% after spinal, 19.7% after general). What does reduce it is the pathway: orthogeriatric care reduced delirium by 19% and in-hospital mortality by 28% across 37 studies, and a peripheral nerve block reduced postoperative delirium (RR 0.68) in a meta-analysis of 12 trials in patients without pre-existing cognitive impairment, which is the one anaesthetic intervention with a delirium signal and the reason the fascia iliaca block belongs in the emergency department rather than the anaesthetic room. Beyond that the measures are old-fashioned: glasses and hearing aids on, a clock and a window, a family member, no anticholinergics, no benzodiazepines, a bladder that is empty and a bowel that moves, oxygen saturation that is normal, pain treated with paracetamol and a block rather than a morphine infusion, and a night that is dark and quiet.

Cognitive assessment at presentation is a baseline, not a diagnosis. A 4AT or an abbreviated mental test on arrival lets the ward recognise the hypoactive delirium that is otherwise charted as “quiet” and lets the anaesthetist know whether the patient in front of them has capacity for the consent conversation, which the Association of Anaesthetists’ dementia guideline sets out in detail. The practical trap is the patient who scores badly because of pain, opioid or the ambulance ride and is then labelled demented; score again after the block.

Medication reconciliation is where the avoidable harm sits. The hip fracture patient arrives on an anticoagulant, an antiplatelet, an antihypertensive, a diuretic, a sulfonylurea, a benzodiazepine and a statin, and each has a decision attached. The anticoagulant decides the anaesthetic technique rather than the timing (see the neuraxial chapter); the antihypertensive and diuretic are held on the morning of surgery in a patient who has been lying on a floor; the sulfonylurea is held because the patient is fasting; the benzodiazepine is continued because withdrawal is a delirium; the anticholinergics for the bladder are stopped. A pharmacist on the orthogeriatric round does this better than the anaesthetist at 07:00, which is part of why orthogeriatric care works.

The time-to-surgery target is the pathway’s hard number. The 2023 Australian standard moved it from 48 to 36 hours from first presentation (from the fracture itself for an inpatient fall), with the explicit instruction that correctable problems (anaemia, anticoagulation, electrolytes, uncontrolled diabetes or heart failure) are optimised promptly rather than used as reasons to wait, and that transfer from a remote site is a reason for delay only in the sense that surgery still happens as soon as possible. In 2022, before the change, 78% of Australian patients had surgery within 48 hours and the hospital averages ranged from 16 to 92 hours; only 45% walked on day 1. HIP ATTACK, which randomised 2,970 patients to surgery at a median of 6 hours against 24 hours, found no difference in mortality (9% versus 10%) or major complications (22% versus 22%), which tells you that heroic acceleration adds nothing and that the harm sits in the long tail beyond a day or two, not in the difference between tonight and tomorrow morning. What justifies delay is a short list: a condition that will be materially better in 24 hours and is dangerous now (decompensated heart failure, a potassium of 6.5, a haemoglobin of 70 g/L with active bleeding, sepsis, an INR above 1.5 that vitamin K will correct), and a decision, made with the patient, to palliate. An echocardiogram for a murmur in a patient who was walking last week is not on the list; the Association of Anaesthetists’ guideline names echocardiography as an area of controversy precisely because it so often delays rather than changes management. The author’s rule is that a hip fracture is never delayed for an investigation that would not change the operation, and that “waiting for the anticoagulant to wear off” means a general anaesthetic tonight, not a spinal in three days.

The anaesthetic itself is covered on the neck of femur card. The chapter-level points are three. The block before surgery is the intervention with the best evidence for both pain and delirium. Intraoperative hypotension is associated with death and should be prevented with a low spinal dose and a vasopressor started early rather than treated after the event. And the hemiarthroplasty that is cemented in a frail patient is the setting for bone cement implantation syndrome, which has its own chapter.

Rheumatoid arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis presents to the orthopaedic list as a patient needing a joint, but it is a systemic disease of synovium, and synovium lines joints the anaesthetist uses. The cervical spine is the first. Atlantoaxial subluxation (anterior, from erosion of the transverse ligament; vertical, from erosion of the lateral masses letting the odontoid rise into the foramen magnum; subaxial, from facet and disc destruction below C2) was present in 42% of 220 outpatients in a 2014 Japanese series despite modern treatment, associated with disease of more than 10 years, advanced radiographic stage and multiple narrowed disc spaces, and neck pain was associated only with the vertical form. The relevant consequence is that a third or more of long-standing rheumatoid patients have an unstable neck and most of them do not know it. The practical rule is that any rheumatoid patient with disease over 10 years, erosive disease, neck pain, neurological symptoms or a planned airway intervention has flexion and extension lateral cervical radiographs before an elective arthroplasty, and that a patient in whom they cannot be obtained in time is managed as unstable: no extension at laryngoscopy, a videolaryngoscope or awake fibreoptic intubation, manual in-line stabilisation, and a neutral neck in the head holder for shoulder surgery. The beach chair position for a rheumatoid shoulder is the case where this matters most, because the head is fixed and out of sight for two hours.

Below the neck the airway is still rheumatoid. The temporomandibular joints limit mouth opening, the cricoarytenoid joints can be fixed by arthritis so that the glottis is narrow and the voice hoarse, and a tube that passed easily can leave a patient with stridor after extubation because the arytenoids have been pushed against a joint that does not move; the case reports of acute airway obstruction from cricoarytenoid arthritis are rare but recurrent. A hoarse voice, dyspnoea on exertion or stridor on a rheumatoid patient’s history earns a smaller tube, a gentle laryngoscopy, and a plan for extubation that assumes swelling. Supraglottic airways and regional techniques avoid most of this, and a spinal for a rheumatoid knee is a good anaesthetic.

Steroid cover has changed. The 2022 American College of Rheumatology and AAHKS guideline for hip and knee arthroplasty recommends continuing the patient’s usual daily glucocorticoid dose through surgery rather than giving supraphysiological stress doses, on the grounds that the evidence for stress dosing is weak and the harms (infection, hyperglycaemia, poor wound healing) are not. The author’s practice follows it: the morning prednisolone is given, hydrocortisone is available for unexplained hypotension, and the patient on a dose above about 20 mg of prednisolone equivalent or with a known adrenal problem gets a specific plan agreed with their rheumatologist. The same guideline continues methotrexate, sulfasalazine, hydroxychloroquine and leflunomide through surgery, withholds biologics by scheduling the operation at the end of the dosing interval (a drug given four-weekly is skipped and surgery falls in the fifth week), restarts them when the wound is healing at about 14 days, and withholds JAK inhibitors for a short interval before surgery. The infection risk from a biologic is real and is the surgeon’s and rheumatologist’s problem to balance against a flare; the anaesthetist’s job is to notice that the dose timing was not planned and to ask before the patient is asleep.

Anaemia in rheumatoid arthritis is the anaemia of chronic disease with iron deficiency from NSAIDs layered on top, and it is common enough that every rheumatoid arthroplasty patient has a ferritin and transferrin saturation in the pre-admission clinic; the chronic disease component does not respond to iron and the deficiency component does. The rheumatoid patient also bleeds more than their haemoglobin suggests because the tissues are friable and the surgery is often revision or complex, so tranexamic acid and a group and hold are routine. Positioning is a separate problem: a patient with fixed, deformed and painful joints cannot be put into the position the surgeon wants without first finding out, awake, which positions they can tolerate, and a joint subluxed by a careless transfer is a real complication.

Ankylosing spondylitis

Ankylosing spondylitis fuses the axial skeleton from the sacroiliac joints upward into a rigid kyphotic column, and the anaesthetic problems follow from that single fact. The cervical spine is fixed, often in flexion, so that the patient cannot look up, cannot extend the neck for laryngoscopy and may not be able to lie flat without a stack of pillows; the fused spine is also brittle and fractures with little force, so forcing extension to get a view risks a cervical fracture with cord injury. The airway plan is decided from the chair in the pre-admission clinic: if the patient can be positioned with a videolaryngoscope and has adequate mouth opening, that is reasonable with a fibreoptic scope in the room; if the chin is on the chest, an awake fibreoptic intubation is the default and the chapter will not pretend otherwise. The temporomandibular joints are involved in a minority and add trismus to the list.

Neuraxial anaesthesia is difficult and not impossible. The interspinous ligaments ossify and the interlaminar spaces close, so the midline approach meets bone. In a 10-year series of 82 procedures in 51 patients, spinal anaesthesia succeeded in 10 of 13 attempts and the epidural failed in all three, which is the usual experience: a spinal through a paramedian or lateral approach, aiming through the gap that remains at the lower lumbar levels, often works; an epidural catheter rarely threads and the spread when it does is unpredictable because the ossified ligamentum flavum and the epidural fat behave differently. Ultrasound finds the interspace that is still open. A spinal placed is a good anaesthetic for a hip replacement in a patient whose airway is hard; a failed spinal in a patient whose airway is hard is the scenario that needs a plan before the needle, which is to say that the fibreoptic scope and a second anaesthetist are in the room for the spinal, not called for afterwards.

The chest wall is fused and the vital capacity reduced, so ventilation is diaphragmatic and the patient tolerates the prone and lateral positions poorly; apical fibrosis is a late complication. The heart is involved in a minority: aortic root dilatation and aortic regurgitation, and conduction defects, are the classic associations and are looked for with an echocardiogram in a patient with a murmur or with long-standing disease, because aortic regurgitation changes the pressure targets (keep the heart rate up, avoid a slow full ventricle) and conduction disease occasionally needs pacing. Hip replacement in ankylosing spondylitis is also one of the operations with heterotopic ossification as a specific risk, which the surgeon may address with a single dose of radiotherapy or an NSAID course, and the surgeon’s view on NSAIDs should be known before the anaesthetist writes them up.

Positioning a fused kyphotic spine on a flat table breaks it. The patient’s own posture is reproduced with pillows and gel under the head and the knees, the table is broken rather than the patient, and the operative position for a hip is confirmed with the patient awake if there is any doubt. A fracture of a fused spine is a catastrophic injury and the mechanism is usually a transfer.

Obesity

Obesity changes four things on the orthopaedic list: the position, the block, the dose and the discharge. The position first. A patient of 140 kg in the lateral decubitus for a hip has a dependent arm, a dependent peroneal nerve and a brachial plexus that each carry more weight than the padding was designed for; the beach chair sits the abdomen against the diaphragm; the prone position for spine surgery has the whole weight on the chest and the abdomen compressed against the frame unless the bolsters leave it free, which is also the condition for keeping the epidural veins empty. The table weight limit is a real number. Pressure areas, brachial plexus traction and compartment syndrome of the dependent limb after long cases have all been reported in obese patients and the author’s practice is a padding and position check at the start, at an hour and whenever the table moves.

Blocks are harder and more valuable. The ultrasound image is deep and poor, the needle long, and the time taken longer; the supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block and the adductor canal are the lower limb blocks that stay feasible at the extremes of habitus, the interscalene remains feasible for the shoulder but the phrenic palsy is tolerated worse, so a superior trunk approach is preferred. A spinal is harder to place (sit the patient up, ultrasound the midline, use a long needle) and the block height is less predictable because the cerebrospinal fluid volume is reduced, which argues for a smaller dose rather than a larger one. The dose question extends to everything: local anaesthetic maximum doses are calculated on lean or adjusted body weight, not actual weight, which matters when the surgeon’s infiltration and the block together approach the ceiling; antibiotics scale up (cefazolin 3 g at or above 120 kg); opioids scale on lean mass; propofol induction on lean mass and infusion on adjusted mass; suxamethonium on actual weight.

Sleep apnoea is present in a large fraction of arthroplasty patients and undiagnosed in most of them. The Society of Anesthesia and Sleep Medicine guideline’s position is that a patient who screens positive (STOP-Bang 3 or more, with higher scores more specific) is managed as if they have it rather than cancelled for a sleep study: regional anaesthesia where possible, opioid-sparing analgesia, continuation of CPAP where the patient uses it, extubation awake and sitting up, and postoperative monitoring proportionate to the opioid given. For the day-case question this is the deciding factor: treated apnoea with a device and an opioid-sparing plan is compatible with same-day discharge, untreated apnoea with an expected opioid requirement is not. Body mass index on its own was not an independent predictor of same-day discharge failure in the pooled data; ASA grade, which captures the metabolic consequences of obesity rather than the weight, was. The author’s practice is to use a BMI of about 40 as the point above which the day-case pathway needs an individual decision rather than a default, and to let the airway, the apnoea and the diabetes decide rather than the number.

Infection and wound complications rise with obesity and are the surgeon’s reason for a BMI threshold; the anaesthetist’s contribution is the antibiotic dose, the glucose, the temperature and the tissue oxygenation. Venous thromboembolism risk rises too, and the chemoprophylaxis dose is weight adjusted.

The young trauma patient

The young patient with a long bone fracture is the orthopaedic patient in whom the complications that textbooks dwell on actually occur. Compartment syndrome is a disease of young men: in 164 cases from a single centre, 69% followed a fracture, half of those were tibial shaft, most patients were men under 35, and one in ten had a bleeding disorder or was anticoagulated. The reason is physiological. Young muscle is bulky, the fascial compartments are tight, the bleeding into them after a high-energy fracture is brisk, and the patient’s own blood pressure keeps perfusing the compartment and raising its pressure until it exceeds venous and then capillary pressure. The anaesthetic consequence is the subject of the nerve injury and compartment syndrome chapter: a regional technique in a tibial shaft fracture, a tibial plateau fracture, a forearm fracture or a crush is a decision taken with the surgeon, with a low-concentration local anaesthetic, no adjuncts that prolong it, hourly observation, and a pressure monitor where the risk is high.

Fat embolism is the other young-patient complication: it follows long bone and pelvic fractures and reamed nailing, and the patient who is hypoxic, confused and petechial 24 to 72 hours after a femoral fracture has it until proven otherwise (chapter). Early fixation reduces it, which is one reason the young femoral shaft is nailed on the day of injury if the physiology allows (polytrauma sequencing).

Three other things are specific to this patient. The first is that they are often intoxicated, often have a full stomach, and often have a second injury that the fracture distracts from; a rapid sequence induction and a tertiary survey are the defaults. The second is that their pain is severe, their opioid requirement high and their risk of persistent opioid use after discharge real, so the regional technique is doing more work than it would in an older patient, and the discharge prescription is written with an end date. The third is that the operation they are having is often the first of several (external fixation, then definitive fixation, then soft tissue cover, then removal of metalwork), and a patient who has a bad experience of the first anaesthetic is a difficult patient for the next three. The young trauma patient is where the catheter techniques earn their place on duration grounds, and where the block that is withheld for compartment syndrome reasons must be replaced by something, usually a ketamine infusion and a PCA, rather than by nothing.

Edge cases

  • The frail patient with a periprosthetic fracture. Same pathway, longer operation, more blood; the 36-hour target applies and is met less often because the surgeon with the implants is not always available. The fracture is covered on its card.
  • The rheumatoid patient on a JAK inhibitor and prednisolone having a revision shoulder in beach chair. Every problem in this chapter at once: unstable neck in a head holder, a hoarse voice, a continued steroid, a withheld biologic and a long case. Awake fibreoptic intubation, neutral neck, a tube one size smaller, an arterial line, and a planned extubation.
  • The ankylosing spondylitis patient for a spinal fracture. A fused spine that has fractured is unstable through the fracture and nowhere else; the patient is moved as a single unit and positioned in their own kyphosis, and the airway is the one described above.
  • The obese patient on a traction table for a femoral nail. The perineal post and the pudendal nerve, the weight on the contralateral leg and the well-leg compartment syndrome after a long case are the risks; the positioning chapter (a06) covers them.
  • The young patient with a tibial plateau fracture who asks for a block. The honest answer is in the compartment syndrome chapter: sometimes, with conditions, and only after the surgeon has said yes.

Key sources

The frailty literature and the hip fracture pathway standards are consensus built on consistent observational data, and the two randomised trials that bear on the pathway (REGAIN for technique and HIP ATTACK for extreme acceleration) are both negative, which is itself the finding. The rheumatoid cervical spine prevalence is from a single modern series and the older literature gave higher numbers; the airway advice is standard practice. The steroid and biologic advice is from the 2022 ACR/AAHKS guideline and is a change from older teaching. The ankylosing spondylitis neuraxial data are from a small 1996 series, which remains the best there is. The obesity dosing rules are consensus pharmacology. Where the chapter says “the author’s practice”, the evidence stops there.
