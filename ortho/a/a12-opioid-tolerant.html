Opioid-tolerant and chronic pain patients

The opioid-tolerant patient has three separate problems that are easy to merge into one: a baseline requirement that must be supplied or withdrawal follows, a shifted dose-response so that acute doses need to be larger, and a sensitised nervous system that makes the same operation hurt more. The plan is built before admission with the pain service and the usual prescriber: verify and continue the baseline including methadone and buprenorphine, put the weight of the analgesia on techniques that do not act at the opioid receptor (a block or catheter, ketamine, an NSAID), size the rescue dose from the patient's own daily total, and watch sedation, because tolerance to respiratory depression is less dependable than tolerance to analgesia. Buprenorphine should not be routinely stopped. About a quarter of arthroplasty patients take opioids beforehand and do worse in absolute terms, but they still benefit from the operation, and those who taper before surgery do as well as patients who never took them. The discharge plan names a date for return to baseline and a single prescriber; pain catastrophising identifies who will struggle but has not been shown to be fixed by pre-operative coping-skills training.

Three problems, not one

A patient who has taken opioids every day for months arrives with three distinct changes, and each needs its own answer.

Dependence. The nervous system has adapted to the drug being present. Remove it and a withdrawal syndrome begins within 6-24 hours for short-acting opioids and later for methadone and buprenorphine: yawning, sweating, gooseflesh, restlessness, abdominal cramps, diarrhoea, tachycardia, hypertension and pain everywhere. In a surgical ward this is misread as uncontrolled surgical pain, anxiety or delirium. The answer is to supply the baseline, every day, by some route.

Tolerance. Receptor desensitisation and downstream adaptation shift the dose-response curve to the right. A dose that would settle an opioid-naive patient does little. The answer is larger acute doses, calculated from what the patient already takes, on top of the baseline and not instead of it. Tolerance is uneven: it develops fully to nausea and largely to analgesia and sedation, but tolerance to respiratory depression is incomplete and unreliable, and it does not protect against the additive effect of benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids and alcohol. Large doses in a tolerant patient are necessary and are still dangerous.

Sensitisation. Long-term opioid exposure and chronic pain both prime the dorsal horn: NMDA receptor activity is up, descending inhibition is down, and the same incision produces more pain for longer. Opioid-induced hyperalgesia is the name when the opioid is the cause. More opioid does not fix it and may worsen it. The answer is analgesia that does not go through the opioid receptor: a block that stops the input arriving, ketamine at the NMDA receptor, an NSAID at the wound.

  Problem          What it looks like             Answer
  ---------------  -----------------------------  --------------------------------
  Dependence       withdrawal if a dose is missed  continue the baseline, any route
  Tolerance        usual doses do nothing          rescue sized from daily total
  Sensitisation    more pain, wider, longer        block or catheter, ketamine, NSAID

The usual working definition of tolerance is 60 mg or more of oral morphine equivalent a day for a week or more, but the plan is needed from well below that, and the buprenorphine patch and tramadol users in every arthroplasty clinic are on the same spectrum.

Who they are in an orthopaedic list

About a quarter of patients presenting for hip or knee replacement have been prescribed opioids for the joint. In the meta-analysis of six studies they had worse pain and function scores after surgery in absolute terms, with a standardised difference of about half a standard deviation, but their relative improvement was not significantly different: they start worse, improve by a similar amount, and finish worse. They should be counselled, not refused. The modifiable part is the dose. In matched cohorts, chronic opioid users who weaned successfully before arthroplasty improved as much as patients who had never taken opioids, and far more than those who did not wean.

The other groups: spinal surgery patients, in whom long-term opioid use is the norm and the operation is among the most painful; patients having repeated operations for infected joints or bone infection; those on methadone or buprenorphine for opioid use disorder; people with implanted intrathecal pumps; cancer patients with metastatic fractures; and amputees with months of ischaemic pain behind them.

Planning with the pain service

The plan is made at pre-admission, not in the anaesthetic bay.

  1. Establish the real baseline: every opioid, dose, formulation and time of last dose, cross-checked against the dispensing record or real-time prescription monitoring and, for methadone and buprenorphine maintenance, with the prescriber or dispensing pharmacy. Include gabapentinoids, benzodiazepines, antidepressants, cannabis and alcohol.
  2. Decide whether there is time to taper. For elective arthroplasty weeks away, a reduction agreed with the general practitioner is worth attempting, on the cohort evidence above. Do not attempt a taper in the fortnight before surgery.
  3. Set expectations with the patient. Pain scores will be higher than the patient in the next bed. The target is function (coughing, sitting out, physiotherapy), not a number. The baseline will be continued. Extra opioid will be given and then withdrawn on a schedule. Agreeing this before surgery prevents most of the conflict afterwards.
  4. Choose the regional technique and book what it needs: a catheter, an epidural, a monitored bed for a ketamine infusion.
  5. Write the plan where the ward will find it: baseline, adjuncts, rescue dose, escalation, and who prescribes at discharge.
  6. Involve the acute pain service from recovery, with daily review.

Continuing the baseline

Oral modified-release opioids are taken as usual on the morning of surgery with a sip of water and restarted at the next due time. If the gut will not be available, the baseline is converted to an intravenous equivalent and given as the background of a PCA or a separate infusion. Conversion ratios are approximations: oral morphine 30 mg is about 10 mg intravenous morphine, oral oxycodone 20 mg is about oral morphine 30 mg, and a calculator is preferable to memory. When rotating to a different opioid, reduce the calculated dose by a quarter to a half for incomplete cross-tolerance; when continuing the same opioid, do not.

Transdermal fentanyl and buprenorphine patches stay on. Note the site, keep forced-air warming and heating pads off a fentanyl patch (heat increases absorption), and check it is still attached after positioning and skin preparation.

Intrathecal pumps keep running. Record the drug, the concentration, the daily dose and the pump site; keep the pump and catheter out of the surgical field and the diathermy path; and remember that the intrathecal dose does not prevent systemic withdrawal if the patient’s oral opioids are stopped.

Buprenorphine

Buprenorphine is a partial agonist with very high receptor affinity and a long duration. The old practice of stopping it days before surgery, so that full agonists could work, exchanged a theoretical problem for two real ones: withdrawal and uncontrolled pain in the gap, and relapse or overdose in patients treated for opioid use disorder. The multi-society consensus is unambiguous: do not routinely discontinue buprenorphine perioperatively.

In practice: low-dose patches (5-40 micrograms/h) are continued and ignored for dosing purposes. Sublingual maintenance doses are continued, and dividing the daily dose into three or four parts is a consensus option (low certainty), because analgesia lasts 6-8 hours while withdrawal suppression lasts 24 hours or more. Full agonists still work on top of buprenorphine, at higher doses than usual, and the potent high-affinity agonists (fentanyl, hydromorphone) are the logical choice for PCA. The consensus panel allows a taper to 16 mg to be considered for patients on higher doses facing very painful surgery, in centres with integrated addiction and acute pain services; that is expert opinion and is done with the prescriber, never unilaterally. Depot buprenorphine cannot be withdrawn and is managed the same way: regional technique, ketamine, and titrated full agonist.

Methadone

Maintenance methadone is continued at the verified dose. Verify before charting: the prescriber or pharmacy confirms the dose and the date last dispensed, because a patient who has missed several days has lost tolerance and a full dose can kill. Once-daily dosing suppresses withdrawal for 24 hours but gives only 6-8 hours of analgesia, so splitting the same total into two or three doses helps. If the patient cannot swallow, give it parenterally at about half to two thirds of the oral dose in divided doses. Check the QT interval before adding ondansetron, droperidol or other QT-prolonging drugs.

Methadone is also a useful intraoperative opioid in its own right, for tolerant and non-tolerant patients, because one dose lasts a day or more and it has NMDA antagonist activity. In a blinded trial in posterior spinal fusion, methadone 0.2 mg/kg IV at the start of surgery roughly halved hydromorphone use on day 1 compared with hydromorphone at closure, with lower use on days 2 and 3 and lower pain scores at most assessments. PROSPECT does not recommend it for complex spine surgery, on comparator and safety grounds. Dose on ideal body weight, reduce in the elderly and in sleep apnoea, and make sure the ward knows a long-acting opioid has been given.

Ketamine

Ketamine is the adjunct with direct evidence in this population. In 102 opioid-dependent patients having major lumbar surgery, ketamine 0.5 mg/kg IV at induction followed by 10 micrograms/kg/min until closure reduced morphine consumption at 24 and 48 hours and, unexpectedly, at six weeks, with lower pain intensity at six weeks and no excess of side effects. The mechanism fits the problem: NMDA antagonism opposes both central sensitisation and opioid-induced hyperalgesia.

The ASRA, AAPM and ASA consensus guideline supports subanaesthetic ketamine for acute pain with opioid-tolerant patients and painful surgery as the leading indications, bolus doses up to about 0.35 mg/kg and infusions generally no higher than 1 mg/kg/h outside intensive care. Ward practice is far lower than that ceiling: 0.1-0.2 mg/kg/h (typically 5-15 mg/h) continued for 24-72 hours gives most of the benefit with few psychotomimetic effects. Contraindications are poorly controlled cardiovascular disease, active psychosis, severe liver disease and pregnancy. Vivid dreams and dysphoria are dose-related; reduce the rate before abandoning the drug.

Regional and catheter techniques

A block is the most effective opioid-sparing measure available because it removes the nociceptive input instead of competing with it at a desensitised receptor. Throughout this book the opioid-tolerant patient is the standing indication to move one step up the ladder: from no block to a single shot, from a single shot to a catheter, from a catheter for two days to one for three or four. For knee replacement that means an adductor canal catheter with surgeon infiltration, and a low threshold for adding a sciatic or IPACK component; for cuff repair and shoulder arthroplasty an interscalene catheter; for revision knee and multilevel fusion, epidural or intrathecal opioid as part of the plan. The menu is in the block menu.

Two cautions. A perfect block does not treat withdrawal: the baseline opioid continues regardless. And the block ends: the rebound when a single shot wears off is worse in a sensitised patient, so the oral and ketamine components must already be running.

Intrathecal morphine works in tolerant patients with a blunted effect for a given dose; conventional doses (100-200 micrograms) are still used, the baseline systemic opioid is continued, and monitoring is as for any patient.

The non-opioid base is used at full strength: regular paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor unless truly contraindicated, and dexamethasone. Gabapentinoids are continued if the patient already takes them and are not started for the occasion, because the added sedation and respiratory depression outweigh a small analgesic gain. Clonidine treats the autonomic symptoms of withdrawal and adds modest analgesia.

Calculating the rescue dose

The rescue dose is derived from the patient’s total daily opioid, not from the standard chart.

  Baseline         oxycodone MR 40 mg twice daily = 80 mg oral oxycodone/day
                   = about 120 mg oral morphine equivalent/day
  Continue         40 mg MR twice daily, unchanged
  Breakthrough     one sixth of the daily total: 80 / 6 = about 13 mg
                   -> oxycodone IR 10-15 mg oral, 2-hourly as needed
  If nil by mouth  baseline = about 40 mg IV morphine/24 h (1.5-2 mg/h background)
                   PCA bolus 2-3 mg morphine, 5 min lockout, reassess at 4 h
  Review           total used in 24 h tells you tomorrow's dose

One sixth of the 24-hour total (some services use one tenth) as an immediate-release dose every two to three hours is the conventional breakthrough dose. For PCA, start with a bolus about double the standard and adjust it to the pattern of use: a patient pressing every lockout with good effect each time needs a bigger bolus, not a background. A background infusion is used only to replace a baseline that cannot be given by mouth. Expect total requirements two to three times those of an opioid-naive patient for the same operation, and expect them to fall more slowly.

Monitor sedation, not just respiratory rate. A rising sedation score in a tolerant patient on a high dose is the early sign of opioid-induced ventilatory impairment, and the fix is a dose reduction and a non-opioid, not naloxone by reflex. If naloxone is needed, titrate in 40 microgram increments: a full ampoule precipitates acute withdrawal and severe pain.

Opioid use disorder

On maintenance treatment. Covered above: continue methadone or buprenorphine, verify the dose, add analgesia on top, and tell the patient plainly that maintenance treatment is not analgesia and that their pain will be treated. Fear of being under-treated is the commonest reason these patients discharge themselves.

Untreated, active use. The baseline is unknown and often understated or overstated. Titrate to effect with a PCA, start ketamine, and use the regional technique. Treat withdrawal when it appears. The consensus panel supports starting buprenorphine during the admission in a patient who wants treatment, with the addiction medicine service; an orthopaedic admission is an opportunity as well as a problem.

In recovery and abstinent. The patient may ask for no opioids at all. A regional technique with ketamine and full non-opioid analgesia can achieve that for many operations. Where it cannot, untreated severe pain is itself a relapse risk; a short, supervised course agreed in advance, with the patient’s support person and general practitioner informed, is the defensible position.

On naltrexone. Oral naltrexone is stopped 72 hours before elective surgery. Depot or implanted naltrexone blocks opioid analgesia for weeks: time elective surgery for the end of the dosing interval, plan for regional and non-opioid analgesia, and be aware that opioid given in escalating doses against the blockade becomes an overdose as the blockade fades.

Discharge and weaning

The consensus statement on preventing opioid-related harm sets the general rules: judge analgesia by function, avoid modified-release and transdermal opioids for acute pain, limit the quantity supplied, give explicit instructions for stopping, no automatic repeats, and advice on storage and disposal. For the tolerant patient three additions apply.

Separate the two prescriptions. The baseline continues under its usual prescriber at its usual dose. The acute supplement is an immediate-release opioid with a quantity and an end date, and it is the only thing the hospital prescribes.

Write the reverse taper. The supplement comes down by about a fifth to a quarter of the starting dose every two to three days, reaching zero within one to two weeks after arthroplasty and two to four weeks after major spinal surgery. Put the schedule in the discharge summary and give the patient a copy.

Close the loop. The general practitioner or pain specialist receives the plan on the day of discharge, including the doses, the stop date and a named contact. Where the operation has removed the reason for the opioid (the arthritic hip has been replaced), the letter says so and proposes a taper of the baseline over the following months.

Pain catastrophising

Catastrophising is a measurable pattern of response to pain: rumination, magnification and helplessness. It predicts worse acute pain, higher opioid use and chronic postsurgical pain. In the meta-analysis of 29 studies, pre-operative anxiety or catastrophising raised the odds of chronic postsurgical pain by about 1.5 to 2 times, the association was most consistent in musculoskeletal surgery, and catastrophising predicted better than general anxiety.

The natural next step, treating it before surgery, has been tested and did not work. In 402 patients with high catastrophising scores having knee replacement, eight sessions of pain coping skills training gave no better pain or function at 12 months than arthritis education or usual care; all three groups improved substantially.

The plain reading is that catastrophising is a marker of risk and not, on current evidence, a target that pre-operative psychology removes. Its use is in triage. The high-scoring patient gets the strongest regional plan, an honest account of what to expect, function-based goals, early pain service review, and resistance to chasing pain scores with escalating opioid, which is the path by which such patients leave hospital on more than they came in with. For the patient with established chronic pain and high distress having an operation whose indication is pain alone, the expected gain is weighed with the surgeon against the risk of the roughly one in five who have persistent pain after knee replacement.

Edge cases

The patient who is drowsy and in severe pain. Sedation limits the opioid before pain is controlled. Stop escalating the opioid; add or increase ketamine, re-site or bolus the catheter, check for withdrawal from a missed baseline dose, and look for another cause of pain (compartment syndrome, haematoma, dislocation).

The tolerant patient for day surgery. Take the usual doses, use a long-acting block with dexamethasone IV, and send the patient home on the baseline plus a small, time-limited supplement. Do not convert a stable patient to a new long-acting opioid for a day-case procedure.

High-dose gabapentinoid or benzodiazepine users. Continue them, because withdrawal from either is dangerous, and reduce the opioid supplement accordingly, with sedation scoring at the higher frequency.

Renal impairment. Morphine and oxycodone metabolites accumulate. Fentanyl, hydromorphone (with care), buprenorphine and methadone are the safer choices for baseline and PCA.

The unknown dose at 02:00 for a fracture. Give no more than half the claimed methadone or buprenorphine dose until it is verified, cover the remainder with titrated short-acting opioid, block the limb, and verify in the morning.
