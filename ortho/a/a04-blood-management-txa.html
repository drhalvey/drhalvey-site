Blood management and tranexamic acid

Orthopaedic bleeding comes from cut bone that cannot constrict and from fibrinolysis switched on by the surgery, so it continues for hours after the wound is closed and much of it never reaches the suction. The plan has three parts: arrive with enough red cells (find and treat anaemia and iron deficiency weeks before elective surgery), lose fewer (tranexamic acid for almost everyone, with position, temperature, pressure and cell salvage where the operation is big enough), and tolerate a lower haemoglobin afterwards (a restrictive threshold of about 80 g/L, or symptoms, is as safe as 100 g/L even in elderly hip fracture patients with cardiovascular disease). Tranexamic acid cuts transfusion by about two thirds in arthroplasty and by a third to a half in hip fracture and spine surgery, works by any route, and has not been shown to raise thrombotic risk in more than 125,000 randomised patients. The anticoagulant timing rules for neuraxial and deep blocks are the other half of the same subject, because the drug that prevents the clot is the drug that causes the haematoma.

Why orthopaedic operations bleed the way they do

Soft tissue bleeds from vessels that can be clipped, tied or cauterised, and that constrict when the pressure falls. Bone does neither. A cut cancellous surface is a sponge of venous sinusoids held open by trabeculae: it cannot contract, it cannot be coagulated effectively, and it keeps oozing until clot, cement or an implant seals it. That is why blood loss in this specialty tracks the area of raw bone (reamed acetabulum, osteotomised femoral neck, decorticated transverse processes, a medullary canal opened from end to end) more closely than the length of the incision, and why it continues into the drain, the joint and the thigh for 24 hours after closure.

Three consequences follow. First, a large share of the loss is hidden. After a knee replacement done under tourniquet the suction canister may hold 100 mL while the haemoglobin falls by 30 g/L over two days, because the blood went into the joint and the soft tissues after the cuff came down. A nailed extracapsular hip fracture has bled into the thigh before the patient reaches theatre and bleeds again from the reamed canal. The measured loss is the floor, not the estimate.

Second, surgery and tourniquet ischaemia both switch on fibrinolysis. Tissue plasminogen activator is released from injured endothelium, plasmin dissolves the clot that was plugging the bone surface, and the ooze restarts. This is the target of tranexamic acid, and it explains why an antifibrinolytic works so well in operations where the bleeding surface cannot be controlled mechanically.

Third, venous pressure at the wound matters more than arterial pressure. Cancellous bone and epidural veins bleed at venous pressure, so a prone abdomen that is compressed, a head-down lateral position or a coughing, straining patient raises the loss more than a systolic of 130 mmHg does. Positioning is blood management; the detail is in positioning and spine essentials.

The plan that follows from this has the three pillars of patient blood management: more red cells at the start, fewer lost, and a lower haemoglobin tolerated at the end.

Before surgery: anaemia and iron

Pre-operative anaemia is the strongest predictor of transfusion that anyone can change. A patient who starts a hip revision at 105 g/L has no reserve for a 1.5 L loss; the same patient at 135 g/L will probably leave without a transfusion. The international consensus statement on perioperative anaemia makes the practical points that matter in a pre-admission clinic. Screen early enough to act, which means at listing rather than the week before. Treat a haemoglobin below 130 g/L as anaemia worth investigating in both sexes: the lower “normal” for women leaves them with a smaller red cell mass facing the same blood loss. Iron deficiency is the commonest cause and the most treatable: a ferritin below 30 micrograms/L is absolute deficiency, and in a patient with inflammation (a raised CRP, which includes most patients with inflammatory arthritis or an infected joint) a ferritin below 100 micrograms/L with a transferrin saturation below 20% means iron-restricted erythropoiesis even though the ferritin looks adequate.

Oral iron works if there are six to eight weeks and a gut that tolerates and absorbs it; a low dose on alternate days is absorbed as well as higher daily doses and is better tolerated. Intravenous iron is the choice when surgery is less than six weeks away, when oral iron has failed, or when inflammation is blocking absorption. A single infusion of ferric carboxymaltose or ferric derisomaltose replaces the deficit in one visit and the haemoglobin response is evident from about two weeks. The honest reading of the evidence is that intravenous iron reliably raises haemoglobin and that the benefit for hard outcomes is less certain; the argument for it in elective arthroplasty rests on the size of the transfusion effect and the fact that the operation can wait. Anaemia without iron deficiency needs its cause found (B12, folate, renal disease, marrow, occult gastrointestinal loss) before the joint is replaced, not after.

Elective arthroplasty in an anaemic patient is a postponable operation. Hip fracture surgery is not: there the anaemia is managed around the operation, with tranexamic acid, a restrictive threshold and iron afterwards.

Tranexamic acid

Tranexamic acid is a lysine analogue that occupies the lysine-binding sites on plasminogen and stops it docking onto fibrin. Clot that has formed on the bone surface stays there. It does not make blood clot where it otherwise would not, which is the mechanistic reason the thrombosis signal has been so hard to find.

Arthroplasty

The effect is large and consistent. In the United States cohort of 872,416 hip and knee replacements, transfusion fell from 20.1% to 7.7% with tranexamic acid, an odds ratio of roughly one third, at every dose category, with no increase in thromboembolic events, renal failure or a combined complication outcome. The multi-society arthroplasty guideline, built on a network meta-analysis of the randomised trials, reached four conclusions that have simplified practice: intravenous, topical and oral routes all work; no route is clearly better than another; higher doses and repeat doses add little that can be measured; and for the intravenous route, giving the dose before the incision (or before tourniquet inflation) is favoured.

The defensible default is therefore TXA 1 g IV (or 10-15 mg/kg) before incision. A second 1 g IV at closure or about three hours later is common, reasonable for the longer operations and revisions, and consistent with the POISE-3 regimen, although the arthroplasty network meta-analysis could not show that it adds to the first dose. Oral TXA 2 g about two hours before surgery is equivalent, cheap and awkward only for the logistics.

Topical versus intravenous

Topical tranexamic acid (1-3 g in 30-100 mL of saline, poured into the joint or injected through the drain after closure and left for a few minutes) puts the drug where the bleeding is, with plasma levels a fraction of those after an intravenous dose. In the meta-analysis of 14 randomised trials it cut transfusion as effectively as the intravenous route, with thromboembolic events no different from placebo. Its place is the patient in whom the surgeon or anaesthetist is unwilling to give a systemic dose: a coronary stent placed in the last few months, a recent stroke or pulmonary embolus, a seizure disorder, severe renal impairment. Combined intravenous and topical dosing is widely used; the evidence that it beats either alone is weak.

Does it cause thrombosis

The fear is intuitive and the data do not support it. In the meta-analysis of 216 randomised trials and 125,550 patients across all surgical disciplines, thromboembolic events occurred in 2.1% with intravenous tranexamic acid and 2.0% without, with no increase in venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction or stroke, and no increase in the trials that included patients with previous thromboembolism. POISE-3 is the necessary caveat: in 9,535 non-cardiac surgical patients, tranexamic acid reduced major bleeding (9.1% versus 11.7%) and the composite cardiovascular outcome was 14.2% versus 13.9%, a hazard ratio of 1.02 whose confidence interval ran to 1.14 and so narrowly failed the prespecified non-inferiority margin. The plain reading is that a small excess of arterial or venous events cannot be excluded, that if it exists it is much smaller than the bleeding benefit, and that the patient with a recent thrombotic event is a judgement, not a contraindication. The arthroplasty guideline says the same thing: the trials excluded most high-risk patients, the observational data in those who were given it anyway are reassuring, and the decision in a patient with a recent stent or embolus is individual.

Two harms are real. Seizures are dose-related and were described with the high doses once used in cardiac surgery; tranexamic acid is renally cleared, so reduce the dose in significant renal impairment and avoid repeat dosing in dialysis patients. And tranexamic acid given intrathecally by ampoule confusion with a local anaesthetic causes refractory seizures, ventricular arrhythmia and death. The ampoules can look alike. Keep tranexamic acid off the spinal tray and out of the drawer that holds heavy bupivacaine.

Hip fracture

The patient is older, already anaemic and has bled before theatre. Two meta-analyses of the randomised trials (13 trials and 1,194 patients; 12 trials and 1,397 patients aged over 65) give a transfusion risk ratio of 0.5 to 0.6 with intravenous tranexamic acid and no detectable increase in thromboembolic events, with the honest qualification that events were too few for certainty. The effect was clear in trials using a restrictive threshold and disappeared where patients were transfused at 100 g/L, which is a reminder that tranexamic acid and the transfusion trigger are one decision. The defensible default for neck of femur fracture surgery is TXA 1 g IV at induction (15 mg/kg in the very small patient), with a lower threshold to omit or go topical in the patient whose fracture followed a stroke or infarct.

Spine

Decorticated bone and epidural veins bleed in proportion to the number of levels. In 11 randomised trials tranexamic acid reduced total blood loss by about 200 mL and the proportion transfused by a third, without a thrombosis signal. Dosing in deformity surgery is typically a loading dose followed by an infusion for the length of the operation (10-50 mg/kg then 1-10 mg/kg/h in published protocols); the higher regimens bled less in one retrospective adult comparison, have not been shown to be better in a randomised trial, and carry the seizure risk. For one- and two-level surgery a single 1 g IV dose is enough. More in spine essentials.

Cell salvage

Cell salvage returns washed red cells at a haematocrit of 50-60% with no plasma, platelets or clotting factors. It earns its set-up cost when the expected loss is more than about 500-1,000 mL or when the loss is unpredictable and potentially large: revision hip arthroplasty, pelvic and acetabular fixation, deformity correction, multilevel fusion, periacetabular osteotomy, and any patient who refuses or cannot receive allogeneic blood. In revision hip arthroplasty the meta-analysis found allogeneic transfusion in 44% of patients with salvage and 65% without. In primary hip and knee replacement with tranexamic acid the loss is too small to make salvage worthwhile.

A collect-only set-up (reservoir and anticoagulated suction, with the processing bowl opened only if the reservoir fills) keeps the option cheap. The suction must be diverted to waste whenever the field contains something that should not be reinfused: povidone-iodine or chlorhexidine irrigation, topical haemostatic agents, antibiotic powder, and methylmethacrylate that has not yet cured. Infection and tumour are relative contraindications: for metastatic spine surgery a leucodepletion filter on the return line is the usual compromise. After a large reinfusion the patient has red cells and nothing else, so check coagulation and fibrinogen once the returned volume passes about half a blood volume.

Transfusion thresholds

FOCUS settled the question for the population this book is about. In 2,016 hip fracture patients aged 50 or over with cardiovascular disease or risk factors, transfusing to keep the haemoglobin above 100 g/L was no better than transfusing only below 80 g/L or for symptoms (chest pain, heart failure, tachycardia or hypotension unresponsive to fluid): death or inability to walk across a room at 60 days was 35.2% versus 34.7%, and coronary events and mortality did not differ. The restrictive group received a median of no units.

The working rule is a threshold of 70-80 g/L in a stable patient, 80 g/L in those with ischaemic heart disease, one unit at a time with reassessment, and symptoms overriding the number in either direction. A patient who is dizzy on standing, tachycardic and unable to do physiotherapy at 82 g/L is under-filled or under-transfused and deserves treatment, not a rule. A haemoglobin on day 1 underestimates the fall; the nadir after hip and knee replacement is on day 2 or 3, after the hidden loss has equilibrated. Postoperative intravenous iron is a reasonable alternative to a marginal transfusion in a patient who was iron deficient going in.

Anticoagulants, antiplatelets and the needle

A vertebral canal haematoma needs a needle or catheter and a patient who cannot clot. The ASRA guideline sets an interval for each drug between the last dose and the needle, and between the needle or catheter removal and the next dose. The intervals are built from the drug’s half-life (about five half-lives for full clearance, two for prophylactic doses) and its time to peak (the next dose must not peak while the puncture site is still fresh). The fifth edition keeps the structure and refines it by dose: it separates low (prophylactic) from high (treatment) doses, with shorter intervals for the low doses of the direct oral anticoagulants than the 72 hours required after treatment doses. The intervals in the table below were checked against published summaries of the fourth edition; the fifth edition’s own tables were not available when this chapter was written, so read the low-dose intervals from the guideline itself. Renal impairment lengthens every interval for enoxaparin, dabigatran and, to a lesser degree, the factor Xa inhibitors, and the elderly hip fracture patient on a DOAC with a creatinine clearance of 35 mL/min is the common case in which the interval collides with the 36-hour surgical target. The practical resolution there is a general anaesthetic with a superficial, compressible block (fascia iliaca above the inguinal ligament is compressible; a lumbar plexus block is not) or, where the service measures them, a drug level or anti-Xa assay to show that the drug has gone.

For peripheral blocks the guideline divides by depth. Deep plexus and deep peripheral blocks at non-compressible sites (lumbar plexus, paravertebral, the deeper quadratus lumborum approaches, and by extension infraclavicular) follow the neuraxial intervals, because a haematoma there cannot be seen or pressed on and has caused major blood loss and nerve injury. Superficial blocks at compressible sites (femoral, adductor canal, popliteal, interscalene, axillary) are a risk-benefit judgement made under ultrasound with a small needle, and are usually done. Catheters carry the rule twice: once at insertion and once at removal, and the commonest error is a catheter pulled three hours after the evening enoxaparin. The timing of the postoperative thromboprophylaxis around a neuraxial block is taken up in venous thromboembolism prophylaxis.

Edge cases

The patient who refuses blood. Establish before the day exactly which products and techniques are acceptable (many accept cell salvage in continuous circuit, and some accept fractions). Then use every pillar at full strength: iron and, with haematology, erythropoietin beforehand; tranexamic acid by both routes; salvage; meticulous positioning; staged surgery where the operation can be divided; minimal blood sampling afterwards.

The recent stent or thrombosis. Keep the aspirin going through arthroplasty: the bleeding cost is small and it does not restrict neuraxial block. For dual antiplatelet therapy within the mandatory period, elective surgery waits. For tranexamic acid, topical dosing is the middle path and a single intravenous dose remains defensible when the bleeding risk is high; the decision is author’s judgement on thin direct evidence.

The tourniquet and the cement. A tourniquet moves the loss from the operation to the recovery room without reducing the total, and cement on a femoral or tibial surface tamponades cancellous bone. Both change when the bleeding shows, covered in the tourniquet and bone cement.

Hypotensive anaesthesia. Deliberate hypotension reduces arterial bleeding and has no effect on venous ooze from bone. With tranexamic acid, neuraxial anaesthesia and good positioning there is little left for it to add in arthroplasty, and in the beach chair and the prone spine patient it is the direct cause of the rare catastrophic injuries. A normal pressure for that patient, a low venous pressure at the wound and an antifibrinolytic is the better combination.

Massive loss. The pelvic fracture, the revision hip with a torn iliac vein and the deformity correction that has passed a blood volume are managed by ratio-based or viscoelastic-guided replacement like any other major haemorrhage. Fibrinogen falls first; give it early (cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate to keep it above 2 g/L), keep the patient warm and the ionised calcium normal, and remember that salvaged blood contains no clotting factors.
