Enhanced recovery and day-case arthroplasty

Enhanced recovery for arthroplasty is a short list of things that each remove one reason a patient stays in bed: anaemia corrected, fasting shortened, a spinal dosed for the operation rather than the tradition, a motor-sparing block and infiltration, dexamethasone, tranexamic acid, no drain, no catheter, and a physiotherapist at the bedside on the afternoon of surgery. Same-day discharge is the same pathway with the patient, the home and the discharge criteria selected in advance; in pooled data it carries no excess readmission or complication, but the cohorts were chosen to succeed. The failures are predictable (orthostatic intolerance, pain when a short block recedes, nausea, urinary retention) and each has a specific prevention.

The mechanism: why patients stay in hospital

Enhanced recovery is not a protocol; it is a list of reasons a patient is still in bed on the afternoon of surgery, each matched to the thing that removes it. The Copenhagen group asked the question directly in 207 unselected hip and knee arthroplasties with a median stay of two days: at 24 and 48 hours the patients who could not go home were held by pain, dizziness and weakness, with nausea, confusion and sedation hardly featuring, and a fifth of them were waiting for a transfusion, a physiotherapist or a radiograph rather than for anything physiological. Everything in the pathway follows from that list.

Pain comes from the operation and from the recession of whatever block was used; dizziness is orthostatic intolerance, a product of the spinal’s sympathectomy, the blood lost, the opioid given and the hours spent lying flat; weakness is the motor block, the quadriceps inhibition of a knee that has been opened, and the deconditioning of a patient who was not fit before surgery. Each of those has a remedy that is anaesthetic rather than surgical, which is why the anaesthetist, not the surgeon, decides whether a day-case joint programme works.

The pre-operative work

Anaemia is the item with the strongest evidence and the longest lead time. Pre-operative anaemia affects 15 to 39% of elective hip and knee patients and is associated with transfusion, infection, longer stay and readmission; the ERAS consensus grades correcting it as high evidence with a strong recommendation, and it is the single pathway item that cannot be done on the morning of surgery. A haemoglobin below 130 g/L in either sex is the trigger for ferritin and transferrin saturation; iron deficiency is treated with intravenous iron at least two to three weeks before the date, and a cause is looked for. Intravenous iron given at wound closure is a weaker version of the same idea: in a randomised trial of 89 knee arthroplasties it halved the rate of anaemia at 30 days (34% versus 62%). The day-case programme in particular depends on this, because the post-discharge transfusion is the complication that the outpatient cohorts in the American NSQIP data had more of than their inpatient matches (4.1% versus 0.1% after discharge), and a transfusion at home is a readmission.

Prehabilitation has less behind it than the enthusiasm suggests. Pre-operative physiotherapy improves early pain and function slightly and does not shorten stay or accelerate discharge criteria; the ERAS consensus does not recommend it as an essential intervention, and a 2026 meta-analysis of 38 trials found a small average benefit in performance-based function, none in patient-reported function, and no evidence that frail patients gain more. The honest advice is that a patient who can do stairs before surgery will do stairs after it, and that the four to six weeks before an arthroplasty are better spent on haemoglobin, glucose, weight, smoking and the drugs than on a supervised exercise class.

Fasting is clear fluids to two hours and solids to six, with the time written for the patient’s actual slot, because a patient who has been nil by mouth since midnight for an afternoon list arrives dehydrated and orthostatic before the spinal has done anything. Carbohydrate loading reduces insulin resistance and improves pre-operative hunger and nausea in small trials, but has not been shown to accelerate discharge in hip or knee replacement, and the ERAS consensus does not recommend it as essential. A clear carbohydrate drink two hours before is harmless and reasonable; it is not what makes the pathway work. Sedative premedication is not given routinely, because it sedates the patient on the afternoon they are supposed to be walking.

Patient education is low-evidence and strongly recommended, which is the right grade for an intervention that costs nothing and determines the patient’s expectations. The expectation that matters most for same-day discharge is that they will walk on the day of surgery and go home that evening; a patient who was told to expect three nights will take three nights.

The anaesthetic skeleton

The ERAS consensus accepts general and neuraxial anaesthesia equally, on moderate evidence, and the reasoning is in the neuraxial chapter. The pathway’s practical preference is a spinal, because it removes intraoperative opioid, lowers blood loss, and in the same-day discharge meta-analysis was protective against failure while general anaesthesia increased it (OR 0.58 for success). The spinal has to be dosed for recovery: a 15 mg bupivacaine spinal at 14:00 is a patient who stands at 20:00 and voids at midnight, which is a one-night stay. The options are low-dose bupivacaine (7.5-10 mg), mepivacaine where it is available (motor recovery 210 versus 229 minutes and urinary retention 36% versus 57% against isobaric bupivacaine 10 mg in a randomised knee trial), or chloroprocaine for the shortest cases. Spinal opioid is not recommended for routine use in the ERAS consensus, because it delays what the pathway is trying to bring forward.

The analgesic skeleton is the same for hip and knee and is now well defined by PROSPECT: paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started before or during surgery and continued regularly, a single intravenous dose of dexamethasone, a motor-sparing regional technique, and opioid only as rescue. For the knee that technique is a single-shot adductor canal block plus surgeon periarticular infiltration; the ERAS consensus grades local infiltration analgesia for the knee as high evidence and says nerve blocks have not shown superiority over it and are not an essential component, which is a statement about femoral blocks and quadriceps weakness rather than about the adductor canal. For the hip it is a supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block or a PENG block, with infiltration as the fallback when a block cannot be done; the ERAS consensus does not recommend infiltration for the hip, PROSPECT allows it when a block is impossible, and the difference between the two reflects how little the hip needs beyond a spinal and a good oral regimen. Gabapentinoids are not recommended by either body; they sedate, they add to orthostatic intolerance, and in the same-day discharge data gabapentin is a risk factor for failure.

Dexamethasone is the cheapest and most useful drug in the pathway. In the DEX-2-TKA trial of 485 knee arthroplasties, a single 24 mg dose reduced 48-hour morphine from 43 mg to 38 mg, with better pain scores, and a second 24 mg dose at 24 hours added nothing. The dose question is unsettled: 8 to 10 mg is what most guidelines name, 24 mg is what the Danish trials used, and a trial of 1 mg/kg against 0.3 mg/kg in low-pain-responder knee patients found no difference at all (moderate-to-severe pain on walking at 24 hours in 56% versus 53%). The author’s practice is 8 mg at induction for every arthroplasty without a specific contraindication, and a higher dose in the patient predicted to have a bad pain course; the diabetic patient gets the same dose and a glucose check on the ward, because the antiemetic and analgesic benefit outweighs a day of hyperglycaemia. The ERAS consensus recommends screening for and giving multimodal PONV prophylaxis, and dexamethasone covers both purposes.

Tranexamic acid is high-evidence and strongly recommended: 1 g IV before incision, with a second dose or topical application as the surgeon prefers. It is the reason the day-case programme can promise not to transfuse. The tourniquet is not recommended routinely for the knee in the ERAS consensus, because of the pain and the quadriceps dysfunction that follow a long inflation; surgeons vary, and the anaesthetic consequence of a tourniquet is covered in the tourniquet chapter. Fluids are given judiciously and stopped after surgery in favour of oral intake, and warmth is maintained throughout.

No drains, no catheters

Drains are not recommended for hip or knee replacement on moderate evidence: level 1 data show they increase blood loss and transfusion without reducing haematoma or infection. Urinary catheters are not recommended routinely, and when used are removed as soon as the patient can void, ideally within 24 hours. The single most useful number in the ERAS consensus is the catheterisation threshold: in a large randomised trial within an enhanced recovery pathway, scanning and catheterising at 800 mL rather than 500 mL significantly reduced the need for catheterisation without increasing urological complications. Retention is largely iatrogenic: in 378 fast-track arthroplasties, 40% of knees and 36% of hips were catheterised for retention, and the strongest associations were intraoperative catheter placement itself and, for hips, age over 60 and more than 1,350 mL of intraoperative fluid. A short-acting spinal, no catheter, restrained fluids, a bladder scanner on the ward and an 800 mL threshold are the whole of the prevention.

Day 0 mobilisation and the discharge criteria

Mobilisation on the day of surgery is the pathway item that converts all the others into a shorter stay, and the ERAS consensus grades it as strong. It requires that the block be motor sparing, that the spinal has receded, that the pressure holds when the patient sits, and that a physiotherapist is on the ward in the afternoon rather than the next morning. The patient who is asked to stand four hours after a low-dose spinal with a working adductor canal block and an NSAID on board usually can; the patient who has had 15 mg of bupivacaine, a femoral block and a morphine PCA cannot, and the difference is entirely the anaesthetist’s.

Discharge is by objective, team-based functional criteria rather than by the calendar: pain controlled on oral medication, independent transfers and walking with the aid they will use at home, stairs if they have them, voiding, tolerating diet, a wound that is dry, and a responsible adult at home for the first night. Written criteria stop the two-day default from reasserting itself, and the ERAS consensus recommends them on low evidence and a strong grade. For a same-day discharge the criteria are the same with a time limit: if they are not met by the evening, the patient stays, and that is a planned outcome rather than a failure.

Patient selection and the Australian context

The outpatient cohorts in the literature were younger, lighter and fitter than the inpatients they were compared with; that is the first thing every meta-analysis says and it is the reason the safety data are reassuring rather than proof. The 41-study review found no difference in complications, readmission or patient-reported outcomes between outpatient and inpatient pathways, with a moderate-to-high risk of bias. The unicompartmental knee data are the cleanest: 88% successful same-day discharge across 9,694 patients, 91% in selected and 76% in unselected cohorts, readmission 3%, complications 4%. The selected patient is the one the programme should start with: ASA 1 or 2, body mass index below about 40, no insulin-requiring diabetes out of control, no sleep apnoea that is untreated, no anticoagulant that cannot be managed, a haemoglobin above 120 g/L, a house without a staircase they cannot manage, an adult at home, a mobile phone, and a willingness to go home. In the same-day failure meta-analysis the risks were female sex, ASA 3 and 4, smoking, more than two allergies, and general anaesthesia.

In Australia the same-day joint sits almost entirely in private hospitals, where the surgeon, the anaesthetist and the physiotherapist can agree a pathway without a committee, and where the funder’s interest in avoiding a two-night stay aligns with the patient’s. The public system runs the same enhanced recovery skeleton with a one- or two-night stay, because its patients are older and sicker and because the ward, not the theatre, sets the pace. Nothing in the evidence suggests that a two-night public stay is unsafe or that a same-day private discharge is unsafe; the difference is selection and logistics. A programme that works has a named clinician who takes the evening phone call, a pathway back to the ward that does not go through the emergency department, and an honest audit of who went home and who came back.

The failure modes

Orthostatic intolerance is the commonest reason a same-day discharge fails and the commonest reason an inpatient is still in bed on day 1. It is dizziness, nausea and presyncope on sitting or standing, occurs in up to half of arthroplasty patients, and is associated with female sex, age, comorbidity, pain above 5/10, gabapentin, spinal anaesthesia, hip rather than knee, and opioid. The mechanism is a failure of vasoconstriction in a patient who is relatively hypovolaemic from blood loss and fasting, vasodilated from the spinal and the inflammatory response, and bradycardic from opioid and lying flat. Prevention is the whole pathway: euvolaemia without overload, tranexamic acid, no gabapentinoid, minimal opioid, a short spinal, and sitting up early rather than lying flat for six hours and standing suddenly. Midodrine 10 mg four to five doses over the first day halved orthostatic intolerance at first mobilisation (18% versus 33%) in a randomised trial of 159 arthroplasties, with syncope only in the placebo group and a median stay of two rather than three days; it is a reasonable addition for the patient with a history of it, not yet a routine.

Pain at the point the block recedes is the second failure, and it is predictable to the hour. A chloroprocaine or mepivacaine spinal is gone before the patient reaches the ward; if the oral regimen has not been given in recovery and the adductor canal block or infiltration is not working, the patient will be in severe pain at 17:00 and will not go home. The fix is timing (first oral doses in recovery, not on the ward), a block that outlasts the spinal, and a written rescue plan for the first night at home that the patient has in their hand. The rebound phenomenon after a single-shot block is discussed in the procedure cards; dexamethasone reduces it.

Nausea is less common than it was because the spinal removes the volatile agent and most of the opioid, and dexamethasone and ondansetron cover the rest. The nauseated patient after a spinal is usually hypotensive; treat the pressure before the next antiemetic.

Urinary retention is prevented as above; the patient who cannot void by the evening with a scan under 800 mL goes home with instructions and a phone number, and the one over 800 mL is catheterised and stays. Neither is a disaster.

The failures that matter are the ones the pathway cannot see: the bleed at home that becomes a transfusion, the wound that dehisces, the pulmonary embolus in a patient whose prophylaxis depended on an injection they did not give themselves. The post-discharge complication rate in the NSQIP outpatient data (6.3% versus 1.1% for matched inpatients, mostly transfusion) is the figure that keeps the programme honest. It is why the haemoglobin is fixed beforehand, why tranexamic acid is not optional, and why the first phone call is the next morning.

Edge cases

  • The afternoon case. A low-dose spinal at 15:00 can still go home, but the margin is gone; a general anaesthetic with blocks and infiltration, or an inpatient pathway, is often the more honest plan for a late list.
  • Bilateral arthroplasty. Not a day case. The blood loss, the duration and the orthostatic load are each doubled.
  • Revision. Not a day case; the pathway’s analgesic skeleton still applies, the stay does not.
  • The patient on an anticoagulant. The spinal interval is in the neuraxial chapter; the discharge question is who gives the next dose and when.
  • The diabetic patient. Dexamethasone is given, the glucose is checked at six hours, and the patient on insulin stays the night unless the programme has a specific protocol for them.
  • Sleep apnoea. Treated apnoea with a device at home and an opioid-sparing regimen is compatible with same-day discharge; untreated or severe apnoea with an opioid requirement is not.
  • Frailty. The frail patient gains the most from the pathway and is least likely to go home the same day. The skeleton is the same; the time limit is removed.

Key sources

The ERAS Society consensus of 2020 is the backbone and gives an evidence level and grade for each of its 17 items; most of what is in this chapter is a restatement of it with the anaesthetic reasoning attached. The Copenhagen “why still in hospital” study explains the list. The same-day discharge evidence is almost entirely observational and selected, with the unicompartmental knee data the best of it; the failure-mode meta-analysis and the orthostatic intolerance trial are the two most useful recent additions. Where the chapter gives a dose or a threshold without a citation (the 800 mL bladder threshold is cited; the 130 g/L anaemia trigger and the 8 mg dexamethasone dose are convention), it is standard practice rather than trial evidence.
