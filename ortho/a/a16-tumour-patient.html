The patient with a bone tumour or metastatic bone disease

Two different patients share the tumour list: the young patient with a primary sarcoma arrives after anthracycline, platinum and ifosfamide with a normal-looking exterior and a damaged heart, kidney and peripheral nerve, for a long operation intended to cure, and the older patient with metastatic bone disease arrives hypercalcaemic, anticoagulated, opioid tolerant and on dexamethasone or a checkpoint inhibitor, for a short operation intended to let them walk without pain for the time they have. In both, the treatment history predicts the anaesthetic problems better than the tumour does, and reaming or cementing a canal full of tumour carries a larger embolic load than the same step in a healthy bone. The randomised trials show no effect of regional anaesthesia on cancer recurrence, so the block is chosen for analgesia. Prognosis decides the implant and should also decide the ceiling of care, which is agreed before theatre.

Two patients, one list

The tumour list holds two patients with little in common. The first is 17, has an osteosarcoma of the distal femur, finished three cycles of methotrexate, doxorubicin and cisplatin a fortnight ago and is having a limb salvage resection (or, for other tumours, a curettage or a hemipelvectomy) that will take five hours and is intended to cure. The second is 71, has renal cell carcinoma with a lytic lesion in the proximal femur that hurts when she stands, takes dexamethasone, apixaban and 120 mg of oral morphine equivalent a day, and is having a nail or a proximal femoral replacement so that she can walk for the months she has.

In both, the treatment already given predicts the anaesthetic problems better than the tumour does, so the pre-operative question is a history: which drugs, what cumulative dose, how long ago, which field was irradiated, and what is being taken today.

What chemotherapy leaves behind

Sarcoma chemotherapy is built from a small number of drugs, each with a signature organ.

Anthracycline cardiotoxicity is the one that kills a young patient with a normal examination. The drug injures cardiomyocytes in proportion to cumulative dose, the injury can be silent for years, and chest radiotherapy adds to it. The European cardio-oncology guideline stratifies risk by cumulative dose, with 250 mg/m2 of doxorubicin or its equivalent the conventional boundary of high-dose exposure, and osteosarcoma and Ewing protocols exceed it. The guideline asks for echocardiography before treatment and after it; the anaesthetist’s task is to find that report. A fall in ejection fraction changes a five-hour resection with a 2 L blood loss from a fluid problem into a heart failure problem: an arterial line, a vasopressor in preference to volume once filling is adequate, and avoidance of the tachycardia the ventricle cannot afford.

Bleomycin has a reputation that outruns the data. In 316 bleomycin-exposed patients having 541 major operations under general anaesthesia, postoperative ARDS occurred in 1.3%, was associated with smoking and with larger crystalloid and colloid volumes, and was not associated with the inspired oxygen fraction. The sensible reading is to titrate oxygen to saturation and to be careful with fluid.

Cytopenias are a matter of timing. The neutrophil and platelet nadir falls 7 to 14 days after most cycles, and elective resection is scheduled for count recovery, usually three weeks after the last cycle and before the next. The pathological fracture does not wait, so the count on the day decides three things. Platelets decide the neuraxial question: the Association of Anaesthetists’ coagulation guideline grades the risk as a continuum and does not give one number, and the author’s practice is a spinal above about 75 x 10^9/L with a stable count and normal clotting, and no epidural catheter in a patient whose count is still falling. Neutrophils decide the catheter question: an indwelling perineural or epidural catheter in a patient with a count below 1.0 x 10^9/L is a foreign body in someone who cannot defend it, and a single-shot block is the defensible choice. Haemoglobin decides the crossmatch, because suppressed marrow does not replace what the operation removes.

Mucositis is the airway finding. Methotrexate and doxorubicin strip the oral and pharyngeal mucosa for one to two weeks after a cycle; the mouth is painful and bleeds on contact, intake has been poor, and the patient is dry and hypokalaemic. Laryngoscopy is gentle and a nasal route is avoided.

Platinum, vincristine and the block

Cisplatin damages the dorsal root ganglion and vincristine and the taxanes damage the axon, so the patient presents with numb, tingling feet and hands in a stocking and glove distribution, sometimes worsening for months after the last dose. The question for the anaesthetist is whether a nerve that is already injured is more likely to be harmed by a block. The regional society’s advisory on pre-existing neurological disease concluded that the evidence has strengthened that diseased nerves are more sensitive to local anaesthetic and perhaps more susceptible to injury, the double-crush idea, while accepting that the absolute risk is not known and that surgery itself produces neuropathy.

That does not prohibit a block; it changes how the block is done and recorded. The deficit is examined and written down before the block, so that a postoperative finding can be compared with a baseline. The dose and concentration are reduced, because the block will be denser and longer than expected for the same drug; adrenaline as an additive is omitted; and ultrasound is used to stay outside the nerve. The patient is told that the numbness may outlast the usual time.

Platinum nephrotoxicity is the other half. A patient with a creatinine that looks normal after cisplatin can have a glomerular filtration rate half of what it was, and magnesium wasting persists for months. NSAIDs, otherwise the right drug for bone pain, are used only with a known filtration rate.

Radiotherapy

Radiotherapy changes tissue in its field permanently. Irradiated skin and subcutaneous tissue are fibrotic and poorly perfused, so wounds break down and the surgeon often plans a flap; irradiated bone is weak and fractures without a lesion in it; the limb may be lymphoedematous and hard to cannulate. A neck or mediastinum that has been irradiated is a difficult airway until shown otherwise, and chest radiotherapy adds coronary and valve disease to anthracycline injury. A plexus that sat in the field can have a radiation plexopathy, which is approached as the chemotherapy neuropathy is.

Hypercalcaemia

Hypercalcaemia is the commonest metabolic complication of malignancy. Calcium makes the patient polyuric, the patient becomes dry, the dry kidney retains more calcium, and the loop closes with confusion, constipation, abdominal pain and a short QT interval. For the anaesthetist it is a volume-depleted patient who will be profoundly hypotensive on induction or after a spinal, with an irritable myocardium and a delirium that will be blamed on the anaesthetic.

The Endocrine Society guideline makes a strong recommendation for denosumab or an intravenous bisphosphonate, and suggests adding calcitonin for severe hypercalcaemia because the antiresorptive takes two to four days to work. Fluid comes first. A corrected calcium above about 3.0 mmol/L or any symptomatic hypercalcaemia is treated before a prophylactic fixation, which can wait those days; a completed fracture goes with the calcium falling and the patient rehydrated. An ionised calcium on the blood gas is the number to follow. The opposite trap is the patient already on denosumab or zoledronate with low vitamin D, who is hypocalcaemic after citrate from a transfusion.

Thrombosis and anticoagulation

Cancer is a prothrombotic state, and metastatic adenocarcinoma, immobility from a painful limb and lower limb orthopaedic surgery stack on it. Three situations arise.

The patient not yet anticoagulated is at high risk and receives pharmacological prophylaxis after surgery unless bleeding forbids it. The oncology society’s guideline supports extended prophylaxis after major cancer surgery and now includes apixaban and rivaroxaban as options; its trials were abdominal and pelvic, so four weeks of prophylaxis after femoral fixation for metastasis is extrapolation, and is the author’s default in a patient who is not walking (see venous thromboembolism prophylaxis).

The patient already on treatment-dose anticoagulant is common, and apixaban is now a guideline option for cancer-associated thrombosis, so many arrive on a direct oral anticoagulant with reduced renal function. The anticoagulant decides the anaesthetic technique, not whether surgery happens: the timing rules for neuraxial block are those in the neuraxial chapter, and a deep vein thrombosis within the previous month is the situation in which interrupting treatment for surgery is most dangerous and a bridging plan with haematology is needed.

The patient with an undiagnosed thrombosis is the one who has been in bed for a week with a pathological fracture. Unexplained hypoxia or tachycardia before fixation is a pulmonary embolus until imaging says otherwise, because reaming will add to it.

Tranexamic acid is used. Across 216 randomised trials it did not increase thromboembolic events, including in patients with a history of thrombosis; trials in active metastatic cancer are few, and the author’s practice is to give it for a vascular lesion or an endoprosthesis.

Mirels score and why the timing matters to you

Mirels proposed the score that decides whether a long bone metastasis is fixed before it breaks.

Mirels score           1                2                3
site               upper limb       lower limb       peritrochanteric
pain               mild             moderate         functional
lesion             blastic          mixed            lytic
size (of cortex)   under 1/3        1/3 to 2/3       over 2/3

total 4-12:   7 or less   irradiate and observe
              8           borderline, clinical judgement
              9 or more   prophylactic fixation, then radiotherapy

In the original 78 lesions the mean score was 7 in those that did not fracture in the next six months and 10 in those that did, and Mirels proposed fixation from a score of 8; later practice treats 8 as borderline. Functional pain, meaning pain on weight bearing that is relieved by rest, is the strongest single item.

The reason to care is that a prophylactic fixation and a fixation after fracture are different anaesthetics. The prophylactic patient walks in, is hydrated, has had the calcium corrected, the anticoagulant stopped on schedule and the vascular lesion embolised, and loses less blood through a closed nailing. The fractured patient has been in bed in pain, is dry, may be hypercalcaemic and anticoagulated, has bled into the thigh, and cannot be positioned without traction on a bone that has no cortex.

The same bone disease is elsewhere in the skeleton. Before the patient is moved, the staging imaging is read for the other lesions: a lytic humerus under the blood pressure cuff or used to pull the patient across, rib lesions under a lateral post, a cervical lesion that turns laryngoscopy into a spinal instability problem (see spinal metastasis), and the other femur.

Vascular metastases and embolisation

Renal cell and thyroid carcinoma metastases and myeloma deposits are arteriovenous sponges inside bone. When the surgeon curettes one, there is no vessel to clamp and no tourniquet for a proximal femur or pelvis; bleeding stops when the cavity is empty and packed with cement, so the loss is brisk, measured in litres, and ends abruptly.

Embolisation in the 24 to 72 hours before surgery is the standard request and the evidence for it is thinner than the practice suggests. The only randomised trial, in 45 patients having spinal metastasis surgery, found no significant reduction in blood loss overall (618 against 735 mL) and no difference in transfusion, with a significant reduction only in the hypervascular subgroup. In long bones the data are retrospective and conflicting: one series of 79 renal cell stabilisations found more blood loss in the embolised group, almost certainly because the worst lesions were the ones embolised, and another found no effect of the interval between embolisation and surgery. The author’s reading is that embolisation is worth having for a large renal or thyroid lesion that will be opened and curetted, and that it does not change the preparation: two large cannulae, an arterial line, tranexamic acid, blood in the room, and a surgeon who has the cement ready before the lesion is entered. Whether cell salvage through a leucocyte depletion filter disseminates disease is not known.

Embolic load at nailing and cementation

Reaming and nailing pressurise the medullary canal, and cementing a stem pressurises it more. In a healthy femur the content that is driven into the femoral veins is fat and marrow (fat embolism). In a metastatic femur it is fat, marrow, tumour and clot, in a patient whose pulmonary vascular bed is already reduced by lung metastases, effusions or previous emboli. That is the mechanism behind three observations.

Pathological femoral fractures carried a 3.3% incidence of fat embolism syndrome in a systematic review, above unilateral high-energy fractures. Cardiac arrest from histologically proven tumour embolism has been reported after bilateral femoral nailing in one sitting. And bone cement implantation syndrome is commoner here than in any other cemented operation: it occurred in about a third of 166 cemented endoprostheses for femoral metastases, and in 26% of 88 cemented tumour operations in a second series, where lung cancer or lung metastases raised the rate to 40% against 15% without.

The surgical measures are a distal vent hole, canal lavage and suction before cement, slow insertion of a long stem, unreamed or gently reamed nailing, and staging bilateral femora on separate days. The anaesthetic measures are to know when the step is coming and to be ready before it: an arterial line in any patient with lung disease or a long cemented stem, the inspired oxygen raised, the circulation filled, and a vasopressor running or drawn up. In the Danish series a joint protocol raised vasopressor use from 59% to 86% of cases and was followed by fewer pulmonary emboli. In the second series the lowest pressure came within 10 minutes of cementation in 87.5% of cases, so those are the 10 minutes in which nothing else is being done.

Steroid cover

The metastatic patient is frequently on dexamethasone: for cord compression, cerebral oedema, appetite or an immune toxicity. Dexamethasone 4 mg is about 25 mg of prednisolone, so any course beyond a few weeks has suppressed the axis. The joint guideline of the Association of Anaesthetists and the endocrine societies sets the threshold at prednisolone 5 mg a day or its equivalent for a month or more, by any route, and is emphatic that supplementation is in addition to the usual dose.

For major surgery the guideline regimen is hydrocortisone 100 mg IV at induction followed by 200 mg over 24 hours by infusion, or 50 mg six-hourly, until the patient is eating and back on at least a doubled oral dose. A patient who is already taking dexamethasone 8 mg or more a day is receiving more glucocorticoid than the stress regimen and needs the usual dose given intravenously on time, not hydrocortisone added. This differs from the advice for the rheumatoid patient on low-dose prednisolone having an elective joint replacement, described in the orthopaedic patient, where the usual dose alone is continued; the difference is the higher exposure and the larger insult. Unexplained hypotension that does not respond to vasopressor in a cancer patient is treated with hydrocortisone 100 mg first and investigated afterwards.

Opioid tolerance and methadone

Bone metastasis pain is treated with strong opioid for months before the fixation, so the patient arrives tolerant and often on a transdermal patch. The principles are those of the opioid-tolerant chapter: the baseline opioid is continued or replaced milligram for milligram, the surgical requirement is added on top, and the opioid-sparing measures carry more weight than in a naive patient.

Three points are specific to cancer. A fentanyl or buprenorphine patch stays on, and the forced-air warmer is kept off it, because heat increases absorption. Methadone has two roles: as the patient’s existing opioid, where it is continued and the QT interval checked against the other drugs that lengthen it (ondansetron, droperidol) and against hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia from platinum; and as an intra-operative drug, where a single dose of 0.2 mg/kg at induction halved opioid consumption for three days after spinal fusion in a randomised trial. Its NMDA antagonism and long half-life suit a tolerant patient having a big resection, and as a first strong opioid for cancer pain it performs as well as morphine. The regional technique is where the largest gain lies: a fixation that removes the mechanical pain of an unstable bone, under a block that covers the first two days, can let the opioid dose fall below the pre-operative baseline, and a ward that keeps giving the old dose will oversedate the patient. Review the dose on day 1.

Regional anaesthesia and recurrence

The hypothesis was that surgery releases tumour cells while the stress response, volatile agents and opioids suppress host defence, and a regional technique reduces all three. Retrospective studies supported it. The randomised trials have not.

Trial Patients Comparison Result
Sessler 2019 2,108 breast cancer resections paravertebral and propofol vs sevoflurane and opioid recurrence 10% vs 10%, HR 0.97 (0.74-1.28)
Xu 2021 400 lung cancer resections epidural plus general vs general recurrence-free survival HR 0.90 (0.60-1.35)
Du 2021 1,712 major thoracic and abdominal, 92% cancer epidural plus general vs general death 42% vs 38%, HR 1.07 (0.92-1.24)

None of these enrolled patients with sarcoma or bone metastases, and no randomised trial has. The accurate statement to a patient or a surgeon is that regional anaesthesia has not been shown to change recurrence or survival in any cancer in which it has been properly tested, and that there is no reason to expect bone to differ. The block is chosen for analgesia, for opioid sparing in a tolerant patient and for early movement, which are sufficient reasons.

Prognosis and the ceiling of care

In metastatic bone disease the surgeon matches the implant to the expected survival: a nail for a patient expected to live months, an endoprosthesis that will not fail for one expected to live years. In the revised Katagiri score, built on 808 patients, a score of 7 or more carried 27% survival at six months and 6% at a year, and a score of 3 or less carried 91% at a year. Such models drift as systemic therapy improves, and an external evaluation of the widely used PathFx model in 1,018 operated patients found reduced performance; a renal cell or lung cancer patient with a targetable mutation or on immunotherapy can outlive the tables by years.

The anaesthetist uses the same estimate for a different decision. A fixation in a patient with weeks to live is a palliative procedure whose success is measured in pain and transfers, and the plan for an intra-operative arrest at cementation, for postoperative ventilation and for intensive care is agreed with the patient and the oncology team beforehand and written down. A standing order not to resuscitate is not automatically suspended in theatre and not automatically honoured there; the usual agreement is that reversible anaesthetic events (hypotension at induction, a short period of ventilation) are treated and that prolonged resuscitation and organ support are not offered. The embolic risk at the canal step is part of consent in a patient with lung metastases.

For the sarcoma patient the calculation is reversed: the intent is cure, the operation is long, and full escalation is the default.

The immunotherapy patient

Checkpoint inhibitors (ipilimumab against CTLA-4; nivolumab and pembrolizumab against PD-1; atezolizumab and durvalumab against PD-L1) are now routine in melanoma, renal cell and lung cancer, the tumours that send metastases to bone. They work by removing a brake on T cells, and the toxicity is autoimmunity in any organ, arising at any time during treatment and for months after it stops. The oncology society’s guideline manages most of these with corticosteroid and treatment interruption; the endocrine ones are different because the gland is destroyed and the deficiency is permanent.

Toxicity What it does What to look for before theatre
Hypophysitis (commonest with ipilimumab and combinations) secondary adrenal insufficiency, often with thyroid and gonadal axes fatigue, headache, low sodium, low morning cortisol; on hydrocortisone replacement
Primary adrenalitis (rare) glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid loss hypotension, high potassium
Thyroiditis transient thyrotoxicosis, then hypothyroidism TSH and free T4; on thyroxine
Insulin-deficient diabetes abrupt onset, ketoacidosis glucose and ketones
Myocarditis, pneumonitis arrhythmia, heart failure; hypoxia troponin and ECG if symptomatic; oxygen saturation, recent imaging

Two rules follow. Any patient who has had a checkpoint inhibitor and is tired, hypotensive, hyponatraemic or hypoglycaemic has adrenal insufficiency until a cortisol says otherwise, and the peri-operative glucocorticoid guideline is explicit that undiagnosed insufficiency can present for the first time under the stress of surgery and that delay can be fatal. A patient known to have hypophysitis is on replacement hydrocortisone for life and receives the full stress regimen. And a patient being treated for colitis, hepatitis or another immune toxicity is on high-dose prednisolone, with axis suppression, hyperglycaemia and infection risk.

Whether a single anti-emetic dose of dexamethasone blunts the effect of immunotherapy is not known. The author’s practice is to give it when the nausea risk is high and to tell the oncologist. A sodium, glucose, thyroid function and morning cortisol belong on the pre-operative bloods of every checkpoint inhibitor patient.
