## What the surgeon is doing and why

A deformed spine is a column that has lost its balance. In the adolescent with idiopathic scoliosis the vertebrae have grown into a rotated, laterally curved stack; the child is healthy, the curve is flexible, and surgery is offered when the Cobb angle passes about 45-50 degrees because curves of that size continue to progress in adult life. In the adult the problem is different and worse. Discs and facets have degenerated asymmetrically, the lumbar lordosis has flattened, and the head now sits in front of the pelvis instead of over it; the patient has to hold themselves upright with their hip extensors and paraspinals all day, and it is that, with the stenosis that accompanies it, which produces the pain and disability. The adult curve is stiff, the bone is often osteoporotic, and the patient is 55-75 years old with the comorbidities of that age.

The operation is the same in outline for both. The surgeon exposes the back of the spine over the whole length to be fused, puts a pedicle screw into most or all of the vertebrae on both sides, loosens the spine enough to move, moves it into the planned shape with two rods, and then makes the corrected segment fuse by decorticating the posterior elements and packing bone graft. Four things decide how big the operation is.

The length. An adolescent thoracic curve is fused over 8-12 levels and stops above the lower lumbar spine to preserve motion. Adult deformity is commonly fused from the upper thoracic spine to the pelvis, 14-16 levels with iliac or sacral-alar-iliac screws, because a long fusion that stops at L5 fails at the level below.

The release. A flexible curve needs only the facet joints excised. A stiffer one needs posterior column (Ponte or Smith-Petersen) osteotomies, in which the facets, the ligamentum flavum and the spinous process are removed at several levels so the back of the spine can close; each gives about 10 degrees and opens the epidural veins. A fixed deformity needs a three-column osteotomy: a pedicle subtraction osteotomy, a wedge taken through the vertebral body so the spine hinges on its anterior cortex for 30 degrees or more of lordosis, or a vertebral column resection, in which a whole vertebra is removed and the spine is held only by temporary rods with the cord draped across the gap. These are the steps with litres of blood loss and the highest cord risk.

The correction. Rods are contoured to the intended shape and the spine is brought to them by rotation, translation, compression and distraction. Every manoeuvre changes the length and the blood supply of the cord. Distraction on the concave side stretches it; closing an osteotomy can buckle the dura or trap a root; a screw can sit medial in the canal.

The bone. In the adult the screws must hold in osteoporotic vertebrae, so the surgeon adds cement, more fixation points, interbody cages at the bottom of the construct, and sometimes tethers or cement at the top to prevent the junction above from failing.

What this costs the patient is recorded honestly in the deformity literature. In a prospective multicentre series of 291 adults, 52% had a perioperative complication, 70% had one by two years, and 28% needed a revision. In the 272-patient Scoli-RISK-1 study of complex adult deformity, leg motor scores had fallen in 22% at discharge and were still down in 11% at six months. Adolescent idiopathic surgery is a different order of risk: in 1,121 consecutive operations, 0.8% woke with a deficit and all recovered.

The pain logic. The dominant mechanism is muscle: the erector spinae stripped off the laminae and transverse processes on both sides over a 30-45 cm wound, denervated and ischaemic under self-retaining retractors for hours. Beneath it is bone (dozens of facetectomies, screw tracks and a decorticated bed the length of the spine) and retension, because the trunk has been made a different shape and every rib, costovertebral joint, hip flexor and hamstring is at a new length. In the 179-procedure cohort, spinal fusion and scoliosis surgery took ranks 2, 3 and 6 for worst pain on day 1, with medians of 7. It earns a 5 on this book's scale for the peak and for the tail: severe for three to four days, movement-evoked for weeks, and in the adult usually superimposed on years of opioid use.

## Steps

### 1. Before the day: the plan, the lungs and the briefing
**Why:** The surgeon has planned the levels, the osteotomies and whether the operation is staged, and that plan sets the blood loss and the duration more than anything you do.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask for four facts: levels, number and type of osteotomies, whether the pelvis is included, and one stage or two. For an adult, treat the work-up as for major vascular surgery: cardiac function, haemoglobin optimised (iron given weeks ahead), anticoagulants planned, opioid dose known. For a neuromuscular or severe thoracic curve the vital capacity predicts whether the patient will come off the ventilator; a forced vital capacity under about 30-40% of predicted means planned postoperative ventilation or non-invasive support. Tell every patient about the wake-up test in plain words (they may be asked to move their feet during the operation, they will not be in pain and may not remember), because a briefed patient performs it and an unbriefed one thrashes.

### 2. Induction, lines and an anaesthetic the monitoring can read through
**Why:** The neurophysiologist needs motor evoked potentials (transcranial stimulation, recorded from leg and hand muscles) and somatosensory evoked potentials throughout, and free-run and triggered electromyography for the screws.
**Anaesthesia:** Total intravenous anaesthesia with propofol and remifentanil, a single intubating dose of relaxant and none afterwards, and no volatile or at most a low fixed fraction; ketamine supports the signals and is the analgesic the guideline grades A, so start 0.5 mg/kg IV then 0.1-0.25 mg/kg/h. Soft bite blocks between the molars on both sides, because transcranial stimulation contracts the masseters hard enough to lacerate the tongue or bite through a tube. Arterial line, two large cannulae and, for adult deformity, central access; urinary catheter; temperature probe. Cell salvage set up from the incision. Tranexamic acid loaded now. Intrathecal morphine, if it is to be yours rather than the surgeon's, goes in before turning: the trial doses in children were 2 and 5 microg/kg, with no analgesic advantage to the higher one.

### 3. Turning prone and the baseline
**Why:** A free abdomen lowers the pressure in the epidural veins, which is the difference between a dry field and a wet one; hip extension preserves the lordosis the surgeon is about to fuse into place.
**Anaesthesia:** Turn with the lines disconnected to the minimum and reconnect in a fixed order. Check the abdomen is hanging free, the head is neutral and at or above the heart, the eyes and nose are clear of the support, the arms are below 90 degrees with the ulnar nerves padded, and the breasts and genitalia are free. Then have baseline potentials recorded before the incision, in the final position at the working blood pressure: a signal that was never present cannot be lost, and an arm signal that fades in the first hour is a brachial plexus under stretch that repositioning cures. Note the time for the eye checks, which continue every 30 min by direct look or a mirror.

### 4. Exposure
**Why:** The paraspinal muscles are stripped subperiosteally from the spinous processes out to the tips of the transverse processes over the whole fusion length, because the screws start at the junction of the transverse process and the facet and the graft has to lie on bone.
**Anaesthesia:** A 40 cm wound bleeds from muscle and bone along its entire length, and the first 500 mL in an adult are lost here. This is the phase in which a lower mean arterial pressure helps: in the adolescent antifibrinolytic trial the benefit depended on a mean below 75 mmHg during exposure. That figure belongs to healthy teenagers before the cord is under threat; for an adult with vascular disease, stay near the patient's own baseline throughout. Start counting: swabs, suction, salvage reservoir, and a blood gas with haemoglobin every hour from now on.

### 5. Facetectomies and pedicle screws
**Why:** Removing the facet joints mobilises each segment and provides fusion surface. Screws are placed through the pedicle into the vertebral body at each level by anatomical landmarks, fluoroscopy, navigation or a robot, 20-30 in a long construct, and checked by imaging and by stimulating the screw to see whether the current reaches a nerve root.
**Anaesthesia:** Triggered electromyography is meaningless under relaxant, which is the practical reason none is given. Screw placement is steady bleeding from cancellous bone at each hole, usually an hour or more. An intra-operative CT spin means everyone leaves the room and you watch from the door: before it, check depth, the infusion volumes and the tube. A sudden burst on free-run electromyography or a motor signal lost in one leg just after a screw is that screw until it is removed.

### 6. Osteotomies
**Why:** Posterior column osteotomies at the stiff levels let the back of the spine shorten. A pedicle subtraction osteotomy or vertebral column resection is done for fixed sagittal or sharp angular deformity: the canal is opened widely, the pedicles and a wedge of body are removed around the dura with temporary rods holding the spine, and the gap is closed by compressing the rods or extending the table.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the blood loss step. Epidural veins and cancellous body bleed fast and cannot be clamped: 1-2 L in 30-60 min is ordinary for a three-column osteotomy. Be ahead of it, with blood in the room and checked, the salvage running, a second gas sent as the surgeon starts on the pedicle, and fibrinogen and platelets requested on trend rather than at 2 L. Calcium with the red cells. It is also the highest-risk step for the cord: the pressure target moves up before closure of the osteotomy, not after, and the neurophysiologist runs motor potentials repeatedly as it closes.

### 7. Rods and the correction
**Why:** The contoured rods are seated and the spine is rotated, translated, compressed and distracted to them. In an adolescent the vertebrae are derotated to bring down the rib hump; in an adult the main gain is lordosis.
**Anaesthesia:** The cord is being lengthened and its radicular supply stretched. Before the first rod is reduced, have the mean arterial pressure at 80-85 mmHg or the patient's awake baseline, whichever is higher, the haemoglobin known and above about 80-90 g/L, the temperature and carbon dioxide normal, and the anaesthetic unchanged for the past 15 min so that any signal change is surgical. Tell the surgeon and the neurophysiologist that this is so. In the 1,121-patient series nine of the 38 alerts were caused by hypotension alone and reversed with pressure, and sensory potentials lagged the motor ones by about five minutes and missed four of seven motor deficits, which is why motor potentials are run after every corrective manoeuvre.

### 8. The alert
**Why:** A fall of 65% or more in motor amplitude, or 50% in sensory amplitude, is treated as a cord at risk until proven otherwise. The consensus checklist divides the response among surgeon, anaesthetist and neurophysiologist so that all of it happens at once.
**Anaesthesia:** Your list, done aloud. Raise the mean arterial pressure above 85-90 mmHg with a vasopressor now. Send a gas: haemoglobin, oxygenation, carbon dioxide, glucose. Check temperature. Confirm no bolus, no relaxant, no volatile change, and that the infusions are running into the patient. Check the limbs for a positional cause if the change is in one arm. The surgeon releases the last manoeuvre, removes or checks the last screw, and looks for compression; the neurophysiologist checks the leads and repeats. If the signals do not return, the correction is reduced or the rods removed, and a wake-up test is the arbiter: stop the propofol and remifentanil, keep the patient supported by two people at the head and legs, and when the patient obeys a command to squeeze a hand, ask for both feet to move. Expect 10-20 min after a long infusion. The dangers are a patient who coughs or rears on the frame, a tube lost prone, air entrained through open bone on a deep breath, and recall, which the pre-operative briefing turns from a complaint into a memory of being asked to wiggle toes.

### 9. Decortication, graft and closure
**Why:** The posterior elements are burred to bleeding bone and covered with local bone, allograft or other graft material; this is what makes the fusion. The fascia is closed watertight in layers over drains.
**Anaesthesia:** Decortication reopens the whole field and adds several hundred millilitres after the difficult part appears to be over; closure of a 40 cm wound takes 45-60 min. Use the time: a coagulation screen or viscoelastic test, a final haemoglobin, warming, and the analgesic load (paracetamol, the ketamine continued, the first long-acting opioid). If the surgeon is placing the intrathecal morphine or epidural catheters, it happens now under direct vision. The eye check continues to the end.

### 10. Turning supine, the face and the first examination
**Why:** The surgeon wants a neurological examination as soon as the patient can cooperate, because a deficit found in the first hour can be taken back to theatre.
**Anaesthesia:** After 6-10 h prone with litres of fluid the face, tongue and airway are swollen. Extubate the adolescent with idiopathic scoliosis on the table or in recovery; for the adult after a long case with large-volume resuscitation, look at the face, do a cuff leak test, and keep the tube overnight if in doubt. Plan the emergence so that the patient moves both feet to command before leaving theatre and write the time. Ask about vision as soon as the patient is lucid: the multicentre case-control study tied ischaemic optic neuropathy to male sex, obesity, the Wilson frame, each extra hour of anaesthesia (OR 1.39) and each extra litre of blood loss (OR 1.34), with colloid protective, and every deformity operation has two of those by design.

### 11. The first three days
**Why:** The construct is stable from the moment the rods are locked, and the surgeon wants the patient standing on day 1 to protect the lungs, the gut and the veins.
**Anaesthesia:** The intrathecal morphine carries the first 18-24 h and then stops, and the day 1 afternoon peak is the rebound of this operation; the patient-controlled opioid, the ketamine infusion and the regular oral drugs have to be running before it. Drains lose another 500 mL or more in the first day and the haemoglobin falls again on day 2. Ileus is usual for two to three days after a long thoracolumbar fusion with opioids. Hyponatraemia from antidiuretic hormone excess is common in adolescents on day 1-2; avoid hypotonic maintenance fluid. In the thin teenager whose spine has been lengthened, bilious vomiting at the end of the first week is superior mesenteric artery syndrome, not opioid nausea.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is 30-45 cm of stripped muscle over a decorticated, instrumented and re-shaped spine, so no single technique covers it and the plan is layered. PROSPECT's complex spine guideline, which includes scoliosis surgery, recommends paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor (grade A), an intra-operative low-dose ketamine infusion (grade A), opioid as rescue, and a surgeon-placed epidural catheter with dilute local anaesthetic at the end of surgery (grade B). It declines to recommend intrathecal opioid, plane blocks, lidocaine or dexmedetomidine for want of procedure-specific trials, and recommends against gabapentinoids and methadone on adverse effects.

The author's plan departs from the guideline in one place and says so. Intrathecal morphine is used: the paediatric scoliosis trial found that 2 or 5 microg/kg cut 24 h patient-controlled morphine from 49 mg to 19 and 12 mg with better pain at rest and no more side effects, and the adult fusion data on the [posterior lumbar fusion card](posterior-lumbar-fusion.html) point the same way. It needs respiratory monitoring for 24 h, which these patients have anyway. Ketamine runs from induction and continues at 0.1-0.2 mg/kg/h for 24-48 h in the opioid-tolerant adult. The NSAID is the contested drug: the guideline grades it A, many deformity surgeons withhold it for fear of non-union in a long fusion, and after a 2 L blood loss the kidneys decide the first 24 h anyway; a short course from day 1 in a patient with normal renal function is the defensible middle, with the surgeon's agreement.

Catheter decision: selective. A surgeon-placed epidural (or two, for a long construct) works and has guideline support, but it competes with the hourly leg examination, it is displaced easily under a long wound, and many units cannot run it. It is the right choice for the adult on long-term opioids in whom the systemic plan is predicted to fail, started after a normal examination. Methadone 0.2 mg/kg IV at the start of surgery is the alternative with the best single trial (day 1 hydromorphone 4.6 against 9.9 mg, with lower pain to day 3) and is reasonable where intrathecal morphine is not used; the two are not combined.

Oral regimen shape: regular paracetamol; NSAID as agreed; a regular modified-release or scheduled opioid from day 1-2 with a weaning plan written before discharge, since deformity surgery is a common route into persistent opioid use; a muscle relaxant such as diazepam for spasm in adolescents is widespread practice without good trials; a laxative from day 0. The adult already on opioids continues the baseline dose from the morning of surgery, with everything else added on top.

Rebound is high and scheduled: the afternoon of day 1. Escalation: pain is expected, so the signals that matter are neurological rather than analgesic. New leg weakness, numbness or sphincter change with rising back pain in the first 48 h is an epidural haematoma until an MRI says otherwise. Chest pain and breathlessness may be a pneumothorax or haemothorax from a thoracic screw or thoracoplasty. Calf or shoulder-tip pain in a patient who cannot yet have anticoagulants has the usual meaning.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Adolescent idiopathic scoliosis. Healthy patients, flexible curves, 4-6 h, about 800-1,000 mL with tranexamic acid, transfusion uncommon with cell salvage, extubated at the end, and home within a week on enhanced recovery pathways. Weight-based dosing throughout. The family is part of the pre-operative conversation, including the wake-up test.
- Neuromuscular scoliosis. Cerebral palsy, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, spinal muscular atrophy, myelomeningocele: fusion to the pelvis, far more bleeding per level, poor respiratory reserve, a cardiomyopathy to look for in Duchenne, no suxamethonium and no volatile in the dystrophies, latex precautions in myelomeningocele, unreliable baseline potentials, seizures and baclofen pumps. Planned intensive care.
- Three-column osteotomy and vertebral column resection. The largest blood loss and the highest neurological risk in elective orthopaedics; a second anaesthetist is reasonable, and staging over two days is a legitimate decision for you to raise.
- Staged and combined surgery. Anterior or lateral interbody fusion first, posterior instrumentation the same day or days later ([anterior and lateral lumbar fusion](anterior-lateral-lumbar-fusion.html)). The second stage meets a patient who is anaemic and has an ileus.
- The request for hypotension. Sensible during exposure in a healthy adolescent, wrong during correction in anyone and wrong throughout in an adult with vascular disease; it is also on the causal path to optic neuropathy.
- High-dose tranexamic acid in older adults. Less bleeding and fewer units in the retrospective adult comparison, with atrial fibrillation in 5% and myocardial infarction in 1.7% of the high-dose group against none; with renal impairment the drug accumulates and seizures are the described toxicity. A moderate regimen for the 70-year-old with coronary disease is the author's judgement.
- Signals absent from the start. Pre-existing myelopathy, neuromuscular disease, a small child or a volatile-based anaesthetic. Decide before the incision whether the case proceeds on sensory potentials alone, with a planned wake-up test after the correction.
- Vision loss. Head neutral and at or above the heart, no pressure on the globes, avoid the Wilson frame, use some colloid, limit the hours by staging, and avoid prolonged hypotension and anaemia. Any visual complaint in recovery needs an ophthalmologist the same day.
- Venous air embolism. A large area of open bone above the heart in a prone patient; a sudden fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide with hypotension during decortication or osteotomy is the presentation.
- Massive transfusion coagulopathy. After one blood volume the bleeding is partly yours; viscoelastic testing and fibrinogen early. The principles are in the [blood management chapter](../a/a04-blood-management-txa.html) and the prone and monitoring detail in [spine essentials](../a/a14-spine-essentials.html).
- Proximal junctional failure and revision. Adult patients come back; a revision through scar bleeds more and takes longer than the index operation.

## Key sources

Blood conservation in adolescent surgery rests on two randomised trials; the adult high-dose data are retrospective and carry a cardiac signal that has not been tested prospectively. Neuromonitoring practice rests on a large multicentre case series and a consensus checklist, not on trials, and will stay that way. The visual loss evidence is one case-control study and an advisory. For analgesia, PROSPECT covers the operation but found too little procedure-specific evidence to back intrathecal morphine, which this card recommends on the strength of a 30-patient paediatric trial and the adult lumbar fusion literature; the methadone trial is single-centre and adult. The adult complication figures come from prospective multicentre registries and are the numbers to quote when a patient asks how big the operation is.
