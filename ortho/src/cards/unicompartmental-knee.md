## What the surgeon is doing and why

A medial unicompartmental replacement is for the knee whose disease is confined to one compartment: full-thickness cartilage loss on the medial tibiofemoral surface with the lateral compartment and, in the classic indication, the anterior cruciate ligament intact. The anterior cruciate matters because it is what keeps the wear pattern anteromedial and the knee kinematically normal; once it is gone the femur rolls back onto the posterior tibia, the wear becomes posteromedial, and a partial replacement in that knee loosens or dislocates its bearing. The medial collateral ligament has to be intact and the varus deformity correctable, because the operation deliberately does not release it. The surgeon's aim is to resurface the one worn compartment and change nothing else. That sentence carries the whole anaesthetic difference from a [total knee](primary-tka.html).

The operation: a short paramedial incision from the medial pole of the patella to the medial border of the tubercle, a mini-arthrotomy that does not enter the quadriceps tendon or the suprapatellar pouch, and no eversion of the patella. Osteophytes are cleared from the medial femoral condyle and the notch. The tibial plateau is cut: a vertical cut beside the tibial spine and a horizontal cut a few millimetres below the worn surface, removing a thin sliver of medial plateau and the meniscus with it. The femoral condyle is prepared by a saw or mill to accept a resurfacing component, in a mobile-bearing design a spherical one; the amount of bone removed is a few millimetres of distal and posterior condyle. Trials go in and the surgeon checks the gap in flexion and extension with feeler gauges, choosing a bearing thickness that restores the ligaments to their natural tension rather than tightening them; in the mobile-bearing system the bearing has to be snug enough not to dislocate and loose enough not to overcorrect the knee into valgus, which is why overcorrection and bearing dislocation are the characteristic failures. Pulsed lavage, cement on two small surfaces (or a cementless impaction), infiltration and closure. A fixed-bearing design does the same with a polyethylene insert locked to the tray. A lateral unicompartmental replacement does it on the other side through a lateral parapatellar incision for the valgus knee with isolated lateral disease; it is less common, technically harder, and the bearing behaves differently because the lateral compartment is lax in flexion. Robotic systems plan and execute the cuts and are widely used for this operation because precision of component position is what the outcome depends on.

What is not done is the point. No quadriceps split, no patellar eversion, no intramedullary femoral rod in most systems, no medial release, no sacrifice of the cruciates, no balancing of the whole joint, no re-tensioning of collaterals and posterior capsule, no posterior-stabilised box cut, and bone cuts that total a fraction of a total knee's six. The randomised comparison against total replacement in medial compartment osteoarthritis found the same Oxford Knee Score at five and at ten years, similar reoperation rates, fewer complications on the intention-to-treat analysis, and better cost-effectiveness, so the surgeon choosing it is not trading durability for comfort. The partial knee's advantages are recovery and the things that produce recovery: a quadriceps that works on day 0, a knee that bends without a tensioned capsule fighting back, and a pain course that is bone, short and modest.

The pain logic follows. The dominant mechanism is bone: two cancellous cuts and a cement interface in one compartment, which hurt for two days and then fade because nothing is loaded through them that was not loaded before. There is a small capsular contribution from the arthrotomy and the osteophyte clearance, and almost no retension, because the collaterals are returned to their own length rather than set to a new one. The posterior capsule is not needled or stretched the way it is in a total knee. That is why the card sits at 3 and the total knee at 4, and why a catheter has no claim here. In the opioid-prescription comparison, patients after a partial knee were less likely to fill a second opioid prescription (50% against 61%) and far less likely to need more than five (6% against 14%).

## Steps

### 1. Anaesthetic choice and the day-case decision
**Why:** The surgeon wants a patient who stands and walks the same afternoon and goes home that day or the next; the operation is selected for that patient and the pathway is built around it.
**Anaesthesia:** Spinal and general anaesthesia are both defensible. A short-acting spinal (a low dose, no intrathecal morphine) covers the tourniquet and the cement and recedes in time for the afternoon walk; a general anaesthetic with the block and infiltration avoids the urinary retention and the motor block that delay discharge in an elderly patient. The neuraxial evidence that favours a spinal for the total knee is weak and mostly about pulmonary and thromboembolic complications in inpatients; for a 60-minute day case in a fit patient it carries less weight, and the author's practice is to choose by the patient's preference and by how long the surgeon takes. Either way the plan is PROSPECT's total knee skeleton scaled down: paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started before surgery, an adductor canal block, the surgeon's infiltration, and intravenous dexamethasone 8-10 mg, which prolongs the block and reduces rebound pain. In the Danish day-surgery series, 59% of operated patients went home the same day and pain and nausea were the reasons the others stayed, which tells you where the effort goes: the oral regimen started in recovery and the antiemetic given early.

### 2. Positioning, tourniquet and antibiotic
**Why:** The leg hangs free from a thigh holder so the surgeon can flex the knee past 110 degrees to reach the posterior femoral condyle through a small incision; a tourniquet is common for the whole of this short case because the field is small and the bone surfaces need to be dry for cement.
**Anaesthesia:** Cefazolin 2 g IV before the cuff inflates. The tourniquet time is short, usually under an hour, so the cuff pain and reperfusion physiology that trouble a long total knee are minor here; the Cochrane case against the tourniquet (more serious adverse events, more day 1 pain) comes from total knee trials and applies to the partial knee only by extrapolation, so it is a conversation to have with the surgeon rather than a rule. The hanging leg puts the common peroneal nerve against the holder at the fibular neck; check the padding, because a foot drop after a partial knee is otherwise inexplicable.

### 3. Mini-arthrotomy and exposure
**Why:** A short paramedial incision and a capsular incision that stops at the medial border of the patella, without entering the quadriceps tendon or the suprapatellar pouch; the patella is retracted laterally, not everted. The surgeon inspects the lateral compartment, the anterior cruciate and the patellofemoral joint, because finding disease there changes the operation to a total knee.
**Anaesthesia:** The conversion decision is made here, in the first ten minutes, and the surgeon will say so. A conversion means the quadriceps tendon is now split, the patella everted, the posterior capsule needled, the cuts multiplied and the spinal needs another 60-90 minutes; the pain plan moves to the total knee card, the day case becomes an inpatient, and the oral regimen and the IPACK question are reopened. A short-acting spinal dosed for a partial knee is the one that runs out.

### 4. Tibial cut
**Why:** A vertical saw cut beside the medial tibial spine and a horizontal cut below the worn surface remove a sliver of medial plateau with the meniscus. The cut is sized to the thinnest bearing that will balance the knee, because every millimetre of tibia removed weakens the plateau and shifts load onto softer bone.
**Anaesthesia:** The first of two cancellous surfaces, and the one that bleeds after the tourniquet comes down. The saw blade sits a few millimetres in front of the popliteal vessels at the back of the plateau; the surgeon protects them with a retractor and the complication, when it happens, declares itself at tourniquet release. A vertical cut that runs too far posteriorly is the mechanism of the tibial plateau fracture that occasionally turns a day case into a protected-weight-bearing inpatient.

### 5. Femoral preparation and trials
**Why:** The distal and posterior medial condyle are prepared with a saw or a spherical mill to seat the femoral component; in the mobile-bearing design the femur is milled to a sphere so the bearing can slide on it. Trial components and feeler gauges set the flexion and extension gaps equal, and the bearing thickness is chosen to restore, not tighten, the medial collateral ligament. Robotic systems execute the cuts to a plan that has already set these gaps.
**Anaesthesia:** The gauges are the balancing of this operation, and the thing to notice is what they are not: there is no release. The ligaments are returned to their own length, so the retension mechanism that dominates the total knee is absent, and the patient's movement-evoked pain on day 1 is the ache of a cut condyle, not a stretched capsule. The flexion needed to mill the posterior condyle rocks the pelvis in a spinal patient and is the moment a lightly sedated patient complains.

### 6. Cementing, infiltration and closure
**Why:** Pulsed lavage of the two surfaces, cement on both, the tibial component first and then the femoral, a trial bearing while the cement cures, then the definitive bearing. Cementless designs are impacted onto a porous tibial tray and femoral pegs. The surgeon infiltrates the posterior capsule before the components go in, the capsular edges and the subcutaneous tissues after, and closes in layers.
**Anaesthesia:** Two small cement surfaces with no canal: bone cement implantation syndrome after a partial knee is a theoretical entity, and this is not a cement-precautions case. The infiltration carries the back of the knee and the lateral side, where the adductor canal block does not reach; ropivacaine 150-200 mg in 60-100 mL is the usual order of magnitude and leaves room under the ceiling for the block. Tourniquet release after a short inflation produces a small blood pressure dip and a modest bleed into the joint; the tranexamic acid should already be in.

### 7. Recovery, the afternoon walk and discharge
**Why:** The surgeon wants full weight-bearing and active flexion from the first afternoon; the implant is stable from the moment the cement sets, and nothing has been repaired that needs protection. The day-case pathway depends on the patient walking with crutches, passing urine, eating, and understanding the night plan.
**Anaesthesia:** The spinal recedes over two to three hours, the block over 12-24 hours with dexamethasone, and the infiltration over a similar period; the trajectory on this card has pain at 2 on the first evening, 4 on days 1 and 2, and 2 by a week. The first oral doses go in before the patient leaves recovery. Rebound pain when the block recedes is less dramatic than after a total knee because the mechanism under it is smaller, but half of all ambulatory block patients describe it after bone surgery and the written night plan (when to take what, which dose is the rescue) is the single thing that keeps the telephone quiet. A patient who cannot pass urine at six hours after a spinal is the usual reason a day case stays; a short-acting local anaesthetic and no intrathecal opioid are the prevention. The Danish series' 59% same-day discharge is honest about the ceiling: pain and nausea keep two in five overnight even with a good protocol.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is bone in one compartment with no re-tensioning, so the plan is a single-shot block that covers the operation and the first evening, a good infiltration for the back and side of the knee, dexamethasone to stretch both, and an oral regimen that starts in recovery and carries the patient from the first night. PROSPECT has no partial knee guideline; its total knee recommendations (paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, single-shot adductor canal block plus local infiltration, intravenous dexamethasone 10 mg or more, opioid as rescue, no femoral or sciatic block, no epidural, no gabapentinoid) are the nearest evidence and apply with the volume turned down. The adductor canal block is the right block for the same reason it is right for the total knee: the saphenous nerve and the nerve to vastus medialis cover the incision and the medial compartment, and the quadriceps keeps working for the afternoon walk. A femoral block is wrong here for the same reason and more so, because a weak quadriceps on a day-case pathway is a cancelled discharge.

The catheter decision is short: no. There is no tendon under tension, no osteotomy that will be loaded, no re-tensioned capsule, and the patient is meant to be at home; the opioid data show a partial knee needs fewer prescriptions than a total knee, not more. The exception is the opioid-tolerant or chronic pain patient, for whom a planned overnight stay with a good infiltration, ketamine where the unit uses it and a pain service plan is still usually enough without a catheter; a catheter there is author's judgement, not evidence. IPACK earns a place for a lateral partial knee, where the incision and the compartment are outside the adductor canal block's territory, and when the surgeon does not infiltrate the posterior capsule.

Escalation triggers: pain above 6 on day 1 with a working quadriceps is treated with the charted rescue opioid and a check that the regular analgesia is actually being taken; a knee that is tense and swollen with pain out of proportion in the first day is a haemarthrosis and needs the surgeon; a patient whose pain on day 2 or 3 is worse than day 1 after a partial knee, with a plateau that hurts on weight-bearing, has a tibial plateau fracture until an X-ray says otherwise. Rebound on the first night is managed by the regimen already written, and a rescue adductor canal block the next morning is rarely needed.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Conversion to total knee. Decided in the first ten minutes on the state of the lateral compartment, the anterior cruciate and the patellofemoral joint. The spinal dose, the day-case plan and the pain plan all change; a short-acting spinal is the one that runs out.
- Lateral UKA. A lateral parapatellar approach, a valgus knee, a lateral compartment that is lax in flexion and a bearing that dislocates more readily in mobile-bearing designs; the adductor canal block covers less of the incision and IPACK or a wider infiltration is reasonable.
- Mobile versus fixed bearing. The mobile bearing can dislocate (about 1% in the medium term) and presents as a locked, painful knee days to years later; a fixed bearing cannot, and the long-term results are similar.
- Cementless. Impaction, no cure time, a case ten minutes shorter, and no cement step at all.
- Robotic. Pins in the femur and tibia, registration time, and the same pain pattern; the precision argument is strongest for this operation because the component position is the whole outcome.
- Tibial plateau fracture. Intra-operative or in the first weeks, from a vertical cut that goes too far back or a thin osteoporotic plateau; presents as new weight-bearing pain, and the patient is readmitted for protected weight-bearing or fixation.
- Overcorrection. A bearing that is too thick sets the knee into valgus and loads the lateral compartment, which then wears; the pain is late, not post-operative.
- Bilateral partial knees under one anaesthetic. Done in selected patients; two tourniquets, two infiltrations and the local anaesthetic ceiling, but still a shorter and gentler case than one total knee.
- The ACL-deficient knee. Outside the classic indication; some surgeons do a partial knee with an ACL reconstruction in the young, which adds the [ACL card](acl-reconstruction.html) mechanisms (graft harvest site pain, tunnels) to this one.
- Urinary retention after spinal. The common reason a day case stays; a low-dose, short-acting spinal without intrathecal opioid, or a general anaesthetic, is the prevention.
- Obesity. Not a contraindication to the operation in current practice, but the leg holder, the tourniquet fit and the day-case criteria all get harder.

## Key sources

There is no procedure-specific guideline for the partial knee and the card borrows PROSPECT's total knee recommendations and turns the volume down; that scaling is the author's, not PROSPECT's. The TOPKAT trial settles that the partial knee is not a compromise on outcome, and the opioid-prescription comparison and the Danish day-surgery series are the evidence that its pain course is lighter and that same-day discharge is realistic but not universal. The technique description rests on the Oxford mobile-bearing series and on standard surgical texts; the pain score and trajectory are calibrated against the total knee card and are author's judgement.
