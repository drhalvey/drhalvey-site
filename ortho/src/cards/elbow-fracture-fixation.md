## What the surgeon is doing and why

Three fractures of one joint, grouped because the surgeon's logic is the same for all of them: the elbow tolerates neither an incongruent joint surface nor six weeks in a cast, so an intra-articular fracture is reduced anatomically, fixed rigidly enough to move within days, and the price is paid in exposure. What differs is how much exposure. An olecranon is subcutaneous and takes 45 min; a distal humerus is a three-dimensional puzzle behind the triceps and takes three hours; a radial head is a small bone on the lateral side with a nerve wrapped around its neck. The anaesthetic question they share is the one in the trap: a block is the best analgesic for all three, and each has a nerve the surgeon may want to examine before the block would allow.

The olecranon is the triceps' lever arm. A fall onto the point of the elbow breaks it across, the triceps pulls the proximal fragment up, and the joint surface gapes. A tension band (two Kirschner wires down the shaft and a figure-of-eight wire over the dorsal surface) converts the pull of the triceps into compression at the joint line, which is elegant, cheap and works for a simple transverse fracture; it also leaves wires under thin skin that migrate and irritate, and in the pooled comparisons it needed removal in 79 of 236 cases against 41 of 332 after plating, with a complication rate twice that of a plate (RR 0.48 in favour of the plate). A precontoured dorsal plate is used for comminuted, oblique and osteoporotic fractures and is now the commoner choice. In a frail patient a displaced olecranon fracture can be left alone; surgeons vary.

The distal humerus is two columns of bone, medial and lateral, joined by the articular spool of the trochlea and capitellum, and an intercondylar fracture separates the columns from each other and from the shaft. The surgeon has to see the joint surface, reconstruct it, and fix each column with its own plate (parallel or perpendicular, a matter of preference). Seeing the joint from behind means getting past the triceps: a paratricipital approach works on either side of it and gives a limited view; a triceps split or reflection gives more; an olecranon osteotomy (a chevron cut through the olecranon, swung up with the triceps attached) gives the whole articular surface, at the cost of a second fracture that needs its own fixation and fails to unite in a few percent, with complications of 46-55% in comparative series against 27-41% for the paratricipital approach. The ulnar nerve is found and mobilised in every case, because the medial plate goes where the nerve lives; it is injured in a quarter to a third of patients (26.8% with an osteotomy and 33.3% without, in a 134-patient trauma centre cohort, not different), mostly transiently, and many surgeons transpose it. In an osteoporotic patient over 65 with comminution the fragments may not hold screws, and the surgeon converts to a total elbow on the table; the randomised trial of that decision is on the [total elbow card](total-elbow-radial-head.html). Everything about this operation is long: a tourniquet that runs past its limit, an exposure that strips the periosteum off both columns, and a joint that will be stiff if it is not moved early.

The radial head is the lateral pillar of the elbow and the secondary stabiliser against valgus. A fall on the outstretched hand drives it into the capitellum and splits it (Mason type II, two pieces, displaced) or shatters it (type III). Two or three large fragments are fixed with headless screws or a small plate through a lateral approach, with the forearm pronated to carry the posterior interosseous nerve away from the field; more than three fragments are replaced rather than fixed, because in the one adequate trial replacement gave a better score (92 versus 72) and fewer adverse events (6 of 36 versus 15 of 31). A radial head fracture with a dislocation and a coronoid fracture is a terrible triad: the same lateral approach then fixes the coronoid, replaces or fixes the head and repairs the lateral collateral ligament, which turns a 60 min case into two hours and adds a ligament under tension.

The pain logic is bone, and the trajectory is the Part B anchor for a 3: a hard peak on day 1 and 2 from fractured and drilled bone, compressed with metal, falling quickly because nothing is loaded through the fixation. Behind it are contributors that scale with the operation: periosteal stripping along two columns and a reflected or osteotomised triceps in the distal humerus, which put that operation at the top of the band with a week-2 movement-evoked component; a handled ulnar nerve with a neuropathic edge; a tourniquet; and a ligament repair in the terrible triad. The olecranon and the isolated radial head are small versions, and in a counselled patient they are day cases.

## Steps

### 1. Neurology, the fasting state and the block conversation
**Why:** The surgeon needs a documented hand before the nerve is moved, an elbow fixed within a few days of the injury before it stiffens, and to know whether the hand will be examinable in recovery.
**Anaesthesia:** Before any drug, examine and write down ulnar (little finger sensation, first dorsal interosseous), median, radial and posterior interosseous (wrist and finger extension) function; a fracture-dislocation often has a deficit already, and that is the fact that protects everybody later. Then the block conversation, which this card exists for. Olecranon and radial head: the author's practice is a single-shot infraclavicular or supraclavicular block with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, under general anaesthesia, with the surgeon's agreement that the hand will be examined when the block recedes. Distal humerus: the surgeon decides whether they want the ulnar nerve examined in recovery; if they do, the operation runs under general anaesthesia with infiltration and a block is offered once the hand has been seen; if they do not, the block goes in before induction with the agreement written down. The lower-leg guideline's principle transfers: dense, long blocks that far outlast the surgery are the ones that delay a diagnosis, neither surgeon nor anaesthetist vetoes the other, and the patient's consent to the chosen technique is documented. A low-concentration block (ropivacaine 0.2%) without adjunct is the compromise that keeps the analgesia and shortens the blind window. The unfasted emergency patient is on the [hand trauma card](hand-trauma.html).

### 2. Position: lateral or prone with the arm over a post
**Why:** The posterior elbow is reached with the patient lateral or prone and the upper arm supported on a padded post so that the forearm hangs at 90 degrees and the surgeon looks down onto the olecranon; the image intensifier comes in from the opposite side. Supine with the arm across the chest is the alternative for an olecranon and for some surgeons' distal humerus; the radial head is supine on an arm table.
**Anaesthesia:** Lateral and prone mean a tracheal tube, the dependent eye, plexus and peroneal nerve padded, the head neutral in a cradle, and a tube that will not be reached once the drapes and the image intensifier are in place. The post under the humerus is the thing to check: it must sit under the mid-shaft, not in the axilla (plexus) and not at the distal shaft where the radial nerve runs in the spiral groove, because a radial palsy from the post looks exactly like one from the surgery. The high sterile tourniquet goes on before the final positioning. A distal humerus runs long enough that a second check of pressure points at 90 min is worth doing.

### 3. Tourniquet, antibiotic and tranexamic acid
**Why:** The field is bony and the surgeon wants it dry to see the joint surface; the tourniquet is inflated after exsanguination and will be up for the whole of an olecranon fixation and for as long as the surgeon can manage in a distal humerus.
**Anaesthesia:** Cefazolin 2 g IV finished before inflation; tranexamic acid 1 g IV for a distal humerus, on borrowed evidence. The distal humerus is the elbow operation that overruns the tourniquet. Say the time at 90 and 120 min; the surgeon's choices are a release and re-inflation after 10-15 min of reperfusion, or completion of the second column in a bleeding field. Under general anaesthesia the slow rise in pressure and heart rate from 45 min is tourniquet pain and does not answer opioid.

### 4. Olecranon: reduction and tension band or plate
**Why:** A posterior incision curved around the point of the elbow, the fracture cleaned of haematoma, the joint surface reduced under direct vision and held with a clamp, then either two wires and a figure-of-eight tension band or a dorsal plate with screws into the shaft and the proximal fragment. The joint is moved under the image intensifier to check that nothing crosses the articular surface.
**Anaesthesia:** The quick one, 45-90 min, inside a single tourniquet. The ulnar nerve is a centimetre medial to the plate and is protected rather than mobilised in a simple olecranon fracture. Day case in a fit patient with a working block and an oral regimen started; the wires under thin skin are the complaint at six weeks, not the pain on day 1.

### 5. Distal humerus: the ulnar nerve and the approach
**Why:** A long posterior incision, the ulnar nerve identified proximally and freed from the arcade of Struthers to the flexor carpi ulnaris, held on a sling for the rest of the case and usually transposed at the end. Then the choice: paratricipital windows either side of the triceps for an extra-articular or simple fracture, a triceps split or reflection, or an olecranon osteotomy for the comminuted articular fracture, which gives the whole joint but adds a fracture that will not unite in about 2-4%.
**Anaesthesia:** The nerve work is the first hour and the reason for the pre-operative examination. The osteotomy is the step that changes the pain course: a cut olecranon fixed with a tension band or a plate is a second bone operation inside the first, with its own hardware under thin skin, and ulnar neuropathy after a screw-fixed osteotomy ran to 21% in the pooled series. Ask which approach was used before writing the discharge plan: an osteotomy or a reflected triceps means more movement-evoked pain in weeks 1-2 than a paratricipital window.

### 6. Distal humerus: articular reconstruction and two-column plating
**Why:** The joint surface is rebuilt first, fragment by fragment, held with wires and then compressed with a screw across the trochlea; the reconstructed spool is then attached to the shaft with a plate on each column, parallel (medial and lateral) or perpendicular (medial and posterolateral), with as many screws into the distal fragments as the bone will take. The periosteum is stripped along both columns to seat the plates. The elbow is then moved through its arc under the image intensifier.
**Anaesthesia:** The long middle of the operation, usually 90-150 min on its own. Warm the patient, because a long case in lateral with an open joint loses heat steadily. The periosteal stripping is the mechanism that makes this fixation hurt more and for longer than its bone volume suggests. In an osteoporotic patient this is the step at which the surgeon finds that the screws will not hold and converts to a total elbow: cement, a frail patient, and the [total elbow card's](total-elbow-radial-head.html) cement step in a patient who has already been under anaesthesia for two hours. If the tourniquet has been released, bleeding from the cancellous surfaces is steady rather than brisk, and the blood loss on this card is mostly accrued now.

### 7. Radial head: the lateral approach and the posterior interosseous nerve
**Why:** A lateral incision over the radial head through the Kocher interval (anconeus and extensor carpi ulnaris) or a common extensor split, the forearm pronated to swing the posterior interosseous nerve away from the radial neck, the fragments reduced and fixed with headless compression screws placed in the safe zone (the 90 degree arc that does not articulate with the ulna) or a low-profile plate. In a terrible triad the coronoid is reached through the same wound before the head is dealt with, and the lateral collateral ligament is repaired to the epicondyle at the end.
**Anaesthesia:** Supine on an arm table, 60-120 min, standard tourniquet. The posterior interosseous nerve is the nerve of this operation; a wrist drop after a radial head fixation is a retractor or a plate that is too distal, and another reason the hand was examined before the block. An isolated radial head fixation is a day case; a terrible triad with a ligament repair stays a night and has a tendon-tension element the isolated fracture does not.

### 8. Ulnar nerve transposition, tourniquet release and closure
**Why:** At the end of a distal humerus fixation most surgeons move the ulnar nerve into an anterior subcutaneous pocket so that it does not lie on the medial plate, checking that it is not kinked at the septum or the new entry. The tourniquet comes down, bleeding is dealt with, a drain goes in, and the posterior wound over the olecranon is closed in layers. An olecranon fixation is closed over the wires or plate with no drain. A splint holds the elbow at 90 degrees for comfort.
**Anaesthesia:** Tourniquet release after a long inflation brings the reperfusion dip and the washout; after a distal humerus the pressure takes minutes to recover. Give the antiemetic. If a block has been placed, the splint and sling go on before the patient lightens.

### 9. Recovery: the hand, the first night and the rescue block
**Why:** The surgeon wants a working hand, a dry wound, and movement started within days, because a fixed elbow that is not moved becomes a stiff elbow that needs a release.
**Anaesthesia:** The hand is examined at the time that was agreed: in recovery if no block was placed, or when the block recedes at 12-24 h if it was. A new ulnar deficit after a distal humerus is in the expected 25-33% and is usually traction; a dense new palsy that was not there before the block is the surgeon's to see. If the surgeon wanted an unblocked recovery, this is where the block is offered: a single-shot block in recovery once the hand has been documented, or the next morning, gives the patient the first night. Oral paracetamol and an NSAID start in recovery; an oral opioid is charted for the first three nights. In the 972-patient ambulatory cohort half had rebound pain, with bone surgery (OR 1.82) and no intravenous dexamethasone (OR 1.78) raising the odds; every operation on this card is bone surgery and the dexamethasone was given for it. The olecranon and radial head patient go home the same day or the next morning; the distal humerus stays one to three nights, longer with an osteotomy, an older patient or a conversion.

## Pain plan

The dominant mechanism is bone, which is moderate, short and NSAID-responsive, so the analgesic shape is a block for the first night and an oral regimen that carries the next three days, with the catheter kept for the long, stripped, early-moved distal humerus. The complication that argues against the block is nerve injury, and the plan has to answer both. There is no procedure-specific guideline for any elbow fracture and no randomised analgesia trial; the plan is the mechanism, the general ambulatory block data, and the principle borrowed from the lower-leg compartment syndrome guideline that a dense block far outlasting the surgery delays a diagnosis and a low-concentration one with good surveillance does not.

The defensible default for an olecranon or radial head fixation is a single-shot infraclavicular or supraclavicular block with 20-25 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% (or 0.2% when the surgeon wants a shorter blind window), intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, paracetamol and an NSAID started before the block recedes, and an oral opioid for the first three nights with a written night plan. The nerve examination is before the block and again when it recedes, and the surgeon has agreed to that sequence.

For a distal humerus fixation the default is decided with the surgeon, case by case, and written down. If the surgeon has seen the nerve intact and untethered and runs an early motion programme, a single shot or a continuous infraclavicular catheter is reasonable, and the catheter earns its place for the osteotomy, the reflected triceps, the opioid-tolerant patient and the stiff elbow that will need physiotherapy twice a day; the author's practice is a single shot in most and a catheter after that conversation. If the surgeon wants the hand examined in recovery, the operation runs under general anaesthesia with infiltration of the wound, and the block is offered as a rescue once the hand is documented. A ropivacaine 0.2% block without adjunct is the middle path: most of the analgesia with a shorter, lighter block that lets a dense motor deficit show through.

Escalation: pain above 7 after the block has gone, with a soft wound, warm fingers and the splint on, is treated with the charted opioid and a check that the regular doses were given. Pain out of proportion in a tense posterior wound after a distal humerus is a haematoma over a transposed nerve and is surgical. A tense forearm with pain on passive finger extension is a compartment syndrome until proven otherwise; it is rare after elbow fixation, but the high-energy fracture-dislocation is the patient it happens to and the reason the lower-leg guideline's logic was applied. Stable ring and little finger dysaesthesia is traction neuritis and settles over weeks; a deficit that worsens over days is a nerve under pressure and is also surgical.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Tension band versus plate for the olecranon. Same fixation principle, twice the complication rate and twice the removal rate with wires; the anaesthetic is identical.
- Osteotomy versus paratricipital for the distal humerus. The osteotomy gives the view and adds a fracture; complications in comparative series 46-55% against 27-41%, with the same ulnar neuropathy rate, and more movement-evoked pain in weeks 1-2.
- Conversion to total elbow. The osteoporotic patient over 65 whose fragments will not hold a screw; five of 21 in the randomised trial. Position and tourniquet stay, cement arrives, and the frailest patient of the day gets the longest case.
- The ulnar nerve. Mobilised in every distal humerus, transposed by most, injured in a quarter to a third, mostly transiently. Documented before, examined at the agreed time, and the timing is the surgeon's decision as much as yours.
- The posterior interosseous nerve. The radial head's nerve; pronation protects it, a distal retractor or plate injures it, and a wrist drop after a block has gone is surgical.
- Terrible triad. Radial head, coronoid, lateral collateral ligament, sometimes the medial; two hours, a ligament under tension, a hinged brace and one night.
- The high-energy elbow. A fracture-dislocation with a swollen forearm is the patient in whom a compartment syndrome is possible and a dense long block is the wrong choice; a low-concentration block without adjunct, hourly observations and a documented decision are the guideline's answer.
- The post under the arm. A radial palsy from a post at the spiral groove is indistinguishable from a surgical one; check the post sits under the mid-shaft before draping.
- Heterotopic ossification. Common after distal humerus fixation, more after a head injury; some surgeons give an NSAID for prophylaxis, which is the NSAID you were charting.
- The open fracture. Its own antibiotic pathway, its own timing and its own card ([open fractures](open-fractures-damage-control.html)).

## Key sources

The surgical evidence is reasonable: a meta-analysis of tension band against plate for the olecranon, a systematic review of 1,700 olecranon osteotomies, a 134-patient cohort comparing osteotomy with the paratricipital approach for ulnar neuropathy, a Cochrane review of radial head fixation against replacement, and the single randomised trial of fixation against arthroplasty for the elderly distal humerus. The anaesthetic evidence is not procedure-specific: the block-versus-nerve-check question is answered by analogy with the Association of Anaesthetists' lower-leg compartment syndrome guideline and by the general data on nerve injury after blocks and on rebound pain, and the catheter advice is author's judgement. The pain score is the Part B anchor for elbow fracture fixation.
