## What the surgeon is doing and why

A knee replacement heals by scar, and a knee that is not moved while it heals scars shut. The suprapatellar pouch, which has to glide 5 cm over the femoral component for the knee to flex, obliterates; the medial and lateral gutters fill; the fat pad fibroses to the front of the tibial tray and tethers the patellar tendon; the posterior capsule, if the knee was never fully straightened, shortens. The patient arrives at six weeks with a knee that flexes to 70 or 80 degrees against a hard end point. Acquired stiffness affects about 3.6-4.0% of knee arthroplasties and manipulation is performed in 2.6-3.6% of them (3.4% in a national database of over half a million knees, cemented and cementless alike). The risk factors in the case-control data are the patient's rather than the surgeon's: diabetes, smoking, a pre-operative range under 100 degrees, and youth (age 65 or over was protective, OR 0.17), so the patient on this list is younger than the average arthroplasty patient.

Stiffness is a diagnosis of exclusion. Before the surgeon books a manipulation they have excluded infection (inflammatory markers and an aspiration), component malposition or oversizing, instability, patella baja and a mechanically blocked knee, because manipulating any of those gains nothing and risks a fracture. What is left is arthrofibrosis: immature scar in a joint that was built correctly. The surgeon's reasoning is mechanical. Young adhesions tear at a force well below the force that breaks the distal femur or avulses the patellar tendon from the tubercle; mature scar does not. That gap is the timing window. Manipulation within 6-12 weeks gains the most range; in a cohort of 84 stiff knees the gain was 28 and 30 degrees when treated within three and six months of the arthroplasty and 21 degrees beyond six months, and every review since 2010 has said the same thing in different numbers. Past three months the surgeon is weighing a smaller gain against a higher fracture risk, and past six they usually move to arthroscopic arthrolysis, which cuts the scar rather than tearing it.

The manipulation itself is a controlled tear. With the patient fully relaxed, the surgeon flexes the hip to 90 degrees, puts a hand on the proximal tibia close to the joint so the lever arm is short, and flexes the knee slowly and steadily, feeling and often hearing the adhesions give in the pouch and the gutters, pausing at each new end point, and stopping when nothing more yields or when the resistance changes character from fibrous to bony. A long lever at the ankle multiplies the moment at the supracondylar femur, which is how the fractures happen, and a sudden jerk is how the patellar tendon comes off the tubercle. If there is a flexion contracture, the heel goes on a bolster and the surgeon presses the distal femur towards the table to stretch the posterior capsule. In the largest cohort, 344 knees manipulated at a mean of 2.9 months, the range improved by 36 degrees and the gain held at five years; the complication rate was 2.3%, and the list of complications (haematoma, wound dehiscence, infection, tibial tubercle fracture, patellar tendon rupture) is the list of what force does to a healing knee.

Arthroscopic arthrolysis is the escalation for the knee that is too late, too tight or has failed one manipulation. Through standard portals the surgeon resects the fibrous mass in the suprapatellar pouch and both gutters with a shaver and a radiofrequency probe, frees the fat pad from the tibial tray and the patellar tendon from the anterior tibia, sometimes releases the posterior capsule, and then manipulates a knee whose scar has already been divided. The gain is the same as manipulation alone (24.8 against 25.4 degrees in the direct comparison), the complication rates are similar, and open arthrolysis gains less than either. Revision arthroplasty for stiffness is the last resort and the worst performer: in the pooled literature 43% of stiffness revisions needed further treatment against 25% of arthroscopic releases and 17% of manipulations, which is why the surgeon tries the small operation first and early.

The pain logic follows from what is torn. The dominant mechanism is capsule: adhesions ripped off the pouch and gutters leave raw, bleeding synovial surfaces and a haemarthrosis, sharp with movement and settling in days, with no bone cut and no tendon repaired. Beneath it is the re-tensioned joint of the [primary arthroplasty](primary-tka.html), pushed hourly through an arc it has not used since surgery; that deep ache is familiar from the first week after the index operation. The score is 3: a block or neuraxial technique is expected, the worst pain is on days 1 and 2 when the knee is being bent through the haemarthrosis, and it falls quickly because nothing is being loaded through a cut. The rebound risk is high for a reason specific to this operation: when a single-shot block recedes at 2 am and the patient stops moving, the price is the range that was won that morning. Manipulated patients end up with less flexion than uncomplicated knees (114 against 125 degrees in one matched cohort) and a higher five-year revision rate (7.5% against 2.7%), which is the measure of how much there is to lose.

## Steps

### 1. The decision: exclusion, timing and what the surgeon wants
**Why:** The surgeon manipulates only a knee that is stiff for want of movement, inside the window in which scar tears before bone breaks, and only if the physiotherapy afterwards can be delivered. The booking should carry three things: the current range, the range they expect to reach, and the plan for the afternoon.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask for all three. A knee at 75 degrees being taken to 110 is a 10-minute day case with a single-shot block; a knee at 50 degrees at four months, or a second manipulation (which gains about half as much as the first), is a knee with a lot to lose overnight and the catheter conversation starts here. Confirm the inflammatory markers and aspiration have been done, because a manipulation in an infected knee is the operation nobody wanted. Confirm the chemoprophylaxis status from the index operation, which sets the neuraxial timing and the size of the haemarthrosis to expect. Note the bone quality, the steroid history and the age: the fracture risk is an osteoporotic risk, and the patient with rheumatoid disease on prednisolone is the one some surgeons will not manipulate at all.

### 2. Anaesthetic technique: a fully relaxed quadriceps and a patient who can bend the knee by lunchtime
**Why:** A guarding quadriceps is the force path that breaks the femur: the surgeon is pushing against the muscle rather than the scar, applies more force to get through it, and the force arrives at the supracondylar bone and the tubercle. The surgeon needs a leg with no tone at all for five minutes and then a patient who can flex actively within hours.
**Anaesthesia:** Two techniques deliver the first requirement and they differ on the second. A spinal gives perfect relaxation and a quiet patient, and its motor block then delays active physiotherapy by two to four hours; a short-acting or low-dose spinal with no intrathecal opioid narrows that gap. A general anaesthetic with full neuromuscular block (rocuronium, with sugammadex to reverse it completely before the patient wakes) gives the same flaccid leg, a fast wake and an afternoon session two hours later; a general anaesthetic without a relaxant, deepened until the leg goes limp, is the technique in which the surgeon pushes against tone and should be avoided. The author's practice for a day-case manipulation is the general anaesthetic with full block and an adductor canal block placed before induction with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg; the spinal is kept for the patient who prefers it or whose airway argues for it. The adductor canal block is chosen over a femoral block because the physiotherapist will ask for active flexion and a straight-leg raise that afternoon. There is no trial of anaesthetic technique for manipulation; this is judgement.

### 3. Position, the table and the range measured asleep
**Why:** The surgeon needs the leg free of straps and the knee clear of the table edge, a flat table so the hip can be flexed to 90 degrees, and a first measurement of range under anaesthesia, which is often 10 degrees more than the clinic figure because the patient is no longer guarding.
**Anaesthesia:** Unstrap the leg, take the foot off any footplate and protect the contralateral leg from the surgeon's weight. The airway in a general anaesthetic has to tolerate the surgeon leaning on the pelvis, which argues for a supraglottic device checked with the hip flexed or a tracheal tube.

### 4. The flexion manipulation
**Why:** Slow, sustained flexion with the hand high on the tibia and the lever short; the adhesions in the pouch and gutters give with a palpable and audible tearing, the surgeon holds at each new end point for the scar to yield rather than the bone, and stops when the end point turns hard. A sudden jerk or a long lever at the ankle is the mechanism of a supracondylar fracture, a tibial tubercle avulsion or a patellar tendon rupture, and an osteoporotic femur or a recent tubercle osteotomy raises the risk.
**Anaesthesia:** Confirm full neuromuscular block before the surgeon starts, not when they comment on the leg; a train-of-four count of zero is the standard for this step in a general anaesthetic. Under a spinal the block height needs to reach the hip flexors, which it will. The manipulation is the one nociceptive moment of the operation and a lightly anaesthetised patient will show it with a pressure and heart rate rise; a brief deepening at the request is correct. Listen: the tearing of adhesions is a series of crepitant releases, a single loud crack followed by a knee that suddenly flexes freely with a soft, abnormal end point is a fracture or an avulsion, and the surgeon will stop and ask for an image intensifier or a radiograph in recovery. A bleeding haemarthrosis begins now.

### 5. The extension manipulation and the posterior capsule
**Why:** A flexion contracture of more than 10 degrees is addressed with the heel on a bolster and firm pressure over the distal femur, stretching the posterior capsule and the hamstring insertions. The surgeon is more cautious here because the posterior capsule is thick, mature scar yields poorly, and the force goes through the femoral component into the distal femur.
**Anaesthesia:** The same requirements: no tone, a short period of deep anaesthesia, and a watch for the crack. Extension loss is also the component of stiffness the physiotherapists find hardest to hold, which is an argument for the catheter when the contracture was the main problem.

### 6. Measurement, injection, dressing and no splint
**Why:** The surgeon measures and records the range achieved, which is the target for the physiotherapist; some inject the joint with local anaesthetic and a corticosteroid, some aspirate the haemarthrosis, and most apply a light dressing with no brace of any kind because the knee has to move within hours. Some put the leg straight onto a continuous passive motion machine in recovery.
**Anaesthesia:** Reverse the block completely and wake the patient promptly; this is a case where a 20-minute delay in recovery is a 20-minute delay in the first physiotherapy session. Give the ondansetron. If the surgeon injects local anaesthetic into the joint, add it to the day's ropivacaine arithmetic alongside the block.

### 7. Arthroscopic arthrolysis
**Why:** For the late, the very tight and the previously manipulated knee, the surgeon divides the scar before manipulating it: anterolateral and anteromedial portals, often a superolateral portal to reach the pouch, resection of the fibrous mass in the suprapatellar pouch and the gutters with a shaver and a radiofrequency probe, release of the fat pad from the tray and of the patellar tendon from the anterior tibia, and sometimes a posterior capsular release from inside the joint. The instruments are working next to a polyethylene insert and a metal femoral component, so the surgeon is slow and careful, and the vascular scar bleeds into the view. The manipulation follows with the scar already cut.
**Anaesthesia:** This is a 45-90 minute arthroscopy of a joint that contains an implant: cefazolin 2 g IV before the knife, a tourniquet in some hands, litres of irrigation and a cold patient, and a larger haemarthrosis afterwards because divided scar bleeds more than torn scar. The pain afterwards is the manipulation's pain plus portals and a distended joint, and a spinal, if one was chosen, has to be dosed for the length. This is the case in which the author places an adductor canal catheter as routine, because the patient has already failed the small operation once and the stakes for the first 48 hours are higher.

### 8. Open arthrolysis and quadricepsplasty
**Why:** Rare, and done when the stiffness is extra-articular or the arthroscope cannot reach it: the old arthrotomy is reopened, the pouch and gutters are cleared under direct vision, the quadriceps is freed from the femur, and for the knee that will not flex because the extensor mechanism is short a V-Y quadricepsplasty lengthens the tendon, or the stiffness is treated by [revision](revision-tka.html) of oversized or malrotated components. The exposure is that of a revision and the quadriceps has been cut.
**Anaesthesia:** This is a different operation wearing the same booking: 90-150 minutes, real blood loss, a quadriceps that has been lengthened and will be weak and painful for six weeks, and a catheter as routine, with the revision card's plan rather than this one's. A quadricepsplasty also changes what the physiotherapist may ask on day 1, and the surgeon's instructions on active extension need to reach the ward.

### 9. Recovery, the first physiotherapy session and the first night
**Why:** The surgeon wants the knee taken through the new arc the same afternoon and several times a day thereafter, either by a physiotherapist or on a passive motion machine, because the torn surfaces will re-adhere within days if left still. The first 48 hours decide whether the manipulation worked.
**Anaesthesia:** The first physiotherapy session happens within two to four hours of the manipulation, so the block has to be working and the patient awake, not nauseated and not sedated. The trajectory on this card has pain at 3 that evening with the block working, 5 on day 1 and 4 on day 2 when the knee is being pushed through the haemarthrosis, and 2 at a week. Regular paracetamol and an NSAID go in before the block recedes; the oral opioid is charted for the nights and for 30 minutes before each physiotherapy session, which is the one timing that matters; and the night plan is explained before the patient leaves recovery, because the patient who wakes in pain at 2 am and lies still until morning has lost range by the time the physiotherapist arrives. Observations on the first evening are for the complications that force produces: a knee that will not straight-leg raise is a patellar tendon or tubercle avulsion until proven otherwise, a knee that is tense and far more painful than expected is a haemarthrosis or a fracture, and both need the surgeon and a radiograph that evening.

### 10. Discharge and the week
**Why:** Day case is reasonable for an early manipulation of a mildly stiff knee with a physiotherapy session completed before discharge, daily outpatient physiotherapy booked and the patient able to do the exercises alone; otherwise one to three nights. The range is checked at a week and at six weeks, and a knee that has drifted back may be manipulated again, with a smaller gain the second time.
**Anaesthesia:** The written plan for the week: regular analgesia for five to seven days, the opioid before exercise sessions rather than after, ice and elevation for the haemarthrosis, and the exercises themselves. A catheter patient goes home with the pump if the unit runs ambulatory infusions, or stays until it comes out and the oral regimen has carried a day of physiotherapy. Pain that rises through the week rather than falling, or a knee that becomes hot, is an infection or a haematoma and is the surgeon's.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is torn capsule and synovium with a haemarthrosis, loaded by deliberate hourly flexion from the first afternoon, so the plan is not built to make the patient comfortable at rest; it is built to make a specific afternoon and a specific next morning possible. The skeleton is the primary arthroplasty guideline's, extrapolated because PROSPECT does not cover manipulation: paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started before the block recedes, a single-shot adductor canal block, intravenous dexamethasone, and opioid as rescue, with the femoral block and the epidural deprecated on functional grounds. For a manipulation the functional argument is the whole argument: the adductor canal block preserves the quadriceps the physiotherapist needs for active flexion and a straight-leg raise, which is also the test that detects an avulsed tendon.

The catheter decision is author's judgement on thin evidence. The one series that used a continuous technique, 65 refractory knees manipulated under an epidural that was then run for one to three days with continuous passive motion and daily physiotherapy, gained 31 degrees and succeeded in 74%, and it also recorded a subdural haematoma and a fatal pulmonary embolism, which is the cost of an epidural in a patient who then does not walk. An adductor canal catheter does the same job for the medial knee without the bladder catheter, the hypotension or the immobility, and the author's practice is to place one for the knee with more than about 30 degrees to recover, the second manipulation, the late manipulation, every arthrolysis, and the patient whose stiffness came with pain out of proportion, in whom the stiffness and the pain are the same disease. For the early manipulation of a mildly stiff knee a single shot with a written physiotherapy plan is enough, and the day case is reasonable.

Rebound is the specific hazard. In the ambulatory block cohort rebound pain occurred in half of all patients, more in the young and in women and less with intravenous dexamethasone on board. What it costs here is range, so the night plan (the oral regimen started before the block recedes, the opioid timed before exercises, a passive motion machine where the unit has one) is part of the operation. Escalation triggers: pain above 7 with a soft knee and a working straight-leg raise is a prescription problem; pain above 7 with a tense, hot, hugely swollen knee is a haemarthrosis and may need aspiration; any loss of active extension is an extensor mechanism injury until the surgeon says otherwise; a crack during the manipulation followed by pain on weight-bearing is a supracondylar or tubercle fracture and the radiograph is taken before discharge, not at the first clinic visit.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The late manipulation. Beyond three months the gain is smaller (about 21 degrees against 28-30 degrees earlier) and the scar is tougher, so the force is higher and the fracture risk with it; many surgeons move to arthroscopic arthrolysis, which is a longer case with a bigger haemarthrosis and the catheter case on this card.
- The second manipulation. Gains about half of the first (16.8 degrees in the French cohort); the patient has already lost one afternoon's range to a bad night and the plan should assume the catheter.
- Osteoporosis, steroids and rheumatoid disease. The fracture risk is a bone-quality risk; the surgeon may limit the force, image the knee first, or decline. Steroid cover applies as usual.
- Flexion contracture as the main problem. The extension manipulation is the harder one to hold and the posterior capsule is not covered by any anterior block; the surgeon's intra-articular injection and the oral regimen carry it.
- The painful stiff knee and complex regional pain syndrome. Stiffness with allodynia, colour change and pain out of proportion is a different disease; the epidural series was built for these patients, a catheter and the pain service are both reasonable, and manipulation without a plan for the pain afterwards makes it worse.
- Anticoagulation. Patients inside the chemoprophylaxis window bleed a larger haemarthrosis and the neuraxial timing rules apply; a patient on long-term anticoagulation needs it paused for an arthrolysis but not necessarily for a closed manipulation, which is the surgeon's call.
- Arthroscopy in a joint with an implant. Cefazolin before incision, instruments kept off the polyethylene, and a lower threshold to call a hot knee in the following week an infection.
- Stiffness after ligament reconstruction or fracture. The same technique on a native knee with a cyclops lesion after an anterior cruciate reconstruction or after a plateau fixation; the fracture risk is in the healing bone rather than the supracondylar femur, and the surgeon often prefers an arthroscopic release first.
- The day case. Reasonable for the early manipulation with a session done before discharge, daily outpatient physiotherapy booked and a written night plan; not for the arthrolysis or the catheter patient unless the unit runs ambulatory infusions.

## Key sources

The surgical evidence is level III and IV throughout: cohorts and systematic reviews of cohorts, consistent on the timing window, the size of the gain and the low but specific complication rate. The one series of a continuous analgesic technique is an epidural cohort of 65 knees from 2006 with two serious complications. There is no trial of anaesthetic technique for manipulation and no procedure-specific analgesia guideline; the block choice is extrapolated from PROSPECT's primary arthroplasty guideline on functional grounds, and the catheter advice and the general-anaesthetic-with-full-block preference are the author's judgement from the mechanism and are labelled as such.
