## What the surgeon is doing and why

The posterior fusion on the previous card reaches the disc by stripping the back muscles, removing a facet and retracting the roots. The anterior and lateral fusions reach the same disc from the front or the side, through the abdomen or the flank, so that none of that happens: the paraspinal muscles are untouched, the canal is never opened, the roots are never retracted, and the surgeon gets a view of the whole disc and both endplates that allows a cage two or three times the size of anything that fits through a foramen. A big cage on the strong ring of cortical bone at the edge of the endplate restores disc height and lordosis better, subsides less and fuses more reliably than a small posterior one, and it decompresses the foramen indirectly by lifting the vertebra off the root. The price is paid in a different currency: between the skin and the disc lie the great vessels, the ureter, the bowel, the sympathetic chain and the hypogastric plexus (anterior), or the psoas and the lumbar plexus running through it (lateral). The pain profile and the catastrophe profile both follow from that.

The anterior lumbar interbody fusion is an operation at L5-S1 and L4-5, occasionally L3-4. The patient is supine with the lumbar spine extended over a break in the table. A 6-7 cm transverse or paramedian incision to the left of the midline, the anterior rectus sheath opened, the rectus mobilised, and the peritoneum swept off the posterior wall by hand into the retroperitoneum, with the ureter identified and carried with it. At L5-S1 the disc lies below the aortic bifurcation and between the common iliac vessels; the median sacral vessels are divided and the superior hypogastric plexus, which lies on the front of the disc and carries the sympathetic supply to the bladder neck, is swept aside without monopolar diathermy because burning it causes retrograde ejaculation. At L4-5 the disc lies behind the left common iliac vein, which has to be mobilised and retracted to the right after the iliolumbar vein that tethers it is ligated; this is the step with the vascular risk, and the reason many spine surgeons work with a vascular or general access surgeon. The disc is excised, the endplates prepared, the space distracted and a large lordotic cage, often with integrated screws, impacted in. Posterior pedicle screws through percutaneous stab incisions, prone, under the same or a second anaesthetic, complete the construct in most hands.

The lateral lumbar interbody fusion (transpsoas, XLIF, DLIF) is an operation at L1-2 to L4-5; L5-S1 is hidden behind the iliac crest. The patient is in true lateral decubitus, taped at the chest and pelvis, with the table broken at the crest to open the space between the ribs and the pelvis. A 3-4 cm flank incision, the abdominal wall muscles split, the retroperitoneum entered and the peritoneum swept forwards, and then the psoas. The lumbar plexus runs within the posterior part of the psoas and migrates forwards at lower levels, so the surgeon passes a stimulating probe and serial dilators through the muscle under directional electromyographic guidance, looking for a corridor that does not fire a quadriceps or an adductor, and then opens a retractor against the muscle for the length of the disc preparation. The disc is removed across its whole width, the contralateral annulus released, and a wide cage placed across both rings of the endplate. The oblique approach (OLIF, anterior to psoas) passes in front of the psoas rather than through it, trading plexus risk for a longer path and the vessels in the way. Both are usually followed by percutaneous posterior screws, prone.

The pain logic. The mechanism is visceral: an abdominal wall or flank incision through muscle, a peritoneum that has been swept and a retroperitoneum retracted for an hour, and a bowel handled from the outside that will be slow for a day or two. Nothing posterior has been stripped, no root has been retracted, and the bone work is confined to the endplates, which are inside the disc and do not hurt the way a decorticated transverse process does. The result is a day 1 peak around 5, driven by the wound and by the first stand, and a quick fall. The lateral approach adds two things of its own: a psoas that has been split and held apart, which gives hip flexor weakness and a deep groin ache for days to weeks, and a lumbar plexus that has been stretched or bruised, which gives the anterior thigh numbness or burning that about one in seven patients has acutely and most lose within three months. That nerve component is the difference between the two approaches for the patient, and the thing to warn them about. When a posterior stage is added, its mechanisms are added with it; the score of 3 on this card is for the anterior or lateral stage alone, and the combined case sits at the [posterior lumbar fusion](posterior-lumbar-fusion.html) score of 4.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative planning: the vessels, the access surgeon and the two-stage question
**Why:** The surgeon has studied the vascular anatomy on the imaging (the level of the bifurcation, the position of the left iliac vein, a high or low confluence), has booked an access surgeon or will do the exposure themselves, and has decided whether the posterior fixation follows under the same anaesthetic.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask the three questions that change the day: which level (L4-5 is the vascular step for an ALIF and the level at which the plexus sits furthest forward in the psoas for an LLIF), who is doing the exposure and whether they stay in the building, and whether the patient is turned prone for screws afterwards. A crossmatch for every ALIF and for multilevel lateral cases, because the venous injury happens in seconds. Document leg neurology and hip flexor strength on the operative side before an LLIF, because weakness afterwards needs a baseline. The opioid history matters less than on the posterior card but is still taken in milligrams.

### 2. Induction, lines and the anaesthetic that neuromonitoring allows
**Why:** The lateral approach depends on directional electromyography of the psoas and the plexus to find a safe corridor; the anterior approach depends on a vascular field that can be controlled at once if it bleeds.
**Anaesthesia:** A tracheal tube; two large cannulae for an ALIF and an arterial line for any case at L4-5 with vein mobilisation, for multilevel lateral work and for the patient with cardiac disease. For an LLIF, no neuromuscular block after the intubating dose, and sugammadex to make sure it is gone before the psoas is entered, because the electromyographic probe needs a muscle that can respond; a volatile anaesthetic is acceptable for electromyography alone, and a propofol infusion is needed only if the surgeon also runs motor evoked potentials. Tell the surgeon which you have done. A urinary catheter for both approaches; a nasogastric tube is the surgeon's call for a transperitoneal exposure and comes out at the end.

### 3. Positioning: supine with extension, or true lateral with the table broken
**Why:** The ALIF needs the lumbar spine extended so the disc opens anteriorly and the cage can restore lordosis; the LLIF needs a true lateral so that the fluoroscopy, the retractor and the cage all pass orthogonally, and a table break to lift the iliac crest out of the way of L4-5.
**Anaesthesia:** ALIF: a bolster or table break under the lumbar spine, arms secured where the fluoroscopy arm can swing, the sacrum and heels padded. LLIF: the patient on the side with the hip over the break, taped across the chest and the pelvis so the surgeon can lean on them, the dependent arm on a board, an axillary roll under the thorax rather than in the axilla, the upper arm on a rest, the dependent knee flexed and the legs separated by a pillow, the head in line with the spine. The break then flexes the patient at the flank, which stretches the plexus on the upper side and loads the dependent hip, and the surgeon will want it as open as possible; a compromise the monitoring team can accept is better than a femoral nerve that does not recover. Check the eyes and the dependent ear once the drapes are on.

### 4. Incision and the retroperitoneal sweep
**Why:** Anterior: the rectus is mobilised and the hand enters the retroperitoneum, sweeping the peritoneum and its contents medially and up until the psoas and then the great vessels are felt. Lateral: a flank incision, the three abdominal wall muscles split in the line of their fibres, and the peritoneum swept forwards with a finger until the psoas is palpable.
**Anaesthesia:** Antibiotic and tranexamic acid finished before the knife. The peritoneum is breached in 2-16% of retroperitoneal cases, usually without consequence; a transperitoneal conversion changes nothing for you except a longer ileus. The sweep is the first moment of vagal bradycardia, and the retractor placed afterwards keeps the stimulus going. The bowel is being handled from outside its peritoneum, which is why the gut is slow tomorrow.

### 5. ALIF: vessel mobilisation and the exposure of the disc
**Why:** At L5-S1 the surgeon works between the common iliac vessels and divides the median sacral vessels; at L4-5 the left common iliac vein is mobilised off the disc after the iliolumbar vein is ligated, and the great vessels are retracted to the right. Monopolar diathermy is avoided on the front of the disc to spare the hypogastric plexus.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step. A torn common iliac vein, a cava or an iliolumbar vein that has pulled off bleeds faster than any orthopaedic injury on this site, with the field filling before the surgeon has said a word. The response is the one you have already set up: blood in the room, a second cannula running, the access surgeon's hands on the vessel, pressure and packing while the anaesthetic catches up, and an arterial line placed at the start rather than now. Venous air embolism is possible with an open vein in a patient whose spine is extended above the heart; a fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide at this moment is air or hypovolaemia and both are treated by flooding the field and lowering the head. Retractor blades resting on the iliac vein for an hour are the mechanism of the post-operative iliac vein thrombosis, and the surgeon will release them periodically. A left-leg arterial occlusion from a retracted iliac artery in an atherosclerotic patient is the quieter catastrophe: a pulse oximeter on the left foot during the exposure tells you before recovery does.

### 6. LLIF: the transpsoas corridor under electromyography
**Why:** A stimulating probe is advanced through the psoas at the position chosen on the lateral image, usually the anterior half of the disc, and the monitoring team reports the current at which it fires the leg; a high threshold means the plexus is far away, a low one means it is close and the surgeon moves forward. Serial dilators and then a retractor open the corridor and hold the muscle apart.
**Anaesthesia:** No block, as planned, and the patient may twitch; a twitch is the test working. The threshold the surgeon accepts before advancing is their convention; a sustained low one at L4-5 is the moment a surgeon abandons the level or changes approach. The time the retractor is open is the mechanism of the thigh dysaesthesia and the hip flexor weakness, because the plexus is being stretched over the blade and the psoas crushed; a reminder of the elapsed time from your side is welcome by most surgeons. The genitofemoral nerve runs on the front of the psoas and is the cause of the groin numbness the patient will mention tomorrow.

### 7. Discectomy, endplate preparation and contralateral release
**Why:** The disc is removed across its width with shavers and curettes, the cartilaginous endplates scraped to bleeding bone, and in a lateral approach the far-side annulus is released with a Cobb elevator pushed across to the opposite edge, so that the cage can sit on both rings of cortical bone.
**Anaesthesia:** In an LLIF the instrument passed across the disc space reaches the far annulus, beyond which lie the opposite psoas, the plexus and, in front, the great vessels; the contralateral release is the vascular step of the lateral approach, and the same signs apply (hypotension with a quiet wound). In an ALIF a curette through the posterior annulus is in the canal, which is rare and presents as a new deficit rather than as bleeding. Bleeding from the endplates is modest and runs into the retroperitoneum, where it is not seen.

### 8. Cage insertion and fixation
**Why:** Trial spacers set the height, then a large lordotic cage packed with graft is impacted into the distracted space; an ALIF cage is often fixed with integrated screws or an anterior plate, and a lateral cage may take a lateral plate. The disc height and the lordosis are restored at this moment, and the foramen opens indirectly.
**Anaesthesia:** Hammering through a lateral retractor transmits to the plexus; a late drop in electromyographic signal here is the retractor, not the cage. Over-distraction of a stiff segment is the moment of an endplate fracture in osteoporotic bone, which the surgeon sees on the image and which changes the fixation plan. The sympathetic chain runs along the anterolateral vertebral body and is injured by either approach: a warm, dry, pink left leg with a cold right one in recovery is a sympathectomy, benign, and worth explaining before someone diagnoses an arterial occlusion on the wrong side.

### 9. Closure, the posterior stage and the turn
**Why:** The rectus sheath is repaired or the flank muscles allowed to fall together, usually without a drain. If posterior percutaneous screws follow, the patient is turned prone on a frame and the screws placed through stab incisions under fluoroscopy, with no muscle stripping.
**Anaesthesia:** Infiltration of the wound is most of the pain plan for the anterior or lateral stage and the moment to ask for it. The turn to prone for the posterior stage is a full second positioning with all the prone checks, on a patient who has been lateral or extended for two hours and whose fluid balance you now reassess; a psoas or retroperitoneal haematoma declares itself as a falling pressure after the turn, when the retractor is gone and the space can fill. Percutaneous screws need the same stimulated screw test and the same unrelaxed patient as the open version. A patient who had a vascular repair stays supine and goes to a high-dependency bed; the posterior stage waits.

### 10. Recovery, the leg check and the ileus
**Why:** The surgeon wants the neurology confirmed (both legs after an ALIF, hip flexion and the quadriceps on the operative side after an LLIF), the abdomen soft, and the patient mobilised early.
**Anaesthesia:** Check both femoral pulses and foot perfusion after an ALIF and compare the sides; a cold, pulseless left leg is an arterial injury or thrombosis and goes back to theatre, while a warm, dry left leg with a cold right one is the sympathectomy. After an LLIF, test hip flexion and knee extension on the operative side against the baseline and tell the patient and the ward that a weak hip and a numb or burning thigh are expected and usually transient, while a dense quadriceps palsy is a femoral nerve injury the surgeon needs to know about today. Ileus is the common complication of the anterior approach: sips, early walking, a minimal opioid load and an antiemetic charted. A rising abdominal girth with a falling haemoglobin is a retroperitoneal haematoma and a surgical conversation.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is visceral, wall-and-retroperitoneum, with an untouched posterior column, so the plan is a wall block and an oral regimen, not a neuraxial opioid. PROSPECT's complex spine guideline is written for instrumented thoracolumbar surgery and its backbone transfers (paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, intra-operative ketamine for the opioid-tolerant, opioids as rescue); its regional advice, the surgeon-placed epidural, does not, because the surgeon is not in the epidural space. The defensible default for an ALIF is bilateral transversus abdominis plane blocks or a surgeon-infiltrated rectus sheath and wound with long-acting local anaesthetic, dexamethasone at induction, and the oral regimen from recovery. For an LLIF the author's practice is a quadratus lumborum block on the operative side, placed after induction with the patient already lateral, because it reaches the flank wall and some of the lumbar plexus territory that wound infiltration does not; the evidence for that choice is the fascial plane block literature in abdominal surgery rather than anything spine-specific, and it is author's judgement. Ketamine is reserved for the opioid-tolerant.

A catheter has no routine place. The wall pain is a single-night problem, the thigh dysaesthesia after an LLIF is neuropathic and responds to time rather than to local anaesthetic, and the psoas ache responds to walking. When a posterior instrumented stage is added under the same anaesthetic, the posterior card's plan applies: intrathecal morphine or the surgeon-placed epidural, ketamine and the oral regimen, with the wall block added for the anterior stage rather than substituted.

Escalation triggers are vascular and visceral before they are analgesic. Hypotension, a distending abdomen, a falling haemoglobin or a pulseless leg are surgical calls. Anterior thigh pain with a weak quadriceps after an LLIF is a femoral nerve injury and the surgeon decides whether to image the psoas for a haematoma. Abdominal distension with vomiting is ileus, and the opioid is reduced rather than increased. A patient still needing regular opioid at a week after an isolated anterior or lateral fusion has something else going on.

## Pitfalls and variants

- ALIF at L5-S1 versus L4-5. The L5-S1 disc sits below the bifurcation and the exposure is between the iliac vessels; L4-5 needs the left common iliac vein mobilised and is the level at which the venous injury happens. The hypogastric plexus and retrograde ejaculation belong to L5-S1, and the transperitoneal approach carries more of it.
- The access surgeon. Most spine surgeons use one for the anterior approach, and vascular control at the moment of a tear is the whole reason. Know whether they are staying for the disc work or leaving after the exposure.
- Transpsoas versus anterior-to-psoas. The oblique approach passes in front of the psoas and spares most of the plexus at the cost of a longer corridor past the vessels; the thigh symptoms fall and the vascular risk rises. Both need the same lateral position and the same unrelaxed patient if electromyography is used.
- The lumbar plexus at L4-5. The plexus is furthest forward in the psoas at this level and the corridor narrowest; many surgeons will not do a transpsoas L4-5 in a patient with an anteriorly placed plexus on the imaging. Expect the highest thigh symptom and hip flexor rates from this level.
- Neuromuscular block and electromyography. A relaxed patient makes the directional probe useless and the surgeon falsely confident; an intubating dose is reversed before the psoas is reached and the surgeon is told.
- The lateral position over hours. Dependent brachial plexus, peroneal nerve at the fibular head, the ear, the eye, and the hip at the break; a multilevel lateral construct is two or three hours of it.
- Sympathectomy. A warm left leg after either approach, from the chain on the anterolateral vertebral body; benign, often permanent, and misread as an occlusion of the other leg.
- Vascular injury on the table. Litres in minutes from the iliac vein; the surgeon packs and calls, you transfuse and lower the head; a lateral retractor blade pulling off a segmental artery is the lateral version. The slower presentation is a pseudoaneurysm or an iliac vein thrombosis days later.
- Anterior column realignment and hyperlordotic cages. The anterior longitudinal ligament is divided to open the disc space to 20-30 degrees for deformity correction; the vessels are directly in front of the release, and the case belongs to the [deformity card](adult-deformity-scoliosis.html) in its blood and positioning risk.
- Obesity. Harder to sweep, harder to tape laterally, more thigh symptoms after an LLIF in the 545-patient cohort, and a retractor that has to be longer and held harder.

## Key sources

The analgesia evidence for the anterior and lateral approaches is almost entirely borrowed: PROSPECT's complex spine guideline for the oral and ketamine backbone, and the abdominal fascial plane block literature for the wall block, neither of which studied these operations. The complication rates are better documented: the ALIF systematic review gives the approach-specific picture of retrograde ejaculation and vascular injury, the 545-patient lateral cohort and the NICE safety summary give the thigh symptom, hip flexor and femoral nerve rates and their time course, and the AO technique reference gives the vessel-by-level anatomy. The block choice and the catheter advice are author's judgement.
