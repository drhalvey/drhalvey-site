## What the surgeon is doing and why

An [anatomic replacement](anatomic-tsa.html) works only while the rotator cuff centres the humeral head on the glenoid. Take the cuff away and the deltoid, pulling almost vertically, drags the head up into the acromion instead of lifting the arm: the patient shrugs and cannot elevate (pseudoparalysis), the unroofed head wears the acromion and glenoid into one eroded socket (cuff tear arthropathy), and an anatomic implant in that shoulder rocks its glenoid loose within a few years. The reverse geometry solves the mechanical problem rather than the anatomical one. The ball goes on the glenoid side (a glenosphere on a screwed-in baseplate) and the socket on the humerus (a polyethylene insert on a tray or stem). That moves the centre of rotation medially onto the glenoid face, so the deltoid works with a longer lever arm and loads the baseplate in compression rather than shear, and it lets the surgeon drop the humerus distally, lengthening the arm by two to three centimetres so that the deltoid is on stretch and more of its fibres are recruited into elevation. A tensioned deltoid is now the engine and the stabiliser; the implant stays reduced mainly because the deltoid compresses it. That is the mechanism difference from the anatomic card, and it drives the pain profile, the nerve risk, the instability pattern and the stress fracture.

The indications have widened from cuff arthropathy in the elderly to massive irreparable tears, proximal humerus fractures in older patients (see [proximal humerus fracture](proximal-humerus-fracture.html)), inflammatory arthritis, osteoarthritis with too much posterior glenoid wear for an anatomic glenoid, failed arthroplasty, and increasingly primary osteoarthritis over 70. The population is older, frailer and more comorbid than the anatomic one, and a good share are revisions.

The operation in order:

1. Get in. Deltopectoral is the common route, with the subscapularis taken down if it exists. Some surgeons use an anterosuperior approach that splits the anterior deltoid off the acromion: a direct view of the glenoid in a stiff shoulder, at the cost of cutting into the muscle the construct depends on.
2. Cut the head off, lower and in less retroversion than for an anatomic replacement, to make room for the humeral socket below the glenosphere.
3. Expose the whole glenoid down to the inferior scapular neck (a circumferential capsular release with the axillary nerve under the inferior capsule), ream, seat the baseplate low with slight inferior tilt, fix it with a central peg or screw and locking screws into the scapular body, and impact the glenosphere onto the taper.
4. Prepare the humerus (stem, short stem or stemless; press-fit or cemented), assemble a trial tray and insert, and reduce.
5. Set the tension. The surgeon pulls on the arm, reduces the trial, and judges how hard it was to reduce, how taut the conjoint tendon is, whether the joint gapes on distraction and whether the arm hangs at the right length. Too loose dislocates; too tight stretches the nerves, loads the acromion and limits motion. Every adjustment of insert or stem is another dislocation and reduction.
6. Fix the definitive components, decide about the subscapularis (surgeons vary: some repair it for stability, some leave it, some have nothing to repair), close, sling.

The pain logic follows. The dominant mechanism is retension: the arm has been lengthened and the deltoid is on stretch from the final reduction, and unlike the subscapularis after an anatomic replacement nobody can rest it, because every elevation from the first physiotherapy session is the deltoid doing its new job. That gives the deep, aching, stiffness-type pain the Part B table assigns to re-tensioned joints, with night pain and a slower fall than pure bone pain. Bone contributes the first 48 hours: osteotomy, reaming, scapular screws, canal preparation. Nerve adds a flavour of its own: in the one prospective EMG study, subclinical lesions were found at 3.6 weeks in 9 of 19 reverse arthroplasties, mainly axillary and mostly resolving, against one in 23 anatomic replacements, with mean arm lengthening of 2.7 cm. Dysaesthesia over the regimental badge area or a burning quality to the early pain is that, not the block.

One correction to folklore. The reverse is widely called the more painful arthroplasty, and the mechanism supports a 4. The largest retrospective comparison does not: 412 reverse patients were less often still on opioids at two weeks than 278 anatomic patients (46% against 62%) and had lower pain at six weeks. The reverse population is older, and younger age predicts severe early pain; and many reverse shoulders have no subscapularis repair to protect. The honest reading is that retension earns the 4 for revision, conversion, fracture, the younger patient with a massive tear and the opioid-tolerant patient, while the uncomplicated elderly cuff arthropathy patient often runs nearer a 3 with a slower tail. The plan is written for the first group and relaxed for the second.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment, block, airway and baseline pressure
**Why:** The surgeon wants an immobile head, a free arm, a working deltoid afterwards and a stable implant, in a patient who is older and sicker than the anatomic patient and often a revision.
**Anaesthesia:** Two decisions cannot be made later. The first is the deltoid: a reverse without a working deltoid is a contraindication, and once the block is in you cannot test the axillary nerve until tomorrow, so document deltoid power and regimental badge sensation in words before the block. The second is the catheter (see the pain plan), decided with the indication in front of you: primary cuff arthropathy and conversion of a failed anatomic are not the same operation. Place the interscalene block (or a superior trunk block in the patient who cannot afford a hemidiaphragm) awake or lightly sedated, with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg at the same time. A tracheal tube is the author's default: the head is away from you for two hours and the patient is often obese or has a poor chest. Cuff on the non-operative arm, awake baseline pressure recorded before any drug.

### 2. Beach chair and head fixation
**Why:** The sitting position drops the scapula and lets the surgeon work down a vertical humerus and across to the glenoid face; some sit the patient lower for the anterosuperior approach.
**Anaesthesia:** The pressure falls as the chair goes up, and the cuff overstates perfusion at the brain by roughly 1 mmHg for every 1.25 cm between cuff and external auditory meatus. This population carries more cerebrovascular disease than the anatomic one, so the margin matters more. Start a vasopressor infusion early, keep the cuff reading near the awake baseline, treat the bradycardia-hypotension episodes that the sitting position and the block provoke, and decline deliberate hypotension for a dry field; the reported strokes happened at cuff systolics in the 80s and 90s. Check the head is neutral and strapped and the eyes are free, then look again after the surgeon has set the arm.

### 3. Antibiotic, tranexamic acid and approach
**Why:** Cutibacterium acnes in the dermal sebaceous glands is the organism behind late, indolent shoulder arthroplasty infection; the surgeon prepares with benzoyl peroxide beforehand and avoids the dermal edge. Deltopectoral: the internervous plane between deltoid and pectoralis major, cephalic vein as the marker, conjoint tendon retracted medially with the musculocutaneous nerve behind it, then the subscapularis taken down if it is there and repairable. Anterosuperior: the anterior deltoid is elevated off the acromion and the surgeon looks straight down onto the glenoid, faster in a stiff or revision shoulder but a knife into the engine of the construct.
**Anaesthesia:** Cefazolin 2 g IV finished before the knife, 3 g over 120 kg, and tranexamic acid 1 g IV at the same time. The cephalic vein is the first bleed and retractors on the conjoint tendon the first traction on the plexus. In a revision the approach is through scar with retained metal and a tethered plexus; this step alone can take 45 minutes and is where the revision earns its blood loss, so a group and hold is sensible for a revision or conversion and rarely needed for a primary. If the surgeon has split the deltoid, add muscle to this patient's pain mechanisms: the split aches with every elevation.

### 4. Humeral head osteotomy
**Why:** The head is delivered by extension, adduction and external rotation, osteophytes are cleared, and the cut is made at the implant's neck-shaft angle (155 degrees in the Grammont design, 135 in the lateralised designs) and in less retroversion than anatomic, so the humeral socket sits below and lateral to the glenosphere.
**Anaesthesia:** The largest single bleed of the operation, from cancellous bone and the circumflex vessels. Forcible external rotation of a stiff, osteoporotic humerus is where a shaft fracture happens and the case doubles in length, and this population is more osteoporotic than the anatomic one.

### 5. Glenoid exposure, reaming and baseplate
**Why:** The baseplate must sit flush on the inferior glenoid with its central fixation driven into the dense bone at the base of the scapular spine, so the whole glenoid face and the inferior neck must be seen: labrum excised, capsule released all the way round, a retractor under the inferior neck where the axillary nerve crosses. The surface is reamed flat, the baseplate positioned low with a few degrees of inferior tilt (both reduce scapular notching), and the fixation driven into the scapula. Bone loss is built up with graft from the resected head or an augmented baseplate.
**Anaesthesia:** The most demanding step for the patient and the one to watch. The posterior humeral retractor levers on the plexus and the inferior retractor sits on the axillary nerve; the surgeon needs the arm still in the position that opens the glenoid, and a request for "something" usually means depth, not relaxant. Scapular screws bleed from cancellous bone and the irrigation hides how much. A revision glenoid with a failed component to remove is the long version of this step.

### 6. Glenosphere and humeral preparation
**Why:** The glenosphere is impacted onto the baseplate taper and locked. The humerus is reamed and broached for a stem, or the metaphysis impacted for a stemless or short-stem implant. Press-fit in good bone; in osteoporotic bone, fracture and revision the stem is cemented, either now or after the trial.
**Anaesthesia:** Humeral canal work vents far less than a femur, but a cemented stem is a real cement step and the one BCIS-relevant moment in shoulder surgery: 16% of 173 cemented shoulder arthroplasties met any-grade criteria in a large series, against 31% of hip hemiarthroplasties, and the patients who do badly are over 75, ASA 3-4 or have renal impairment, which describes much of this population. When cement goes in, the canal is lavaged and dried, the pressure is already supported, the oxygen is up and you are watching end-tidal carbon dioxide as the stem goes home. Press-fit stems carry no such moment and a higher rate of intraoperative fracture in soft bone; that is the trade the surgeon is making.

### 7. Trial reduction and deltoid tensioning
**Why:** The surgeon assembles a trial tray and insert, pulls the arm into traction and reduces the socket onto the glenosphere. The feel of that reduction, the tautness of the conjoint tendon, the gap on distraction and the range through which the insert does not lever off are the information used to pick the insert thickness and, if needed, reseat the stem. Each change is another dislocation and reduction. The target is enough tension to be stable and no more; the systematic review of arm lengthening puts the goal at 0-2 cm beyond the other side, with shortening raising dislocation and excess raising nerve injury.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step the trap is about. Reduction stretches the axillary nerve and the plexus by the full length of the lengthening, in a patient whose nerves are often tethered by scar, and the pressure dips with each reduction as the arm is pulled and the joint levered. Do not give a relaxant to make the reduction easier: a reduction that only goes in with the patient paralysed will dislocate on the ward when tone returns, and the surgeon needs the resting tone to judge the tension. When the surgeon says the reduction is "tight", the mechanism of this card's pain is being created in front of you.

### 8. Definitive components, subscapularis, closure and sling
**Why:** The definitive stem or metaphyseal component is seated (impacted or cemented), the tray and insert locked on, and the final reduction tested through the range, with attention to adduction (the insert must not impinge on the scapular neck) and to extension with internal rotation, the position that dislocates a reverse. Surgeons vary on the subscapularis: some repair it for anterior stability, some leave it because a repaired subscapularis limits external rotation and may not heal to a lengthened humerus, and in many cuff arthropathy shoulders there is nothing to repair. The anterosuperior approach needs the deltoid repaired to the acromion and protected for six weeks. A sling, often with a small abduction wedge, goes on.
**Anaesthesia:** A cemented stem has its cement step here if not at step 6, with the same precautions. The final reduction is the last stretch on the nerves; note its time, because this is where you start to lighten. Give the antiemetic. The sling goes on before the patient lightens: an awake patient with a dense block will lift the dead arm by the wrist with the other hand, and the position that does it (arm swung across the body) is the dislocating position. Emergence is unremarkable; the arm is insensate.

### 9. Recovery, the first night and physiotherapy
**Why:** The implant is stable from the final reduction provided the arm stays out of extension, adduction and internal rotation. The surgeon wants early passive and active-assisted elevation because the deltoid is the operation, a radiograph before discharge, and no hand-behind-back for six weeks.
**Anaesthesia:** The block lasts 12-24 hours with dexamethasone. The first regular paracetamol and NSAID dose goes in before the patient leaves recovery, and the ward is told that the arm comes back to life overnight with a deep ache rising over a few hours, which the regular regimen treats. A deltoid that does not work when the block has receded is the differential that matters: residual block (commonest, and it has a timescale), an axillary nerve stretched at the reduction (subclinical in nearly half on EMG, usually recovering), or a true injury. Record what you find and when. Day 1 physiotherapy loads the deltoid, and this is where the reverse separates from the anatomic: the first elevation is pain through a stretched muscle, and a patient who cannot do it because the oral regimen was late loses the day.



### 10. Later presentations that come to you first
**Why:** Three complications present as new pain or a dead arm in the weeks after discharge. The acromial or scapular spine stress fracture is the deltoid's own tension breaking the bone it hangs from: pooled incidence 5%, usually in the first months, presenting as new pain over the acromion or spine and a sudden loss of elevation, with worse outcomes whether or not it is fixed. Dislocation (about 2% overall, higher in revision and the absent-subscapularis shoulder) is early, anterior, and follows extension-adduction-internal rotation: pushing up from a chair, or the sling off. Scapular notching is the insert eroding the inferior scapular neck in adduction, radiographic rather than painful in the early years.
**Anaesthesia:** You will be asked whether the block caused the weak deltoid at six weeks. It did not; the stress fracture and the stretched axillary nerve are the candidates and the radiograph and an EMG decide. Reduction of an early dislocation is a short general anaesthetic with full relaxation; closed reduction fails more often than it succeeds, so the surgeon may go on to an open revision of the insert.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a lengthened arm with a tensioned deltoid loaded from day 1, over bone work that hurts for 48 hours and nerves that have been stretched; the population is older and less rebound-prone than the anatomic one. The plan is built for the mechanism and relaxed for the patient: the anatomic card's skeleton with a lower threshold for a continuous technique.

The defensible default is an interscalene block with 15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% (or a superior trunk block with the same volume when the patient cannot afford a hemidiaphragm; it matches interscalene at 24 h with complete phrenic paresis in 5% rather than 73%), intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg at induction, regular paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor where renal function allows (often it does not, which is one reason the oral regimen is weaker here than on the anatomic card), and oral opioid for the first three days. PROSPECT has no arthroplasty guideline; the rotator cuff repair guideline is the nearest and recommends this skeleton with intravenous dexamethasone preferred over perineural.

The catheter. The one randomised comparison in shoulder arthroplasty found that a continuous interscalene infusion lowered day 1 pain and opioid use against a single shot but did not shorten the stay, produced more adverse events and lost 10% of its catheters early; the trial that added an infusion on top of dexamethasone and full multimodal analgesia, in a mixed arthroplasty and cuff repair population, found no difference at all. The author's reconciliation is the same as on the other shoulder cards: the catheter's advantage shows when the single shot is bare and shrinks when it is prolonged with dexamethasone and backed by a regimen that is given. What differs here is who sits in the group the catheter helps. Conversion of a failed arthroplasty, revision of a reverse, the fracture reverse, the opioid-tolerant or chronic pain patient, the younger patient with a massive tear, and anyone whose previous shoulder operation produced a catastrophic night: for these the catheter is the defensible choice, because the deltoid will be loaded on day 1 whether or not the pain is controlled. A catheter needs fixation that survives a sling; the common failure is the catheter in the bed.

Rebound pain: half of ambulatory block patients after bone surgery describe it, with younger age, female sex and the absence of intravenous dexamethasone raising the odds. Escalation: pain above 7 after the block has receded with the sling on and the hand warm and moving is treated with the opioid already charted and a check that the regular doses were given; pain out of proportion with a tense, swelling deltopectoral wound is a haematoma for the surgeon; new sharp pain over the acromion in the first weeks with loss of elevation is a stress fracture and goes back to the surgeon.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Primary cuff arthropathy versus everything else. The primary in a 75-year-old is a 90-minute case with 300 mL of blood loss and a course nearer a 3. Conversion of a failed anatomic, revision of a loose reverse and the fracture reverse are two- to three-hour cases through scar, with more bleeding, longer plexus traction, a cemented stem and a catheter-grade pain course. Book them differently.
- Anterosuperior approach. Splits the deltoid: faster exposure, a deltoid repair to protect, and muscle added to the pain mechanisms.
- Lateralised designs. Lateralising the glenosphere or the humerus reduces notching and improves rotation but changes how the deltoid is tensioned; surgeons vary and the pain plan does not.
- Stemless and short-stem reverse. No canal work, no cement, less bleeding, shorter case; needs good metaphyseal bone.
- Cemented stem. Fracture, revision and osteoporotic bone. BCIS is less frequent than in a hip but it is the same syndrome in the same frail patient; the precautions are the same. The 2016 systematic review found no difference in loosening or revision between cemented and press-fit stems, so the choice is the surgeon's and the bone's.
- Poor tone. Parkinson's disease, neuromuscular disease and heavy alcohol use take away the deltoid compression that keeps the implant reduced; instability is commoner and the sling matters more.
- Beach chair physiology with carotid disease, uncontrolled hypertension or a previous stroke. The cuff-to-brain gradient is the problem and this population has more of these patients. An arterial line transduced at the ear is the honest monitor.
- Renal impairment. Takes away the NSAID and raises the BCIS risk; the oral regimen becomes paracetamol and opioid, which argues for the best block you can place.
- Rheumatoid arthritis and obesity. Cervical spine for the head holder, steroid cover and osteoporotic bone for the one; airway access in the chair and a diaphragm that cannot afford the phrenic palsy for the other.

## Key sources

There is no PROSPECT guideline for shoulder arthroplasty of either geometry; the rotator cuff repair guideline is extrapolated. The mechanism claims rest on small prospective series on arm lengthening and nerve lesions, not trials; the acromial fracture and instability figures are reviews of heterogeneous reports. The block evidence is the 2019 randomised comparison of continuous and single-shot interscalene block in arthroplasty and the 2024 trial showing no added benefit from an infusion once dexamethasone and multimodal analgesia are in place; the catheter advice is the author's reconciliation of those two with the mechanism. The claim that reverse patients use less opioid than anatomic patients is a single-centre retrospective series: a corrective to folklore, not evidence that the reverse is a mild operation.
