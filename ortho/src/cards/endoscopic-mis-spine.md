## What the surgeon is doing and why

A disc fragment or a thickened ligament is pressing on a nerve root. The open operation that removes it ([lumbar microdiscectomy and decompression](lumbar-microdiscectomy-decompression.html)) does most of its collateral damage on the way in: multifidus is stripped off the lamina and held under a retractor, and that muscle, not the nerve, is what hurts afterwards. Every technique on this card is an attempt to reach the same target while leaving the muscle attached.

There are three families, and they differ for you more than they differ for the root.

1. Transforaminal full-endoscopic discectomy. A needle, then a guidewire, dilator and a 7-8 mm working cannula are passed from 8-14 cm lateral to the midline, through the back muscles, into the foramen through Kambin's triangle (bounded by the exiting root above, the superior articular process behind and the endplate below). The foramen is often enlarged with a reamer or burr on the superior articular process. The endoscope carries its own working channel and continuous saline irrigation; the surgeon removes the fragment from inside or beside the disc. No lamina is touched, nothing is stripped, and the dura is seen only at the end. This is the operation that is done awake.
2. Interlaminar full-endoscopic discectomy and biportal endoscopy. The same endoscope, or two separate portals (one for the camera, one for instruments), are passed through the interlaminar window from the back. This is the route for L5-S1, where the iliac crest blocks the lateral trajectory, and for central stenosis. The ligamentum flavum is opened and the root and dural sac are retracted, which is too stimulating and too risky for most patients to tolerate awake.
3. Tubular surgery. Sequential dilators split multifidus along its fibres and an 18-22 mm tubular retractor is docked on the lamina; the surgeon then performs a standard microdiscectomy or an "over the top" bilateral decompression down the tube with a microscope. This is an open operation through a smaller door, done under general anaesthesia, dry rather than irrigated.

The evidence that the small door matters is modest and consistent. In the largest randomised comparison, 613 patients in four Dutch hospitals, transforaminal endoscopic discectomy was non-inferior to open microdiscectomy for leg pain at a year, with less blood loss, earlier mobilisation and a shorter admission, and the differences in patient-reported outcome, though in favour of the endoscope, were small. Reoperation within a year was the same (5% against 6%). The cost is a learning curve steep enough that the trial set aside 125 learning cases, which matters to you because the learning-curve case is the long one.

The pain logic: the dominant mechanism is still muscle, but dilated rather than stripped, plus a small amount of facet bone. The operation removes the leg pain. What is added is specific to the transforaminal route: the cannula sits beside the dorsal root ganglion of the exiting root, and a ganglion that has been pressed or warmed produces burning dysaesthesia in that root's dermatome for days to weeks. That is a nerve mechanism and it does not respond to opioid. Tubular bilateral decompression in an older patient sits at the top of the card's range and behaves like a small open laminectomy.

## Steps

### 1. Choosing the anaesthetic with the surgeon
**Why:** The traditional case for the awake transforaminal operation is that the patient is the nerve monitor: a cannula or reamer touching the exiting root produces a shout of leg pain before it produces an injury. Surgeons trained in that school want a patient who can report. Others accept general anaesthesia, sometimes with electromyography, and for the interlaminar, biportal and tubular operations general anaesthesia is the norm.
**Anaesthesia:** The pooled comparison (14 studies, 1,655 patients) found no difference between local, epidural and general anaesthesia in post-operative pain, function or operating time, more complications under general anaesthesia than under local, and better patient satisfaction with general or epidural anaesthesia than with local alone. That is a fair summary of the trade: local alone is safe and unpleasant. The defensible default for a transforaminal case is local infiltration by the surgeon with sedation that leaves the patient able to answer; for everything through the interlaminar window, a general anaesthetic with a tracheal tube. Agree which it is, and what the surgeon will accept if the patient cannot lie still, before the patient is on the table.

### 2. Positioning prone, awake
**Why:** Hip flexion on a frame opens the foramen and the interlaminar window. The surgeon needs true anteroposterior and lateral fluoroscopy before anything is inserted, because the entire operation is navigated from those two views.
**Anaesthesia:** The awake patient positions themselves, which is the best pressure-area and neck check available. A patient with sciatica severe enough to need surgery may not tolerate lying prone for an hour without analgesia on board, so give paracetamol and a small opioid dose before the turn rather than after. Once prone and draped the airway is out of reach: nasal oxygen with capnography, the face visible to you, and a plan to turn the patient that everyone has heard. The fluoroscopy arm will be in and out for the whole case; lead for you and distance from the tube.

### 3. Sedation
**Why:** The surgeon needs stillness during needle and cannula placement and a verbal report during the minutes when the cannula passes the exiting root.
**Anaesthesia:** Dexmedetomidine suits this better than propofol because the patient stays rousable and breathing while prone. A loading dose of 0.5-1 microg/kg IV over 10 min, then 0.2-0.7 microg/kg/h, with fentanyl 25 microg IV increments, is a common shape; in a randomised comparison of 230 patients a dexmedetomidine-opioid sedation gave lower pain scores than local alone at needle insertion, cannula placement and annulotomy, with higher satisfaction and no more complications. Expect bradycardia and a fall in pressure with the load. The target is a patient who answers a question, not one who is asleep: a prone patient snoring through a foraminoplasty has lost both the airway margin and the nerve monitor, which were the two reasons for doing it awake.

### 4. Needle, discography and local infiltration
**Why:** The needle is walked off the facet into the disc under fluoroscopy; many surgeons inject contrast mixed with a blue dye to stain the degenerate nucleus so the fragment can be recognised down the scope. Lidocaine is injected into skin, track, facet capsule and annulus.
**Anaesthesia:** The annulus and the facet capsule are the painful structures, and the injection into the disc often reproduces the leg pain for a few seconds. Count the local anaesthetic: a long track infiltrated generously with lidocaine 1% in a small patient reaches the dose limit, and dilute solutions (0.5%) are the surgeon's usual answer. Local anaesthetic that tracks into the epidural space gives a transient leg weakness or numbness that is alarming but harmless; local anaesthetic through an unrecognised dural puncture gives a spinal, with hypotension in a prone sedated patient.

### 5. Dilation, foraminoplasty and docking the cannula
**Why:** The foramen is widened by reaming the tip of the superior articular process so the cannula can sit at the fragment without crowding the exiting root.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the most painful step of the awake operation and the one where sedation is tested: bone reaming through a locally anaesthetised capsule, transmitted to a compressed root. A fentanyl increment before it, not during. Leg pain shouted at this point is information for the surgeon and must not be sedated away. Under general anaesthesia there is no report, so some surgeons run free-running electromyography, which needs the relaxant worn off.

### 6. Irrigated endoscopic discectomy
**Why:** Continuous saline irrigation distends the working space, washes blood out of the view and tamponades epidural veins. The fragment is pulled out piecemeal, the annulus is shrunk with a radiofrequency probe, and the surgeon watches the root pulsate free.
**Anaesthesia:** The saline is under pressure in the epidural space, and that pressure is transmitted up the canal. In a series of 16,725 endoscopic discectomies four patients had an intra-operative seizure (0.02%); all four complained of neck pain first, all were long cases, and when the same group measured cervical epidural pressure in a further 33 patients, neck pain occurred in those whose pressure rose. The risk factors were duration and infusion speed. So: neck pain, occipital headache, visual disturbance, or new hypertension with bradycardia in an awake patient means stop the inflow, lower the bag or the pump pressure, and wait. Under general anaesthesia the only signs are an unexplained pressor response and a slow or agitated emergence, which is one argument for keeping the pump pressure low (the surgeon's usual setting is in the range of 30-50 mmHg, lower if the dura has been opened) and for knowing the running time. A dural tear converts epidural irrigation into intrathecal irrigation and the same syndrome arrives faster. Irrigation fluid is at room temperature unless someone warms it; an hour of it cools a small patient.

### 7. Tubular and biportal variants
**Why:** Through a tube the surgeon removes lamina and ligament with a drill and rongeurs under the microscope and can undercut to the opposite side; through two endoscopic portals the same decompression is done under irrigation.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia, tracheal tube, prone, as for [lumbar microdiscectomy and decompression](lumbar-microdiscectomy-decompression.html), with the prone physiology described in [spine essentials](../a/a14-spine-essentials.html). Blood loss is trivial and the abdomen being free matters less than in an open case, but epidural venous ooze down a narrow tube stops the operation, so a normal venous pressure and a normotensive, not hypotensive, patient are still the request. Biportal cases carry the same irrigation pressure hazard as full-endoscopic ones with no patient report. A durotomy down a tube is hard to suture: expect a patch and sealant and a surgeon's instruction about bed rest that changes the day-case plan.

### 8. Withdrawal, closure and emergence
**Why:** The cannula or tube is withdrawn, the muscle closes behind it, and one or two skin sutures are placed. Some surgeons inject steroid and local anaesthetic around the root.
**Anaesthesia:** Infiltration of the track with long-acting local anaesthetic for the general anaesthesia cases. Stop the dexmedetomidine at the start of closure because its offset is slow and the patient has to walk. For a general anaesthetic, a smooth emergence without straining protects the epidural veins. Perineural steroid is the surgeon's decision; it adds a glucose rise in a diabetic.

### 9. Recovery and discharge
**Why:** The surgeon wants to see the foot move and hear that the leg pain is gone before the patient leaves.
**Anaesthesia:** Document leg power and sensation in recovery. A numb or weak leg in the first hour is usually epidural spread of local anaesthetic and is recovering by the time it is examined; one that is not recovering is the surgeon's problem and not an analgesic one. Discharge criteria are the ordinary day-case ones plus passing urine, since both epidural local anaesthetic and the compressed sacral roots delay it. Dexmedetomidine leaves a dry mouth and orthostatic light-headedness for an hour or two.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a dilated muscle track and a small bony window, so the plan is the least that works: surgeon infiltration, regular paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor for three to five days, and a short supply of oral opioid that most patients after a transforaminal discectomy will not finish. No block and no catheter earn a place; the pain score of 2 is set by the tubular bilateral decompression at the top of the card's range, and the awake transforaminal discectomy on its own would be a 1. The large pain cohort's general lesson applies: small operations with nothing charted score higher on day 1 than they should, so the regular simple analgesia is written, not left to request.

Two patterns need a different answer. Burning, hypersensitive skin in the dermatome of the exiting root (L4 after an L4-5 transforaminal approach) appearing from day 1 to day 5 is dorsal root ganglion irritation: it is neuropathic, it settles over two to six weeks in most, and a short course of a gabapentinoid or a tricyclic at night is the conventional treatment, on consensus rather than trial evidence. The second is the opioid-tolerant patient with months of sciatica behind them: the operation removes the reason for the opioid, and the plan that matters is the written reduction afterwards (see [the opioid-tolerant patient](../a/a12-opioid-tolerant.html)), not the day of surgery.

Escalation: leg pain that is worse than before surgery, a new foot drop, or retention is a retained fragment, a root injury or a haematoma until the surgeon has examined the patient. Headache that is worse upright is a dural tear that was not seen through the irrigation.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The awake patient who cannot continue. Have the conversion plan agreed: turn supine, general anaesthetic, tracheal tube, turn prone again. Inducing a prone patient on a spine frame is the option to avoid by keeping the sedation light enough to ask early.
- Sedation chosen to please the surgeon. A motionless, silent patient under deep propofol sedation, prone, with oxygen under the drapes and a radiofrequency probe in the field, combines an unprotected airway with a fire risk and removes the reason for being awake.
- L5-S1 and the high iliac crest. The transforaminal route may be impossible; the surgeon converts to interlaminar, and the anaesthetic converts with it.
- Thoracic and cervical endoscopy. The same irrigation physics next to the cord rather than the cauda equina; general anaesthesia, and pump pressure matters more.
- Retroperitoneal fluid and vessel or bowel injury. A cannula that passes anterior to the disc is in the retroperitoneum. Unexplained abdominal pain, distension or hypotension after a transforaminal case is investigated, not observed.
- The learning-curve case. Duration is the common factor in the irrigation complications and in the patient who can no longer lie still. Ask how long the surgeon expects and tell them when that time has passed.
- Obesity. A long track, more local anaesthetic, worse fluoroscopy, and a sedated prone airway with less reserve: the threshold for a general anaesthetic is lower.
- Minimally invasive fusion through tubes belongs on the [posterior lumbar fusion](posterior-lumbar-fusion.html) card; the tube changes the blood loss and the muscle injury, not the neuromonitoring or the pain score by more than a point.

## Key sources

The comparative effectiveness evidence is one good pragmatic randomised trial and several smaller ones. The anaesthetic comparisons are mostly retrospective or single-centre and come from units with very high volumes, so the low complication rates travel imperfectly. The irrigation pressure hazard rests on one large series with a small physiological sub-study and on case reports; the mechanism is coherent and the prodrome is specific, which is why it carries the trap line. The pump pressure range quoted is common practice, not a tested threshold. Nothing procedure-specific exists from PROSPECT for endoscopic spine surgery.
