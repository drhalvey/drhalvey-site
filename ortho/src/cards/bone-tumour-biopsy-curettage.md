## What the surgeon is doing and why

Two operations share this card because they share a patient: someone, often young, with a lesion in bone or soft tissue whose nature is not yet certain or has just been established.

The biopsy is the first. Its purpose is tissue for the pathologist, and its rule is that the track will be contaminated by whatever the lesion is. If the lesion proves to be a sarcoma, the track has to be removed in one piece with the tumour at the definitive resection, so the surgeon who will do that resection decides where the needle or the knife goes: a longitudinal incision, through one muscle compartment and not between two, away from the neurovascular bundle, with meticulous haemostasis because a haematoma carries tumour cells wherever it spreads. The classic series on the hazards of biopsy showed that a badly planned one produces wrong diagnoses, wound problems and changed treatment, and that this happens more often outside the treating centre. Most biopsies are now image-guided core needle samples under local anaesthesia; the ones that come to theatre are open biopsies, children, deep or painful sites, and cases where a frozen section will decide whether the surgeon proceeds to curettage under the same anaesthetic.

Curettage is the second, and it is the treatment for lesions that are benign but locally aggressive (giant cell tumour of bone, aneurysmal bone cyst, chondroblastoma) or low grade (enchondroma, atypical cartilaginous tumour). These sit in the metaphysis and epiphysis next to a joint, most often the distal femur, proximal tibia, distal radius and proximal humerus. Resecting the bone would cure the lesion and cost the joint. The surgeon accepts instead an intralesional operation, one that goes into the tumour, and then works to make it as thorough as a resection:

1. A cortical window as large as the lesion, so that no corner is curetted blind.
2. Curettage of all visible tumour, then a high-speed burr over the whole cavity wall to take a further millimetre or two of bone. This is "extended" curettage.
3. An adjuvant to kill what is left in the wall: phenol swabbed on and neutralised with alcohol, hydrogen peroxide, an argon beam, or freezing with liquid nitrogen (poured in through a funnel, or delivered through a closed probe).
4. A filler. Cement is the usual choice for giant cell tumour: it gives immediate strength, its heat of polymerisation adds to the kill, and a recurrence shows as a lucent line at its edge. In a pooled analysis of 1,293 patients, recurrence after bone grafting was about twice that after cementation. Graft or substitute is preferred by some surgeons in children and under joint surfaces.
5. A plate across the window if the remaining bone is likely to fracture.

The pain logic follows. A biopsy is a small incision into one muscle and scores 1. Curettage is a bone operation: window, burr, thermal injury to endosteum, a block of cement. There is no stripped muscle and no tendon under tension, so the pain is moderate, peaks on day 1 and is NSAID-responsive. The lesion itself was often painful, particularly with an impending fracture, and that pain goes.

## Steps

### 1. Reading the plan before choosing the anaesthetic
**Why:** The surgeon has chosen the biopsy track or the window with the possible later resection in mind. Tissue planes they intend to keep clean matter more to them than anything else that day.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask where the lesion is and which compartments are in play before any regional technique. The principle, which is author's judgement and consistent with the surgical rule, is that no block needle or catheter passes through or beside the tumour or along the line of a future incision: no femoral block next to a proximal thigh mass, no interscalene catheter exiting through skin over a proximal humeral sarcoma. If a neuraxial technique is planned, check the staging imaging for spinal disease. Where lymphoma is in the differential, give no dexamethasone until the tissue is out. Many curettage patients with giant cell tumour have had denosumab beforehand: check the calcium.

### 2. Anaesthetic for the biopsy
**Why:** An open biopsy takes 20 minutes; a frozen section adds 20-30 more while the pathologist confirms that the tissue is lesional or gives a working diagnosis.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway for most; the waiting time is real, so do not run the anaesthetic as though the case ends at the last stitch. Children having a biopsy for a suspected sarcoma commonly have bone marrow sampling, a central line or imaging added to the same anaesthetic, and it is worth asking the oncology team before induction. If infection is a possibility the antibiotic is held until samples are taken, by agreement at the brief.

### 3. Tourniquet without exsanguination
**Why:** A bloodless field lets the surgeon see the difference between tumour and bone at the cavity wall. Compression bandaging of a limb that contains a tumour is avoided because of the concern, theoretical but universally observed, about expressing tumour cells into the circulation.
**Anaesthesia:** The limb is elevated for a few minutes and the cuff inflated. The limb therefore holds more blood than usual: the field is less dry, the surgeon may ask for a higher cuff pressure, and the volume returned on deflation is larger. Tourniquet time is usually under 90 minutes; the rules are in [the tourniquet](../a/a01-tourniquet.html). For the proximal femur, pelvis, sacrum and shoulder there is no tourniquet and the plan is that of a bleeding operation: two cannulae, tranexamic acid, a group and screen, and an arterial line for a large pelvic or sacral aneurysmal bone cyst or giant cell tumour, some of which are embolised the day before.

### 4. Cortical window and curettage
**Why:** The lesion is unroofed and scooped out; giant cell tumour is soft, brown and vascular, a cartilage lesion is gritty and blue-white, an aneurysmal bone cyst is a cavity of blood under pressure.
**Anaesthesia:** Under tourniquet this is quiet. Without one, opening an aneurysmal bone cyst or a vascular giant cell tumour produces brisk bleeding that stops only when the cavity is empty, so the surgeon works fast and you should have blood checked and in the building before the window is cut. Send-away specimens leave now.

### 5. High-speed burr
**Why:** The burr removes the ridges where tumour hides and takes the wall back to normal bone.
**Anaesthesia:** Irrigation fluid and bone dust under pressure in a closed cavity can enter the venous sinusoids, though the tourniquet holds it in the limb for now. The burr near the subchondral plate or a thin posterior cortex is where the surgeon may break through towards a joint or the popliteal vessels.

### 6. Adjuvant
**Why:** Phenol denatures protein to a depth of a fraction of a millimetre and is neutralised with alcohol; hydrogen peroxide foams debris out of the trabeculae; liquid nitrogen freezes the wall to a depth of several millimetres in two or three freeze-thaw cycles; an argon beam cauterises the surface.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step to watch. Liquid nitrogen boils at body temperature and expands several hundredfold; poured into a cavity with open venous channels it has caused pulmonary gas embolism under anaesthesia, reported from a cancer centre in 1990 and again as two cases with serious haemodynamic compromise in a later series, although the same group then found no exhaled nitrogen in 15 monitored patients. The event is rare and real. Hydrogen peroxide releases oxygen in the same closed space and carries the same hazard. Avoid nitrous oxide, watch the end-tidal carbon dioxide for a sudden fall, and treat a fall with desaturation or hypotension as gas embolism: tell the surgeon to stop and flood the cavity with saline, give 100% oxygen and support the circulation. Closed probe systems avoid free liquid; a prospective series of 36 cases recorded no embolic events or skin burns. Phenol is absorbed in small amounts from a swabbed cavity and systemic toxicity is not a practical concern at these volumes. Freezing next to a nerve (the common peroneal nerve at the proximal fibula, the radial nerve at the distal humerus) produces a neurapraxia that will be found in recovery.

### 7. Cement or graft
**Why:** Cement is mixed, packed in by hand in a doughy state and moulded to the window; the surgeon cools the surface with saline as it cures to protect cartilage and skin.
**Anaesthesia:** There is no intramedullary pressurisation, so bone cement implantation syndrome in the sense of [the cement chapter](../a/a02-cement-bcis.html) is unusual. It is not impossible: a large metaphyseal cavity that communicates with the canal, in an older patient, is a small cemented stem by another name. Note the time of cementation, since curing is 10-12 minutes of waiting. If iliac crest graft is harvested instead, the donor site becomes the most painful part of the operation and needs its own infiltration.

### 8. Tourniquet release and closure
**Why:** Some surgeons deflate before closing to cauterise bleeding points, because a post-operative haematoma is both a wound problem and a route for local spread; others close over a drain and deflate afterwards.
**Anaesthesia:** Deflation returns, in one go, a larger-than-usual pooled volume, cold acidotic blood, any gas or debris that entered the veins during burring and adjuvant treatment, and methylmethacrylate monomer. Expect a fall in pressure and a rise in end-tidal carbon dioxide; a fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide instead is embolic. The block, if not placed before, goes in now.

### 9. Recovery and weight-bearing
**Why:** The surgeon wants to confirm nerve function and will restrict weight-bearing according to how much cortex is left.
**Anaesthesia:** If a nerve was at risk from freezing or from the window, agree beforehand whether the block is deferred until it has been examined. A cemented, plated distal femur may be allowed to bear weight at once; a grafted one is protected for weeks, and a pathological fracture through the cavity in the first months is the well-known complication of cryosurgery in particular.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is bone, moderate and short, so the plan is a single-shot block for the first night with regular paracetamol and an NSAID from the start, and a few days of oral opioid. For the knee region an adductor canal block with 15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% preserves quadriceps function for early protected mobilisation; add a popliteal sciatic block for a proximal tibial lesion only when the surgeon does not need to see the foot move. For the upper limb use the brachial plexus block that fits the site. Intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg prolongs the block and reduces rebound, which in ambulatory block patients occurred in about half and more often after bone surgery and in the young: this population is both. Dexamethasone is withheld only when lymphoma is still possible.

There is no trial of regional techniques in tumour curettage. The recommendation is extrapolated from bone surgery at the same sites, and the constraint on needle placement relative to the tumour is the author's reading of surgical oncology principle. NSAIDs are not restricted on bone-healing grounds when the cavity is cemented; with bone graft, surgeons vary.

A catheter is rarely needed. The exception is a large pelvic or proximal femoral curettage in an opioid-tolerant patient, where a fascia iliaca or lumbar plexus catheter, or an epidural, is reasonable if its track is clear of the surgical field.

Escalation: pain that rises after day 2, with swelling, is a haematoma or a fracture through the window. Pain on weight-bearing that returns after weeks of comfort is a fracture or a recurrence and goes back to the surgeon.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The biopsy that becomes a resection. A frozen section showing a benign lesion may lead straight to curettage; one showing sarcoma ends the operation at closure of the biopsy. Know both plans before induction.
- Soft tissue tumours. Core or open biopsy of a soft tissue mass is the same anaesthetic; excision of a superficial lipoma is minor, and wide resection of a deep sarcoma is a different operation with nerves and vessels at stake.
- Pelvis and sacrum. No tourniquet, large blood loss, prone or lateral position, and often pre-operative embolisation; the physiology belongs with [sacrectomy and hemipelvectomy](sacrectomy-hemipelvectomy.html) on a smaller scale.
- Pathological fracture through the lesion. The patient arrives in pain and splinted; a pre-operative block is kind and still has to respect the compartment rule.
- Children. Aneurysmal bone cysts and chondroblastomas present in the first two decades; the physis is beside the lesion, cement is used less, and blood loss is proportionally larger.
- Metastasis presenting as a solitary lesion. A biopsy is still required before any nail is passed, because nailing a primary sarcoma contaminates the whole bone; the fixation operation is on the [metastatic bone disease](metastatic-long-bone-fixation.html) card.
- Hydrogen peroxide. Foaming in the cavity is expected; a sudden change in capnography is not.

## Key sources

Cement against graft for giant cell tumour rests on a meta-analysis of retrospective cohorts. The gas embolism hazard of open-pour cryosurgery rests on case reports and one small monitored series, enough to justify vigilance and not enough to state an incidence.
