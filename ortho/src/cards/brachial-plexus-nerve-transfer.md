## What the surgeon is doing and why

A traction injury to the brachial plexus, usually in a young man thrown from a motorcycle, either stretches the nerves, ruptures them beyond the foramen, or pulls the roots out of the spinal cord. The three behave differently. A stretched nerve in continuity may recover. A rupture leaves a proximal stump with live motor axons that can be bridged to the distal stump with a cable of expendable sensory nerve. An avulsed root has no proximal stump: nothing can be grafted, and the root's territory can only be reinnervated by borrowing axons from somewhere else.

Time is the second constraint. A denervated muscle loses its motor end plates over 12-18 months, and axons regrow at about 1 mm a day, so a graft at the neck for a biceps 30 cm away is a race that is often lost. This is why surgery is done within about six months, and why the field has moved from long grafts to nerve transfers: take a working, expendable motor nerve or fascicle close to the target muscle, cut it, and coapt it to the paralysed muscle's nerve a few centimetres from the end plates.

The operation is assembled from a menu:

- Supraclavicular exploration. A transverse neck incision, omohyoid divided, the phrenic nerve found on anterior scalene and followed up to C5, the roots and trunks dissected out of scar. Each root is stimulated and inspected; a stump may be sent for frozen section to see whether it contains fascicles. This decides what is graftable.
- Nerve grafts. Sural nerve from one or both legs, cut into cables, laid from root stump to target and joined under the microscope with 9-0 suture and fibrin glue.
- Spinal accessory to suprascapular nerve transfer, for shoulder abduction and external rotation, through the same neck incision or posteriorly.
- A triceps branch of the radial nerve to the axillary nerve, through a posterior arm incision, for deltoid.
- The Oberlin transfer: one fascicle of the ulnar nerve (and in the double transfer, one of the median) to the motor branches of biceps and brachialis, through a medial arm incision. In pooled series about 96% reach grade 3 elbow flexion or better.
- Intercostal nerves (three or four, harvested through a chest wall incision) to musculocutaneous, when the lower roots are also gone and there is no ulnar fascicle to borrow. Phrenic and contralateral C7 are donors in some units.
- A free functioning gracilis transfer for the patient who presents late: a muscle with its artery, vein and nerve moved to the arm, which performs better than nerve reconstruction after about a year and is a microvascular free flap anaesthetic.

What the surgeon cannot do without is intraoperative stimulation. Root stumps are judged partly by what happens when they are stimulated; the ulnar fascicle chosen for an Oberlin transfer is the one that fires flexor carpi ulnaris with little intrinsic hand response; the triceps branch is chosen the same way; and donor nerves are proven to work before they are cut. Some units add evoked potentials to test whether a root is in continuity with the cord.

The pain logic is unlike any other card. The incisions are soft tissue and several, none over bone, and the limb is largely insensate. The pain that dominates the patient's life is neuropathic: in a 327-patient operated cohort 58% had it, with total plexus injury and delay to surgery the main associations. It arises in the dorsal horn after avulsion, it is not driven from the periphery, and no block or repair switches it off. Surgery adds donor-site dysaesthesia (the ulnar border of the hand after an Oberlin, the lateral foot after sural harvest, chest wall after intercostals).

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative assessment
**Why:** By the time of reconstruction the patient is months out from a high-energy injury, and the surgeon has mapped the deficit with examination, electromyography and MRI or CT myelography.
**Anaesthesia:** Take the neurology from the surgeon's map and write the summary in your record. Three things change the anaesthetic. A raised hemidiaphragm on the chest film means the phrenic nerve went with C5 (a lower-root avulsion also gives a Horner's syndrome); with intercostal harvest on the same side planned, that is a patient with reduced reserve after surgery. The accompanying injuries (head, cervical spine fixation, subclavian artery repair, rib and long bone fractures) set airway and access. And the medication list will include a gabapentinoid, a tricyclic or SNRI and often an opioid: all are continued on the morning of surgery. Screen for depression and say so if it is evident; it was present in more than half of the cohort above.

### 2. Induction, airway and the rule about relaxants
**Why:** The surgeon will stimulate nerves from the first half hour to the last and needs every response to be real.
**Anaesthesia:** Intubate with a short-acting agent or without a relaxant: rocuronium 0.5 mg/kg IV at induction for a case where stimulation starts 45 min later is acceptable only if train-of-four on the opposite arm or a leg is back to four strong twitches before the surgeon stimulates, with sugammadex given if it is not. No top-ups. Stillness comes from remifentanil and adequate hypnotic depth. Reinforced tube taped to the side away from the neck incision, eyes padded, and the head turned only as far as a neck that may have been injured tolerates. A bite block if evoked potentials are run, and propofol-based maintenance in that case.

### 3. Lines and positioning for a long microsurgical case
**Why:** The surgeon needs the neck, shoulder, arm, chest wall and one or both legs, and will move the arm from abduction to across the chest several times.
**Anaesthesia:** Both arms are unavailable or awkward: cannulae and the arterial line go in the opposite arm on an extension, or in the feet if the opposite arm is beside the surgeon. Urinary catheter, temperature probe, forced-air warming over what is left exposed (little), fluid warmer. Pressure care for eight hours: heels, sacrum, occiput, the opposite ulnar nerve. The insensate operative arm cannot report its own traction or pressure injury, and the surgeon's repositioning is checked each time. Legs: frog-leg or hip flexed and internally rotated for sural harvest supine, which strains the hip and knee in a long case; some surgeons harvest prone or lateral first and then turn.

### 4. Supraclavicular exploration and root assessment
**Why:** The roots and upper trunk are found in scar between the scalenes and each is stimulated. A root that produces no distal response and looks empty is avulsed or ruptured; a stump with fascicles is a graft source.
**Anaesthesia:** The phrenic nerve is in the field and stimulated on purpose: the diaphragm twitches on the ventilator trace. Venous bleeding from the transverse cervical and external jugular veins is usual and minor; the subclavian vessels and the pleural dome are just beneath, and a pneumothorax or a vessel injury is the unusual event. The slight head-up tilt reduces venous ooze and adds a small risk of air entrainment through an open neck vein. Frozen section adds 20-30 min of waiting per specimen.

### 5. Sural nerve harvest
**Why:** The sural nerve is long, purely sensory and expendable, and 30-50 cm can be taken from each leg through a long posterior calf incision or several step incisions.
**Anaesthesia:** A second team often harvests while the neck is explored. If a thigh tourniquet is used, note the time. Ask for the harvest incision to be infiltrated with ropivacaine 0.2% at closure: it is a long way from any nerve being stimulated and it is the wound the patient feels most, since the rest of the surgery is in a numb limb. The donor deficit is numbness along the lateral foot, tolerated well.

### 6. Distal transfers in the arm
**Why:** Through a medial arm incision the biceps motor branch is found on the musculocutaneous nerve and divided; the ulnar nerve is opened and its fascicles stimulated one at a time until one giving mainly wrist flexion is found, which is cut and turned to the biceps branch. The posterior approach repeats this for a triceps branch to the axillary nerve.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step where your contribution decides the result. The surgeon is choosing a fascicle to sacrifice on the strength of a twitch at 0.5 mA. Any residual neuromuscular block, a tourniquet that has been up for more than 20-30 min, or a cold hypoperfused limb degrades the response. Confirm four full twitches with the monitor on the non-operative side, keep the patient warm and normotensive, and say aloud that relaxant has not been given. The surgeon should not infiltrate local anaesthetic with adrenaline into this wound beforehand.

### 7. Intercostal nerve harvest
**Why:** The third to sixth intercostal nerves are dissected from under their ribs through a curved inframammary incision, divided anteriorly and swung into the axilla to reach the musculocutaneous nerve directly.
**Anaesthesia:** The pleura is opened in a minority; the surgeon tests with saline and a held inflation, and may leave a small drain. Intercostal harvest removes some of the motor supply to that hemithorax; with a phrenic palsy on the same side it is a measurable loss of vital capacity. After reinnervation the biceps fires with breathing and coughing for months, which the patient should be told.

### 8. Graft inset and coaptation under the microscope
**Why:** Cables are cut to length with slack for neck and shoulder movement and coapted with a few 9-0 sutures and fibrin glue. The repairs are fragile and tension-free only in the position in which they were sewn.
**Anaesthesia:** Two to three hours of immobility under the microscope: no coughing, no movement, stable pressure. From this point the head and arm position is fixed, and it is the position the patient will wake in. Plan the emergence now.

### 9. Free functioning muscle transfer, when it is part of the plan
**Why:** Gracilis is raised on its pedicle and obturator nerve branch, anchored to the clavicle or acromion, woven into the biceps or finger flexor tendons, revascularised from the thoracoacromial or thoracodorsal vessels and reinnervated from the spinal accessory or intercostals.
**Anaesthesia:** The priorities become those of a free flap: warm, full, vasodilated, haemoglobin not too high or low, no vasopressor bolus without telling the surgeon, urine output maintained. The principles are in [soft tissue cover for limb trauma](orthoplastic-flap-coverage.html). The case is now 8-12 hours.

### 10. Closure, splintage and emergence
**Why:** A shoulder immobiliser or a pre-made brace holds the arm against the chest and limits head turn for three weeks so that the coaptations are not pulled apart.
**Anaesthesia:** The brace goes on before the patient is light. Extubate awake but smooth: remifentanil run down slowly, antiemetics given, coughing suppressed, because one violent head turn or retch can disrupt a graft. Chest radiograph in recovery after supraclavicular or intercostal work. Give the evening doses of the patient's neuropathic agents.

### 11. The first days
**Why:** The surgeon wants a quiet patient with a still neck and no expectation of early recovery: the first flicker of biceps is four to six months away.
**Anaesthesia:** Wound pain is treated with paracetamol, an NSAID and small doses of oral opioid, and is usually over in three days. The neuropathic pain continues; a flare in the first week is common and is treated by restoring and then titrating the gabapentinoid and antidepressant, not by escalating opioid. Refer to the pain service before discharge if they are not already involved.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is nerve, and it predates the operation, so the plan has two halves that should not be confused.

Nociceptive: several soft-tissue wounds. Paracetamol and an NSAID regularly, surgeon infiltration of the sural and chest wall wounds, oral opioid for breakthrough for two to four days. Intraoperative remifentanil for stillness is followed by a longer-acting opioid before the end, in a dose that allows for the patient's baseline tolerance. An erector spinae plane block after closure is reasonable for a three- or four-rib intercostal harvest; this is author's judgement.

Neuropathic: continue everything the patient was taking, on the day of surgery and every day after. Low-dose ketamine through the case and for 24-48 h is the author's practice in a patient with established deafferentation pain, on the general evidence for opioid-tolerant and neuropathic patients and not on a plexus-specific trial. Whether reconstruction itself reduces avulsion pain cannot be stated from the literature: a 2026 systematic review found pain reporting too inconsistent to pool. For pain that drugs fail, dorsal root entry zone lesioning gives meaningful relief in about 70% in pooled series and is the referral to know about.

No block and no catheter on the operative side before or during surgery. The reasons are mechanical (the block is in the field), functional (it abolishes the responses the surgeon needs) and logical (the pain generator is central to the block). A regional technique for the leg is unnecessary for one sural harvest.

Escalation: breathlessness is a pneumothorax or a diaphragm that was already paralysed; neck swelling is a haematoma or a chyle leak on the left; severe new pain in the hand after an Oberlin transfer is a donor nerve problem and goes to the surgeon.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The isolated distal transfer. An Oberlin or triceps-to-axillary transfer on its own is a two-hour operation with one arm incision and an overnight stay. The relaxant rule is identical.
- Evoked potential monitoring. Total intravenous anaesthesia, no nitrous oxide, steady depth, and the same relaxant restriction for motor potentials.
- Phrenic nerve as donor, or injured. A unit that uses it avoids it in anyone with lung disease; the fall in vital capacity is tolerated by the young and matters when intercostal nerves are taken from the same side.
- Contralateral C7 transfer. A second neck dissection on the good side, with transient weakness and paraesthesia in the normal arm afterwards; lines go in the legs.
- Associated vascular repair. A previous subclavian or axillary artery graft lies in the dissection; have blood available and an arterial line away from that limb.
- Obstetric plexus palsy. The same operation in an infant of three to nine months: a long case, a small blood volume, temperature and fluid management as the main risks. See [the child](../a/a15-the-child.html).
- Opioid tolerance and dependence. Common after months of pain; see [the opioid-tolerant patient](../a/a12-opioid-tolerant.html).
- Late reconstruction with [tendon transfers](tendon-transfers-upper-limb.html) or shoulder fusion is a different, shorter anaesthetic in the same patient years later, and the neuropathic pain will still be there.

## Key sources

The surgical outcome literature is large and almost entirely retrospective series pooled in systematic reviews. The anaesthetic literature is nearly absent: the relaxant and local anaesthetic rules are consensus from the requirements of intraoperative stimulation, not trial findings. The prevalence of neuropathic pain is well documented; its response to reconstruction is not.
