## What the surgeon is doing and why

A knee that has dislocated has torn at least two of its four main ligament groups and usually three: both cruciates plus the medial side, the posterolateral corner, or both. The Schenck classification counts them (KD I one cruciate plus a collateral, KD II both cruciates, KD III both cruciates and one side, medial or lateral, KD IV everything, KD V with a fracture). Many dislocations reduce themselves before anyone sees them, so the patient arrives with a swollen, grossly unstable knee and a normal-looking radiograph, and the injury is under-called. The tibia has moved a full bone's width relative to the femur with the popliteal artery tethered above at the adductor hiatus and below at the soleus arch, and the common peroneal nerve wrapped round the fibular neck. In the pooled literature 18% of dislocations have a vascular injury and 25% a nerve injury, almost always the peroneal, and the pattern most at risk is the one with the lateral side torn.

The surgeon's problem is that a knee with no cruciates and no collateral cannot be made stable by repairing what is left. Primary repair of the posterolateral corner fails two to three times as often as reconstruction, and cruciate stumps do not hold sutures. So the operation rebuilds each ligament with a tendon graft passed through bone tunnels placed at the anatomical attachments:

1. Posterior cruciate: a tunnel from the front of the tibia to the back, exiting a few millimetres in front of the popliteal artery, and one or two femoral tunnels. This is the central pillar and is done first.
2. Anterior cruciate: tibial and femoral tunnels as in an ordinary [ACL reconstruction](acl-reconstruction.html), positioned so that they do not collide with the others.
3. Posterolateral corner: through a lateral incision the peroneal nerve is found and freed, then one or two grafts rebuild the fibular collateral ligament, popliteus tendon and popliteofibular ligament through a fibular head tunnel, a tibial tunnel and two femoral tunnels.
4. Medial side: the medial collateral and posterior oblique ligaments are repaired if acute and of good tissue, or reconstructed with a graft through femoral and tibial tunnels.

That is six to ten tunnels in one knee and three or four grafts. A patient has only so many spare tendons, so the grafts come from both hamstrings, the quadriceps tendon, the other leg, or a tissue bank; allograft is used far more here than in isolated ACL surgery. Each graft is tensioned and fixed at a specific angle (the posterior cruciate at 90 degrees with the tibia pulled forward, the lateral structures at about 30 degrees, the anterior cruciate near extension), in a fixed order, because the first graft sets the position of the joint for the rest.

Timing is a trade-off the surgeon makes before you meet the patient. Operating in the first three weeks gives better tissue planes, repairable collateral structures and better scores in the pooled comparisons; operating in the first few days means a swollen limb, a torn capsule that leaks arthroscopy fluid into the leg, and more arthrofibrosis. Most surgeons wait one to three weeks for the capsule to seal and the swelling to settle, with the knee in a brace or, if it will not stay reduced or the artery has been repaired, a spanning external fixator. Some stage the reconstruction (collaterals and posterior cruciate first, anterior cruciate months later) to shorten each operation and reduce stiffness.

The pain logic explains the score. In the 179-procedure cohort, open knee ligament reconstruction was among the most painful operations of all on day 1, and that was for lesser procedures than this one. The dominant mechanism is tendon-tension: several grafts fixed under tension and then deliberately moved, because the commonest complication is arthrofibrosis (a manipulation under anaesthesia in about 9% in the acute posterolateral series) and the only prevention is early range of motion. Underneath it is bone (every tunnel is a drilled osteotomy), muscle (wide medial and lateral dissections and a harvest site or two), and a long tourniquet. The early peak is no higher than a [total knee replacement](primary-tka.html); what earns the 5 is that the movement-evoked pain is renewed at every physiotherapy session for weeks.

## Steps

### 1. Before the day: artery, nerve, timing and the plan
**Why:** The surgeon needs to know that the limb is perfused, what the peroneal nerve was doing before surgery, which ligaments are being rebuilt, with which grafts, and in how many stages.
**Anaesthesia:** Read the vascular work-up yourself. An ankle-brachial index below 0.90 after a dislocation predicted an arterial injury needing surgery in every case in the prospective series, and 0.90 or above excluded one; a CT angiogram is the usual next step and intimal injuries can declare late. A patient with a popliteal repair or bypass arrives with a vein graft behind the knee, fasciotomy wounds, an antiplatelet or anticoagulant, and a surgeon who will not want a tourniquet: that changes the block timing, the blood loss and the duration. Record the peroneal nerve function (ankle and great toe dorsiflexion, first web space sensation) in your own notes; one in four of these patients has a deficit before anyone operates, and a foot drop found after your block will otherwise be yours. Ask which leg the grafts are coming from. Screen for the things that travel with high-energy trauma, and for the low-velocity dislocation in the patient with a BMI over 40, who has a higher neurovascular injury rate and a harder airway.

### 2. Anaesthesia, the catheter and the examination under anaesthesia
**Why:** With the patient asleep and relaxed the surgeon examines both knees: drawer, Lachman, varus and valgus at 0 and 30 degrees, the dial test for the posterolateral corner. This is the definitive map of what is torn and decides the final plan.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with a secured airway is the default because of the duration; a spinal does not last and an epidural hides the leg afterwards. Place the adductor canal catheter before induction or immediately after it, at the mid-thigh level and well clear of the tourniquet and the medial incision; the cuff sits high on the thigh, so tunnel the catheter proximally and anteriorly or accept that it goes in at the end of the case under a sterile drape change. Dexamethasone 8 mg IV now. Urinary catheter for anything planned beyond four hours, arterial line only for the patient's own comorbidity or a fresh vascular repair, forced-air warming on the upper body. Full relaxation for the examination is helpful and brief.

### 3. Position, tourniquet strategy and graft harvest
**Why:** The knee must move freely from extension to 120 degrees and hold at 90 degrees hands-free. Autografts are harvested first (hamstrings through an anteromedial incision, quadriceps tendon through a short suprapatellar one, sometimes from the other leg) and prepared on a side table while the arthroscopy starts; allografts are thawed.
**Anaesthesia:** Agree the tourniquet plan aloud: when it goes up, the 120 min limit, and which part of the operation will run without it (see [the tourniquet](../a/a01-tourniquet.html)). Antibiotic in before inflation and again at four hours. A contralateral harvest means a second painful leg that nobody blocked: a single-shot adductor canal block on that side is the fix, and the combined local anaesthetic dose across catheter bolus, second block and surgeon infiltration needs adding up for a case this size. Pad the other leg, the heels and the ulnar nerves for a five-hour case, not a one-hour one.

### 4. Arthroscopy and the fluid that goes where the capsule lets it
**Why:** The joint is inspected, torn menisci are repaired or resected, the cruciate stumps are cleared and the tunnel sites prepared. The surgeon needs fluid distension to see.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step with the limb-threatening complication. A dislocation tears the capsule, most often posteriorly, and the tear is under-recognised on MRI; arthroscopic fluid follows it into the calf or the thigh. Surgeons counter it by waiting for the capsule to seal, using gravity inflow or low pump pressure, making an outflow incision and working quickly. Your part is surveillance: a nominated person feels the calf and thigh every 15 min through the drapes during the arthroscopic phase and says the result aloud. A calf that becomes tense means stop, convert to dry or open technique and reassess; it does not mean finish the tunnel first. The same fluid can track proximally in a long case, and litres of room-temperature irrigation cool the patient.

### 5. Posterior cruciate reconstruction
**Why:** The tibial tunnel is drilled from the front of the tibia to the posterior cruciate footprint on the back, under arthroscopic and fluoroscopic control, with a guard on the guide wire because the popliteal artery lies immediately behind the posterior capsule. The femoral tunnel or tunnels are drilled inside-out or outside-in. The graft is pulled up through the tibia, round the "killer turn" at the back, and into the femur.
**Anaesthesia:** The guide wire and reamer breaking through the posterior tibial cortex is the moment the popliteal vessels are at risk. Under tourniquet a vascular injury is silent until the cuff comes down, so the first sign may be a wound that fills, a pale foot or a pressure that sags at deflation; without a tourniquet it is a field that suddenly cannot be cleared. Blood is grouped and screened for every multiligament case, and you should know how to reach a vascular surgeon from that theatre before the case starts. Some surgeons use an open tibial inlay through a posteromedial approach instead, which may mean a position change in mid-operation.

### 6. Anterior cruciate tunnels and graft passage
**Why:** Tibial and femoral tunnels are placed anatomically while avoiding convergence with the posterior cruciate and collateral tunnels; the graft is passed but not yet fixed on the tibial side.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing new physiologically; note the tourniquet clock. If the plan is to deflate for a reperfusion interval, this is the usual place, and the deflation brings the usual fall in pressure, rise in end-tidal carbon dioxide and a warm acidotic bolus from a large limb. Re-dose muscle relaxant only if the surgeon needs it for flexion; tensioning later is easier to judge without it.

### 7. Posterolateral corner and the peroneal nerve
**Why:** A lateral hockey-stick incision. The common peroneal nerve is identified behind biceps femoris and released around the fibular neck, both to protect it and because it is often tethered in scar and haematoma. A tunnel is drilled through the fibular head, another through the proximal tibia from front to back, and two in the lateral femoral condyle; one or two grafts are routed to rebuild the fibular collateral, popliteus and popliteofibular ligament.
**Anaesthesia:** The nerve is handled, retracted and sometimes found already stretched or ruptured; what the surgeon sees here is the best prognostic information anyone will have, so ask and write it down. This step is the reason the sciatic block waits. A dense popliteal block placed before surgery makes a new foot drop uninterpretable for 12 to 24 hours in the one operation where the surgeon has just dissected the nerve, and it also removes the pain that announces a calf compartment syndrome. The lateral and posterior dissection is infiltrated by the surgeon before closure instead.

### 8. Medial side
**Why:** A medial incision; the superficial medial collateral and the posterior oblique ligament are repaired to bone with anchors if the tissue is good, or reconstructed with grafts through femoral and tibial tunnels. The saphenous nerve and its infrapatellar branch cross the field.
**Anaesthesia:** The medial incision sits close to an adductor canal catheter entry point: this is why the catheter is placed proximally or at the end. Numbness lateral to the incision afterwards is the infrapatellar branch, a surgical consequence and not a block complication, and worth saying to the patient in advance.

### 9. Tensioning and fixation in sequence
**Why:** With every graft passed, the knee is reduced and the grafts are fixed one at a time: posterior cruciate at 90 degrees with an anterior drawer to restore the tibial step-off, then the posterolateral grafts at about 30 degrees with a valgus and internal rotation force, then the anterior cruciate near extension, then the medial side at 20-30 degrees with varus. The position at each fixation is held by an assistant and checked on the image intensifier.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon needs a motionless patient and a leg that is not fighting the assistant; a stable depth is the contribution, not a late dose of relaxant that will still be there at closure. Every interference screw is one more point of graft fixed under tension. From here the mechanism on this card exists, and the catheter should be bolused before the end rather than after emergence.

### 10. Tourniquet down, haemostasis, closure and brace
**Why:** The cuff is released, bleeding from tunnels and dissections is controlled, drains are placed by some surgeons, the wounds are closed in layers and a hinged brace is locked in extension, with a posterior calf support to stop the tibia sagging on the new posterior cruciate.
**Anaesthesia:** At deflation, look at the foot and feel the pulses with the surgeon before the dressings go on: capillary refill, a palpable or Doppler dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial, and a calf that is soft. A pulse that was present before surgery and is absent now is a popliteal injury or a tunnel-related problem until proven otherwise and is dealt with on the table. Give the antiemetic, check the temperature after a long irrigated case, and start the catheter infusion.

### 11. Recovery and the first 48 hours
**Why:** The surgeon wants the knee protected in the brace, the quadriceps firing, the patella mobile, and passive flexion started within days on a set protocol, with touch or partial weight-bearing for six weeks.
**Anaesthesia:** Three things are examined in recovery and written down with a time: foot perfusion, peroneal function (dorsiflexion of the ankle and great toe), and the compartments. The adductor canal infusion blocks the saphenous territory and the anterior capsule; it does not block the deep peroneal or tibial nerves, so ischaemic calf pain and pain on passive toe movement still get through, which is the reason it was chosen. Pain that escalates on a running catheter with a tense calf is a surgical review, not a bolus. Once the peroneal examination is documented, posterior and lateral pain that the oral regimen cannot hold is a fair indication for a single-shot popliteal sciatic block with dilute local anaesthetic, agreed with the surgeon and with compartment checks continuing. The catheter runs for 48-72 h, through the first physiotherapy sessions. Chemoprophylaxis starts by unit protocol, timed against the catheter.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is grafts under tension in a knee that has to be moved, on top of a great deal of drilled bone, so this is one of the few knee operations where a continuous technique is the author's default rather than the exception. The plan: general anaesthesia; an adductor canal catheter with a bolus of ropivacaine 0.5% 15-20 mL and an infusion of ropivacaine 0.2% for 48-72 h; surgeon infiltration of the lateral, posterior and harvest-site dissection with a weight-checked dose; intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg; regular paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor from the first evening unless the surgeon has a specific objection; and an oral opioid, modified-release for the first two or three days in most patients with immediate-release rescue, tapered over the first fortnight. Ketamine by low-dose infusion is reasonable intra-operatively and for the first night in the opioid-tolerant patient.

The block choice follows from two competing facts. The pain is circumferential: anterior and medial from the tunnels and the medial dissection (femoral and saphenous territory), posterior and lateral from the posterior cruciate tunnel, the capsule and the posterolateral corner (sciatic territory). A femoral plus sciatic combination would cover all of it. But the sciatic territory is also where the surveillance is: the peroneal nerve the surgeon has just dissected, and the anterior, lateral and deep posterior compartments of a leg that has had trauma, fluid and a tourniquet. The defensible default is to block the half that can be blocked safely and continuously, cover the other half with infiltration and systemic analgesia, and add a sciatic block later only as a deliberate, documented decision. The adductor canal is preferred over the femoral nerve because the patient is brace-dependent and needs a quadriceps; in ACL reconstruction the randomised comparison found the adductor canal block noninferior for analgesia with better quadriceps strength. None of this has been tested in multiligament surgery, where the literature on analgesia is close to empty.

The rebound problem is larger than after an ACL. The patient is young, the surgery is bone, and a single shot ends at 2 am on the first night with the worst of the pain still to come: the three risk factors from the rebound cohort and a curve that peaks on day 1 and 2. A unit that cannot run a catheter should keep the patient in for at least two nights and plan a repeat single-shot block on the morning of day 1.

Escalation: pain above 7 with a soft calf, a warm foot and a firing extensor hallucis is treated with the rescue opioid, a catheter bolus and a check that the regular drugs were given. Pain out of proportion with a tense calf, or pain on passive toe movement, is compartment syndrome until pressures or a surgeon say otherwise (see [compartment syndrome](compartment-syndrome-fasciotomy.html)). A tense, hot knee in the first day is a haemarthrosis. A cool or pale foot at any time is the popliteal artery.

## Pitfalls and variants

- After a popliteal artery repair. A vein graft or stent behind the knee, healed or healing fasciotomies, antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy, usually no tourniquet (so a wetter, longer case with real blood loss), and a surgeon who will limit flexion. Deep blocks and catheters are timed against the anticoagulant, and foot perfusion checks continue hourly overnight.
- Staged reconstruction. Each stage is shorter and less painful than the single-stage operation; the first stage (collaterals and posterior cruciate) carries most of the neurovascular risk and the catheter indication, the second is close to an ordinary ACL.
- Spanning external fixator in place. The first operation may be fixator removal, examination under anaesthesia and reconstruction in one sitting, through pin sites that are colonised.
- Pre-existing foot drop. A quarter of dislocations have a nerve injury before surgery. Document it, tell the patient that the block did not cause it, and still keep the sciatic territory examinable, because a partial lesion can worsen.
- The low-velocity dislocation in severe obesity. A fall from standing height dislocates the knee in a patient with a very high BMI; neurovascular injury is more common, the tourniquet fits badly, the ultrasound views are poor and the airway and ventilation over five hours are their own problem.
- Allograft versus autograft. Allograft shortens the operation and removes the harvest-site pain; bilateral autograft harvest adds a second painful leg and a second block.
- Open tibial inlay posterior cruciate. A posteromedial approach beside the popliteal vessels, sometimes with a turn to prone or lateral in mid-case: the airway, eyes and pressure areas are re-checked as for a new position.
- Arthrofibrosis. The commonest complication and the reason analgesia matters beyond comfort: the patient who cannot tolerate flexion in the first weeks returns for a [manipulation under anaesthesia](knee-mua-arthrolysis.html).
- Thromboembolism. Trauma, a long tourniquet, a brace and restricted weight-bearing in one limb. A swollen calf at two weeks is a thrombosis, and a tense calf at two hours is a compartment.
- Opioid-tolerant and chronic pain patients. Common in a trauma population that has waited weeks for surgery on opioids. The catheter is not optional for them, and the pain service should know before the day.

## Key sources

The neurovascular figures rest on a systematic review of 862 dislocations and a small, clean prospective study of the ankle-brachial index. Timing and repair-versus-reconstruction rest on systematic reviews of low-level series. The fluid extravasation risk is described in case reports and narrative review, with no reliable incidence. There is no trial of any analgesic or regional technique in multiligament reconstruction: the block strategy on this card is the author's reasoning from the innervation, the surveillance needs and the ACL literature, and should be read as that.
