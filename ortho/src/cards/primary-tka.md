## What the surgeon is doing and why

Knee osteoarthritis wears the medial compartment first in most patients, so the tibia drifts into varus, the medial collateral ligament and posteromedial capsule contract, the lateral structures stretch, and the joint ends up as a stiff, bowed hinge grinding at a single point. A total knee replacement does not restore that knee; it builds a new one. The surgeon cuts both ends of the joint flat, fits a metal femoral component and a tibial tray with a polyethylene insert between them, and then adjusts the insert thickness and the length of the ligaments until the new joint is stable and tracks straight through its arc. The cruciates are sacrificed (the posterior cruciate is kept in a cruciate-retaining design and replaced by a cam and post in a posterior-stabilised one); the collaterals and the posterior capsule are the only passive stabilisers left, and the whole operation is organised around getting their tension right.

That is why this is the painful arthroplasty. A hip replacement cuts one neck, reams one socket and broaches one canal, deep under a muscle envelope left at its own length; the pain is bone, and it fades once nothing is loaded through the cut (see [posterior approach THA](tha-posterior.html)). A knee replacement makes five cuts on the femur and one on the tibia, strips the medial periosteum off the tibia, splits the quadriceps tendon to get in, everts the patella on its own tendon, and then deliberately sets the collaterals and posterior capsule to a tension they have not had for years, all under a thin subcutaneous layer with no muscle cover. Within hours the physiotherapist bends it to 90 degrees, because an arthroplasty that is not moved becomes a stiff one, and every flexion loads the tensioned ligaments and shears the raw cancellous surfaces against the cement. The hip is bone pain at rest. The knee is retension pain under movement, and it stays that way for the first week.

The surgeon's sequence is: get in through a medial parapatellar arthrotomy; clear the osteophytes and release the medial sleeve; make the cuts (one tibial, five femoral, referenced off an intramedullary rod or off pinned trackers and a registered plan); balance the flexion and extension gaps by releasing the tight side, adjusting a cut or choosing a thicker insert, or in the robotic workflow by moving the plan so that less tissue is released; deal with the patella; lavage, cement and implant; infiltrate, close and let the tourniquet down.

The pain logic follows that sequence. The dominant mechanism is retension: ligaments and capsule set tight and loaded by every flexion, the deep ache and stiffness pain that the physiotherapists fight for a fortnight. Underneath it is bone, from six cancellous cuts and the cement interface, and periosteum, from the medial release. The posterior capsule is tensioned, so the back of the knee hurts, and the adductor canal block does not reach it. The tourniquet adds an ischaemia-reperfusion contribution the randomised trials can measure on day 1. And the operation finishes with an everted patella and a split extensor mechanism that have to work on day 0. Compare the [unicompartmental replacement](unicompartmental-knee.html), which keeps the cruciates, balances nothing, makes two small cuts and sits two points lower on the scale.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative decisions: spinal or general, block, dexamethasone, tranexamic acid
**Why:** Everything the surgeon wants (a still, relaxed leg through the cuts, a dry field, a patient who can bend the knee that afternoon) is decided before the patient reaches the table.
**Anaesthesia:** The defensible default is a spinal with sedation, an adductor canal block, intravenous dexamethasone and tranexamic acid, and the surgeon's infiltration. The ICAROS consensus prefers neuraxial anaesthesia for knee arthroplasty as a weak recommendation on low-level evidence, with lower odds of pulmonary complications (0.69), deep venous thrombosis (0.77), infection (0.80) and transfusion (0.84) and no mortality difference; the practical case for the spinal is that it covers the tourniquet and the cement step without a deep general anaesthetic in an elderly patient. A general anaesthetic with the same block and infiltration is the right answer for the patient who refuses, is anticoagulated, or has a spine that will not give a spinal in reasonable time. Keep the spinal dose modest and avoid long-acting intrathecal opioid on a same-day or next-morning mobilisation pathway: PROSPECT allows intrathecal morphine 100 micrograms only for an inpatient when neither the block nor infiltration is possible. Dexamethasone 10 mg IV is a PROSPECT recommendation in its own right here, not only an antiemetic; it prolongs the block and its absence nearly doubled the odds of rebound pain in the ambulatory cohort. The adductor canal block takes 15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%, and PROSPECT's reason for choosing it over the femoral block is quadriceps function, not analgesia, which the two provide about equally.

### 2. Positioning, tourniquet decision and antibiotic timing
**Why:** The surgeon needs the knee to flex past 90 degrees under its own weight against a post or holder, with the foot resting in a bump at the chosen angle, and a tourniquet on the thigh if one is being used. The tourniquet is a surgical habit from the days when cementing needed a bloodless bone surface; many surgeons now use it only for the cement step, and some not at all.
**Anaesthesia:** Cefazolin 2 g IV must be in before the cuff inflates, because nothing given afterwards reaches the leg. The tourniquet question deserves one honest sentence to a surgeon who still uses it for the whole case: in 41 randomised trials and 2,819 patients, a tourniquet raised serious adverse events from 29 to 59 per 1,000 (RR 1.73, moderate certainty), raised day 1 pain by 1.25 points, and made no difference to function at a year. In a spinal the cuff is silent for the first hour and then produces the slow rise in blood pressure and the restless patient at 60-90 minutes, which is tourniquet pain through a working spinal and not a failing one; sedation or a small dose of ketamine and a word to the surgeon about time are the treatment. Under general anaesthesia the same rise reads as light anaesthesia and gets more vapour, which is why a long tourniquet case under GA ends in a hypotensive patient when the cuff comes down. Check the contralateral heel: the operated leg is pushed against a post for an hour and the other leg is forgotten against a hard edge.

### 3. Medial parapatellar arthrotomy and patellar eversion
**Why:** The arthrotomy runs through the quadriceps tendon above the patella, along the medial patellar border and down the medial edge of the patellar tendon, so the extensor mechanism can be swung laterally; the patella is everted or subluxed and the knee flexed to deliver the joint. Midvastus and subvastus approaches spare the quadriceps tendon at the cost of exposure, and the claimed benefit is faster quadriceps recovery.
**Anaesthesia:** The first real nociception of the case, and the first test of a borderline spinal. Eversion of the patella in a stiff knee strains the patellar tendon at the tubercle, and the surgeon wants full quadriceps relaxation here, which a spinal provides and a general anaesthetic needs neuromuscular block to match. Nothing bleeds much under a tourniquet; without one, the suprapatellar fat and synovium bleed steadily and the surgeon reaches for diathermy and the blood pressure target.

### 4. Osteophyte clearance, medial release and tibial delivery
**Why:** Osteophytes tent the capsule and mimic contracture, so they come off first. The deep medial collateral ligament and posteromedial capsule are lifted subperiosteally off the proximal tibia with an elevator, which both exposes the tibia and begins the varus correction; the tibia is then delivered forward in flexion with a lever behind it.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the periosteal contribution to the pain, proportional to the deformity: a 15-degree varus knee needs a long medial sleeve release, a straight knee almost none. The posterior lever sits against the popliteal vessels and the tibial nerve, which are the structures at risk through the whole middle of the operation.

### 5. Bone cuts: femur and tibia, conventional, navigated or robotic
**Why:** The distal femoral cut sets the extension gap and the coronal alignment; the tibial cut sets the slope and the other half of the extension gap; the four remaining femoral cuts set the flexion gap and the rotation of the femoral component. Conventional instruments reference a rod passed up the femoral canal. Navigation uses pinned trackers and a mapped surface; robotic platforms add a haptic boundary or a guided saw to the plan, and in the newer workflows the cuts are planned to produce balanced gaps with the ligaments as they are.
**Anaesthesia:** The intramedullary femoral rod is the fat embolism step of the knee: the canal is opened and a rod driven up it, forcing marrow into the venous system as a reamed femur does, less because no pressurised cement follows it. A transient fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide and saturation as the rod goes in is the signature, and the patient with poor reserve is the one to watch. Navigation and robotic systems avoid the rod but add pins through the cortex of femur and tibia, each a small osteotomy with its own pain and a stress riser that has produced fractures through pin sites. Registration takes 10-20 minutes with the limb manipulated through its range, which the sedated spinal patient tolerates and the awake one does not. The saw through cancellous bone is the bone mechanism, finished in minutes, and the loudest part of the case for a lightly sedated patient.

### 6. Soft-tissue balancing: the mechanism this card is built on
**Why:** With trials or spacer blocks in, the surgeon checks that the extension gap and the flexion gap are rectangular and equal, that the knee reaches full extension, and that neither collateral opens more than a millimetre or two to stress. A tight medial side gets more release off the tibia; a tight lateral side in a valgus knee gets a release of the iliotibial band or posterolateral capsule; a flexion contracture gets posterior capsular release or more distal femur; a loose knee gets a thicker insert. The target is a joint whose collaterals are taut through the whole arc.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the retension mechanism being created in front of you. Ligaments and capsule that have been slack or contracted for years are set to a new length and will be loaded by every flexion from this afternoon, and that deep, stiff ache, worst with movement on days 1 and 2, is the pain the adductor canal block only partly reaches and the oral regimen has to carry. Robotic balancing, which adjusts the plan rather than the ligaments, releases less tissue; whether that is less pain in a randomised comparison is not settled and the card does not claim it. The surgeon manipulates the knee through its arc repeatedly here and the hip with it; a sedated patient who is light reacts to the stress tests.

### 7. Patella and lateral retinacular release
**Why:** The patella is resurfaced with a cemented polyethylene button in many practices, after a cut that leaves about 12-15 mm of bone, and left native with its edge denervated by diathermy in others. If it tracks laterally with the trial in, a lateral retinacular release is made from inside the joint.
**Anaesthesia:** A patellar cut too thin fractures later, and reappears as the patient with a fracture through a resurfaced patella at six weeks. The lateral release is a raw capsular edge that bleeds after the tourniquet comes down, and it is the usual reason for a larger haemarthrosis.

### 8. Cementing and implantation
**Why:** Pulsed lavage clears the cancellous surfaces of fat and blood so cement can interdigitate; cement goes onto the tibial surface and the keel recess, the tray is impacted, then the femoral component, then the insert, and the knee is held in extension under compression until the cement cures. Cementless components are impacted onto a porous metal surface and rely on bone ingrowth.
**Anaesthesia:** The cement load is two flat surfaces and a short keel, not a pressurised femoral canal, so bone cement implantation syndrome is uncommon after a knee and almost always mild; it does occur in the frail patient with a cemented stemmed tibial component, and the preparations in the [cement chapter](../a/a02-cement-bcis.html) apply to that patient. The practical point is time: a tourniquet-for-cement-only surgeon inflates here, and a whole-case surgeon is now at 60-90 minutes with the pressure rising. The 10-12 minutes while the cement sets is dead time for the surgeon and the moment for the second tranexamic acid dose if the unit uses one, the ondansetron, and a look at the fluid balance.

### 9. Periarticular infiltration, closure and tourniquet release
**Why:** The surgeon injects a large-volume local anaesthetic mixture around the joint: into the posterior capsule before the final components go in (the only moment the back of the knee is accessible), then the collaterals, the cut capsular and retinacular edges, the quadriceps, the periosteum round the tibial cut and the subcutaneous tissues at closure. Recipes vary: ropivacaine 0.2-0.375% to a total of 200-300 mg in 100-150 mL is the common skeleton, with adrenaline, ketorolac and sometimes morphine or a corticosteroid added; PROSPECT regards the added value of adrenaline and ketorolac as questionable and the optimal site and volume as unknown. The arthrotomy is closed in flexion or extension by preference, a drain is unusual now, and the tourniquet comes off before or after closure.
**Anaesthesia:** The infiltration is half of the analgesic plan, because it is the only thing that covers the posterior capsule and the lateral side; a surgeon who skips it leaves you with a block that covers the front of the knee and nothing else, and that is the case for adding an IPACK block before the patient leaves theatre. Watch the arithmetic: 300 mg of ropivacaine in the infiltration plus 100 mg in the block is near the ceiling for a small elderly patient, and tourniquet release delivers the infiltrated dose to the circulation over the following 20 minutes. Release also produces the fall in blood pressure and the rise in carbon dioxide and potassium from the reperfused leg, proportional to inflation time, and the spinal patient who was hypertensive from the cuff is now hypotensive and bleeding into the joint; a vasopressor drawn up and the second tranexamic acid dose in are the preparation. Without a tourniquet none of this happens.

### 10. Recovery, the first night, day 0 physiotherapy and discharge
**Why:** The surgeon wants the knee moving the same day: standing with a frame in the afternoon, flexion to 90 degrees within a day or two, full extension maintained from the start. Stiffness at six weeks is the complication they fear most and the one that brings the patient back for a [manipulation](knee-mua-arthrolysis.html).
**Anaesthesia:** The spinal recedes over two to four hours, the adductor canal block over 12-24 hours with dexamethasone, and the infiltration over a similar period. Day 0 is the quiet day: the trajectory on this card has pain at 3 that evening and 6 on days 1 and 2 with the plan working. Regular paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor go in before the spinal has worn off, not after the first complaint; the opioid is charted for rescue and for the nights, and the night plan is explained before the patient leaves recovery, because pain arriving at 2 am as the block recedes is the rule after bone surgery. Rebound pain in the ambulatory cohort occurred in half of all patients, more often after bone surgery, in women and in the young, and less often with intravenous dexamethasone on board. Day 1 physiotherapy is the real test: movement-evoked pain of 6-7 with a quadriceps that works is what the plan is designed to produce, and it beats a femoral block with a painless knee that buckles. Stay is one to three nights. Same-day discharge is an established pathway for the selected patient (younger, lower comorbidity, support at home, a unit that telephones the next morning); the pooled non-randomised data show more complications in outpatients (16.1% versus 10.5%, no readmission difference) at very low certainty, which reads as a selection problem rather than a verdict, and the [day-case chapter](../a/a09-eras-day-case.html) sets out the criteria.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is retension and bone loaded by movement from day 0, with the posterior capsule involved, so the plan has to cover the front and the back of the knee, preserve the quadriceps, and run for three days without a block. PROSPECT's 2022 guideline for total knee arthroplasty is the strongest procedure-specific evidence in this book. Its skeleton: paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started before or during surgery and continued; a single-shot adductor canal block; surgeon-delivered local infiltration, with the combination preferred over either alone; intravenous dexamethasone of at least 10 mg; and opioid reserved for rescue. It does not recommend femoral or sciatic blocks, on functional recovery grounds, nor epidural analgesia, gabapentinoids, ketamine, dexmedetomidine or continuous intra-articular infusion. Intrathecal morphine 100 micrograms is allowed only for an inpatient under spinal when neither the block nor infiltration is possible.

The block hierarchy follows from the anatomy. The adductor canal block covers the saphenous nerve and the nerve to vastus medialis, which is the anteromedial knee and the incision, with a quadriceps that still fires; that is the whole argument against the femoral block, which gives the same analgesia and weakens the muscle the physiotherapist needs. The posterior capsule is supplied by tibial and obturator articular branches that no anterior block reaches; the surgeon's infiltration into the posterior capsule is the first way to cover it and an IPACK block is the second. The pooled trials of adding IPACK to multimodal analgesia show lower rest pain in the first 12 hours (by about 0.85 points) and on day 1, less opioid to 48 hours and a stay shorter by about 0.4 days, nothing by day 3 and no change in range of motion or quadriceps strength: real, early and modest. The author's practice is to add IPACK when the surgeon does not infiltrate the posterior capsule, for the opioid-tolerant, and for a same-day discharge, and otherwise to rely on the infiltration.

The catheter decision: an adductor canal catheter is not the default for a primary knee with a good infiltration, because the gain over a single shot with dexamethasone is small and the catheter gets in the way of the mobilisation that is the point of the pathway; this is author's judgement on top of a guideline that does not mention catheters. It earns its place for the opioid-tolerant or chronic pain patient, the stiff knee that will be worked hard on day 1, the patient with a previous catastrophic night after a block, and the unit that cannot guarantee a 2 am oral dose. An epidural does the same job with a bladder catheter, hypotension and a patient who cannot walk, which is PROSPECT's objection to it.

Escalation: pain above 7 on day 1 with a quadriceps that fires and a soft calf is treated with the charted rescue opioid and a check that the regular analgesia was actually given; pain out of proportion with a tense, swollen knee in the first 24 hours is a haemarthrosis and needs the surgeon; calf pain, a tense calf or numb toes under a block that should have receded is compartment syndrome or a vascular injury until proven otherwise, and the block does not excuse a delay. A rescue adductor canal block the next morning is reasonable and does not weaken the leg; a rescue femoral block does.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Tourniquet or not. The randomised evidence is against the whole-case tourniquet on pain and complication grounds; a cement-only tourniquet is the common compromise, and no tourniquet means a wetter field, steady loss through the case and a quieter emergence. Plan the vasopressor and the second tranexamic acid dose around whichever the surgeon does.
- Robotic and navigated knees. No femoral rod, so less marrow embolisation; pins in both bones, so pin-site pain and a fracture risk through the tracts; registration adds time under the spinal, so dose it for the longer case early in a surgeon's learning curve.
- Cementless. Younger patients with good bone; impaction rather than cement, no cure time, and a surgeon who may restrict weight-bearing on day 1 by implant design.
- Bilateral simultaneous replacement. Two infiltrations and the local anaesthetic ceiling reached easily; double the blood loss and the marrow load; the spinal is dosed for both knees from the start or the case runs under general anaesthesia with bilateral blocks. Selection is stricter than for one knee and the complication rate higher.
- Valgus knee. The releases are lateral, the common peroneal nerve is at risk when a fixed valgus and flexion deformity is corrected, and a foot drop after a valgus knee is the surgeon's correction, not the block. Document peroneal function before any block.
- The stiff or previously operated knee. More release, a longer case, more bleeding, and a pain course at the top of the range; this is the catheter patient.
- Opioid-tolerant patients. Retension pain under movement is worst in exactly the patient whose opioid receptors are least useful; a catheter, ketamine and a pre-agreed plan with the pain service are all reasonable even though PROSPECT does not recommend ketamine for the standard patient.
- Diabetes. Dexamethasone 10 mg raises glucose for a day and the infection consequences of a knee are grave; it is still worth giving for the block and the rebound effect, and the ward needs to know.
- Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. Infiltration plus block is the largest cumulative dose in elective orthopaedics, delivered to the circulation at tourniquet release; lipid emulsion lives in the theatre, not the pharmacy.
- Infection. Cefazolin before the cuff, repeated for a case beyond three hours or a loss beyond 1.5 L, and the [infection card](periprosthetic-joint-infection.html) for what happens when it fails.

## Key sources

PROSPECT's knee guideline is the backbone and the block hierarchy on this card is its hierarchy; the statements about catheters, IPACK selection and the tourniquet conversation are the author's reading of the Cochrane tourniquet review, the IPACK meta-analysis and the rebound pain cohort, not guideline statements. The ICAROS consensus favours neuraxial anaesthesia for the knee as a weak recommendation on low-level evidence, and the card says so rather than claiming more. The outpatient data are non-randomised and very low certainty. The Gerbershagen cohort is not cited because its knee replacement figure sits in a figure the author could not read; the claim that the knee is the painful arthroplasty rests on the mechanism.
