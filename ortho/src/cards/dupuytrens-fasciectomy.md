## What the surgeon is doing and why

Dupuytren's disease is a fibromatosis of the palmar fascia. Myofibroblasts in the normal longitudinal bands of the palmar aponeurosis lay down collagen, the bands thicken into nodules and then into cords, and the cords shorten over years and pull the metacarpophalangeal and proximal interphalangeal joints into flexion. The ring and little fingers go first. The disease is painless, which is the first thing to understand about the pain plan: the patient comes to surgery with a bent finger and no pain, and everything they feel afterwards is the operation. The second thing is that the cord is not a separate structure that can be lifted out: it is the patient's own fascia, wrapped around the digital nerves and arteries, which it has displaced and spiralled around so that the bundle may lie on top of the cord in the finger where the surgeon expects it to be underneath. The operation is a dissection of two nerves and two arteries out of diseased tissue under magnification, with a knife, in a hand that will scar everything it can.

The surgeon's choices, in rising order of what they do to the hand:

1. Needle fasciotomy: a hypodermic needle passed through the skin to divide the cord at several levels, under local anaesthetic, in a clinic room. Quick, cheap, and the contracture recurs earliest, with the highest recurrence of the three in prospective data over three to four years.
2. Collagenase injection into the cord, with the finger manipulated straight a day or more later. The DISC trial of 672 patients found it did not meet non-inferiority against limited fasciectomy at one year, with more minor complications (skin tears, haematoma, oedema) but fewer moderate or severe ones (2% against 5%), and weak evidence of more recurrence; in the pooled randomised trials recurrence was about equal to needle fasciotomy and well above fasciectomy.
3. Limited (selective) fasciectomy, the standard operation: a zigzag Bruner incision along the palm and into the finger, skin flaps raised off the cord, the diseased fascia excised from its proximal end to the finger with the neurovascular bundles identified and followed throughout, and the finger straightened. The joints are then assessed: a metacarpophalangeal joint straightens once the cord is gone, a proximal interphalangeal joint that has been flexed for years has a contracted volar plate and collateral ligaments and may need a capsular release, which the surgeon does cautiously because it adds swelling and stiffness. The skin, which has shortened with the cord, is closed with Z-plasties or left open in part (the McCash open palm) to heal by secondary intention. Recurrence is lowest with fasciectomy (about 16% in prospective series with long follow-up) and the hand therapy and night splinting afterwards are what hold the correction.
4. Dermofasciectomy: the diseased skin is excised with the fascia and replaced with a full-thickness skin graft from the forearm or groin, for recurrent disease or aggressive diathesis, on the principle that the disease does not recur under a graft. A longer case, a donor site, a graft that must be immobilised, and the top of this card's range.

The complications that reach you are in the 20-year literature review: overall surgical complication rates of 3.6-39.1%, major complications in 15.7%, with digital nerve injury in 3.4%, digital artery injury in 2%, infection in 2.4%, haematoma in 2.1%, and complex regional pain syndrome in 5.5%; wound healing problems in 22.9% and a "flare reaction" (a swollen, red, stiff, painful hand at two to four weeks) in 9.9%. In recurrent disease the nerve and artery injury rate is about ten times higher (20% against 2%), because the bundle is in scar and cannot be found by its usual landmarks.

The pain logic is surface and skin rather than bone or tendon: a long incision with raised flaps, a dissection that touches the nerves along their length, a joint pushed straight that does not want to be, and a palm that swells. The day 1 pain is a 4 and it is the swelling and the tension on the flaps; the pain after that is stiffness pain, worst in the mornings and with therapy, and the operation's enemy is not nociception but the fibrosis, oedema and flare that make a stiff finger. The two things that matter for a 2-scoring operation are therefore the tourniquet, which hurts an awake patient at the top of a two-hour case, and the hand therapy, which needs a patient who can move the fingers from day 1 and is not frightened of them. Complex regional pain syndrome at 5.5% is the complication the anaesthetic plan can try to influence, and the Dutch guideline's secondary prevention advice (adequate peri-operative analgesia, shorter operating time, limited tourniquet, regional anaesthesia) is what underlies the choice of a block for the bigger cases.

## Steps

### 1. The extent of the operation decides the anaesthetic
**Why:** A single-ray limited fasciectomy is 45-90 min; a three-ray fasciectomy with a PIP release, or a dermofasciectomy with a graft, is two to three hours with a tourniquet that will reach its limit. The surgeon knows which from the examination and the plan.
**Anaesthesia:** For a single ray in a surgeon who uses WALANT, surgeon infiltration of 40 mL of buffered lidocaine with adrenaline per ray with no tourniquet is the established approach: in the 148-finger series complication rates were the same as under general anaesthesia with a tourniquet, and the range of motion was similar finger by finger. For more than one ray, a revision, a PIP release or a dermofasciectomy, the author's practice is an axillary, supraclavicular or infraclavicular block as the sole anaesthetic with light sedation and intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, because the block covers a tourniquet that WALANT cannot replace at those volumes, gives a sympathetic block that may matter for the complex regional pain syndrome risk, and lets the patient go home the same day. A general anaesthetic with a supraglottic airway on top of the block is for the patient who will not be awake for two hours, or where the surgeon wants an immobile hand for the graft. Document the digital nerves before the block: a numb fingertip the next day is one of the 3.4%, and in recurrent disease one of the 20%.

### 2. Position, lead hand, loupes and the tourniquet
**Why:** Supine with the arm on a hand table and the hand held flat and open on a lead hand or by an assistant; the surgeon works under loupes and needs the fingers held straight against the cord's pull and then straighter as the cord is released. The upper arm tourniquet is inflated after exsanguination and is the surgeon's view of the neurovascular bundles.
**Anaesthesia:** An awake patient under a block can lie for 90 min without much complaint and for 150 min with more; the shoulder at 90 degrees, the head turned away, a screen, and the sedation reserved for the second hour. The tourniquet is the limit on the awake technique: the block covers the arm below it, the intercostobrachial injection or the sedation covers the cuff for the first 45-60 min, and after that an awake patient on a long case is uncomfortable whatever is done. Say the time at 60 and 90 min, because a multi-ray fasciectomy in a scarred hand is the operation that drifts past 120 min while the surgeon is still finding a nerve.

### 3. Antibiotic and the first incision
**Why:** A Bruner zigzag incision in the palm and along the finger, designed so that the flaps can be closed as Z-plasties to lengthen the skin; or a longitudinal incision converted to Z-plasties at the end. The flaps are raised off the cord with the skin thin and adherent over nodules, which is where the skin tears and the buttonholes happen.
**Anaesthesia:** Cefazolin 2 g IV before the tourniquet is many surgeons' practice for an operation with flaps and sometimes a graft, and it is reasonable without being evidence-based. Nothing else to do for the first 20 min of flap raising.

### 4. Finding the neurovascular bundles
**Why:** The surgeon identifies the digital nerves and arteries in the palm, proximal to the disease, where they are in their normal position, and then follows them distally into the cord, releasing the fascia from around them. The spiral cord of the finger wraps the bundle and brings it to the midline and superficial, so the surgeon dissects from known to unknown and never cuts what has not been seen.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the slow middle of the operation and the step that sets its length; in a revision the bundle is in scar and the dissection can double the time. In an awake patient the surgeon's silence here is concentration. Movement of the hand is the hazard: a lightly sedated patient who jerks when the tourniquet aches is a cut nerve, so the second hour's sedation is spent keeping the hand still, and a patient who cannot keep still is converted to a general anaesthetic before this step, not during it.

### 5. Fasciectomy and the joint release
**Why:** With the bundles safe, the cord is excised from the palm to its insertion on the flexor sheath, the middle phalanx or the lateral digital sheet; the finger is then straightened. If the PIP joint will not extend, the surgeon decides whether to release the check-rein ligaments and the volar plate, accepting that a released joint swells and stiffens and may lose flexion for extension. Some surgeons accept 30 degrees of residual PIP contracture rather than release.
**Anaesthesia:** The joint release is a capsular step that raises the pain and stiffness of the first week and is the reason the card's day 2 and 3 figures are not lower. The finger straightened for the first time in years is a stretch on a nerve and an artery that have been short for as long; a finger that goes white when straightened has an artery in spasm or a shortened artery, and the surgeon will flex it back, warm it and wait, which is time on the tourniquet.

### 6. Dermofasciectomy and the graft
**Why:** In recurrent or aggressive disease the skin is excised with the fascia and a full-thickness graft from the medial forearm, the antecubital fossa or the groin is sewn in, quilted and dressed with a tie-over bolster. The graft needs an immobile bed for a week, so the finger is splinted and the therapy starts later.
**Anaesthesia:** A second wound, which may be outside the block (the groin, or the forearm above an axillary block's reliable territory), and a longer case; the donor site is often the sorest part of the week. For a groin donor site under a brachial plexus block, the surgeon infiltrates the donor site, or the case is done under general anaesthesia. This is the operation at the top of the card's range and the one for which an overnight stay for elevation is reasonable.

### 7. Tourniquet release, haemostasis and closure
**Why:** The tourniquet is released before closure and every bleeding point in the palm is found, because a haematoma under a palmar flap lifts the skin off its bed, kills the flap edge and starts the fibrosis; the open palm technique leaves a transverse wound in the palm unclosed to let blood drain and the skin to lengthen. Z-plasties are closed, the finger dressed in a bulky dressing with the fingers extended, and the hand elevated.
**Anaesthesia:** Reperfusion after a 90-120 min tourniquet is noticeable but benign in a fit patient; under a block alone the patient feels the warmth and a deep ache in the arm for a few minutes. The dressing goes on before any sedation lightens.

### 8. Recovery, day 1 and the therapy that is the operation
**Why:** The surgeon wants the hand elevated, the fingers moving within the dressing from day 1, a check of the wound and graft at a week, and night extension splinting under a hand therapist for three to six months; the therapy decides the result.
**Anaesthesia:** The block recedes at 12-24 h and the palm is tight and sore when it does, with the rebound pattern that half of ambulatory block patients describe; paracetamol and an NSAID start in recovery and an oral opioid is charted for the first two or three nights. The WALANT patient has no rebound to speak of, because the local wears off in the afternoon with the patient awake. Nearly every patient goes home the same day; the dermofasciectomy patient may stay a night for elevation. Numbness in a fingertip that persists past the block is a nerve injury and the surgeon is told; a cold white finger at any point is an artery and is urgent.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is surface and skin with a joint release in some, and the score is a 2: the pain is real for a week, it is swelling and tension rather than bone, and it responds to elevation, movement and simple analgesia. The anaesthetic is chosen for the surgeon's needs (a dry field, a still hand, a tourniquet or no tourniquet) and for the two things the plan can influence, a comfortable patient who will do the therapy, and a lower risk of complex regional pain syndrome. There is no PROSPECT guideline and no randomised analgesia trial for fasciectomy; the plan is the mechanism and author's judgement, with the Dutch complex regional pain syndrome guideline as the only external anchor.

The defensible default for a single ray is surgeon infiltration under WALANT where the surgeon practises it, with paracetamol and an NSAID started before the local wears off and continued for a week, and a small supply of oral opioid for the first two nights. For a multi-ray fasciectomy, a revision, a PIP release or a dermofasciectomy, the default is a brachial plexus block as the sole anaesthetic (axillary with a musculocutaneous and intercostobrachial supplement, or supraclavicular or infraclavicular) with 20-30 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%, intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, light sedation, and the same oral regimen with a written night plan for the rebound. The Dutch guideline's secondary prevention list for complex regional pain syndrome (adequate peri-operative analgesia, a short operating time, a limited tourniquet, regional anaesthesia) is a reason to choose the block over a general anaesthetic alone for the big cases, and the author treats it as a reason rather than as proof.

A catheter is rarely warranted. The author's judgement is that it earns its place only for the revision multi-ray dermofasciectomy in a patient who has had complex regional pain syndrome before, where a sympathetic block for several days is the aim, and for that patient a conversation with the pain service before the operation is more useful than a catheter decided on the day. Rebound pain at 2 am after a block is expected and is a tight, throbbing palm; the oral regimen started in recovery is the treatment. Escalation: a tense, bulging dressing with pain out of proportion in the first hours is a haematoma under the flap and is surgical; a finger that is white or blue is an artery; a hand that at two to four weeks is swollen, red, shiny, stiff and painful beyond what the wound explains is either a flare reaction or the start of complex regional pain syndrome, and both are referred to the hand therapist and the surgeon early rather than reassured, with vitamin C, early movement and a short course of a neuropathic agent being author's judgement rather than evidence.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Needle fasciotomy and collagenase. Clinic-room procedures under local infiltration, no anaesthetist; higher and earlier recurrence, fewer severe complications; the patient who has had collagenase and comes to fasciectomy later has a scarred cord and a harder dissection.
- Single ray versus multiple rays. The single ray is a WALANT or Bier's block case; three rays with a PIP release is a two-hour block case with a tourniquet at its limit.
- Revision surgery. Nerve and artery injury about ten times commoner (20% against 2%); a longer case, a dermofasciectomy more often, and the surgeon's quietest hour.
- The PIP joint release. Adds swelling, stiffness and a day of pain; some surgeons do not do it, accepting residual contracture.
- The open palm. A transverse palmar wound left to heal by secondary intention; it looks alarming on the ward and drains for two weeks, and it is deliberate.
- Dermofasciectomy. A graft, a donor site outside the block in some cases, a splint for a week, a possible overnight stay.
- The tourniquet in an awake patient. Tolerable for 45-60 min on sedation alone, and the second hour is where the awake technique fails; the time is said aloud, and a surgeon who wants more than 120 min on a block-alone patient needs to know before the start.
- Haematoma. The complication the surgeon is most afraid of; the tourniquet comes down before closure, and a bulging dressing in recovery is surgical.
- Complex regional pain syndrome. 5.5% in the literature review, the highest of any operation in this section, and more after a long tourniquet and a big dissection; the block, the short operation and the early therapy are the modifiable parts.
- Diabetes, epilepsy, alcohol and the diathesis. Dupuytren's patients are often older men with these; the NSAID and the sedation are planned around them, and the aggressive diathesis (young, bilateral, ectopic disease) is the one that gets a dermofasciectomy.
- Anticoagulation. A superficial block at a compressible site is reasonable; the surgeon's haemostasis is the issue, not the block.

## Key sources

The surgical evidence is good: a 20-year review of fasciectomy complications, the 672-patient DISC trial of collagenase against limited fasciectomy, two systematic reviews of randomised trials across the three treatments, and a prospective-data review of recurrence with long follow-up, which together say that fasciectomy corrects most, recurs least and has the most serious complications. The anaesthetic evidence is one retrospective series showing fasciectomy under local anaesthetic with adrenaline matches general anaesthesia with a tourniquet, and the WALANT lecture that gives the volumes. The link between regional anaesthesia, tourniquet time and complex regional pain syndrome rests on a multidisciplinary guideline's secondary prevention statement, not on a trial in Dupuytren's patients, and the block, catheter and sedation advice is author's judgement. The score is calibrated between the carpal tunnel and distal radius anchors.
