## What the surgeon is doing and why

The carpus is two rows of bones with no tendons attached to the proximal row. The scaphoid, lunate and triquetrum move only because ligaments tie them to each other and to the rows either side: the scaphoid wants to flex, the triquetrum wants to extend, and the lunate in between is held neutral by the scapholunate and lunotriquetral ligaments. When the scapholunate ligament tears (a fall on the outstretched hand, often missed), the scaphoid flexes and rotates away, the lunate tips into extension, and load that was shared across the radius concentrates on the rim of the scaphoid fossa. Over years this wears a predictable path: radial styloid, then the whole scaphoid fossa, then the joint between capitate and lunate. This is scapholunate advanced collapse. A scaphoid nonunion produces the same pattern by the same mechanics. The radiolunate joint is spared until late, and both salvage operations depend on that fact.

The surgery follows the stage.

Before arthritis, the aim is to restore the link. An acute tear is repaired to bone with anchors and protected with wires across the scapholunate and scaphocapitate joints for eight weeks. A chronic but reducible tear is reconstructed: a strip of flexor carpi radialis is passed through a drill tunnel in the scaphoid from volar to dorsal, across the back of the lunate and anchored (the three-ligament tenodesis and its descendants), or a dorsal capsular flap is tethered to the scaphoid. A perilunate dislocation is the acute, high-energy version, reduced, pinned and repaired, sometimes through dorsal and volar incisions with a carpal tunnel release.

Once the radioscaphoid joint is arthritic, the scaphoid has to go, and the surgeon chooses between two motion-preserving salvage operations:

- Proximal row carpectomy. Scaphoid, lunate and triquetrum are excised and the head of the capitate drops into the lunate fossa of the radius. No fusion, no metal, no nonunion, mobilised at three to four weeks. It needs intact cartilage on the capitate head and lunate fossa and an intact radioscaphocapitate ligament, which the surgeon must not cut while shelling out the scaphoid.
- Scaphoid excision and four-corner fusion. The scaphoid is excised and the capitate, lunate, hamate and triquetrum are fused into one block, with the lunate first corrected out of extension. The joint surfaces are burred to cancellous bone, packed with graft from the excised scaphoid or the distal radius, and compressed with a dorsal circular plate, staples or headless screws. It keeps the native radiolunate joint and carpal height, costs six to eight weeks of immobilisation, and carries a pooled nonunion rate near 9%.

Pooled comparisons find the two broadly equivalent, with slightly better motion and fewer complications after carpectomy. Other limited fusions (scaphotrapeziotrapezoid, radioscapholunate, lunocapitate) are variations on the fusion theme.

The approach is nearly always dorsal: a longitudinal incision, the extensor retinaculum opened through the third compartment, extensor pollicis longus lifted out, and a ligament-sparing capsulotomy. On the floor of the fourth compartment lies the terminal posterior interosseous nerve, which carries only wrist joint afferents at this level; most surgeons excise a centimetre of it routinely, and some add the anterior interosseous branch through the interosseous membrane. That neurectomy is a small built-in analgesic.

The pain logic: for a four-corner fusion the mechanism is bone, with four decorticated surfaces and a graft site, behaving like the [wrist fusion](trapeziectomy-wrist-fusion.html) on a smaller scale: a 3, with a high day 1 that falls by day 3. In the 179-procedure cohort small-joint arthrodeses ranked far above their size. Carpectomy and ligament reconstruction are a notch lower (a high 2 to a 3) because nothing is being compressed, and they share the capsule component of any open wrist operation.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative decisions and the block
**Why:** The surgeon may not know which operation will be done until the capitate head has been seen; consent is usually for carpectomy or four-corner fusion.
**Anaesthesia:** Plan for the longer and more painful option. A plexus block with ropivacaine 0.5% 20-30 mL and dexamethasone 8 mg IV is the anaesthetic and the first night's analgesia; choose the site by the patient (axillary or infraclavicular for the patient who cannot afford a phrenic palsy). Document median and ulnar sensation first: a chronic collapsed wrist often has carpal tunnel symptoms, and a perilunate dislocation presents with acute median compression. Sedation or a supraglottic airway by patient preference; 90-150 min on a narrow table with a tourniquet is long enough that many choose to sleep.

### 2. Arthroscopy, when it comes first
**Why:** The wrist is distracted in finger traps and the scapholunate interval, the capitate head and the lunate fossa are inspected, which decides between reconstruction, carpectomy and fusion.
**Anaesthesia:** Traction through the fingers on a blocked arm with a counterweight on the upper arm: check the elbow padding and that the cuff is not pinched by the strap. Irrigation fluid extravasates into the forearm, harmlessly in a short look.

### 3. Tourniquet, dorsal approach and neurectomy
**Why:** Retinaculum opened, extensor pollicis longus transposed, the posterior interosseous nerve excised from the floor of the fourth compartment, capsule opened as a radially based flap.
**Anaesthesia:** Antibiotic before inflation; note the time. The tourniquet is the only stimulus a good block does not fully cover in a long case: cuff pain appears as a slow rise in pressure and restlessness after 60-75 min and responds to a small dose of opioid or deeper sedation, not to more local anaesthetic.

### 4. Scaphoid excision, or excision of the whole proximal row
**Why:** The scaphoid is removed piecemeal or whole with an osteotome and rongeur, staying on bone to protect the volar ligaments and the radial artery; in a carpectomy the lunate and triquetrum follow. The capitate head is inspected.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing physiological. This is the decision point: ask which operation it has become, since it changes the duration by up to an hour and the discharge prescription.

### 5. Carpectomy completed
**Why:** The capitate is seated in the lunate fossa, a radial styloidectomy is added if the trapezium impinges, and the capsule is closed, sometimes with a temporary wire.
**Anaesthesia:** A 60-90 min tourniquet in total. Raw bone has been removed, not created, and the day 1 peak is lower than after a fusion.

### 6. Four-corner fusion: correction, decortication and graft
**Why:** The lunate is levered out of extension with a joystick wire and pinned to the radius or capitate; the four facing surfaces are burred to bleeding bone and packed with cancellous graft from the scaphoid or from a window in the distal radius.
**Anaesthesia:** Fluoroscopy repeatedly; lead for you. This step sets the pain: four raw surfaces and a metaphyseal graft site, all within the block. A distal radius window is inside the same field. Iliac crest is rare here and belongs to [scaphoid nonunion surgery](wrist-arthroscopy-scaphoid.html) and revisions; if it is planned, it is the one wound the block does not cover and should be infiltrated.

### 7. Fixation
**Why:** A recessed circular plate with radiating screws, compression staples or crossed headless screws hold the block; the plate must sit below the dorsal surface or it impinges on the radius in extension.
**Anaesthesia:** The tourniquet clock is now the constraint; the surgeon will ask how long. Above 120 min, discuss a 10-minute deflation (see [the tourniquet](../a/a01-tourniquet.html)).

### 8. Ligament repair or reconstruction instead
**Why:** For a reducible scapholunate dissociation: the scaphoid is derotated with joystick wires, a tunnel drilled through it, a strip of flexor carpi radialis harvested through small volar incisions and passed dorsally to the lunate, and the reduction is transfixed with wires or a screw for eight weeks.
**Anaesthesia:** Volar and dorsal wounds, both within the block. Buried or protruding wires across the carpus are the source of the ache at two to six weeks and of a second short anaesthetic for removal ([removal of metalwork](removal-of-metalwork.html)).

### 9. Closure, tourniquet release and cast
**Why:** Capsule and retinaculum are repaired with extensor pollicis longus left superficial, the tourniquet is released, and a bulky dressing with a volar slab is applied.
**Anaesthesia:** The slab must allow for swelling: the cancellous surfaces bleed into a closed space after release. A cast applied tight over a blocked hand gives no warning. Give the antiemetic; if the patient is asleep, wake without coughing the arm off the table.

### 10. Recovery and the discharge conversation
**Why:** The surgeon wants strict elevation and moving fingers.
**Anaesthesia:** First doses of paracetamol and NSAID in recovery. Tell the patient when the block will end (12-18 hours with dexamethasone), that the pain rises over an hour or two when it does, and that the oral opioid is to be taken at the first tingling, not at the peak. Sling until the arm obeys, then elevation on pillows.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is bone in a cast with a hard edge at the end of the block. The plan: a single-shot plexus block with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg; paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started in recovery and continued for five to seven days; and oral opioid supplied for three days after a four-corner fusion, for one or two after a carpectomy or ligament reconstruction. Short NSAID courses are not withheld on fusion grounds in the author's practice; a surgeon who objects is usually thinking of weeks, and a COX-2 inhibitor for five days is the usual compromise.

Rebound is the predictable failure. Bone surgery, younger age and omitting dexamethasone were the risk factors in the 972-patient ambulatory cohort, and this population has all the first two. Two ways to blunt it: dexamethasone and pre-emptive oral dosing as above, or the split technique (a short-acting plexus block for surgery with long-acting, low-concentration blocks of the median, radial and ulnar nerves at the elbow or forearm), which gives a comfortable hand overnight with a shoulder and elbow that work. The trial evidence for the split technique is in the [trapeziectomy](trapeziectomy-wrist-fusion.html) card and is extrapolated here.

No catheter as a routine: the pain is over the hump by day 3 and the randomised comparison in [distal radius fixation](distal-radius-orif.html) found no opioid saving. A continuous infraclavicular catheter is defensible for the opioid-tolerant patient or a revision fusion with iliac crest graft.

Escalation: steadily rising pain with tight, swollen, dusky fingers is the dressing; split it to skin before anything else. New median nerve paraesthesia after a perilunate injury or a volar approach is acute carpal tunnel syndrome and is surgical. Pain that is burning, with colour change and stiffness beyond the second week, is the early picture of complex regional pain syndrome, which the wrist produces more often than most sites; early hand therapy referral is the response.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The intraoperative change of plan. Carpectomy becomes four-corner fusion when the capitate head is worn. Chart the discharge analgesia after the operation, not before.
- Perilunate dislocation. An emergency or next-day case after high-energy trauma, with median nerve compression, other injuries, a full stomach and a swollen wrist; closed reduction under sedation or block first, then open repair. A block is still appropriate, with the pre-block median nerve findings written down.
- Staged or bilateral disease. The other wrist is often next; what worked overnight this time is the plan for the second side.
- Wrist denervation as a stand-alone operation. Posterior and anterior interosseous neurectomy through one or two small incisions for an arthritic wrist: 30 min, pain score 1-2, often predicted by a diagnostic block that you may be asked to perform.
- Total wrist fusion or arthroplasty as the alternative for pancarpal arthritis: see [trapeziectomy and wrist fusion](trapeziectomy-wrist-fusion.html).
- Smokers. Nonunion of a four-corner fusion is more likely; the surgeon may prefer carpectomy. Not your decision, but it explains the consent.
- Manual workers and compensation cases are over-represented, with higher baseline pain and opioid use; ask, and plan for [the opioid-tolerant patient](../a/a12-opioid-tolerant.html) when the answer is yes.

## Key sources

The comparison between carpectomy and four-corner fusion rests on meta-analyses of retrospective series that disagree in detail and agree that neither is clearly better. There is no analgesic trial specific to these operations; the block and catheter advice is extrapolated from distal radius, wrist fusion and trapeziectomy studies and from the general ambulatory rebound data, and the pain ranking from the 179-procedure cohort is by analogy with other small-joint arthrodeses.
