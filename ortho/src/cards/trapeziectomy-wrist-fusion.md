## What the surgeon is doing and why

The trapeziometacarpal joint is a saddle with almost no bony constraint. Its stability is ligamentous, mainly the anterior oblique (beak) ligament and the dorsoradial ligament, and the loads across it are large: a pinch at the fingertip is multiplied many times by the time it reaches the thumb base. When the ligaments stretch the metacarpal base subluxes dorsally and radially, the volar cartilage wears, the joint grinds, the metacarpal drifts into adduction so the first web closes, and the metacarpophalangeal joint hyperextends to make up the lost span (the Z-thumb). The patient is usually a woman in her sixties or seventies, often with the same disease in the other hand, and she presents with pain at the base of the thumb on pinch, a positive grind test and a hand that drops cups. The surgical options share a single idea: take the joint out.

Trapeziectomy removes the trapezium, whole or in pieces, through a dorsal or a volar incision over the thumb base. That leaves a gap the height of the bone between the scaphoid and the metacarpal base, and the metacarpal would settle into it and impinge on the scaphoid, so the second half of the operation is about holding the metacarpal up. The variants differ only in how:

1. Simple trapeziectomy holds it with nothing but the haematoma that fills the space and fibroses, a K-wire across the gap in some hands, and a thumb spica cast.
2. Ligament reconstruction and tendon interposition (LRTI) harvests half of flexor carpi radialis (or a slip of abductor pollicis longus) through forearm incisions, passes it through a drill hole in the metacarpal base to suspend the metacarpal, and rolls the remainder into the space as a spacer.
3. Suture-button suspensionplasty drills across the bases of the first and second metacarpals and tensions a suture between two buttons so the thumb metacarpal hangs from the index; no tendon is harvested.
4. Implant arthroplasty keeps the trapezium, reams it for a cup, broaches the metacarpal for a stem and fits a ball-and-socket joint; motion and pinch return faster, at the price of a revision rate trapeziectomy does not have.
5. Arthrodesis, for the young manual worker who needs pinch more than motion, cuts both surfaces and plates them.

The meta-analysis of seven randomised trials and one controlled trial (656 thumbs) found that adding ligament reconstruction to trapeziectomy improved grip and tip pinch at a year, changed neither pain nor the DASH score, and doubled the complications at three months; the authors recommend the simple operation unless strength is specifically needed. Surgeons vary, and all five variants are in routine use. If the metacarpophalangeal joint hyperextends beyond about 30 degrees the surgeon adds a capsulodesis or a fusion of that joint, a second bone operation inside the first.

The wrist operations on this card are the end of a different road: a scapholunate or scaphoid non-union collapse (SLAC or SNAC) that has worn through the radiocarpal and midcarpal joints, a rheumatoid wrist that has subluxed and destroyed itself, Kienböck's disease, or a failed partial fusion. Total wrist arthrodesis takes the pain away by removing every moving joint between the radius and the third metacarpal: a dorsal approach, decortication of the radiocarpal and midcarpal surfaces, bone graft packed into every gap, and a precontoured plate from the distal radius over the capitate to the third metacarpal in about 10-15 degrees of extension. The result is a strong, painless wrist that does not move. Total wrist arthroplasty resurfaces the same joints with a radial component on a stem and a carpal component screwed into a fused distal carpal row, for the low-demand (usually rheumatoid) patient who wants to keep some motion; the carpal component loosens over years. In the systematic review of rheumatoid wrists, 343 arthrodeses and 618 arthroplasties had complication rates of 17% and 19%, both relieved pain, and the long-term survival data for the arthroplasty are still missing. The partial fusions (four-corner fusion, proximal row carpectomy) are the midcarpal alternatives for an earlier stage of collapse and share the anaesthetic of the total fusion.

The pain logic is bone in a small, richly innervated hand that is then immobilised. The trapeziectomy leaves bleeding cancellous surfaces on three bones, a drilled tunnel, a harvested tendon with its forearm incisions, and an incised capsule under lifted thenar muscle; the wrist fusion decorticates three or four joints, strips dorsal periosteum under a plate, and takes graft from the distal radius or the excised carpal bones. None of it is loaded afterwards, because the cast does the loading, so the pain peaks on day 1-2 and falls fast. The size of the incision misleads. In the 179-procedure cohort hand resection arthroplasty ranked 11th and metacarpophalangeal arthrodesis 8th for day 1 pain, above most major operations, and in the one randomised trial that measured it, 92% of trapeziectomy patients given a short-acting block and systemic analgesia alone reported moderate-to-severe maximum pain on day 1 and 81% on day 2. With a long-acting block, dexamethasone and an oral regimen that starts before the block recedes, it is a 3: an expected block, opioid for two or three nights, and a fast fall. Wrist fusion sits at the upper end of the same band, with a day 1 peak that can reach 6; a [distal radius plate](distal-radius-orif.html) sits a band below, because there a fracture that hurt before surgery is fixed rather than a joint newly cut out.

## Steps

### 1. The patient, the anaesthetic choice and the day-case decision
**Why:** The surgeon wants a still hand, a dry field under a tourniquet for 45-150 minutes, and a patient who can go home the same day with a cast on; for a wrist fusion they also want fluoroscopy and sometimes a bone graft from a second site.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient decides the anaesthetic more than the operation does. She is in her seventies, often on an anticoagulant, sometimes diabetic, sometimes rheumatoid with the airway and cervical spine that implies, and the other hand is arthritic too. A brachial plexus block with sedation avoids the general anaesthetic, covers the operation and the first evening, and is the author's default. A supraclavicular or infraclavicular block covers the whole hand and the radial forearm in one injection; an axillary block does the same only if the musculocutaneous nerve is included, because the thumb base incision and the forearm incisions for the tendon harvest sit in the lateral antebrachial cutaneous and superficial radial territories as well as the median one. In the anticoagulated patient the axillary approach has the compressible vessels. Dexamethasone 8 mg IV goes in with the block. The day-case decision rests on who is at home and whether the patient can manage a cast on her dominant hand, a block that has not yet worn off and tablets on a timetable; nearly 40% of the WALANT trapeziectomy cohort said afterwards that they had not understood their post-operative medication, which is the gap to close now.

### 2. Arm table, tourniquet and antibiotic
**Why:** The arm is laid out on a radiolucent table with the shoulder abducted no more than 90 degrees and the tourniquet high on the arm; the thumb base is approached in mid-pronation with the hand on its ulnar border, and the wrist fusion in full pronation. The surgeon wants the cuff up before the skin incision and down before the dressing goes on so that the bed can be checked for bleeding.
**Anaesthesia:** Cefazolin 2 g IV must be in before the cuff inflates, because nothing given afterwards reaches the hand; suture buttons, K-wires, plates and prostheses are implants. The cuff is the one thing the block does not cover. At 45-60 minutes of upper-arm tourniquet a sedated patient becomes restless with a dull ache under the cuff and the blood pressure drifts up; the treatment is more sedation and a word about time, not more local anaesthetic in the arm. Under a general anaesthetic the same rise reads as light anaesthesia. Some surgeons use a forearm cuff for a trapeziectomy, which is better tolerated and sits within a distal block's territory; a wrist fusion needs the upper arm. Pad the ulnar nerve of the other arm and watch for an assistant leaning on the arm table and levering the shoulder into abduction.

### 3. Approach to the thumb base: radial artery and superficial radial nerve
**Why:** A dorsal incision over the first metacarpal base passes between the extensor pollicis brevis and abductor pollicis longus tendons on one side and extensor pollicis longus on the other; a volar (Wagner) incision follows the glabrous border and lifts the thenar muscles off the trapezium. Branches of the superficial radial nerve cross the dorsal incision, and the radial artery runs across the floor of the snuffbox directly over the scaphotrapezial joint, the deep margin of the dissection.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing here needs your hand, but two things need your attention later. A superficial radial nerve branch cut or caught in scar produces a neuroma or dysaesthesia over the dorsum of the thumb, which presents as pain that outlasts the trajectory on this card and is not treated with more opioid. The radial artery, if nicked, bleeds only when the cuff comes down, and the surgeon will want the cuff deflated before closure to see it.

### 4. Capsulotomy and trapeziectomy
**Why:** The capsule is opened longitudinally and the trapezium freed from the metacarpal, the trapezoid and the scaphoid, and from the flexor carpi radialis tendon that runs in a groove on its volar surface. It comes out whole with a towel clip or in pieces with an osteotome and rongeur; the surgeon checks that no fragment is left against the tendon and that the scaphotrapezial surface is clean.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the bone step and the mechanism of the card: three raw cancellous surfaces in a space that will fill with haematoma. Under a block it is silent; under a general anaesthetic without a block the heart rate climbs here and again at the drill hole. Osteotome work against a held hand transmits through the arm table, and a sedated patient notices the hammering through the ears more than the hand. The trapeziectomy for an implant arthroplasty is instead a reaming of the trapezium for a cup and a broaching of the metacarpal canal for a stem, the same mechanism on a smaller scale with a press-fit at the end.

### 5. Suspension: tendon harvest and bone tunnel, suture button, or implant
**Why:** For LRTI, half of flexor carpi radialis is harvested through one or two transverse forearm incisions and delivered into the wound, a drill hole is made through the base of the first metacarpal, the tendon is passed through it and sutured to itself under enough tension to hold the metacarpal level with the index, and the rest is rolled and parked in the space. The suture-button technique drills obliquely across both metacarpal bases and tensions a suture between buttons until the thumb metacarpal sits level with the index without pinching the two together. The implant is impacted and the joint reduced and tested through its range.
**Anaesthesia:** The tendon harvest adds the second site of pain, in the forearm: a muscle that has lost half its tendon aches for a week in a way the patient attributes to the cast, and the forearm incisions are the ones an axillary block without the musculocutaneous component leaves uncovered. The drill hole through the metacarpal base is the second bone injury. The suspension is the one point where the thumb is held under tension and the surgeon wants the hand absolutely still for the tying; under a block that is automatic, and under a general anaesthetic a request for relaxation here is a request for depth, not for a neuromuscular blocker. An over-tightened suture-button pulls the metacarpals together and impinges, and the surgeon checks for it now by moving the thumb through opposition.

### 6. The metacarpophalangeal joint and the K-wire
**Why:** A hyperextended metacarpophalangeal joint will collapse the reconstruction, so a joint that hyperextends past about 30 degrees gets a volar capsulodesis, an extensor pollicis brevis transfer, or a fusion with a plate or compression screw. Some surgeons drive a K-wire across the trapezial space or into the second metacarpal to hold the thumb distracted for four to six weeks; others rely on the suspension and a cast.
**Anaesthesia:** A metacarpophalangeal fusion turns a 3 into the operation the 179-procedure cohort ranked eighth of all procedures for day 1 pain, because it adds a second set of cut cancellous surfaces compressed under a plate; the trajectory on this card then peaks a point higher and the night plan matters more. The K-wire adds a pin tract that aches at its skin exit and a second visit for removal under local anaesthetic in clinic. Neither changes the block.

### 7. Closure, tourniquet release, splint and the night plan
**Why:** The capsule is repaired over the space, the thenar muscles laid back, the skin closed, and the thumb put into a bulky dressing and a spica splint or cast with the thumb abducted and the interphalangeal joint free. The surgeon wants the cuff down before the dressing so that the bed and the radial artery can be inspected.
**Anaesthesia:** Cuff deflation after 60-90 minutes produces the usual brief fall in pressure and the flush of the arm; a small patient under sedation notices the tingling, the warmth and the reperfusion ache for a few minutes. The splint goes on before the patient is awake enough to move the hand, and the arm is slung before transfer, because a patient with a dense block will let the hand hang or lie on it. The first paracetamol and NSAID dose is timed from here, and the night plan is written in words the patient can read with one hand: the block lasts 12-20 hours with dexamethasone, the tablets start before the numbness wears off, the opioid is for the first two nights, and the hand is kept above the heart.

### 8. Wrist fusion: dorsal approach, neurectomy, decortication and graft
**Why:** A straight dorsal incision over the third metacarpal and the distal radius, the extensor retinaculum opened over the third compartment with extensor pollicis longus transposed out of the way, a posterior interosseous neurectomy on the floor of the fourth compartment to denervate the dorsal capsule, and a dorsal capsulotomy. The radiocarpal and midcarpal surfaces (radius, lunate, capitate, and the capitate to third metacarpal joint) are decorticated with a burr or rongeur to bleeding bone and the gaps packed with cancellous graft taken from the distal radius through the same wound, from the excised bones, or from the iliac crest when the defect is large or the bone rheumatoid.
**Anaesthesia:** This is a 90-150 minute tourniquet case with the bone mechanism multiplied across three or four joints and a strip of stripped dorsal periosteum, which is why the wrist fusion runs at the top of the card's band. The iliac crest, if used, is the second operative site and the one no upper-limb block reaches: it needs the surgeon's infiltration and is the harvest that keeps patients in overnight with a sore hip rather than a sore wrist. The neurectomy is deliberate denervation of the dorsal capsule and does not change the block. Tourniquet time is the thing to watch and announce at 90 minutes, because the surgeon still has a plate to fit.

### 9. Wrist fusion plate and cast; the total wrist arthroplasty variant
**Why:** A precontoured dorsal fusion plate is laid from the distal radius across the carpus to the third metacarpal shaft, the wrist held in 10-15 degrees of extension and slight ulnar deviation, and fixed with locking screws at both ends and a compression screw across the capitate; fluoroscopy checks the position before the retinaculum is closed over the plate and the wrist is casted for six weeks. For an arthroplasty the distal radius is cut and broached for a stem, the proximal carpal row is partly resected, the distal row is fused around a carpal plate with screws into the capitate, hamate and metacarpals, and the components are seated press-fit or cemented according to the design.
**Anaesthesia:** The plate lies along bone whose periosteum has been stripped the length of the wound: the periosteal mechanism, raw and incisional, on top of the bone. The fusion is rigid from the moment the screws are locked, so nothing moves afterwards and the pain falls after day 2. The arthroplasty's cement, where it is used, is a few millilitres in a distal radius with no canal pressure behind it and carries no cement implantation syndrome risk worth a precaution; its broaching and impaction are the bone work. The rheumatoid patient having either operation has thin skin that tears under tape, a wrist that bleeds more than the operation suggests, and a contralateral hand that cannot take over, which is the real reason for the overnight stay.

### 10. Recovery, discharge and the first night
**Why:** The thumb reconstruction is protected by the cast for four to six weeks and then mobilised; the surgeon wants the fingers moving from day one, the hand elevated, and the patient back for a wound check and K-wire removal. The wrist fusion is in a cast for six weeks with the fingers free.
**Anaesthesia:** The block recedes at home, overnight, and the pain that follows is the whole problem of this operation. In the ambulatory cohort rebound pain occurred in half of all block patients, more often after bone surgery, in women, and when intravenous dexamethasone had been omitted. In the observational comparison of axillary block against general anaesthesia for hand surgery, pain scores with the block rose to meet the general anaesthesia group at about three hours on the ward and never overshot it: the block defers pain rather than creating it, and a regimen started before it recedes meets the pain at the level a general anaesthetic patient would have had all along. Discharge needs a sling, a demonstrated ability to take the tablets, written instructions and a telephone number; a dense block is not a reason to keep the patient, and a cast with fingers that cannot be moved for pain is a reason to call the surgeon.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is bone, small and well innervated, in a cast: a high early peak that falls by day 3 and needs no cover beyond that. No PROSPECT guideline exists for hand surgery, so the plan is built from the mechanism, the day-case rebound literature and one procedure-specific trial. The defensible default is a brachial plexus block with 20-25 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% (supraclavicular or infraclavicular for complete cover of the radial forearm and thumb base; axillary with a deliberate musculocutaneous component in the anticoagulated patient) placed before the tourniquet, intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, sedation for the cuff, regular paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started in recovery rather than at the first complaint, and an oral opioid for the first two nights. The block lasts 12-20 hours with dexamethasone; the patient is told it will wear off at night and what to do when the fingers start to tingle.

The alternative with the strongest trial behind it is the Barcelona design: a short-acting block for the operation and levobupivacaine 0.125% on the radial and median nerves at the elbow for about ten hours of analgesia without motor block, which cut moderate-to-severe day 1 pain from 92% to 33% and day 2 pain from 81% to 21%, with less rescue analgesia and less nausea. It gives the patient a hand she can use that evening, which matters when the other hand is arthritic, and the author's reading is that it is the better plan for the patient who has had a bad night after a dense block before.

WALANT is a third plan rather than a compromise. Tumescent lidocaine 1% with adrenaline 1:100,000, buffered, infiltrated at the thumb base and along the tendon harvest 25-30 minutes before incision, no tourniquet, no sedation and no fasting in an established unit; in the two series on this card the patients were satisfied, all those followed to two years would choose it again, complex thumb reconstructions ran around 73 minutes, and one patient in 16 was converted for anxiety. The authors of the second series advise an anaesthetist present and a fasted patient for a unit's first cases. The night plan under WALANT is the same as after a block; the local anaesthetic lasts hours, not a day.

A catheter is rarely right. The pain falls by day 3, a cast covers most of the territory, and an infraclavicular catheter at home in a woman in her seventies is a management problem of its own; it earns its place for the bilateral or revision case, the opioid-tolerant patient and the one whose last block ended in an unmanageable night. Escalation triggers: pain above 7 after the block has receded, with warm pink fingers that move, is treated with the charted opioid and a check that the regular doses were taken; pain out of proportion inside a cast with tight swollen fingers is a cast problem and the cast is split; burning pain with colour change and hypersensitivity in the second or third week is complex regional pain syndrome, which the wrist and hand produce more often than any other region, and it needs the surgeon and the pain service early rather than a larger opioid dose.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Simple trapeziectomy versus reconstruction. The randomised evidence favours the simple operation on pain and function at a year, with fewer early complications; the tendon harvest adds a forearm site and a block that must cover it. Ask which the surgeon is doing before the block is chosen.
- Suture-button suspension. No tendon harvest, a second metacarpal drilled, and a fracture of the index metacarpal base as the characteristic complication; the K-wire is usually omitted.
- Implant arthroplasty. Reaming and broaching rather than excision, a faster return of pinch, and a revision population a decade on who come back for a trapeziectomy with a loose cup in a sclerotic bed: a longer, bloodier case.
- The Z-thumb. A metacarpophalangeal fusion or capsulodesis added to the trapeziectomy moves the day 1 peak up a point and is the item the 179-procedure cohort ranked eighth of all operations.
- Bilateral disease. One hand at a time; the second operation is scheduled when the first can hold a cup. A patient who turns up for the second side with a vivid memory of the first night is the one for whom the distal-block or catheter plan is chosen.
- The anticoagulated patient. The axillary approach is compressible; the supraclavicular is not. Neither operation bleeds enough to need the anticoagulant stopped for the surgery itself, which makes the block choice the only decision.
- Rheumatoid arthritis. Cervical spine instability and a difficult airway argue for the block; steroid cover; thin skin; anaemia; biologics held by the surgeon's timetable; and a contralateral hand that cannot take over, which is the overnight stay.
- Diabetes. Dexamethasone 8 mg raises glucose for a day and is still worth giving; the ward or the patient needs to know.
- Iliac crest graft. A second site that no upper-limb block reaches, with its own week of pain, a lateral femoral cutaneous nerve at risk and a haematoma; infiltrate it, and expect it to be the complaint on day 1.
- Total wrist arthroplasty. The carpal component loosens over years; the revision is a fusion with a large defect and an iliac crest graft, and belongs on this card at its longest and bloodiest.

## Key sources

One randomised trial measured day 1 and day 2 pain after trapeziectomy directly and showed what a long-acting distal block does to it, and the 179-procedure cohort ranks hand resection arthroplasty and metacarpophalangeal arthrodesis in its top dozen. The surgical variant evidence is a meta-analysis of randomised trials that favours the simple operation. The WALANT series are small and uncontrolled and are cited for feasibility and patient preference, not for pain. The wrist evidence is a systematic review of rheumatoid cohorts with complication rates and no pain trajectory, so the wrist half of the trajectory is the author's extrapolation from the mechanism, and the block, catheter and night-plan advice is author's judgement built on the ambulatory rebound cohort and the observational comparison of axillary block with general anaesthesia.
