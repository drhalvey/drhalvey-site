## What the surgeon is doing and why

A child aged 4-7 years (range 2-12) falls on an outstretched hand with the elbow locked in hyperextension. The olecranon levers against the thin plate of bone between the coronoid and olecranon fossae and the distal humerus snaps just above the condyles. In the extension type, which is about 95% of these injuries, the distal fragment goes backwards and the sharp anterior spike of the proximal fragment goes forwards through brachialis towards the three structures that lie on it: the brachial artery, the median nerve and its anterior interosseous branch. The Gartland grade says how far it has gone: I undisplaced, II hinged on an intact posterior cortex, III completely off with no cortical contact. That anatomy explains everything the anaesthetist needs to know. In the pooled series one child in nine has a nerve palsy at presentation, most often the anterior interosseous nerve (no flexion of the thumb tip or index tip, and no sensory loss to find it by), and a displaced fracture can tent, kink, trap or tear the artery.

The surgical solution is to reverse the injury and hold it. Traction restores length, the fragment is translated and rotated back, and the elbow is flexed with a thumb pushing the olecranon forwards so that the intact posterior periosteum acts as a hinge and locks the reduction. Two or three smooth 1.6-2.0 mm wires driven through the skin hold it for three to four weeks. Nothing is cut. Open reduction is kept for the fracture that will not reduce (something is trapped in it, often brachialis, sometimes the artery or median nerve), the open fracture, and the hand that stays white.

Forearm fractures are the commoner list item and run to an older age (4-14 years). The same logic applies: thick periosteum and remodelling potential mean that most are manipulated and held in a moulded cast. Fixation is for the fracture that cannot be held, the older child with little growth left, and the redisplaced fracture: flexible titanium nails passed up or down the canal from a small metaphyseal entry (elastic stable intramedullary nailing), or plates in the adolescent, which is [adult forearm surgery](distal-radius-orif.html) in a smaller arm.

The pain logic is the reverse of most cards in this book. The operation removes the pain generator, an unstable fracture, and adds almost nothing. Mechanism is bone, short and responsive to paracetamol and an NSAID. So the block question is not about analgesic need, which is small, but about what a block would hide: a nerve examination that may have been impossible before surgery in a frightened five-year-old, and the rising pain that is the first sign of compartment syndrome or Volkmann ischaemia.

## Steps

### 1. Triage by the hand, not by the radiograph
**Why:** The surgeon sorts the child into one of three groups. A warm pink hand with a pulse is urgent, not an emergency. A pink hand without a pulse (capillary refill under 2 s, warm, oximeter trace present) has a brachial artery in spasm, kinked or injured, with collaterals around the elbow keeping the hand alive. A white, cold hand is an ischaemic limb and goes to theatre now.
**Anaesthesia:** The timing evidence is the surgeon's licence to wait and yours to insist on daylight for the first group: 12 studies and 1,735 fractures showed no difference in open reduction, nerve injury or infection between surgery at about 11 h and later, and after-hours series show more malalignment. That evidence explicitly excludes vascular compromise. For the pulseless hand of either colour the stomach is not a reason to delay; nor is a wait for vascular imaging, because reduction is both the treatment and the test. Record what the hand looked like on arrival in the anaesthetic room, put the oximeter probe on the injured hand, and ask which nerves were examined: an anterior interosseous palsy found for the first time after surgery cannot be dated.

### 2. The unfasted child and the induction
**Why:** The fracture needs complete relaxation of biceps, brachialis and triceps for a gentle reduction; a child fighting the reduction is how the anterior spike does more damage.
**Anaesthesia:** Gastric emptying slows from the moment of injury, more so with pain, fear and opioid, so the interval from last food to the fall matters more than the hours fasted since. A child who ate within two hours of injury is treated as full for the night. The author's default for the urgent case is a tracheal tube after a modified rapid sequence: intravenous induction (propofol 3-4 mg/kg), rocuronium 1 mg/kg as the default relaxant with sugammadex available (16 mg/kg for immediate reversal, 2-4 mg/kg at the end), suxamethonium 2 mg/kg (3 mg/kg under 1 year) only where sugammadex is not to hand, because this is the age and sex of the undiagnosed dystrophy, and gentle mask ventilation at under 12 cmH2O while the relaxant works, because a 20 kg child desaturates in under a minute and classic apnoeic technique trades a small aspiration risk for a certain hypoxic one. The fasted elective forearm manipulation is a supraglottic airway case. Cannulate the uninjured arm; topical local anaesthetic cream will have been put on the wrong hand as often as not.

### 3. Position and the image intensifier
**Why:** The surgeon needs anteroposterior and lateral views without moving a reduction held only by elbow flexion, so the C-arm is inverted and its flat receiver becomes the arm table, or the arm lies on a radiolucent board. The lateral view is obtained by rotating the C-arm or the shoulder, not the fracture.
**Anaesthesia:** The child is pulled to the edge of the table with the shoulder over the side, and a small child slides further with each traction manoeuvre: fix the pelvis and head, and check the tube after every repositioning because the surgeon's assistant is leaning on the circuit. The C-arm, the surgeon and the scrub nurse occupy the space where you would stand, so lines and airway go to the opposite side and extensions are needed. Some surgeons prefer prone or lateral with the arm hanging over a bolster so gravity helps the reduction: that is a reinforced tube, eye protection and chest rolls for a 30-minute case. Radiation scatter at the head end is real when the receiver is the table.

### 4. Closed reduction
**Why:** Longitudinal traction with the elbow in slight flexion, correction of medial or lateral shift and rotation, then hyperflexion with forward pressure on the olecranon and pronation to lock the fragment on its periosteal hinge. A soft, rubbery feel and failure to hold means interposed brachialis or a trapped neurovascular bundle.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the stimulating minute of the case and it is brief. Fentanyl 1-2 microg/kg IV before it and a deep plane are enough; top-up relaxant is rarely needed if the induction dose is still working. Hyperflexion of a swollen elbow can itself obliterate the radial pulse and the oximeter trace on that hand, and that loss is expected while the elbow is held flexed. What matters is what returns when the surgeon lets the elbow out to about 60-90 degrees after pinning.

### 5. Percutaneous pinning
**Why:** Two or three divergent lateral-entry wires cross the fracture and engage the medial cortex. Some surgeons add a medial wire for the unstable fracture, because crossed wires resist rotation better. The ulnar nerve lies directly behind the medial epicondyle and subluxes forwards when the elbow is flexed, so a medial wire is placed with the elbow extended, often through a small incision.
**Anaesthesia:** No physiological event, but a point to record: iatrogenic nerve injury runs at 3.4% with lateral-only wires and 4.1% once a medial wire is added, the excess being ulnar. A block would make that injury undetectable until the next day. Stillness matters more than depth: a cough with a wire half across the physis means a second pass.

### 6. The pulse check after reduction
**Why:** With the fracture fixed the surgeon reassesses perfusion. A pulse that returns ends the question. A pink hand that stays pulseless is the controversy: 70% of such hands in one review had a documented arterial injury, yet in the pooled observational data the pulse returned within 24 h in 61% without exploration, a quarter of the watched group came to surgery, and long-term function did not differ. A hand that is white after reduction, or was pink and turns white, is explored at once, as is a pulse lost by reduction, which means the artery or median nerve is trapped in the fracture.
**Anaesthesia:** You are the perfusion monitor. Move the oximeter to the injured hand if it is not there, warm the child and the theatre (a vasoconstricted 18 kg child at 35.5 degrees C has no pulse on either side), keep the mean pressure at the awake value, and give the surgeon five to ten minutes after the elbow is let out before anyone decides. Have a hand-held Doppler in the room. If the decision is observation, that means a ward that does hourly checks overnight and a plan the vascular or plastic surgeon has agreed, and it changes your analgesia: nothing that hides pain.

### 7. Open reduction and brachial artery exploration
**Why:** An anterior or anteromedial approach through the hole the proximal spike has already made in brachialis. The surgeon frees the bundle from the fracture, reduces under vision and pins. A kinked or tethered artery often pulses once released; spasm is treated with warmth, topical papaverine or lidocaine and time; an intimal tear or transection needs a vein patch or reversed vein graft by a vascular or plastic surgeon.
**Anaesthesia:** The case has changed from 30 minutes to two or three hours. Cefazolin 30 mg/kg IV (max 2 g) if not already given, a second cannula, active warming, maintenance fluid and a bladder check in a small child. If heparin is requested for the repair the usual dose is 50-100 units/kg IV. Ask for the saphenous vein leg to be prepared before draping. Reperfusion of a forearm that has been ischaemic for hours is the setting for compartment syndrome, and most surgeons add forearm fasciotomy at the same sitting if ischaemia exceeded about six hours; see [compartment syndrome and fasciotomy](compartment-syndrome-fasciotomy.html).

### 8. Forearm fractures: manipulation, flexible nails or plates
**Why:** Manipulation uses the intact periosteal hinge and three-point moulding in a cast. Flexible nails are introduced through a 1 cm incision at the distal radius (avoiding the physis and the superficial radial nerve) and the proximal ulna, and passed across the fracture by rotation under screening. Plates need a full approach with periosteal stripping.
**Anaesthesia:** Manipulation is ten minutes under a supraglottic airway with paracetamol, an NSAID and fentanyl 1 microg/kg IV. Nailing is deceptively stimulating at the moment of passage and is the forearm operation with a compartment risk of its own: repeated failed closed passes drive swelling into the flexor compartment, and compartment syndrome is reported after difficult nailing. The time and the number of attempts are worth noting, and surgeons who cannot pass the nail in a few attempts open the fracture through a small incision. Plating in the adolescent can take a single-shot block on its merits if the injury was low energy, the nerve examination is documented and the surgeon agrees; see the pain plan.

### 9. Cast, emergence and the first look at the hand
**Why:** A supracondylar fracture is rested in a backslab at 60-90 degrees of flexion, never beyond the angle at which the pulse was present, with nothing circumferential in the antecubital fossa. Forearm casts are split or applied as slabs if swelling is expected.
**Anaesthesia:** Plaster moulding needs a still arm for five minutes after the last stimulus: do not lighten until the slab is set. Extubate awake if the stomach was full. A calm emergence matters more than usual, because a thrashing child is the one who cannot be examined: an intra-operative opioid, paracetamol 15 mg/kg IV (max 1 g), and in the anxious preschool child clonidine 1 microg/kg IV or dexmedetomidine 0.3 microg/kg IV before the end reduce emergence delirium. In recovery, document colour, refill, pulse or Doppler signal, and what the child will do with the thumb and fingers: the "OK" sign (anterior interosseous), thumb up (radial), fingers crossed or spread (ulnar).

### 10. The first night
**Why:** Swelling peaks at 12-36 h. The surgeon wants the arm elevated, the fingers visible and hourly neurovascular observations.
**Anaesthesia:** Chart regular paracetamol 15 mg/kg orally every 6 h (max 60 mg/kg/day, max 4 g/day) and ibuprofen 10 mg/kg every 8 h (max 400 mg per dose) from 3 months of age, with oxycodone 0.1 mg/kg orally (max 5 mg) every 4 h as required. Write on the chart that a second opioid dose within four hours, or pain on passive finger extension, is a call to the orthopaedic registrar, not to the anaesthetist for more analgesia.

## Pain plan

A reduced and immobilised fracture in a child is a score of 2. Regular paracetamol and ibuprofen cover most of it; a few oral opioid doses cover the first night; by day 3 most children take nothing. The plan that fits the mechanism is therefore systemic and simple, and the drug doses are those in step 10 with fentanyl 1-2 microg/kg IV or morphine 0.05-0.1 mg/kg IV intra-operatively. Surgeon infiltration of the pin sites or wound with ropivacaine 0.2% (up to 1 mL/kg, 2 mg/kg) is harmless and modestly useful.

The block debate deserves an honest statement of both sides. The joint European and American paediatric regional anaesthesia advisory found no evidence-based data that regional anaesthesia increases the risk of acute compartment syndrome or delays its diagnosis in children, and the paediatric epidural review found that breakthrough pain through a working block was the warning sign in the reported cases: ischaemic pain breaks through dilute local anaesthetic. Against that, absence of evidence in case series is not safety, the displaced supracondylar fracture is the classic cause of Volkmann contracture, the child often cannot report paraesthesia or cooperate with a motor examination, and the surgeon needs to date any nerve palsy. The analgesic gain is one or two points for 12 hours in an injury that scores 2 without one.

The defensible default, and the author's practice, is therefore no plexus block for the acute displaced supracondylar fracture, the pulseless hand at any stage, the floating elbow, the high-energy or crushed forearm, and the difficult nailing. A block is reasonable where the risk has passed and the surgery itself is the pain generator: elective plating of a redisplaced forearm fracture days later, corrective osteotomy for cubitus varus, removal of nails. There an axillary or infraclavicular block with ropivacaine 0.2% 0.5 mL/kg (max 20 mL) gives 8-12 h of analgesia without dense motor block, and dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV (max 8 mg) prolongs it. A catheter has no role.

Escalation is the important paragraph. Rising analgesic need, pain out of proportion, pain on passive extension of the fingers, and in the young child the three As (anxiety, agitation and an increasing analgesic requirement) mean the cast is split to skin, the elbow is extended, and the surgeon examines the arm. Pulse and colour are late signs and a normal oximeter trace excludes nothing. The general argument is in [nerve injury and compartment syndrome under regional anaesthesia](../a/a11-nerve-injury-compartment.html).

## Pitfalls and variants

- The white hand. An emergency whatever the fasting time: rapid sequence induction and reduction. In a series of 33 pulseless fractures, four of the nine poorly perfused hands needed vascular repair and two developed compartment syndrome; none of the 24 well-perfused hands did.
- The pink pulseless hand that deteriorates on the ward. Loss of the oximeter trace, rising pain or a new median nerve deficit means exploration that night; the plan made in theatre should already say who is called.
- Flexion-type fractures (about 5%, a fall on the point of the elbow). Unstable in flexion, pinned in extension, often opened, and the ulnar nerve is the one at risk.
- Floating elbow. Supracondylar plus ipsilateral forearm fracture is the pattern most associated with forearm compartment syndrome; both are fixed, nothing circumferential is applied, and no block.
- Medial wires. The ulnar nerve deficit is usually a neurapraxia that recovers over weeks to months; it still needs to be found on the night, not at the clinic.
- Under 2 years. A supracondylar-looking injury in a non-walking infant is a transphyseal separation until proven otherwise and raises the question of inflicted injury, as on the [femoral fracture card](paediatric-femur-spica.html).
- Monteggia and radial neck fractures. Look the same on the list, take longer, and the posterior interosseous nerve is the one to examine.
- Emergency department reduction. Intranasal fentanyl 1.5 microg/kg, nitrous oxide with a haematoma block, ketamine sedation and intravenous regional anaesthesia are all used for forearm manipulation outside theatre in children over about 5 years; the child arriving in theatre has usually failed one of them and has had opioid and a stomach to match.
- Wire removal at three to four weeks is done in clinic without anaesthesia in most units. Buried wires and flexible nails come back for a 10-minute day-case general anaesthetic.
- Adolescents over about 50 kg are dosed as adults: the ceiling doses above are the adult doses.

## Key sources

The surgical questions are better studied than the anaesthetic ones, but almost entirely by retrospective series: the timing meta-analysis grades its own evidence low or very low, and the pink pulseless hand literature supports either observation or exploration depending on which outcome is counted. On blocks and compartment syndrome there are no trials and there will not be; the paediatric advisory statement is an absence-of-evidence statement, and the card's default of withholding plexus block from the acute displaced fracture is the author's judgement about an asymmetric risk, not a guideline. Drug doses are standard paediatric formulary doses and are consensus tier. The general paediatric points are gathered in [the child having orthopaedic surgery](../a/a15-the-child.html).
