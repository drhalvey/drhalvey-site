## What the surgeon is doing and why

A lumbar fusion is offered when a motion segment is the pain generator or is unstable: a degenerative or isthmic spondylolisthesis in which one vertebra has slipped forward on the next, a disc that has collapsed and is loading the facets, a recurrent prolapse at a level already operated on twice, or a stenosis so severe that decompressing it would remove the facets and leave the segment unstable. The surgeon's aim is to turn two vertebrae into one: decompress the nerves, restore the disc height and the lordosis, hold the segment rigid with screws and rods, and pack bone graft where it will fuse over six to twelve months. The hardware is scaffolding. The fusion is the bone, and everything the surgeon does to raw bone (decortication, burring, packing) is in the service of getting it to knit.

Three constructs share the card. A posterolateral fusion puts pedicle screws into the vertebrae above and below, connects them with rods, decorticates the transverse processes and the facets, and lays bone graft across them; the disc is left alone. A posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) adds a cage into the disc space from behind, which means a bilateral laminectomy and retraction of the thecal sac and both roots to the midline. A transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (TLIF) reaches the same disc through one side, by removing a facet joint and entering the foramen, so the dura is barely retracted and the opposite lamina and facet are left for the posterolateral graft. The TLIF has largely replaced the PLIF because of the durotomy and root injury rates the bilateral retraction carried. The minimally invasive TLIF does the same operation through tubes and percutaneous screws, trading a smaller muscle injury and less blood for a longer time under fluoroscopy.

The sequence of an open TLIF is the spine of the card: a midline incision, bilateral subperiosteal stripping of the paraspinal muscles out to the transverse processes, and self-retaining retractors that stay in for the rest of the case; pedicle screws above and below the disc under fluoroscopy or navigation, often with a stimulated screw test; a unilateral facetectomy and laminotomy; a complete discectomy through the foramen with the endplates scraped to bleeding bone, because a cage fuses to marrow and not to cartilage; distraction across the screws and a cage packed with graft hammered into the disc space; rods contoured into lordosis and compression across the screws so the cage is locked and the segment re-tensioned into its new shape; decortication of the contralateral facet and transverse processes with graft laid over them; a drain in most hands and layered closure.

The graft is where the iliac crest enters the story. Autograft from the posterior iliac crest was the standard and is still used by some surgeons; it adds a raw cancellous surface under gluteal muscle that hurts for weeks and that patients remember long after the back has settled. Most surgeons now use the local bone from the laminectomy and facetectomy, supplemented with allograft or a bone substitute, and reserve the crest for revisions and pseudarthroses. Which the surgeon does is worth a question before the case, because it changes the pain plan.

The pain logic. In the 179-procedure cohort, spinal fusion and scoliosis surgery took three of the top six places of all operations for worst pain on the first day, with median scores of 7, and epidural analgesia was not used in those patients. The dominant mechanism is muscle: multifidus and longissimus stripped bilaterally off three or four vertebrae and crushed under retractors for three hours, which produces an aching, position-dependent back pain that is worst when the patient first stands and turns. Layered on it are bone (screws through four pedicles, a facetectomy, decorticated transverse processes and scraped endplates, each a raw cancellous surface), nerve (a root retracted to place the cage, and in a PLIF two roots and the sac), and retension: the segment has been distracted, lordosed and compressed into a new shape, and every step loads it through the screws from day 1. That last mechanism is why the pain does not fall away as quickly as a laminectomy's, and why the trajectory stays at 4 on day 3. Compare the [microdiscectomy](lumbar-microdiscectomy-decompression.html), a one-sided muscle window with nothing re-tensioned, and the [deformity correction](adult-deformity-scoliosis.html), the same mechanisms over ten levels.

## Steps

### 1. The pre-operative conversation, neurology and the opioid history
**Why:** The surgeon has selected a level and a construct from the imaging and the symptoms, and wants a documented neurological baseline, a known bone quality and a plan for the chronic pain that brought the patient to them.
**Anaesthesia:** Write down the leg neurology (L4 knee extension, L5 dorsiflexion, S1 plantar flexion, the dermatomes) because new weakness after a fusion is a screw, a haematoma or a retracted root, and it can only be called new against a baseline. Take the opioid history in milligrams, not adjectives: most of these patients have had months or years of back pain, many are on a long-acting opioid, some on a gabapentinoid, and the plan for them is the one in the [opioid-tolerant chapter](../a/a12-opioid-tolerant.html), with ketamine and the usual dose continued through the day of surgery. Ask what graft the surgeon is taking and whether there is neuromonitoring, because both change the anaesthetic. A haemoglobin, a group and screen (crossmatch for multilevel or revision), and the NSAID question: a surgeon who forbids NSAIDs for fear of non-union removes a grade A recommendation from the plan, and that conversation happens before the day, not in recovery.

### 2. Induction, lines, neuromonitoring set-up and the turn
**Why:** Three hours prone with the abdomen free, lordosis preserved, a fluoroscopy arm swinging under the table, and a motionless field with low epidural venous pressure. If the surgeon uses motor evoked potentials, the monitoring team needs baselines and an anaesthetic that will not abolish them.
**Anaesthesia:** A reinforced tracheal tube, well secured and with a bite block if motor evoked potentials are planned (the tongue and the tube take the bite). Two good cannulae; an arterial line for multilevel, revision, the cardiac patient and any case where the surgeon's blood loss history says so. A urinary catheter for anything over two hours. The anaesthetic for neuromonitoring: a propofol and remifentanil infusion, no volatile or a low fraction only if the monitoring team accepts the signals they are getting, and no neuromuscular block after the intubating dose, because motor evoked potentials and triggered electromyography of the screws need a working neuromuscular junction. Rocuronium at intubation reversed with sugammadex once the exposure is done gives the surgeon a relaxed patient for the stripping and the monitoring team a clean baseline afterwards; tell the surgeon the patient may move with the first screw stimulation. The turn: eyes taped and then checked in the head support, neck neutral, shoulders below 90 degrees, elbows padded, abdomen free, breasts and genitals clear, knees and shins padded, feet free. Then look again after the surgeon has set the lordosis and the table height.

### 3. Antibiotic, tranexamic acid and the marking film
**Why:** Metal, a deep wound and hours of exposure; the surgeon marks the levels with a lateral image and counts from the sacrum.
**Anaesthesia:** Cefazolin finished before the knife and repeated at 4 h, with vancomycin powder in the wound at closure in many hands. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV before incision, repeated at 3 h or as an infusion; across 49 randomised lumbar trials it reduced intra-operative and total blood loss, drain output and transfusion without an increase in thrombosis, and this operation bleeds from muscle, decorticated bone and epidural veins for its whole length. Lead for everyone; the image intensifier is in and out all day.

### 4. Exposure: bilateral muscle stripping and retraction
**Why:** The paraspinal muscles are taken off the spinous processes, laminae and facets out to the transverse processes on both sides, over the levels to be fused and the screws' entry points, and held with self-retaining retractors.
**Anaesthesia:** The longest sustained stimulus of the case and the mechanism of most of the post-operative pain; this is the moment for the ketamine to be running and the opioid ahead of the knife. Muscle bleeds steadily and the surgeon controls it with cautery; 500 mL by the end of a multilevel exposure is normal and the time to have the first unit checked. The abdomen must be hanging free: a frame that compresses it fills the epidural veins and the field, and the surgeon's request for a lower pressure is usually a request for a better position. Relaxant, if used, is reversed by the end of this step.

### 5. Pedicle screw placement
**Why:** Screws go through the pedicle into the vertebral body above and below the disc, by landmarks and fluoroscopy, navigation or a robot. The medial wall of the pedicle is the spinal canal and the inferior wall is the exiting root; a breached screw is the complication the stimulated screw test exists to catch.
**Anaesthesia:** Each screw is stimulated and the monitoring team reports the current at which it fires the leg muscles: a threshold below about 6-8 mA suggests a breach and the surgeon redirects it. The test needs no neuromuscular block, and a patient who twitches is the test working. Fenestrated screws with cement in osteoporotic bone bring a small cement step: the volume is low but the vertebral venous plexus drains straight into the lungs, and a transient fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide or a rise in airway pressure during injection is cement embolism, usually tolerated, occasionally not.

### 6. Decompression and facetectomy
**Why:** Laminotomy, flavectomy and a unilateral facetectomy (TLIF) or bilateral laminectomy (PLIF) free the roots and open the way to the disc. The bone removed is saved as graft.
**Anaesthesia:** Epidural venous bleeding begins here and continues until the cage is in. A durotomy at this step is repaired as on the microdiscectomy card, and in a fusion the repair is under the construct and harder to reach; it adds a Valsalva, a smooth emergence and a line on the record. Root retraction is the nerve mechanism; a PLIF retracts the sac and both roots to the midline for longer than a TLIF retracts one, which is why the PLIF carries the higher durotomy and radiculopathy rates.

### 7. Discectomy, endplate preparation and distraction
**Why:** The disc is removed through the foramen, the cartilaginous endplates are scraped to bleeding bone, and the disc space is distracted across the screws or with spreaders to restore height and open the foramen.
**Anaesthesia:** Distraction is the moment the neuromonitoring team watches most closely, because stretching a root shows as a drop in motor evoked potentials; the usual response is to release the distraction, raise the mean pressure, check the haemoglobin and the temperature, and wait. The instruments in the disc space reach the anterior annulus, behind which lie the iliac vessels and the aorta; sudden hypotension with a quiet wound is the same vascular injury as on the microdiscectomy card and is handled the same way. Blood from the scraped endplates is steady and is the second large contribution to the loss.

### 8. Cage insertion, rod placement and compression
**Why:** A cage packed with graft is hammered into the distracted disc space, the rods are contoured into lordosis and locked, and the screws are compressed towards each other so the cage is loaded and the segment set in its new alignment.
**Anaesthesia:** The hammering transmits through the table and the patient; the monitoring team will see artefact and ask for a pause for a clean trace once the cage is seated. Compression is the retension mechanism being created, and the signals are checked again when the rods are locked. If motor evoked potentials fall and do not recover with pressure, haemoglobin and warmth, the surgeon loosens the construct and looks; that decision is theirs, the physiological optimisation is yours, and both happen in the first minutes.

### 9. Posterolateral graft, the iliac crest and the local anaesthetic
**Why:** Transverse processes and the remaining facet are decorticated to bleeding bone and graft laid along them; if the crest is harvested, a window is cut in the posterior ilium and cancellous bone curetted out.
**Anaesthesia:** Decortication is the last bone bleeding of the case. The crest donor site is the sleeper: it hurts more and for longer than the back in many patients and is not covered by anything placed in the spinal wound, so if the crest is harvested, infiltration of the crest with long-acting local anaesthetic is the one thing to ask for by name. The surgeon-placed epidural catheter or the intrathecal morphine, if given at closure, goes in now under direct vision; agree the dose with the surgeon, because it is their injection and your monitoring.

### 10. Closure, drain and the turn back
**Why:** Layered closure over a drain in most hands; a vancomycin powder dusting in many. The construct is stable from the moment the rods are locked.
**Anaesthesia:** No relaxant has been given for hours, so none to reverse. Give the second antiemetic. Turn supine and inspect the face, the eyes and the arms before the patient wakes; a swollen or painful eye needs an ophthalmologist now, not in the morning. A smooth emergence without bucking protects a dural repair and keeps the epidural veins from refilling under the wound. Extubate a patient who can move both feet to order; one who cannot is a surgical conversation before they leave the table.

### 11. Recovery, the first night and the first stand
**Why:** The surgeon wants the neurology confirmed, the drain watched, the patient out of bed on day 1 and the wound dry.
**Anaesthesia:** Neurological check against the baseline in recovery and at intervals through the first night, because an epidural haematoma under a laminectomy presents as rising back pain, then new leg pain, then weakness and retention, over hours, and the window for evacuation is short. A patient who has had intrathecal morphine is monitored for respiratory depression for 24 h with the ward told in writing what to watch, and the pruritus and nausea are treated as expected effects. The regular paracetamol and NSAID start in recovery; the rescue opioid is charted, not held in reserve; the ketamine stops at closure. The first stand hurts and is the treatment; a patient who has not been told so will refuse it.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is bilateral muscle stripping over a bone-and-nerve operation with a segment re-tensioned and loaded from day 1, so the plan is multimodal from the start and built around a neuraxial opioid rather than a peripheral block. PROSPECT, writing for exactly this operation, recommends paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor (grade A), an intra-operative low-dose ketamine infusion (grade A) and opioids as rescue only, with a surgeon-placed epidural catheter at the end of surgery using a low concentration of local anaesthetic as the regional option (grade B). It does not recommend intrathecal morphine, erector spinae or interfascial plane blocks, lidocaine, dexmedetomidine, magnesium or wound infusion, each for lack of procedure-specific evidence rather than evidence of harm, and it advises against gabapentinoids and methadone for their adverse effects.

The author's default differs from the guideline on one point and follows it on the rest. Intrathecal morphine, 0.1-0.2 mg given by the surgeon under direct vision before closure or by you with a spinal needle before induction, is the single intervention that most reliably changes the first night: in the 150-patient placebo-controlled trial of instrumented fusion, 0.2 mg reduced pain at rest and on movement over 24 h and opioid use without an excess of respiratory depression, and the meta-analysis of 12 trials across spinal surgery found lower pain scores to 24 h and more pruritus with no significant excess of respiratory depression or retention. Its benefit is gone by 48 h, which is why it is combined with the ketamine infusion (0.5 mg/kg at induction and 10 microg/kg/min to closure in the opioid-dependent trial, which reduced opioid use at 48 h and pain at six weeks) and the oral regimen. The ward must be able to monitor a patient after neuraxial morphine; where it cannot, the plan reverts to the surgeon-placed epidural or to ketamine and opioid alone.

The catheter decision. A surgeon-placed epidural catheter with a low-concentration local anaesthetic infusion is PROSPECT's grade B regional recommendation and the author's choice for the multilevel fusion, the opioid-tolerant patient who will not be covered by a single dose of intrathecal morphine, and the patient who cannot have an NSAID. Its limits are the surgeon's willingness to place it, the risk of masking a haematoma or a new deficit (dilute solutions and a motor check every shift are the answer), and the fact that it comes out on day 2 or 3 when the pain is still a 4. For the routine single-level TLIF the default is intrathecal morphine without a catheter. Bilateral erector spinae plane blocks are the fallback when neuraxial morphine is refused or contraindicated, and an adjunct rather than a substitute. The iliac crest, if harvested, needs its own infiltration.

Escalation: rising back pain in the first 24 h with new leg symptoms, weakness or retention is a haematoma until imaged, and the surgeon is called before any analgesic change. Pain that is manageable at rest and severe on standing is the retension mechanism and the expected course; the oral opioid is timed before physiotherapy, not after. Pain still 6 or more at a week with a dry wound is the chronic pain patient's baseline reasserting itself: a pain service referral, not a dose escalation.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Open versus minimally invasive TLIF. The tubular version replaces stripping with dilatation and percutaneous screws, cuts blood loss to a few hundred millilitres and stay to a night or two, and in selected programmes goes home the same day; the pain mechanism shifts from muscle towards bone and nerve, and the trajectory falls by about a point. The fluoroscopy time triples.
- PLIF versus TLIF. The PLIF's bilateral retraction of the sac is the reason for its durotomy and root injury rates; expect more residual leg dysaesthesia and a slower first week.
- The iliac crest. A separate wound over gluteal muscle and a raw cancellous bed; the most under-treated pain of this operation. Infiltration at the very least, and a warning to the patient.
- Neuromonitoring and the anaesthetic. Propofol and remifentanil, no relaxant after exposure, a bite block, and a willingness to raise the mean pressure, transfuse and warm when the signals fall. A volatile anaesthetic with full relaxation gives the monitoring team nothing and the surgeon false reassurance.
- The opioid-tolerant patient. The commonest phenotype on this list. Continue the baseline opioid, run ketamine, place the intrathecal morphine or the epidural, and involve the pain service before the second bad night rather than after it.
- Osteoporosis and cemented screws. Small cement volumes into a venous plexus that drains to the lungs; watch the end-tidal carbon dioxide during injection.
- Revision fusion. Scar, retained metal to remove, a durotomy rate several times the primary, double the blood loss and time, and a patient whose pain predates the first operation. Arterial line, crossmatch, catheter plan.
- The anterior vascular injury. Instruments through the anterior annulus into the iliac vessels: unexplained hypotension with a quiet wound, turn supine, call the vascular surgeon.
- Postoperative visual loss. A long prone case with blood loss and a head-down tilt is the described risk profile; the head support, the eye checks, avoiding anaemia and sustained hypotension, and a neutral or head-up table are the modifiable parts (see [spine essentials](../a/a14-spine-essentials.html)).
- Chemoprophylaxis timing. A dry epidural space matters more than a day's delay in enoxaparin; the surgeon's timing is written, and an epidural catheter changes it.

## Key sources

This operation has the best analgesia evidence in the spine, because PROSPECT built its complex spine guideline on it, and its recommendations for paracetamol, NSAID, ketamine and a surgeon-placed epidural are the backbone of the card. The guideline's decision not to recommend intrathecal morphine rests on the thinness of procedure-specific trials rather than on a negative result; the 150-patient randomised fusion trial and the 12-trial meta-analysis are what the author leans on to use it anyway. The tranexamic acid pooling across 49 trials is large but mixes routes and doses. The neuromonitoring thresholds and anaesthetic rules are consensus practice rather than trial evidence, and the Gerbershagen cohort supplies the rank, not a mechanism.
