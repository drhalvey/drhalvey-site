## What the surgeon is doing and why

Both operations on this card exist to keep a young person's own hip. They are joint-preserving procedures for a mechanical problem: a femoral head or neck that is the wrong shape, or a femur that points the wrong way.

Surgical dislocation is an approach, not a single operation. Some hip problems cannot be reached or reshaped through an arthroscope: a head flattened and enlarged by Perthes disease, the posterior and circumferential bump left by a slipped epiphysis, a retroverted neck that needs lengthening, a femoral head fracture, a joint full of synovial chondromatosis. To work on these the surgeon needs the head out of the socket and visible all the way round, and the obstacle is the blood supply. The femoral head lives on the deep branch of the medial femoral circumflex artery, which runs behind the neck under the short external rotators. A conventional posterior dislocation divides those rotators and puts the vessel at risk. The solution described in 2001 is to leave the rotators and obturator externus intact, osteotomise the greater trochanter as a thin wafer that carries gluteus medius above and vastus lateralis below (the trochanteric flip), swing that wafer forwards, open the capsule from the front and dislocate the head anteriorly. In the original 213 hips there was no avascular necrosis, and in the later multicentre series of 334 hips there was none either. With the head out, the surgeon reshapes the head-neck junction with osteotomes and a burr, repairs or trims the labrum, trims the acetabular rim, and then reduces the hip, closes the capsule loosely and screws the trochanter back.

Proximal femoral osteotomy changes the direction of the femur below the joint. An intertrochanteric varus osteotomy tips the head deeper into a shallow socket or unloads a damaged area; a valgus osteotomy converts shear to compression across a femoral neck nonunion or moves a deformed head to a better arc; a derotation osteotomy, intertrochanteric or subtrochanteric, corrects excessive anteversion or retroversion that causes impingement or instability. The surgeon cuts the femur with a saw, rotates or angulates the fragments by a planned number of degrees, and holds them with a blade plate, a locking plate or, for a subtrochanteric derotation, an intramedullary nail. It is often added to a surgical dislocation or to a [periacetabular osteotomy](periacetabular-osteotomy.html) in the same anaesthetic.

The pain logic follows from the cuts. A trochanteric osteotomy and a femoral osteotomy are two fractures made and fixed; cut metaphyseal bone hurts for days and settles as it stabilises, so the Part B mechanism is bone. Two things lift this above the 3 of a simple fracture fixation. The trochanteric fragment is the insertion of the abductors, so every active abduction and every stance phase pulls on a healing osteotomy. The femoral osteotomy is loaded with partial weight from the first days, as in a [high tibial osteotomy](hto-dfo.html). The capsulotomy adds sharp pain with rotation. Nothing has been lengthened and no tendon has been repaired under tension, so the curve falls steadily after day 2, and the operation sits at a 4.

## Steps

### 1. Planning and the anaesthetic choice
**Why:** The surgeon templates the correction on CT and radiographs: how much bone to remove from the head-neck junction, how many degrees of varus, valgus or rotation, which implant. A combined procedure is planned in a fixed order so the correction of one cut is judged before the next.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient is usually 15-40 years old and otherwise well, sometimes with years of hip pain and opioid use behind them. General anaesthesia is the default because the case runs two to four hours in a rigid lateral position; a spinal suits the isolated 90-minute osteotomy. Decide the regional plan from the operation: dislocation alone gets a single-shot block, the combined case earns a continuous technique. Record sciatic and femoral function beforehand. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV and cefazolin 2 g IV before the incision, a group and hold, two cannulae, and an arterial line for the combined procedure.

### 2. Lateral positioning
**Why:** The pelvis must be vertical and immobile so the surgeon can judge version and so the leg can be flexed, externally rotated and dropped over the front of the table into a sterile bag when the hip is dislocated.
**Anaesthesia:** Pelvic supports on the sacrum and the pubis or the anterior superior iliac spines, clear of the femoral vessels of the lower leg and of the abdomen. Axillary roll, dependent peroneal nerve padded at the fibular neck, upper arm on a gutter with no more than 90 degrees of abduction, neck neutral. Check the down eye and ear after the final adjustment. The anterior support must not block the leg going forward, so the surgeon will test the dislocation manoeuvre before draping; watch the tube and lines while they do. Forced-air warming over the upper body and a urinary catheter for the longer cases.

### 3. Approach and the trochanteric flip osteotomy
**Why:** A straight lateral or Kocher-Langenbeck incision, the fascia lata split, and the posterior border of gluteus medius identified. The surgeon cuts a trochanteric wafer about 1.5 cm thick with an oscillating saw, exiting just in front of the posterior fibres of medius so that the piriformis tendon and the short external rotators stay on the stable femur and keep covering the artery. The wafer, with medius and vastus lateralis attached, is flipped forwards.
**Anaesthesia:** The first bone cut and the first cancellous bleeding. The sciatic nerve lies posterior to the short rotators the surgeon is deliberately not dividing, which protects it during the approach; its risk comes later, from leg position.

### 4. Capsulotomy
**Why:** The interval between piriformis and gluteus minimus is developed, minimus is lifted off the capsule, and a Z-shaped capsulotomy is made along the axis of the neck and then along the acetabular rim, staying anterior to the lesser trochanter so the retinacular vessels on the posterosuperior neck are not cut.
**Anaesthesia:** A full capsulotomy is the capsule mechanism on this card, and it is why hip rotation hurts for the first week. The surgeon may infiltrate the capsule now or at closure.

### 5. Dislocation
**Why:** The hip is flexed and externally rotated, the ligamentum teres is divided with curved scissors, and the head is delivered anteriorly. The leg goes into the sterile bag over the front of the table, which exposes the whole head; lowering and raising the leg brings the acetabulum into view.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon needs a relaxed patient: the abductors are already displaced with the trochanter, but adductor and hamstring tone resist the dislocation, so give a top-up of neuromuscular blocker before this step. The dislocated position is flexion and external rotation; with the knee straight it stretches the sciatic nerve, and with the knee flexed it does not, so the knee stays bent in the bag. The one long-term deficit in the multicentre series was a sciatic neurapraxia. Note the time the hip is out.

### 6. Checking head perfusion
**Why:** Before and after the reshaping the surgeon confirms the head is alive by drilling a 2 mm hole in a non-weight-bearing area and watching for bleeding, or with a laser Doppler probe. A head that does not bleed changes the operation.
**Anaesthesia:** This test reads your haemodynamics as much as the artery. Keep mean arterial pressure near the patient's baseline and the patient warm for this step; do not run induced hypotension through it, and tell the surgeon the pressure when they drill.

### 7. Work inside the joint
**Why:** The aspherical part of the head-neck junction is resected with osteotomes and a burr against spherical templates until the head clears the rim in flexion and internal rotation. The labrum is taken down, the rim trimmed and the labrum reattached with anchors; cartilage flaps are debrided. For Perthes or slip deformity the surgeon may raise a retinacular soft tissue flap carrying the vessels and lengthen the neck or reduce the head on it.
**Anaesthesia:** The steady middle of the case, 45-90 min. Bleeding is from raw cancellous bone on the neck, sealed with bone wax. The retinacular flap procedures are long, delicate and intolerant of hypotension for the reason in step 6.

### 8. Femoral osteotomy (when part of the plan)
**Why:** Guide wires mark the plane and the rotation. The femur is cut at the intertrochanteric or subtrochanteric level, a wedge is removed or the fragments are rotated or angulated to the planned correction, and a blade plate or locking plate is applied under compression. A subtrochanteric derotation over a nail involves reaming the canal, cutting with an intramedullary saw or through a small lateral window, rotating and locking.
**Anaesthesia:** The bloodiest step: a transverse cut through the proximal femur opens the medullary canal and the perforating vessels of the vastus lateralis origin. Check a haemoglobin here in a combined case and have blood available. The surgeon checks limb rotation and length clinically and with the image intensifier, so expect the leg to be moved and the table tilted. Reaming for a nail carries the marrow embolic load described in [femoral shaft nailing](femoral-shaft-nailing.html), small in a healthy young patient.

### 9. Reduction, capsular closure and trochanteric fixation
**Why:** The hip is reduced by traction and internal rotation, the capsule is closed loosely (a tight closure could stretch the retinacular vessels), and the trochanteric wafer is fixed back, sometimes advanced distally, with two or three screws.
**Anaesthesia:** Relaxation helps the reduction. The trochanteric screws are what the abductors will pull against for six weeks, which is the reason for the post-operative ban on active abduction and for crutches. Periarticular and wound infiltration goes in now if the surgeon uses it; confirm the total local anaesthetic dose against what the block has already used.

### 10. Closure, emergence and the first check
**Why:** Fascia lata, fat and skin are closed, usually without a drain. The surgeon wants the leg in neutral or slight abduction and no adduction or forced flexion in the early hours.
**Anaesthesia:** Turn supine with a pillow between the legs. Examine dorsiflexion of the foot and great toe as soon as the patient obeys commands, against the pre-operative record; a foot drop after a posterior approach and an hour of dislocation is reported at once. A fascia iliaca block weakens the quadriceps and does not touch the sciatic distribution, so it does not confuse that check; an epidural does, which is the argument for a dilute infusion.

### 11. The ward
**Why:** Touch or partial weight-bearing on crutches for six to eight weeks, no active abduction and no adduction past the midline until the trochanter has united, and in some surgeons' hands a course of indometacin or another NSAID against heterotopic ossification, which was the commonest complication in the multicentre series.
**Anaesthesia:** The trajectory on this card is 3 on the first evening with the block working, 6 on day 1 when it recedes and physiotherapy starts, 5 on day 2, 4 on day 3 and 3 at a week. Haemoglobin on day 1. If an epidural is running, the anticoagulant is once-daily prophylactic low molecular weight heparin and nothing else: the first dose is given at least 12 h after the needle, the catheter comes out at least 12 h after a dose, and the next dose waits 4 h. A direct oral anticoagulant is not given while the catheter is in place; where that is the surgeon's agent, the patient has heparin for the catheter days and switches no sooner than 6 h after removal. The same 6 h applies after the needle of a single-shot spinal. The rules are set out in [venous thromboembolism prophylaxis](../a/a05-vte.html).

## Pain plan

The mechanism is bone at one or two osteotomy sites plus a capsulotomy, in a young patient who must get up on crutches the next day. The innervation is the same as any proximal femoral and hip capsule surgery: femoral and obturator articular branches anteriorly, sacral plexus branches posteriorly, and the lateral femoral cutaneous and subcostal nerves for the incision. No randomised trial of regional technique in surgical hip dislocation or adult femoral osteotomy was found in drafting, so the recommendation is the author's judgement, built on the nearest evidence, which is the cohort literature for periacetabular osteotomy summarised on [that card](periacetabular-osteotomy.html). In a single-institution cohort of 724 of those operations an epidural gave a pain score of 2.9 on days 0-1 against 4.7 with periarticular injection and a stay of 3.4 days against 2.2; in a cohort of 146 an epidural gave a day 0 score of 3.5 against 4.5 with an erector spinae plane block, with more weakness, numbness and hypotension. The pattern is that an epidural buys one to two points of early pain at the price of slower mobilisation, and applying it to the smaller operations on this card is an extrapolation.

The defensible default for a surgical dislocation alone is general anaesthesia, a supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block with 30-40 mL of ropivacaine 0.2-0.25% before incision, dexamethasone 8 mg IV, paracetamol and a short-acting opioid, with the surgeon's infiltration of the capsule and trochanteric site at closure. The block covers the femoral and lateral cutaneous contributions and leaves the sciatic nerve examinable. For the combined case, or when a periacetabular osteotomy is added, a continuous technique is justified by the size of the bone injury: a lumbar epidural at low concentration (ropivacaine 0.1-0.2% with or without fentanyl) for 24-48 h, or a fascia iliaca catheter where the unit prefers working legs. Intrathecal morphine with a spinal is a reasonable single-dose alternative for the isolated osteotomy, with the usual respiratory monitoring.

Orally: regular paracetamol, an oral opioid for the first five to seven nights, and an anti-inflammatory if the surgeon permits. Surgeons divide on this operation in an unusual way: some want an NSAID for three to six weeks as heterotopic ossification prophylaxis, others restrict NSAIDs for fear of osteotomy nonunion. Whichever applies decides whether the regimen has its most useful drug, so it is settled per surgeon and written on the plan. Low-dose ketamine intra-operatively for the patient on pre-operative opioids.

Rebound is high when the plan is a single shot: the block recedes at 12-18 h, on the night before the first physiotherapy session, the trajectory steps from 3 to 6, and young, female patients having bone surgery are the group with the highest odds of rebound in the 972-patient cohort, lowered by intravenous dexamethasone. Give the first oral opioid and the regular doses before the block recedes, not after. Escalation triggers: buttock or posterior thigh pain with a weak foot is the sciatic nerve or a haematoma; sudden lateral pain with loss of abduction is the trochanteric fragment displacing; pain out of proportion in the thigh with a tense compartment after a long nailing is rare and urgent; a hot wound at a week is infection.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Dislocation alone, osteotomy alone, or both. The isolated subtrochanteric derotation over a nail is a 90-minute fracture-style operation with a score nearer 3. The dislocation with relative neck lengthening or head reduction, plus a femoral osteotomy, is a four-hour combined procedure at the top of this card's ranges.
- Added to a periacetabular osteotomy. Blood loss, duration and pain are then set by the pelvic operation; use that card's plan.
- The adolescent with a slipped epiphysis or Perthes deformity. The same approach in a smaller patient with an open physis; the paediatric considerations are on the [slipped epiphysis and Perthes card](sufe-perthes.html).
- Trauma use. The same flip osteotomy gives access to a femoral head fracture, where the review of approaches found it had the lowest complication rate; see [hip dislocation and femoral head fracture](hip-dislocation-femoral-head.html).
- Sciatic nerve. Protected during the approach by the intact rotators, stretched by a dislocated leg with a straight knee, and at risk from posterior retractors. A dense neuraxial block that outlasts the first examination hides it.
- Trochanteric nonunion or migration. Presents weeks later as lateral pain and a Trendelenburg gait; smokers and patients who abduct actively early are at risk.
- Heterotopic ossification. Frequent and usually trivial; the prophylaxis decision changes your analgesic regimen.
- Valgus osteotomy for femoral neck nonunion. An older or previously operated patient, scarred tissue, old metalwork to remove first, and more bleeding.
- Hypothermia. A long lateral case with a large wound and a young, thin patient: warm from induction, because cold also falsifies the perfusion check.
