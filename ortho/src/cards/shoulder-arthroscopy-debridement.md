## What the surgeon is doing and why

This card is the short shoulder list: the operations where the surgeon looks, tidies and leaves, and repairs nothing under tension. They share an anaesthetic logic. The operation itself produces little pain, the portals and the irrigation are the irreducible part, and the block decision is driven more by the kind of anaesthetic and recovery you want than by what the patient will feel on day 1. The exception, and the reason the card does not score a 1, is the calcific shoulder, where the surgeon cuts into a tendon and leaves a chemically irritant deposit behind, and the course is measured in months.

Diagnostic arthroscopy has shrunk as MRI has improved, but it still has a place: the painful shoulder with imaging that does not explain it, the suspected infection, the stiff shoulder where the surgeon wants to see the capsule before committing to a release, and the athlete whose labrum and biceps anchor cannot be read confidently on a scan. The surgeon inspects the glenohumeral joint from a posterior portal in a fixed order, then enters the subacromial space. Anything found is treated, which is why the pre-operative conversation matters: a look can become a tenotomy, a debridement, a decompression or, rarely, a repair, and the pain plan moves card.

Debridement is the surgeon tidying what will not heal and does not need to. A partial-thickness cuff tear involving less than about half the tendon thickness is debrided of its frayed edge rather than repaired, because the remaining tendon is intact at its normal tension and the fraying is the pain generator. Labral fraying is shaved to a stable rim; a degenerate biceps tendon is cut; loose bodies are retrieved; synovitis or a chondral flap is shaved. The point is what is not done: nothing is reattached, nothing is pulled, no bone is decorticated for healing, and the sling afterwards is for comfort. The patient uses the arm within days.

Calcific tendinitis is a different disease wearing a similar operation. Hydroxyapatite is deposited inside the substance of the supraspinatus (less often infraspinatus or subscapularis) by a cell-mediated process that is neither degenerative nor caused by overload; it runs through a formative, a resting and a resorptive phase, and it is the resorptive phase, when the deposit liquefies, swells and leaks crystals into the bursa, that produces the sudden, severe, sleep-destroying pain that brings a 45-year-old to the emergency department convinced the shoulder is septic. Most deposits resorb on their own. The surgeon's job is to shorten the course in the patient who cannot wait or whose chronic, hard deposit is not resorbing.

Two procedures do that. Barbotage (ultrasound-guided needling and lavage) is done in radiology under local anaesthetic: a needle is passed repeatedly through the deposit, saline is flushed and aspirated until the calcium clouds the syringe, and steroid is usually injected into the bursa afterwards. Against a subacromial steroid injection alone, barbotage plus steroid gave a better Constant score at one year (86 versus 74) and fewer patients needing further treatment; at five years the scores had converged, but 16 of the steroid-only group had needed something else against four. The steroid afterwards is for the flare: with saline instead of methylprednisolone after lavage, the worst pain in the first week was about 12 mm worse on a 100 mm scale, and the steroid did not impair resorption. The first week can be worse than the week before.

Arthroscopic removal is for the deposit that has failed barbotage, shock wave therapy and time. The surgeon does a diagnostic round, clears the bursa, finds the deposit by its bulge and chalky colour (or localises it with a spinal needle), incises the tendon along its fibres and curettes or shaves the calcium out, producing the "snowstorm" that fills the irrigation. What follows varies: some surgeons add an acromioplasty, some repair the residual tendon defect with an anchor, and many leave it. The evidence favours leaving it: in 99 patients whose cuff defect was not repaired, pain and function improved regardless of deposit size, location or completeness of excision, and 23 of the 29 with a follow-up MRI had healed. Complete removal is not the goal either; residual calcium resorbs. Repair of the defect bought better scores at two years in one cohort at the price of worse pain at three and six months, and both decompression and cuff repair were negative prognostic factors for early recovery in another. Which version this surgeon does sets the day-1 pain and the length of the tail.

The pain logic. For a look and a debridement the mechanism is surface: portals, irrigation fluid in the deltoid, an hour in abduction or traction. Nothing is under tension and nothing is healing to bone, so the pain peaks on day 1 when the block recedes and is gone within days; the card scores a 2 rather than a 1 only because the calcific version shares it. Calcific removal adds a tendon incised and curetted (not repaired, not tensioned, so not the mechanism that defines [rotator cuff repair](rotator-cuff-repair.html)), crystals in a bursa that was already inflamed, and often a raw acromial surface, the bone component that raises the odds of rebound pain and the mechanism on the [subacromial decompression](subacromial-decompression-acj.html) card. The day-1 peak is no worse than a decompression. The tail is what differs: in the series that measured it, pain fell below 3 out of 10 only at six months and complete resolution took nearly six months on average. That is not a reason for a catheter or an opioid; it is a reason to tell the patient the truth before surgery.

## Steps

### 1. Barbotage in the radiology department, the procedure that usually comes first
**Why:** The radiologist is trying to fragment and wash out a liquefying deposit and to provoke resorption, without an incision and without a general anaesthetic. The needle passes through the deposit many times, saline is flushed and aspirated, and steroid goes into the bursa at the end to blunt the crystal-induced flare of the first week.
**Anaesthesia:** You are rarely involved. When you are, it is for a patient who cannot lie still or tolerate the needling, and the request is for sedation, not a block: a plexus block for a 20 min needle procedure costs the patient a numb arm into the night for no analgesic return. The radiologist's local anaesthetic in the bursa is the anaesthetic. Tell the patient the week after can be worse than the week before, and that the steroid is what limits it; a diabetic patient will see a glucose rise for a day or two from the depot.

### 2. Decide what the block is for before anyone is asleep
**Why:** The surgeon's plan sets the pain plan. A look or a debridement produces almost nothing to treat after the first night; a calcific removal with an acromioplasty is a bone-and-tendon operation for rebound purposes; and a look that might become a repair needs a plan that can be upgraded.
**Anaesthesia:** For a debridement in a fit day-case patient the block is the anaesthetic: an interscalene or superior trunk block with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg and sedation gives a patient who bypasses the recovery room, walks early and goes home hours sooner than after a general anaesthetic (76% versus 16% recovery room bypass and 123 versus 286 min to discharge readiness in the randomised comparison, with no unplanned admissions against four of 25). The honest counter-argument is that the operation does not need the block for pain, and a patient who would rather have a short general anaesthetic with surgeon infiltration of the portals than a flail arm for 12 hours is making a reasonable choice. For calcific removal the block is for the pain as well, and the interscalene first choice in the PROSPECT cuff repair guideline is the nearest evidence. A superior trunk block gives the same surgical anaesthesia with hemidiaphragmatic paralysis in 4.8% rather than 71.4%, and is the block for anyone whose lungs matter. Decide the airway now: a supraglottic airway for a short case in a slim patient; a tube for the long calcific case in a heavy patient, because the neck swells with irrigation and the head is behind a screen.

### 3. Positioning: beach chair or lateral, and the pressure question in a short case
**Why:** Beach chair gives the surgeon the familiar upright anatomy and an easy conversion to open; lateral decubitus with 5-7 kg of longitudinal traction opens the subacromial space and some surgeons prefer it.
**Anaesthesia:** The hazards are the same as for any shoulder arthroscopy and a short case does not shorten them. In beach chair the cuff overstates brain perfusion by roughly 1 mmHg for every 1.25 cm between cuff and ear, and sitting, interscalene sympathetic spread and sedation together drop the pressure in the first 10 minutes; keep the cuff reading near the awake baseline, start a vasopressor infusion early rather than chasing boluses, and treat the vagal bradycardia-hypotension episodes promptly. In lateral the airway moves to the side, the dependent arm, eye and peroneal nerve need padding, and the traction is sustained plexus tension you cannot see. Check the head again after the surgeon sets the arm.

### 4. Portals, irrigation pump and the diagnostic round of the glenohumeral joint
**Why:** The posterior portal goes in through the soft spot below the posterolateral acromion, the anterior portal through the rotator interval, and the surgeon inspects the joint in a fixed sequence: biceps anchor and tendon, labrum, cartilage, subscapularis, the undersurface of the cuff, the capsule and the axillary pouch. Findings decide the rest of the list.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing to do but listen. A sedated patient with a working block feels pressure, not pain; one who flinches on portal placement needs the skin and capsule topped up by the surgeon, not more sedation. The pump runs at 40-60 mmHg and the surgeon will ask for a low blood pressure if the view is bloody; the answer is the same as on the [rotator cuff repair](rotator-cuff-repair.html) card, that you will hold the pressure near baseline and that adrenaline in the irrigation and the pump are their levers. A surgeon who announces a full-thickness tear or an unstable labrum has just changed the operation, and the day-case plan with it.

### 5. Debridement of what will not heal
**Why:** A partial-thickness cuff tear on either surface is debrided back to stable tendon with a shaver if less than about half the thickness is involved; a frayed labrum is trimmed to a stable rim; a diseased long head of biceps is tenotomised (or tenodesed, which adds an anchor, 20 min and a different pain profile covered on the [SLAP repair and biceps tenodesis](slap-repair-biceps-tenodesis.html) card); loose bodies are retrieved; a synovitis or a chondral flap is shaved. None of it is repaired and none of it is under tension.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the surface-only version of the card: a minute or two of shaver noise per lesion and nothing physiological. Track time and fluid, not nociception. A surgeon who converts to a repair here has converted the pain plan as well, and a day-case debridement patient and a day-case cuff repair patient go home with different nights ahead.

### 6. Subacromial space: bursectomy and the acromioplasty decision
**Why:** The scope is redirected into the subacromial space, the thick inflamed bursa is resected so that the bursal side of the cuff and the acromion can be seen, and the surgeon decides whether to burr the acromial undersurface. For a calcific deposit the bursa is often florid, because leaked crystals are an intense chemical irritant, and the bursectomy is where the deposit is found.
**Anaesthesia:** Irrigation now extravasates into the deltoid and tracks towards the neck over a long case; watch the airway pressures and the supraclavicular fossa. If an acromioplasty is done the card has acquired a raw bone surface, the mechanism behind the higher odds of rebound pain after bone surgery, and the night plan becomes the one written for a decompression. Litres of room-temperature irrigation cool the patient; forced-air warming from the start.

### 7. Finding and removing the calcific deposit
**Why:** The deposit bulges the bursal surface of the tendon and looks chalk-white or yellow; if it does not, the surgeon localises it with a spinal needle, or by a needle track that weeps toothpaste-like calcium. The tendon is incised along its fibres over the deposit, and a curette and shaver remove as much as comes freely; the surgeon is not trying to remove every grain, because residual calcium resorbs and chasing it enlarges the tendon defect.
**Anaesthesia:** The snowstorm of calcium in the irrigation spoils the view and the pump pressure goes up; expect the low-pressure request again. This is the step that creates the tail: an incised, curetted tendon that is not repaired and an inflamed bursa now full of crystal debris. It is not the tendon-under-tension of a cuff repair and does not need the cuff repair's catheter; it is a low-grade, months-long ache, which is the fact the patient needs to have heard before today.

### 8. The residual defect: repair, acromioplasty or leave alone
**Why:** Surgeons vary. Most leave a defect under half the tendon thickness alone, because the series that did so had excellent outcomes and most defects heal on MRI; some place an anchor and close it, which gave better scores at two years in one cohort at the cost of worse pain at three and six months; some add an acromioplasty.
**Anaesthesia:** If an anchor and a side-to-side closure go in, the arm will be held still for the knots, the patient leaves with a repair-type sling and protocol, and the day-1 and day-2 pain is closer to a small cuff repair than to a debridement. If the defect is left, the sling is for comfort and comes off in days. Ask which it was before you write the discharge analgesia.

### 9. Closure, sling and emergence
**Why:** Portals are closed with a stitch or a strip and a bulky dressing goes on. A sling is for comfort after debridement and calcific removal without repair, and for protection after a repair.
**Anaesthesia:** Put the sling on before the patient lightens so that an awake patient with a dense block does not lift the flail arm by the wrist. A patient under block and sedation is sitting up talking within minutes and is the one who bypasses the recovery room. After a general anaesthetic for a long calcific case, inspect the neck before removing the airway: subcutaneous irrigation fluid in the supraclavicular fossa is usually harmless and occasionally enough to matter.

### 10. Recovery, discharge and the written night plan
**Why:** The surgeon wants early movement after a debridement, pendular movement and comfort after a calcific removal, and a radiograph on the day to see what calcium is left.
**Anaesthesia:** The first paracetamol and NSAID go in before the patient leaves, not when the arm wakes up. The block lasts 12-24 h with dexamethasone; the patient who was comfortable at discharge is the one who telephones at 2 am if the rebound was not planned for, and in the ambulatory cohort half of all block patients met the definition of rebound pain, with bone surgery and the absence of intravenous dexamethasone raising the odds. A minor operation is not a reason for a minor prescription: the cohort that ranked 179 procedures found many small operations among the most painful on day 1 because little was prescribed for them. Write a short opioid supply for the first two nights, more after a calcific removal with acromioplasty, and tell the calcific patient in words that the dull ache can run for months and is the disease, not a failed operation.

## Pain plan

The dominant mechanism is surface, and the plan follows: a single-shot block chosen for the anaesthetic, simple analgesia started before the block recedes, and a few opioid doses for the first night or two. The defensible default for a debridement in a day-case patient is an interscalene or superior trunk block with 15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% and intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, light sedation, no airway, and a patient who walks out within a couple of hours. The superior trunk block is the author's preference for this list, because the operation does not need the extra cover the interscalene gives and the diaphragm is spared in nearly everyone. General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway, surgeon infiltration of the portals and no block is a reasonable alternative for the patient who would rather be asleep without a numb arm, and the pain cost after a pure debridement is small; it was not small in the randomised day-case comparison, where the general anaesthesia group had more pain, more recovery room time and four unplanned admissions, but that trial included cuff surgery and its findings scale with the bone and tendon work done.

For calcific deposit removal the block is for the pain as well. The nearest guideline is PROSPECT's for rotator cuff repair, and its skeleton applies: interscalene or superior trunk block, intravenous dexamethasone, paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started pre-operatively and continued for a week, oral opioid for the first two or three nights, and no subacromial infusion. An NSAID has a particular claim because the bursal inflammation is crystal-driven, and the author's practice is to continue it for a fortnight where there is no contraindication; that is judgement, not trial evidence. There is no argument for a catheter on this card: nothing is under tension, the first night is covered by a single shot, and the long tail after calcific removal is low-grade and not improved by three days of ropivacaine.

Rebound pain is the planning target, as on every shoulder card. A look that stays a look has the lowest odds; a calcific removal with acromioplasty is bone surgery for rebound purposes, and intravenous dexamethasone lowers the odds. Escalation: pain above 7 after the block has gone, with a warm hand and a correctly fitted sling, is treated with the opioid already charted and a check that the regular analgesia was given; a hugely swollen shoulder in the first hours is extravasated irrigation and settles; severe pain at a week in a calcific patient with a hot shoulder and a fever is an infection or a crystal flare and needs the surgeon; and the patient still aching at three months after a calcific removal is on the expected curve and needs reassurance and physiotherapy, not imaging.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The look that becomes a repair. A diagnostic arthroscopy booked as a day case under block and sedation is sometimes a full-thickness tear with anchors by the time the surgeon has finished looking. The block already placed is the right block for the repair; what changes is the night plan, the sling and the counselling.
- Barbotage versus surgery. Barbotage is cheaper, needs no anaesthetist and works for most resorptive-phase deposits; arthroscopic removal is for the failed, chronic, hard deposit. The flare after barbotage is real and the steroid injected afterwards is what limits it; a dreadful barbotage week is not the same as a failed barbotage.
- Acromioplasty with calcific removal. Adds a raw bone surface, was a negative prognostic factor for early recovery in the series that looked, and moves the night plan to the decompression version.
- Repair of the residual defect. Changes the sling, the protocol and the early pain for a two-year functional gain in one cohort and no difference in others. If it is done, treat the first two days as a small cuff repair.
- The patient with COPD, a single functioning lung or a contralateral phrenic palsy. Superior trunk block rather than interscalene; same surgical anaesthesia, 4.8% rather than 71.4% hemidiaphragmatic paralysis.
- Septic arthritis on the differential. A diagnostic arthroscopy for a hot shoulder is a washout, not a day case: antibiotics after samples, a general anaesthetic rather than a block in a febrile patient, and admission. The acutely resorbing calcific shoulder mimics it and the radiograph separates them.
- Frozen shoulder found at arthroscopy. A tight, red, thickened rotator interval in a patient booked for a look often leads to a capsular release and manipulation on the spot; that operation has its own card ([frozen shoulder](frozen-shoulder.html)) and a much more block-dependent pain plan.

## Key sources

The anaesthetic evidence for this list is about recovery rather than pain: the randomised day-case comparison of interscalene block against general anaesthesia and the superior trunk trial in ambulatory arthroscopy. The calcific tendinitis evidence is orthopaedic and radiological and is cited for the shape of the course, not for analgesic technique. No PROSPECT guideline exists for any procedure on this card; the rotator cuff repair guideline is borrowed, and the NSAID duration and the superior trunk preference are the author's judgement.
