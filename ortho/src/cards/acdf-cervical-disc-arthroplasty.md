## What the surgeon is doing and why

A cervical disc prolapse or a spondylotic bar compresses a root in the foramen (radiculopathy: arm pain, numbness, weakness in one root's territory) or the cord in the canal (myelopathy: clumsy hands, a stiff-legged gait, hyperreflexia, and a cord that is being slowly strangled). The posterior approach can decompress a foramen but cannot reach a central disc or an osteophyte in front of the cord without moving the cord, so the surgeon goes in from the front, through a plane that contains no muscle to split and no nerve to cut, and removes the disc and the bar under direct vision. Having emptied the disc space, they have two choices. Fill it with a cage and bone and plate it so the level fuses (ACDF), which trades motion at that level for a rigid segment and, over years, more wear at the levels above and below. Or fill it with an articulating implant (cervical disc arthroplasty) that keeps the level moving, which in the pooled randomised trials at four years or more gives fewer index-level reoperations and probably less adjacent segment degeneration, at the cost of needing good bone, no facet arthritis and no instability. The approach, the discectomy and the first week are the same operation whichever is chosen; what differs is the implant and the decades afterwards.

The approach is the Smith-Robinson. A transverse skin crease incision on one side of the neck (surgeons vary on the side; the recurrent laryngeal nerve is more predictable on the left and most right-handed surgeons prefer the right), platysma divided, and then a bloodless plane developed between the carotid sheath laterally and the trachea, oesophagus and strap muscles medially, down to the prevertebral fascia and the front of the spine. Nothing is cut on the way in; the longus colli is elevated off the vertebral bodies on each side and the self-retaining retractor blades are hooked under it, so that the oesophagus and trachea are held to one side and the carotid to the other for the duration. That retraction is the mechanism of almost every complication on the card: the recurrent laryngeal nerve pinned between the retractor and the tracheal tube cuff, the oesophagus stretched and bruised, the pharynx and larynx oedematous, and the sympathetic chain on the longus colli bruised into a Horner's syndrome.

With the disc exposed and the level confirmed on a lateral image, the annulus is incised and the disc removed back to the posterior longitudinal ligament with curettes and rongeurs, the uncovertebral osteophytes and the posterior bar drilled away under the microscope, the posterior longitudinal ligament opened or left, and the foramina undercut until the root and the dura are free. Distraction pins in the vertebral bodies above and below open the space; the endplates are burred to bleeding bone for a fusion (and left intact for an arthroplasty, which needs a flat seat and a preserved rim); a trial is sized, and the cage, filled with local bone or a substitute, or the arthroplasty, is impacted in. An anterior plate screwed to the two vertebral bodies holds a fusion; a stand-alone cage with integral screws is a lower-profile alternative that some surgeons use to reduce dysphagia. A corpectomy removes the whole vertebral body between two discs for a multilevel myelopathy or a tumour, replaces it with a strut or an expandable cage, and plates across; it is a longer, bloodier operation with the epidural veins behind the body in play.

The pain logic is bone in a muscle-free approach. The endplates have been burred, the uncovertebral joints drilled, two vertebral bodies screwed and a space distracted and wedged, so there is a deep, central, sore neck for two or three days that an NSAID treats. No muscle has been stripped or split, which is why the trajectory falls faster than any posterior spine card. What the patient complains of instead is the retraction: a sore throat, painful and awkward swallowing, a hoarse voice and a stiff front of the neck, which are oedema and bruising of the pharynx, oesophagus and larynx rather than nociception from the operation, which peak at a day or two and which, in a minority, outlast the neck pain by months. The pre-operative arm pain is usually gone in recovery, and a myelopathic patient's symptoms stop progressing but do not reverse. The pain scale places the ACDF at 3 alongside the ankle fracture and the anatomic shoulder, which is right for the first two days and generous after that.

## Steps

### 1. The neck, the airway and the cord before anything is given
**Why:** The surgeon has decided the approach from the imaging and wants the pre-operative neurology written down, because the operation is on the cord's front door and a deficit afterwards has to be timed.
**Anaesthesia:** The airway assessment on this card has two extra questions. First, whether the cord is at risk from laryngoscopy: a myelopathic patient with a tight canal, an unstable segment or a rheumatoid neck can be injured by extension, and the answer is a videolaryngoscope with the neck held neutral, or an awake fibreoptic intubation when the surgeon and the imaging say the canal has no room, with the reasoning in the [posterior cervical card](posterior-cervical-fusion.html). Second, whether the airway itself is abnormal: a large anterior osteophyte, a previous anterior fusion, a retropharyngeal mass or a radiated neck all distort the larynx. Document the neurology: grip and intrinsic hand function, the gait, the reflexes, the dermatome of the radiculopathy, and whether the patient already has a hoarse voice or a swallowing problem. A previous anterior approach on the other side means the contralateral cord may already be silent, and the surgeon has it checked first; a bilateral palsy is an emergency airway.

### 2. Induction, the tube and the cuff
**Why:** The surgeon needs a tracheal tube that will tolerate being pushed sideways by a retractor for two hours, a patient who will not move or cough, and a table position that gives a lateral image of the lowest level.
**Anaesthesia:** A standard or reinforced tracheal tube, taped on the side away from the surgeon, with the cuff pressure measured after intubation and again after the retractor goes in, because the retractor compresses the cuff against the larynx and the pressure rises. Deflating and reinflating the cuff to the lowest sealing pressure after the retractor is placed is a widely used manoeuvre aimed at the recurrent laryngeal nerve; the evidence is a randomised trial in which it did not reduce vocal cord immobility and an observational study in which it did, so it costs nothing and proves little. Keep it under 20-25 mmHg either way. Relaxant is at your discretion; if the surgeon runs motor evoked potentials for a myelopathic cord, the anaesthetic is propofol-based and unrelaxed after intubation. An oesophageal temperature probe or a nasogastric tube sits exactly where the surgeon is about to retract and is left out unless the surgeon wants one to palpate the oesophagus.

### 3. Positioning, traction and the shoulder tape
**Why:** Slight extension on a roll opens the anterior disc spaces and brings the lower cervical spine forward; the shoulders are taped down so that C6-7 and C7-T1 are visible on the lateral image; some surgeons apply a few kilograms of halter or tong traction to open the disc space.
**Anaesthesia:** Extension is the step that compromises a myelopathic cord; set it with the surgeon, not for them, and less is more in a tight canal. The shoulder tape pulls on the brachial plexus, and a tape that has been applied too hard is the mechanism of the post-operative shoulder and arm dysaesthesia that gets blamed on the surgery; check that the arms are tucked with the elbows padded and the tape is on the shoulders, not the arms. Eyes taped and padded, because the surgeon and the microscope work beside the face. Confirm the tube is still at the right depth after the extension.

### 4. Incision and the approach to the prevertebral space
**Why:** Platysma divided, then blunt dissection between the carotid sheath and the visceral column to the front of the spine; the omohyoid is retracted or divided; the superior and inferior thyroid vessels are the obstacles above and below.
**Anaesthesia:** Cefazolin finished before the knife. The carotid sheath is being retracted laterally: a carotid sinus reflex produces bradycardia and hypotension when the surgeon's retractor sits on the bifurcation, and the first episode is usually at this step. Tell the surgeon; they will move the blade. A patient with carotid disease has a sheath that is better not leaned on, which the surgeon knows from the imaging. Bleeding from the thyroid vessels is the first blood loss and is small.

### 5. Retractor placement and the hours of retraction
**Why:** The longus colli is elevated off the vertebral bodies and the self-retaining retractor is seated under it, holding the oesophagus and trachea medially and the carotid laterally, where it stays until closure.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the moment the cuff pressure rises and the manoeuvre, if used, is done. The oesophagus is now stretched over the retractor blade: this is the dysphagia mechanism, and the longer the retraction the worse it is. The recurrent laryngeal nerve runs in the tracheo-oesophageal groove on the surgeon's medial side and is injured by stretch or pressure between the blade and the cuff, at about 2% irrespective of the side of approach; a hoarse voice tomorrow is that, and a bilateral palsy after a two-sided history is the airway emergency. The sympathetic chain lies on the longus colli; a retractor placed too laterally bruises it, and a Horner's syndrome in recovery is the sign. Movement, coughing or straining with the retractor in place drives the blade into the oesophagus; this is an anaesthetic for a still patient.

### 6. Discectomy and decompression under the microscope
**Why:** The disc is removed back to the posterior longitudinal ligament, the uncovertebral and posterior osteophytes are drilled under the microscope, the ligament is opened and the cord and roots decompressed.
**Anaesthesia:** The burr is a millimetre from the cord and the dura; this is the step for a motionless patient and a steady pressure, and for the neuromonitoring team, if present, to be watching. Epidural venous bleeding from behind the ligament is controlled with haemostatic agents and patience; a surgeon asking for a lower pressure here is asking for lower venous pressure, which a slight head-up tilt gives. A durotomy in front of the cord is uncommon and hard to repair, and its consequence is a cerebrospinal fluid collection in the neck or a fistula to the pharynx; a smooth emergence and a line on the record follow. The vertebral artery runs in the foramen transversarium lateral to the uncovertebral joint, and a burr that drifts laterally is the catastrophe: sudden arterial bleeding in the depth of the wound, packing, an angiogram afterwards.

### 7. Distraction, endplate preparation and implant insertion
**Why:** Pins in the bodies above and below distract the space; the endplates are burred to bleeding bone for a fusion, or kept intact and squared for an arthroplasty; the trial is sized under the image; the cage or the disc is impacted in; and for a fusion a plate is screwed to the bodies, or a stand-alone cage with integral screws is used.
**Anaesthesia:** The distraction and the impaction are the bone mechanism, and the impaction is a hammer on a tube that reaches the cord through the implant; the neuromonitoring team sees artefact and then, if something has gone wrong, a loss. The plate's screws go into the bodies in front of the cord under the lateral image; a screw that is too long is a cord injury and the image is checked before anything is tightened. An arthroplasty needs the endplates left intact and the implant seated exactly, which means more fluoroscopy and a longer step. Some surgeons place a dose of steroid in the retropharyngeal space before closure for swelling and dysphagia; the umbrella review grades the evidence for that and for intravenous steroid as low confidence, and it is their call.

### 8. Haemostasis, drain and closure
**Why:** The retractor comes out, the longus colli falls back, haemostasis of the prevertebral space is checked, a drain is placed by most surgeons for a multilevel case or a corpectomy and by some for everything, and platysma and skin are closed.
**Anaesthesia:** The retractor coming out is the moment the oesophagus and the cuff are released; recheck the cuff pressure. A smooth emergence without coughing or straining matters more here than almost anywhere: a cough raises venous pressure in a prevertebral space that has just been closed, and the haematoma that follows has nowhere to go but into the airway. Deep extubation is not the answer in a neck that may swell; a tube tolerated with remifentanil or a lidocaine-laden cuff and removed on command, with the patient sitting up, is. Look at the neck before and after extubation and compare it with how it looked at the start; a neck that is already full is an early haematoma and a reason to keep the tube in.

### 9. Recovery and the first 24 hours: the neck, the voice and the swallow
**Why:** The surgeon wants the neurology confirmed, the neck soft, the drain output low, the patient sitting up and swallowing, and an early radiograph of the implant.
**Anaesthesia:** The airway complication of this operation is a retropharyngeal haematoma, which closed the airway in 1.15% of 785 fusions in the retrospective series (literature range 0.2-1.9%), with two thirds presenting within 24 h and the remainder at about three days. Its early signs are a full or tense neck, difficulty swallowing saliva, a change in voice, anxiety and a need to sit forward; stridor and desaturation are late. The response is in the trap line: sit the patient up, call the surgeon, open the skin and platysma at the bedside to let the haematoma out, and only then secure the airway, with a videolaryngoscope and a surgical airway ready, because the larynx will be displaced and oedematous and the intubation may fail. Observation on a ward that can see the neck, with the clip remover at the bedside, is the standard for the first night. Check the arms and legs against the baseline. A hoarse voice is a recurrent laryngeal palsy and a swallow that is painful but safe is retraction; a swallow that is unsafe needs a speech pathologist before the first meal. Day-case discharge of a single-level ACDF or arthroplasty is practised in selected units with a long observation period and a patient who lives near the hospital; it rests on the timing data above, and the first night at home is the one the haematoma series is about.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is bone work through a muscle-free approach plus retraction oedema, so the plan is short and oral: regular paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started in recovery, a few doses of oral opioid on the evening of surgery and the first day, and intravenous dexamethasone at induction, which treats the nausea and does something for the throat. No regional block earns its place. PROSPECT's complex spine guideline was not written for the anterior cervical spine, but its oral backbone (paracetamol, NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor, opioid as rescue) transfers without difficulty. Many spine surgeons forbid NSAIDs after a fusion for fear of non-union, and some allow a short course or a COX-2 inhibitor; the question is settled before the day and the alternative, if the answer is no, is paracetamol, a short opioid course and the knowledge that the neck pain is over by day 3 whatever is given. An arthroplasty has no fusion to protect and the NSAID objection does not apply.

The catheter question does not arise. The infiltration of skin and platysma by the surgeon is the only local anaesthetic in the operation, and a superficial cervical plexus block on the side of the approach is an adjunct of small value that is reasonable for a multilevel case and unproven for any. Ketamine belongs to the opioid-tolerant patient, who is less common on this list than on the lumbar cards but exists.

The thing the patient complains about is not the pain. The sore throat, the dysphagia and the hoarse voice are retraction effects, worst on day 1 and 2, present in most patients in the first fortnight and in about one in eight at a year in the umbrella review's pooled figures; they respond to time, soft food, sitting up and a speech pathologist for the ones who aspirate, and not to opioid. The patient who has been told this beforehand copes; the patient who has not reports the operation as a failure.

Escalation triggers are the neck and the cord before they are the pain. Neck swelling, a voice change, drooling or distress in the first 24 h is the haematoma. New arm or leg weakness is a cord or root injury, a screw or a graft in the wrong place, and the surgeon is called before any opioid is given. Pain that rises after day 2, with fever, a wound that leaks or a swallow that worsens, is an oesophageal injury or an infection until proven otherwise.

## Pitfalls and variants

- ACDF versus arthroplasty. Same approach, same retraction, same first week; the arthroplasty keeps the endplates and needs more fluoroscopy, has no non-union to protect from NSAIDs, and in the randomised trials at four years or more has fewer reoperations at the index level. The anaesthetic does not change.
- One level versus three, and the corpectomy. Retraction time is the variable: a three-level fusion or a corpectomy means two or three hours of oesophageal retraction, more dysphagia, more bleeding from the epidural veins behind a removed body, a drain, and the highest haematoma and airway risk on the card. Many surgeons keep a multilevel or corpectomy patient intubated overnight or observe them in a high-dependency bed; the decision is made before the drapes come off, not at the first stridor.
- The myelopathic cord. Neutral neck for intubation, modest extension on the table, a mean pressure kept at the patient's baseline throughout because a compressed cord has no autoregulatory reserve, and neuromonitoring in many hands. A deficit after the operation in a myelopathic patient is the one that was coming anyway or the one the position caused, and the timing of the baseline examination is what tells them apart.
- Horner's syndrome. A retractor on the sympathetic chain; a ptosis and a small pupil in recovery, usually transient, and worth telling the patient about before they find it in a mirror.
- Oesophageal perforation. Rare, from the retractor, the burr or a screw that backs out years later; presents as fever, neck pain, crepitus and a leaking wound, early or very late. Nothing to eat until the surgeon is happy.
- Vertebral artery injury. A burr lateral to the uncovertebral joint; torrential bleeding in the depth of the wound, packing, an angiogram, and a stroke risk afterwards.
- Rheumatoid disease and ankylosing spondylitis. Atlantoaxial instability in the first, a brittle and fixed neck in the second; both change the intubation, the extension and the shoulder tape, and the [orthopaedic patient chapter](../a/a10-the-orthopaedic-patient.html) carries the detail.
- Obstructive sleep apnoea and obesity. A thick neck in which swelling is harder to see and an airway that is harder to secure in a hurry; a longer observation and a lower threshold for the high-dependency bed.
- Day-case discharge. Reasonable for a single-level case in a slim, fit patient with a short retraction time and a surgeon who places a drain or does not; the haematoma timing data say the first night is the risk, and the discharge decision has to say how that night is covered.

## Key sources

There is no procedure-specific pain guideline for the anterior cervical spine, and the card's oral regimen is PROSPECT's lumbar backbone transferred. The haematoma data come from a single retrospective series and the literature range it quotes, and the cuff manoeuvre rests on one negative randomised trial and one positive observational study cited in a trial protocol whose own results the author has not seen. The dysphagia figures come from an umbrella review that rates almost all of its component reviews as critically low confidence, and the card presents them as orders of magnitude. The arthroplasty-versus-fusion comparison is cited to establish that the early course is the same operation, not for its long-term claims, several of which did not survive the authors' own sensitivity analysis.
