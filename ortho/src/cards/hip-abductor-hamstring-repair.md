## What the surgeon is doing and why

Two operations share this card because they are the same idea at opposite corners of the pelvis: a tendon has come off its bony footprint, and the surgeon sews it back with anchors and then asks the patient not to pull on it for six weeks.

The gluteal tendon tear is the rotator cuff tear of the hip. Gluteus medius and minimus insert on the lateral, superoposterior and anterior facets of the greater trochanter and hold the pelvis level in single-leg stance. The tendon fails by degeneration in women over 50, often after years labelled as trochanteric bursitis, or it fails to heal after a lateral-approach hip arthroplasty. The patient has lateral hip pain lying on that side, a Trendelenburg gait and weak abduction. In the pooled series most tears were partial thickness, medius was involved in almost all and minimus in about half. The surgeon opens the iliotibial band over the trochanter, excises the thickened bursa, finds the tear (many are on the deep surface and are found only by splitting the tendon in line with its fibres), freshens the footprint to bleeding bone with a burr, and ties the tendon down over two to four suture anchors, usually in two rows. If the muscle is fatty and the tendon will not reach, the options are a patch augment or a transfer of the anterior gluteus maximus and tensor fascia lata. Trochanteric bursectomy with iliotibial band lengthening alone, for recalcitrant trochanteric pain without a tear, is the same approach without the anchors and behaves as a soft tissue day case.

The proximal hamstring avulsion is an injury, not a degeneration: a forced hip flexion with the knee straight (water-skiing, a slip into the splits, a sprint) pulls the conjoint tendon of semitendinosus and biceps femoris, with or without semimembranosus, off the ischial tuberosity. Two or three tendons off with retraction is the usual indication. The muscle shortens down the thigh and scars to the sciatic nerve, which runs just lateral to the tuberosity, and this is why timing matters to the surgeon: in the meta-analysis, repair within four to six weeks carried about a quarter of the risk of postoperative sciatica and half the risk of retear compared with later repair. The surgeon works through a transverse incision in the gluteal crease or a longitudinal one for a chronic tear, lifts the lower edge of gluteus maximus, protects the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve, finds and frees the sciatic nerve, clears and decorticates the tuberosity, and ties the tendon stump to three to five anchors with the knee flexed.

The pain logic is tendon under tension on raw bone, the Part B mechanism that produces severe and prolonged pain. What moderates it is that the lower limb can be unloaded in a way a shoulder cannot: crutches take the abductors out of stance, and a brace that blocks hip flexion or knee extension keeps the hamstring short. The first three days are a 5 out of 10 at worst with a plan in place; the tail is movement-evoked and position-evoked (lying on the trochanter, sitting on the tuberosity) and runs for weeks. There is also a nerve component after hamstring repair that is specific and worth naming to the patient: numbness or burning down the back of the thigh from the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve, reported in 2.4% of pooled cases.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative assessment and the anaesthetic choice
**Why:** The surgeon confirms the tear and the retraction on MRI and, for a hamstring avulsion, documents sciatic function, because a chronic retracted tear can present with sciatica from scar tethering before any operation.
**Anaesthesia:** Two different patients. The gluteal repair is typically a woman in her 60s or 70s, often with a hip replacement on that side and a sleep disturbed for years by lateral hip pain; spinal or general anaesthesia both work in lateral position. The hamstring avulsion is a fit 30- to 55-year-old who will be prone for one to two hours, so general anaesthesia with a tracheal tube is the default. Record foot dorsiflexion, plantarflexion and sensation on the operative side before anything is given, because the sciatic nerve will be dissected (hamstring) or retracted near (posterior gluteal exposure) and the comparison in recovery needs a baseline. That same reasoning rules out a sciatic block and argues against a long-acting spinal for the hamstring repair.

### 2. Positioning
**Why:** Gluteal repair: lateral decubitus with the pelvis held by supports and the leg free, exactly as for a posterior hip approach, so the surgeon can abduct the hip to bring the tendon to the footprint. Hamstring repair: prone, with the table broken a little at the hips to open the gluteal crease and the knee flexed over a bolster to slacken the muscle.
**Anaesthesia:** Lateral: axillary roll, dependent peroneal nerve padded, neck neutral, and the supports clear of the femoral vessels. Prone: the usual list (eyes, face, abdomen free, arms no more than 90 degrees, genitalia and breasts clear), plus one item specific to this operation: the knee bolster must stay in place for the whole case, because the surgeon is relying on knee flexion to get the tendon to the bone. A reinforced tube taped securely; the head of the table is a long way from the wound and the drapes come up to the lower ribs. The incision is close to the perineum, so the surgeon will want an occlusive drape sealing off the natal cleft before the skin preparation.

### 3. Approach
**Why:** Gluteal: a longitudinal lateral incision centred on the trochanter, the iliotibial band split in line, and the trochanteric bursa excised so the tendon and its footprint can be seen. Hamstring: the incision in the gluteal crease, the gluteal fascia opened, and the inferior border of gluteus maximus lifted proximally; the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve crosses the field under the fascia and is found and protected first.
**Anaesthesia:** Stimulation is modest at this stage. Bleeding is from the bursa and from perforating vessels and is controlled with diathermy. In the endoscopic gluteal repair the peritrochanteric space is distended with pumped fluid through portals, as in the lateral compartment work of a [hip arthroscopy](hip-arthroscopy.html); fluid tracks into the thigh, and occasionally proximally, so a long case with a high pump pressure deserves a look at the abdomen and the temperature.

### 4. Finding the sciatic nerve (hamstring) or the tear (gluteal)
**Why:** Hamstring: the sciatic nerve lies lateral to the tuberosity and in a chronic tear is bound to the tendon stump by scar; the surgeon identifies it, often placing a loop around it, and frees it before any traction is put on the stump. Gluteal: the surgeon probes the tendon, and where the superficial surface looks intact splits it longitudinally to expose a deep-surface tear and the bare facet beneath.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step that generates the 1.7% rate of sciatic injury in the pooled hamstring series. Some surgeons use a nerve stimulator to confirm what is nerve and what is scar; if so, the patient must not be paralysed at this point, so an intubating dose that has worn off, or no further relaxant after induction, is the plan to agree at the start. A twitch of the foot under the drapes during dissection is information for the surgeon and is reported, not suppressed.

### 5. Mobilising the tendon
**Why:** A tendon that has retracted has to be freed from the surrounding scar until it reaches the footprint without excessive tension: in the thigh that means dissecting distally along the hamstring muscle bellies; at the hip it means releasing the tendon from the capsule and the overlying fascia.
**Anaesthesia:** The chronic hamstring is the long and bloody version: a longitudinal extension down the thigh, large veins around the muscle bellies, and the sciatic nerve in the field throughout. Tranexamic acid is reasonable here. This is also when the surgeon asks for muscle relaxation to gain length, which conflicts with nerve stimulation in step 4; the order is nerve first, relaxant after.

### 6. Preparing the footprint and placing anchors
**Why:** The cortical surface of the trochanteric facet or the ischial tuberosity is burred to bleeding cancellous bone, because tendon heals to bone and not to cortex or scar. Anchors are drilled or tapped into the bone: two to four at the trochanter, three to five at the tuberosity.
**Anaesthesia:** The decorticated footprint is the bony half of the pain mechanism and is where the surgeon's local anaesthetic does most good: periosteal and deep infiltration around the footprint, then the fascia and skin at closure. Osteoporotic trochanteric bone in the older gluteal patient holds anchors poorly, which lengthens the step and is one reason the post-operative restrictions are strict.

### 7. Tying the repair under tension
**Why:** The sutures are passed through the tendon and tied with the joint held in the position that shortens the muscle: hip abducted for the gluteal repair, knee flexed 30-45 degrees and hip extended for the hamstring. From the moment the knots are down, any movement the other way loads the anchors.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing for you to give, but the position the surgeon holds now is the position you must preserve through the turn and emergence. Ask what the safe arc is before the drapes come off.

### 8. Augmentation and transfer (variants)
**Why:** For an irreparable gluteal tear with a fatty, retracted muscle the surgeon adds a patch over the repair or swings a flap of anterior gluteus maximus and tensor fascia lata onto the trochanter to substitute for the abductors. In the meta-analysis, high-grade fatty infiltration reduced the functional gain from repair but did not change pain scores.
**Anaesthesia:** A longer incision, more muscle dissection and more blood loss: a muscle mechanism added to the tendon one, an extra 45-60 min, and a patient who stays one or two nights.

### 9. Closure, brace and the turn
**Why:** The iliotibial band or gluteal fascia is closed, then skin. A hip brace limiting flexion, a knee brace locked in flexion, or a simple harness between waist and heel is applied for hamstring repairs by most surgeons; in the systematic review, braced series reported fewer retears than unbraced ones. Gluteal repairs get crutches, touch or partial weight-bearing for six weeks and, in some hands, an abduction brace.
**Anaesthesia:** Have the brace on before the turn. Turning a prone hamstring repair supine and then sitting the trolley up flexes the hip with the knee straight, which is the mechanism of the original injury: keep the trolley flat or flex the knee over pillows, and tell recovery staff in words. For the gluteal repair, roll with a pillow between the knees so the leg does not fall into adduction. Aim for an emergence without thrashing; a patient who draws the knees up or kicks out against the brace is loading the repair.

### 10. Recovery and discharge
**Why:** The surgeon wants a neurological examination of the foot, a comfortable patient who can transfer within the restrictions, and no sitting directly on a hamstring repair.
**Anaesthesia:** Examine ankle dorsiflexion, plantarflexion and sensation as soon as the patient is awake and compare with the baseline; a new deficit is told to the surgeon before discharge from recovery, because a haematoma or a tight suture is reversible early. The trajectory on this card is 3 on the first evening with infiltration working, 5 on days 1 and 2, 4 on day 3 and 3 at a week. Day-case discharge needs crutch competence within the restrictions, a written analgesic plan, and for the hamstring patient a way to travel home that is not sitting upright on the repair.

## Pain plan

No peripheral nerve block has a clean claim on either operation, and that follows from the anatomy. The trochanter and its soft tissue envelope draw on the femoral nerve, the superior gluteal nerve, branches of the sacral plexus and, for the skin, the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve and the lateral cutaneous branches of the subcostal and iliohypogastric nerves. The ischial tuberosity is sacral plexus territory, with the posterior femoral cutaneous nerve to the skin. Covering the hamstring field would mean blocking the sciatic nerve or its neighbours, which is the one nerve the surgeon needs examined in recovery. No randomised analgesic trial specific to either operation was found in drafting; what follows is the author's judgement from the mechanism.

The defensible default is surgeon-delivered infiltration (20-40 mL of ropivacaine 0.2-0.375%, within the weight-based maximum) placed at the footprint, the deep fascia and the wound, on top of paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor where the patient and the surgeon allow it, dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction, and a short-acting opioid intra-operatively. For the open gluteal repair in an older patient, a supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block adds cover of the lateral thigh skin and part of the deep innervation at the cost of some quadriceps weakness in a patient who must use crutches; it is an option for the patient with poor opioid tolerance, not a routine. Spinal anaesthesia suits the lateral gluteal repair and gives a comfortable first two to three hours. For the hamstring repair the author avoids neuraxial techniques because the block delays the sciatic check, and the operation is short.

Orally: regular paracetamol and an anti-inflammatory for one to two weeks, an oral opioid for the nights of the first three to five days, and a specific instruction about position, because position is half the analgesia. The gluteal patient does not lie on the operated side and sleeps with a pillow between the knees. The hamstring patient does not sit upright on a hard chair for the first weeks, perches on the other buttock or uses a cushion with a cut-out, and keeps the brace on. Some surgeons restrict NSAIDs for fear of impaired tendon-to-bone healing; the clinical evidence for that fear in these operations is absent, and a short course is the author's default where the surgeon agrees.

Rebound is moderate, not high, because the plan does not depend on a dense plexus block wearing off; the step up comes when the infiltration fades at 6-12 h, and in the 972-patient ambulatory cohort bone surgery, female sex and youth raised the odds of rebound and intravenous dexamethasone lowered them. Escalation triggers: pain that is burning, runs below the knee, or comes with foot weakness is the sciatic nerve and needs the surgeon the same day; a tense, swollen buttock or posterior thigh in an anticoagulated patient is a haematoma, which can compress the nerve; a sudden pop with loss of the improvement is a failed repair.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Endoscopic against open gluteal repair. Similar retear rates in the pooled data, a lower complication rate endoscopically (0.7% against 7.8%), and, in the fatty infiltration meta-analysis, greater pain improvement after open repair. For you the endoscopic operation is a traction-table or lateral arthroscopy with pumped fluid; the open one is a hip-approach wound.
- The gluteal tear after hip arthroplasty. An older patient, a prosthesis under the field, scarred tissue and a higher chance of augmentation. Infection in this wound threatens the joint, so antibiotic timing is not negotiable.
- Chronic retracted hamstring tears. A longer incision, sciatic neurolysis as the main event, sometimes an allograft bridge, more bleeding and more postoperative sciatica; the meta-analysis found sciatica and retear both more likely after delayed repair.
- Endoscopic hamstring repair. Prone, with portals around the tuberosity and fluid in the subgluteal space next to the sciatic nerve; early series report fewer wound complications than open repair, with selection bias acknowledged by the reviewers.
- Venous thromboembolism. A braced, partially weight-bearing leg after an operation next to the deep veins of the thigh: 0.8% in the pooled hamstring series. The chemoprophylaxis decision is the surgeon's; if it starts on the evening of surgery, it is a reason to prefer general anaesthesia or a single-shot spinal with attention to timing.
- The patient on long-term opioids for years of trochanteric pain. Common in the gluteal group. Continue the baseline, add ketamine intra-operatively, and expect the first week to be harder than the card's trajectory; see [the opioid-tolerant patient](../a/a12-opioid-tolerant.html).
- Wound trouble. The gluteal crease is moist and close to the perineum; the lateral hip wound lies under body weight in bed. Both are late presentations that are not yours to treat but explain a surgeon's insistence on dressings and positioning.
- Isolated bursectomy and iliotibial band lengthening. No tendon under tension: a score of 2, a day case, infiltration and simple analgesia.

## Key sources

The surgical literature here is level IV: systematic reviews of case series, which give credible complication rates (the sciatic and posterior femoral cutaneous nerve figures, the retear rates) and weak comparative statements. The bracing and timing conclusions are associations from pooled series, not trials. There is no analgesic trial for either operation, so the block advice, the pain score and the trajectory are the author's calibration from the tendon-tension mechanism and from the neighbouring cards, chiefly [rotator cuff repair](rotator-cuff-repair.html) and [hip arthroscopy](hip-arthroscopy.html).
