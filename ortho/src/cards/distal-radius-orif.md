## What the surgeon is doing and why

The distal radius is the commonest fracture an anaesthetist meets, and it comes in two patients: the older woman who falls on an outstretched hand onto osteoporotic metaphyseal bone and gets a dorsally angulated, impacted, extra-articular fracture, and the younger patient with a high-energy injury who gets an intra-articular fracture with a displaced lunate facet and a die-punch fragment. The bone is cancellous, it bleeds, and it crushes rather than snaps; the problem the surgeon is solving is that a radius that heals short and dorsally tilted shifts the load onto the ulna and the dorsal lip, and the wrist is weak and painful for life. The thresholds for operating are arguments about millimetres of shortening and degrees of tilt, and in the older patient the honest evidence is that the function at a year is about the same whether the fracture is plated or pinned (the DRAFFT trial, 461 patients, no difference in the patient-rated wrist score at 12 months, with K-wires cheaper) or, in the over-65s, whether it is operated on at all. Surgeons vary, and the volar locking plate is what most of them do.

The volar locking plate exists because the volar cortex is thick, flat and well covered by muscle, and because a plate with screws that lock into it at a fixed angle acts as a scaffold under the subchondral bone and holds a reduction in osteoporotic bone that a conventional screw would pull out of. The surgeon's sequence is fixed:

1. Get to the volar surface through the flexor carpi radialis sheath (the "FCR approach"), retracting the tendon and the radial artery laterally and the median nerve and the flexor tendons medially. The palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve runs along the ulnar side of the tendon and is the nerve that gets cut.
2. Lift pronator quadratus off the radius from its radial edge to expose the fracture. This is the muscle mechanism, small but real, and some surgeons repair it at the end.
3. Reduce the fracture: traction, volar pressure on the dorsal fragments, and often a lever (a periosteal elevator through the fracture) to tease the impacted dorsal cortex and the articular fragments back out to length. Intra-articular fragments are checked with the image intensifier or, in some hands, an arthroscope.
4. Apply the plate proximal to the watershed line (the ridge where the volar cortex turns distally towards the joint), fix it to the shaft with a cortical screw, confirm the position on the image intensifier, then place the distal locking screws under the subchondral bone, with care that none of them protrudes through the dorsal cortex into the extensor tendons or into the joint.
5. Check the screws on a tilted lateral view, close pronator quadratus if the surgeon does, close the skin, and put the wrist in a volar slab or a removable splint. Fingers move from the first day; the wrist moves at two weeks.

The complications that present to you are tendon ones: a plate that sits distal to the watershed, or a long screw through the dorsal cortex, irritates and then ruptures a tendon (flexor pollicis longus on the volar side, extensor pollicis longus dorsally) months later; a median nerve that is swollen in the carpal tunnel from the fracture haematoma presents as numbness the next morning and is the surgeon's to decompress. Complex regional pain syndrome is the other late complication and the distal radius is its commonest cause.

The variants matter less to you than the volar plate. Dorsal plating is used for dorsal shear fractures and dorsal comminution that the volar plate cannot hold, through an incision between the extensor compartments, with more tendon irritation and a longer case. Fragment-specific fixation uses several small plates and pins for a shattered articular surface and takes twice as long. A dorsal and volar combination is the long, intra-articular reconstruction in a younger patient. K-wires are percutaneous, quick, and cheaper, with a cast afterwards. An external fixator spans the wrist for an open or grossly comminuted fracture and is the bridge to definitive surgery. None of them changes the block; the fragment-specific and combined cases change the tourniquet time and the duration of sedation.

The pain logic is bone, and this is the Part B anchor for a 2: a cancellous metaphysis reduced and drilled, a small muscle lifted and sometimes repaired, periosteum raised over a few centimetres, and nothing under tension afterwards. The fracture was the pre-operative pain generator and fixing it removes it, so by day 3 most patients are more comfortable than they were the day before surgery. The course is short and NSAID-responsive. What makes the card worth writing is the shape of the first 24 h under a block: a patient with a dense brachial plexus block has a pain score of 0 at discharge and the worst pain of the whole course at 2 am, and in the 972-patient ambulatory cohort half of block patients had exactly that pattern (a score of 3 or less rising to 7 or more within 24 h), with bone surgery (OR 1.82), younger age and female sex raising the odds and intravenous dexamethasone lowering them. The one randomised comparison of block against general anaesthesia for this operation showed the same thing from the other direction: the block group had less pain and used less opioid at two hours and went home sooner, the general anaesthesia group had lower pain at 12 and 24 h, and total analgesic use and satisfaction were the same. The block does not reduce the pain of a distal radius fixation; it moves it.

## Steps

### 1. The patient, the fasting state and the block as the anaesthetic
**Why:** Most distal radius fixations are semi-elective trauma on a day-surgery list within a week or two of injury; the surgeon wants a fixed wrist, a moving hand and a patient who goes home the same day.
**Anaesthesia:** The block is the anaesthetic. A supraclavicular, infraclavicular or axillary block with 20-30 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% or an equivalent, intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, light sedation, and no airway is the author's default for the fasted day-case patient, and the operation is then 45-90 min of an awake patient listening to drilling, which some patients want and some do not. The axillary block needs a separate musculocutaneous injection for the lateral forearm and does not cover the tourniquet; the supraclavicular and infraclavicular blocks cover more of the arm and leave the intercostobrachial area to an intercostobrachial injection or the sedation. For the patient who refuses to be awake, the anxious patient, the fragment-specific case over two hours, or the surgeon who wants a still patient for an arthroscopically assisted reduction, the same block goes under a general anaesthetic with a supraglottic airway. The patient having general anaesthesia alone, with infiltration, will have more pain at two hours and less at 2 am, and that trade is worth explaining. Examine and document median, ulnar and radial function before the block; acute carpal tunnel symptoms from the fracture haematoma are common and the surgeon will want to know they predate the block.

### 2. Position, arm table and the image intensifier
**Why:** Supine with the arm on a radiolucent table, abducted to 90 degrees and supinated, and the image intensifier either at the end of the table or over the arm; the surgeon sits on the axillary side of the arm and the assistant opposite. The tourniquet goes on the upper arm.
**Anaesthesia:** In an awake patient, the position has to be tolerable for 90 min: the shoulder abducted to 90 and no more, the head on a pillow turned slightly away from the field, the drape held off the face on a screen, and the contralateral arm free for the pulse oximeter and the cannula. The image intensifier swings over the arm repeatedly; a sedated patient who turns towards the noise moves the arm. Lead for the patient's trunk and for you. An awake patient feels the tourniquet from about 30-45 min as a deep ache that the block does not cover, and that is where the sedation is spent.

### 3. Tourniquet, antibiotic and the awake patient's first minutes
**Why:** The field is small and the surgeon wants it dry for the reduction and the plate position; the tourniquet is inflated after exsanguination and stays up for the fixation.
**Anaesthesia:** Cefazolin 2 g IV before inflation. Test the block before the tourniquet goes up, not after: pinprick over the volar wrist in the median and radial territories, and a check that the patient cannot flex the fingers against resistance. A block that is patchy at this point is supplemented now, with a distal median or radial nerve injection at the elbow or a surgeon's infiltration, rather than discovered at the skin incision.

### 4. The FCR approach and pronator quadratus
**Why:** A 6-8 cm incision over flexor carpi radialis, the sheath opened, the tendon retracted radially with the radial artery, the floor of the sheath divided, and pronator quadratus elevated off the radius from its radial border to show the fracture. The palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve is on the ulnar side of the tendon and is the nerve that the retractor finds.
**Anaesthesia:** The dissection is not stimulating under a working block. In an awake patient the first sensation that breaks through is usually the retractor pulling on the proximal forearm, which is musculocutaneous territory in an axillary block without a supplement, or the tourniquet. The pronator quadratus elevation is the muscle mechanism; small, but it is why the forearm aches for a few days.

### 5. Reduction
**Why:** Traction, direct pressure, and an elevator through the fracture to lever the dorsal and articular fragments back out to length and tilt, checked on the image intensifier in two planes. An impacted osteoporotic fracture that has been there for two weeks is harder to disimpact and may need the dorsal cortex broken through to get the length back.
**Anaesthesia:** Traction on the hand is traction on the shoulder in an awake patient; a sedated patient will tense the arm and the surgeon will say so, and the answer is a little more sedation and a hand on the elbow, not a deeper plane than the airway can tolerate. The reduction is the one moment at which a patchy block declares itself. This is also the step that lengthens the case: a fracture that will not come out to length is the one that becomes a fragment-specific or a combined dorsal-volar fixation.

### 6. Plate, shaft screw and the distal locking screws
**Why:** The plate is positioned proximal to the watershed line and fixed to the shaft through an oval hole so it can be slid before the first screw is tightened; then the image intensifier confirms the position, and the distal locking screws are placed under the subchondral bone in the row that supports the articular surface. Screw length is measured and checked on a tilted lateral view, because a screw that protrudes dorsally will rupture an extensor tendon in months, and a plate distal to the watershed will rupture flexor pollicis longus.
**Anaesthesia:** Drilling through the far cortex and the locking of screws is the bone mechanism of this operation and the reason the rebound odds are those of bone surgery. The awake patient hears and feels the drill as vibration and may find it the hardest part. Nothing to do except keep the arm still and the sedation steady; the image intensifier checks are the pauses.

### 7. Tourniquet release, closure and the splint
**Why:** Pronator quadratus is repaired over the plate by surgeons who believe it protects the flexor tendons, the tourniquet is released, bleeding points are controlled, the skin is closed and a volar slab or removable splint is applied with the fingers free. Some surgeons infiltrate the wound.
**Anaesthesia:** Reperfusion after a 60-90 min tourniquet is unremarkable in a fit patient. Ask the surgeon to infiltrate the wound even under a block, because it will outlast nothing but it costs nothing. The splint goes on before any sedation lightens; a patient with a flail arm will otherwise reach for it with the other hand.

### 8. Recovery, the rebound and the night plan
**Why:** The surgeon wants the fingers moving from the first hour, the hand elevated, and the patient home with a plan for two weeks until the wrist starts to move.
**Anaesthesia:** This step decides the operation. The patient leaves recovery with a dense block and a pain score of 0, and the block recedes 12-24 h later, at night. The first dose of oral paracetamol and an NSAID goes in before discharge, not at the first tingle; the opioid is for the first two nights and is explained as the drug for when the hand comes back to life, with a written plan that says what to take at bedtime, what to take when the fingers tingle, and who to telephone. The one randomised trial that compared a single infraclavicular shot with a continuous infraclavicular catheter and with an opioid PCA found that the catheter cut the pain at 9 and 12 h (median 0 versus 2, and 0.5 versus 3) without reducing total opioid use, and the PCA arm used nine times the opioid; the single shot with a plan is what most units do and the catheter is for the exception. Intravenous dexamethasone lengthens the block and lowers the pain at 4 and 8 h in the randomised comparison, raises the glucose for a day, and its absence carried an OR of 1.78 for rebound pain in the ambulatory cohort; it is given. Discharge is the same day; a patient who had a general anaesthetic alone is the one who needs more opioid in recovery and less at 2 am.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is bone, short and NSAID-responsive, with a muscle lifted and nothing under tension; the whole problem is timing. The block is the best anaesthetic for the operation and the worst possible teacher of what the operation feels like, because the patient's first experience of their wrist is at 2 am with no one to ask. The plan is built around that.

The defensible default is a supraclavicular, infraclavicular or axillary block with 20-30 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% (or an equivalent long-acting agent), intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started in recovery and continued for five to seven days, and an oral opioid for the first two nights, with a written night plan. The patient and the carer are told in plain words that the hand will come back to life between bedtime and the early morning, that the pain will rise over an hour or two to the worst of the week, that the tablets taken before bed are what blunt it, and that this is expected and not a complication. Sedation rather than general anaesthesia for the fasted day-case patient is the author's practice; a general anaesthetic with the block for the patient who wants it or the long case.

The catheter question has a randomised answer for this operation, and it is nuanced: a continuous infraclavicular block abolished the rebound window at 9 and 12 h without reducing opioid consumption at all, and the single-shot group's total opioid was modest (a median of 37.5 opioid-equivalent units against 30 with the catheter, not different). That is a catheter that does one thing well and nothing else, and for a day case it brings a flail arm for two days and a pump to manage at home. The author's practice is a single shot for nearly all, and a catheter for the bilateral fracture, the revision or malunion osteotomy, the opioid-tolerant patient, and the one who has had an unmanageable rebound night before. The alternative that answers the same problem differently is general anaesthesia with infiltration and no block, which the randomised comparison found gave more pain at two hours and less at 12 and 24 h with the same satisfaction; for a patient who lives alone and will have no one to help at 2 am, that is a defensible choice and not a failure of regional anaesthesia.

Score calibration: this is a 2, the Part B anchor for distal radius ORIF, and the trajectory on the card (1 on the evening under the block, 4 on day 1 when it has gone, then 3, 2, 1) is the shape of a short bone operation with a dense block in front of it. The first-day figure is honest about the rebound; in a patient who had a general anaesthetic alone the evening figure is higher and the day 1 figure a little lower, and the curve meets by day 2. Escalation: pain above 7 after the block has gone with warm, moving fingers and a splint that is not tight is treated with the charted opioid and a check that the paracetamol and NSAID were actually taken; pain with numb fingers in the median distribution that was not there before the block is an acute carpal tunnel syndrome and is surgical; pain out of proportion at a week, with a swollen shiny hand and allodynia, is the start of complex regional pain syndrome and is referred, not reassured.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Volar plate versus K-wires versus nothing. The function at a year is similar for a dorsally displaced fracture in the DRAFFT trial; the K-wire patient has no incision, a cast, and the same block question; the older patient managed in plaster is not on your list.
- Fragment-specific and combined dorsal-volar fixation. The intra-articular fracture in a younger patient; two hours, a tourniquet that may need a break, and a block under general anaesthesia rather than sedation.
- Dorsal plating. A longer case through the extensor compartments, more tendon irritation, more swelling; the same block.
- The axillary block's gaps. The musculocutaneous nerve (lateral forearm, where the retractor sits) and the intercostobrachial territory under the tourniquet; both are supplemented or the patient is sedated through them.
- Block for the awake patient. The patient who hears the drill; a headset, a screen and a steady level of sedation are the tools, and a patchy block is converted to a general anaesthetic before the incision, not after.
- Acute carpal tunnel syndrome. Median numbness from the fracture haematoma, present before surgery in some and appearing the next morning in others; documented before the block, and decompressed by the surgeon if it is progressive.
- Tendon rupture. Flexor pollicis longus months later from a plate distal to the watershed, extensor pollicis longus from a long dorsal screw; late, surgical, not yours.
- Complex regional pain syndrome. The distal radius is its commonest precipitant; a swollen, shiny, allodynic hand at one to three weeks is referred early.
- The older patient on anticoagulants. The commonest patient on the list; the block is a superficial one with a compressible site, and the usual rules apply.
- The patient who lives alone. The rebound night is the argument for a general anaesthetic with infiltration, or for an overnight stay, not for a weaker block.
- The unfasted patient on the trauma list. An isolated distal radius fracture in an unfasted patient can wait for fasting or have the block as the sole anaesthetic with no sedation; the urgent unfasted hand is on the [hand trauma card](hand-trauma.html).

## Key sources

This is the best-evidenced operation in the wrist and hand section for the anaesthetic question, and the evidence is specific: a randomised comparison of brachial plexus block against general anaesthesia for distal radius fixation showing the block moves the pain from two hours to 12-24 h without changing total analgesic use or satisfaction; a three-arm randomised trial showing a continuous infraclavicular catheter removes the rebound window at 9-12 h without reducing opioid consumption; a randomised trial of dexamethasone with the block showing a longer block and lower early pain; and the 972-patient ambulatory cohort that defines rebound pain and its risk factors, of which this operation has most. The surgical evidence (DRAFFT, the complication review) is cited for the variants and the complications, not for pain. There is no PROSPECT guideline for distal radius fracture, and the day-case advice and the catheter exceptions are author's judgement.
