## What the surgeon is doing and why

The patellofemoral joint is a sesamoid running in a groove under the pull of the largest muscle in the body. Two diseases bring it to theatre. In the young it dislocates: a shallow trochlea (dysplasia), a tubercle set too far lateral, a patella that sits too high to engage the groove (alta), and a torn medial patellofemoral ligament after the first dislocation combine so that the patella jumps the lateral ridge whenever the quadriceps fires near extension. In the middle-aged, usually after years of maltracking, the joint wears while the tibiofemoral compartments stay good, and the pain is on stairs and on rising from a chair. The operations on this card address each cause in turn, and the surgeon combines them according to which anatomy is at fault.

Medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction replaces the primary soft-tissue restraint to lateral displacement in the first 30 degrees of flexion. A gracilis or semitendinosus tendon is harvested from the pes anserinus (or an allograft or quadriceps strip used), passed through tunnels or onto anchors in the medial edge of the patella, run deep to the medial retinaculum, and fixed in a tunnel at an anatomic femoral point just anterior and distal to the adductor tubercle, the point that keeps the graft isometric through flexion. The graft is tensioned with the knee at 20-30 degrees, set so the patella can still be pushed a third of its width laterally, and fixed with an interference screw. The femoral point is everything: a tunnel a few millimetres too proximal tightens the graft in flexion, overloads the medial facet and gives a stiff, painful knee. The tendon-tension mechanism here is a graft between two bone tunnels loaded gently by a brace-limited arc; it is a smaller tension load than a cuff repair or an ACL graft because the knee is kept near extension for the first weeks.

A tibial tubercle osteotomy moves the attachment of the patellar tendon. The anterior tibial cortex is cut with a saw in a long oblique plane from the tubercle distally (a Fulkerson anteromedialisation moves the tubercle medially and forward in one cut; an Elmslie-Trillat moves it medially on a flat cut; a distalisation detaches the shingle entirely and moves it down for alta), the shingle with the tendon attached is shifted 8-15 mm, and it is fixed with two or three cortical screws. The periosteum is stripped to make the cut, the cut runs through the thickest cortical bone in the leg, and from the moment the patient wakes the quadriceps pulls on the moved fragment with every contraction. This is a bone cut under load: the mechanism of a [high tibial osteotomy](hto-dfo.html), on a smaller scale but on a fragment with a muscle attached to it. In the published cohorts, full weight-bearing waits for six weeks, delayed union occurs in a quarter (42% with distalisation), the screws are painful in a fifth and removed in a quarter, and a tibial fracture through a screw hole is the feared complication.

Trochleoplasty deepens the groove. The surgeon lifts the articular cartilage of the trochlea as a flap on a thin shell of subchondral bone, removes cancellous bone beneath to create a groove, lays the flap back down and fixes it with sutures or anchors. It is reserved for severe dysplasia, done by a few surgeons, and it is cartilage surgery on raw bone in a young patient: a haemarthrosis, a capsule that has been opened widely, and a knee that is stiff and sore for weeks.

Patellofemoral arthroplasty resurfaces the trochlea with a metal onlay (the current generation cuts the trochlea flat with a saw and seats the component on the cut, like the anterior cut of a total knee) and the patella with a cemented polyethylene button, through a medial parapatellar approach with the patella everted. The cruciates and the tibiofemoral compartments are untouched; there is no balancing and no tibial cut. Against a total knee for the same indication, the systematic review finds the same function scores, slightly better flexion, fewer complications (not significant) and a shorter stay, with a revision rate that is not different at five and ten years but carries wide confidence intervals; disease progression in the tibiofemoral compartments is the main reason it fails.

The pain logic depends on which operation is done. Isolated MPFL reconstruction is a 2-3: a hamstring harvest, two drilled tunnels and a graft under modest tension, kept quiet by a brace; the harvest site and the haemarthrosis are the early pain, and it is a day case. Patellofemoral arthroplasty is a 3: a bone cut and cement in an anterior compartment with no balancing and no re-tensioning, a smaller version of the [total knee](primary-tka.html)'s bone mechanism in a younger patient. The tubercle osteotomy is a 4 and sets the card's score: a periosteum-stripped cortical cut that the quadriceps loads with every contraction for six weeks, in a young woman who has often had previous knee surgery, which puts her squarely in the group whose rebound pain after a single-shot block is worst. The Gerbershagen cohort's lesson applies: the 40 most painful of 179 procedures had median day 1 scores of 6-7 and many were small orthopaedic operations on bone and ligament in the young, with open knee ligament reconstruction at 15th; the size of the incision is no guide to the pain. Combined procedures stack the mechanisms and run two to three hours.

## Steps

### 1. Which operation, which patient, which plan
**Why:** The surgeon chooses by the anatomy: MPFL reconstruction alone for instability with normal bony anatomy, an osteotomy added for a lateralised or high tubercle, a trochleoplasty for severe dysplasia, and an arthroplasty for the worn joint. The booking slip may say "patellar stabilisation" for any of these.
**Anaesthesia:** Find out whether bone is being cut before the patient is asleep, because that decision is the whole plan: an isolated MPFL reconstruction is a day case under general anaesthesia with a single-shot adductor canal block, infiltration and dexamethasone; an osteotomy or trochleoplasty is an overnight stay with a catheter or a written plan for the second night and chemoprophylaxis; an arthroplasty follows the total knee skeleton. The patient is typically young and female and has often had the knee operated on before; those are the rebound risk factors from the ambulatory cohort (bone surgery OR 1.82, female sex OR 1.52, younger age), and intravenous dexamethasone 8-10 mg is the one thing that lowers them. Spinal anaesthesia is reasonable for the osteotomy and the arthroplasty and unusual for a day-case MPFL reconstruction in a 19-year-old.

### 2. Positioning, tourniquet and antibiotic
**Why:** Supine with a lateral post or a leg holder so the knee can be flexed to 30 degrees for graft tensioning and to 90 degrees for the tunnels; the osteotomy needs the leg flat with the anterior tibia exposed. A tourniquet is the norm for the osteotomy and the arthroplasty and common for the MPFL reconstruction.
**Anaesthesia:** Cefazolin 2 g IV before the cuff. Tourniquet times are 45-90 minutes and the reperfusion at release is modest, but the osteotomy bleeds into the anterior compartment of a young patient's leg when the cuff comes down, and the tranexamic acid should be in before incision rather than at release. The common peroneal nerve is at the fibular neck beside the osteotomy site and under the lateral post.

### 3. Arthroscopy and lateral release
**Why:** Most instability and arthroplasty operations begin with an arthroscopy to grade the cartilage, confirm the tracking and treat loose bodies or chondral flaps; a lateral retinacular release or lengthening is added when the lateral structures are tight, less often than it used to be because an isolated release makes instability worse.
**Anaesthesia:** Irrigation fluid in the suprapatellar pouch and a haemarthrosis from the release are the early swelling, and they settle. Note the cartilage grade the surgeon calls out: a bare trochlea in a 20-year-old is the knee that gets a trochleoplasty or a later arthroplasty.

### 4. Graft harvest for MPFL reconstruction
**Why:** The gracilis (or semitendinosus) is harvested through a small incision over the pes anserinus with a stripper, folded double to about 10 cm, and prepared on the back table; a quadriceps tendon strip left attached to the patella is an alternative that avoids the harvest site, and allograft avoids it altogether.
**Anaesthesia:** The harvest is the same mechanism as the hamstring harvest for an [ACL reconstruction](acl-reconstruction.html) and is the dominant early pain of an isolated MPFL reconstruction: a muscle stripped off its tendon, bleeding into the posteromedial thigh, and sore with hamstring contraction for a fortnight. The infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve crosses the harvest site and is cut or stretched in a proportion of patients, which leaves a numb patch over the anterolateral knee that the adductor canal block will mask on day 0. The adductor canal block covers the harvest site.

### 5. Patellar and femoral tunnels and graft fixation
**Why:** Two blind tunnels or two anchors in the medial patellar edge take the folded graft; the femoral tunnel is drilled at the anatomic point, located on a lateral image with the intensifier, the graft is passed between the layers of the medial retinaculum, tensioned with the knee at 20-30 degrees so the patella still displaces a third of its width, and fixed with an interference screw. The surgeon then runs the knee through its arc to check the graft neither tightens nor slackens.
**Anaesthesia:** The image intensifier comes in for the femoral point. A transverse patellar tunnel that breaches the far cortex is a stress riser, and a patellar fracture weeks later is the complication that brings the patient back. The graft set under tension between two tunnels is the tendon-tension mechanism of this operation, but a modest one because the brace keeps the knee near extension and the graft is near-isometric; the retinacular dissection and the haemarthrosis are what hurt on day 1. The surgeon wants the knee held still at the tensioning angle; a twitch here changes the graft tension for good.

### 6. Tibial tubercle osteotomy: the cut, the shift and the screws
**Why:** Through an anterior incision the periosteum is lifted off the tubercle and the anteromedial tibia, and a saw cut is made from the tubercle distally in an oblique plane 6-8 cm long, thick enough to hold screws and thin enough to hinge; the shingle is shifted medially and forward (or detached and moved down for alta) and fixed with two or three bicortical screws. The surgeon then checks tracking through the arc.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the bone-under-load mechanism being created: a cortical cut through the thickest bone in the leg, periosteum stripped, and a fragment that the quadriceps will pull on with every contraction from the moment the patient is awake. It is more bone than an ACL reconstruction and it behaves like a small high tibial osteotomy. The anterior compartment starts to fill after tourniquet release; the compartment syndrome risk is small but real, and the block will not hide it if the calf is examined. The screw heads sit under the skin on the front of the tibia and are the commonest reason for a second operation. A fracture through the distal screw hole is the catastrophe, usually after a fall in the brace at six to eight weeks.

### 7. Trochleoplasty
**Why:** For severe dysplasia the surgeon lifts the trochlear cartilage on a thin osteochondral flap, excavates a groove in the cancellous bone beneath with a burr, lays the flap into the new groove and fixes it with sutures, vicryl tape or anchors; an MPFL reconstruction nearly always follows in the same sitting.
**Anaesthesia:** Raw cancellous bone over a wide area of the distal femur, a joint opened widely, and a cartilage flap that must not be loaded: a haemarthrosis is certain, the pain is bone and capsule for days, stiffness is the main complication, and continuous passive motion or early supervised flexion is the surgeon's answer to it. This is the second operation on this card that earns a catheter or an overnight stay with a second-night plan.

### 8. Patellofemoral arthroplasty: trochlear cut, patellar cut and cement
**Why:** Through a medial parapatellar arthrotomy with the patella everted, the trochlea is cut flat with a saw guided off the anterior femoral cortex (or reamed to an inlay in older designs), the patella is cut to leave 12-15 mm of bone, trial components check tracking, and both components are cemented. Nothing is done to the tibia or the cruciates.
**Anaesthesia:** One femoral cut and one patellar cut: the bone mechanism of a total knee without the balancing, the medial release, the tibial cut or the intramedullary rod. Cement on two small surfaces carries no practical bone cement implantation risk. The eversion of the patella and the arthrotomy are the same as a total knee's and the quadriceps is sore in the same way; the post-operative course is a total knee's first two days without the retension ache that follows, which is why the stay is shorter.

### 9. Infiltration, closure and the brace
**Why:** The surgeon infiltrates the harvest site, the capsule and the subcutaneous tissues; closes in layers; and fits a hinged brace locked in extension for the MPFL reconstruction, the osteotomy and the trochleoplasty, with flexion released week by week to 90 degrees over four to six weeks. The arthroplasty goes into a total-knee protocol with no brace.
**Anaesthesia:** The brace goes on before the patient lightens; a young patient with a numb leg will otherwise flex it. Tourniquet release after a short inflation is unremarkable; the osteotomy bleeds into the anterior compartment and the joint afterwards, and a drain is sometimes used. The local anaesthetic total across the block and the infiltration is far from the ceiling in this population.

### 10. Recovery, discharge and the quadriceps
**Why:** The surgeon wants isometric quadriceps work from the first week, weight-bearing as tolerated in the brace for the ligament reconstruction and the arthroplasty, protected weight-bearing for six weeks for the osteotomy, and full range of motion by six weeks for all of them. The quadriceps is the structure the rehabilitation is built around; a weak or inhibited quadriceps is a maltracking patella.
**Anaesthesia:** The adductor canal block is the right block for every operation on this card because it leaves the quadriceps working; a femoral block has no place in a patient whose rehabilitation is quadriceps activation in a brace. The isolated MPFL reconstruction goes home the same day with a block that lasts 12-24 hours, the oral regimen started in recovery, and a written night plan for the rebound; its trajectory is lower than the card's, peaking at 4-5 on day 1. The osteotomy stays a night or two with a catheter running 24-48 hours or, where the unit does not run ambulatory catheters, a single shot plus a plan that accepts a hard first night; its trajectory is the card's, 2 on day 0, 6 on day 1, 5 on day 2, and the quadriceps pulling on the fragment is why it stays at 3 at a week. Regular paracetamol and an NSAID are the backbone; the surgeon's view on NSAIDs after an osteotomy is worth asking for, because some restrict them for the union and the delayed union rate is already a quarter.

## Pain plan

The mechanism depends on the operation and the plan follows it. For isolated MPFL reconstruction the mechanism is a hamstring harvest, two tunnels and a graft under modest tension, so the plan is a day-case one: a single-shot adductor canal block with intravenous dexamethasone, the surgeon's infiltration, regular paracetamol and an NSAID started before discharge, oral opioid for the first two nights, and the night plan written down. For patellofemoral arthroplasty the mechanism is bone and cement in an anterior compartment with no balancing, and PROSPECT's total knee skeleton (adductor canal block plus infiltration, dexamethasone, paracetamol and an NSAID, opioid as rescue, no femoral block) applies with a shorter tail. For the tubercle osteotomy and the trochleoplasty the mechanism is bone under load or raw bone under a cartilage flap, in a young patient with every rebound risk factor, and the defensible default is an adductor canal catheter for 24-48 hours or an overnight stay with a plan for the second night; that is author's judgement, because there is no procedure-specific guideline and no randomised comparison of catheter against single shot for this operation.

The block choice is the same throughout: adductor canal, because the quadriceps is what the brace, the tracking and the rehabilitation depend on, and a femoral block takes it away. The saphenous and vastus medialis territory covers the medial patella, the pes harvest and the anteromedial tibia where the osteotomy is; the lateral side and the tubercle itself rely on the surgeon's infiltration. IPACK is not needed for anterior knee surgery. Gabapentinoids and ketamine have no evidence here and PROSPECT does not recommend them for the knee; ketamine is a reasonable adjunct in the opioid-exposed patient with several previous operations, which this population often is.

Rebound is the planning target. The osteotomy patient is young, female and has had bone surgery, which in the ambulatory cohort put rebound pain at well over half; the moment the block recedes at 2 am the quadriceps pull on the fragment is unmasked, and a patient without a running catheter or a taken oral regimen will telephone. Escalation triggers: pain above 7 after the block has receded, with a brace correctly fitted, a warm foot and a soft calf, is treated with the charted opioid; a tense anterior compartment, pain on passive toe flexion or numbness in the first web space after an osteotomy is compartment syndrome until proven otherwise, and the adductor canal block does not anaesthetise the deep peroneal territory, so those signs remain readable; a hot, hugely swollen knee at a week is a haemarthrosis or an infection and the surgeon's.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Combined procedures. Osteotomy plus MPFL reconstruction, or trochleoplasty plus MPFL reconstruction, runs two to three hours, stacks the harvest, the tunnels and the bone cut, and sits at the top of the card's range; it is the catheter case.
- Distalisation. The shingle is detached entirely to move the tubercle down for patella alta; delayed union rises to 42% and the fragment is less stable, so weight-bearing is more restricted and the pain tail longer.
- Quadriceps tendon or allograft MPFL. No pes harvest, so no hamstring pain; the allograft version is the lightest operation on the card.
- Patellar fracture. Through a transverse tunnel after MPFL reconstruction or a thin patellar cut after arthroplasty; presents weeks later as sudden pain and an extensor lag.
- Painful hardware. A fifth of osteotomy patients; screw removal is a short second operation under a block, and it is the commonest reason these patients return.
- The adolescent. Many MPFL reconstructions are in 14-18-year-olds with open physes, in whom the femoral tunnel is placed to spare the distal femoral physis; a paediatric day case with the same block and a parent who needs the night plan explained.
- Hypermobility and connective tissue disorders. Over-represented in patellar instability; the graft stretches, recurrence is higher, and the patient may carry several previous operations, a chronic pain history and opioid exposure into this one.
- Patellofemoral arthroplasty conversion. If the tibiofemoral compartments turn out to be worn at arthrotomy the surgeon converts to a total knee; the plan moves to that card and the stay lengthens.
- NSAIDs and union. Surgeons vary; some restrict NSAIDs after an osteotomy, which removes the main non-opioid from a plan that needs it. Ask before the drug chart is written.

## Key sources

No procedure-specific analgesia guideline exists for any operation on this card; PROSPECT's total knee guideline is borrowed for the arthroplasty and the block choice, and the catheter recommendation for the osteotomy and trochleoplasty is author's judgement built on the Gerbershagen cohort's lesson that small bone-and-ligament operations in the young rank among the most painful, and on the rebound pain cohort's risk factors. The osteotomy complication figures come from a two-centre retrospective cohort and a systematic review of mostly low-quality studies. The arthroplasty comparison is a systematic review of four small trials and five cohorts with wide intervals. The MPFL technique description rests on a published technical note and standard sports surgery texts.
