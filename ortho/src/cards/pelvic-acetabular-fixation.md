## What the surgeon is doing and why

The pelvis is a ring: two innominate bones joined at the front by the symphysis and at the back to the sacrum by the strongest ligaments in the body. A ring cannot break in one place and displace, so a displaced pelvic injury is always at least two lesions, front and back. The posterior lesion decides everything. If the posterior ligaments hold, the pelvis is stable or only rotationally unstable (the "open book"). If they are torn, one half of the pelvis can move up and back relative to the spine, and the venous plexus on the front of the sacrum, the internal iliac branches and the lumbosacral nerve roots are torn with it. That is the injury that kills on day 0 by bleeding into a retroperitoneal space that an open pelvis has enlarged.

The acetabulum is a different problem in the same bone. It is a joint surface, the socket of the hip, formed where an anterior and a posterior column of bone meet. The femoral head is driven into it, in a car crash or increasingly in an elderly fall, and breaks off a wall, a column or both. Nobody dies of an acetabular fracture, but a socket that heals with a step of more than a couple of millimetres wears out within years. So the surgery is a joint reconstruction: anatomical reduction and rigid plates, done within about two weeks before the fragments stick.

The operations therefore fall into three groups, usually on different days.

1. Emergency control of the pelvic ring: a binder at the level of the greater trochanters, then an anterior external fixator or a posterior clamp, with angioembolisation or preperitoneal packing for the bleeding. The aim is volume reduction and tamponade, not reduction of the fracture.
2. Definitive fixation of the ring: percutaneous iliosacral screws across the sacroiliac joint or sacral fracture under fluoroscopy, and a plate across the symphysis through a Pfannenstiel incision, or an internal or external anterior fixator.
3. Acetabular reconstruction, by an approach chosen for the fracture. Posterior wall and posterior column fractures: Kocher-Langenbeck, through gluteus maximus with the short external rotators divided and the sciatic nerve in the field throughout. Anterior column and most both-column fractures: the ilioinguinal approach, which lifts the abdominal wall off the iliac crest and inguinal ligament and works through three windows around the iliopsoas and the external iliac vessels, or the anterior intrapelvic (modified Stoppa) approach, which goes through the midline behind the rectus and along the pelvic brim from the inside.

The pain logic follows from the size of what is lifted. These are the largest muscle dissections in orthopaedic trauma: the whole of the iliacus off the inner pelvis, or the gluteus maximus split and the rotators cut, with subperiosteal exposure of the bone and long plates. Nerves are retracted for hours. The fracture itself bled into the same tissue planes days earlier. Afterwards nothing is rested: the patient is touch weight-bearing for 8 to 12 weeks, and every transfer, every turn in bed and every hour sitting loads the pelvis. Muscle is the dominant tag, with periosteum, bone and nerve behind it, and the curve is the reason for the 5: a peak on days 1 and 2 no higher than other major operations, then a slow decline over weeks in a trauma population with a high rate of persistent pain.

## Steps

### 1. Day 0: the unstable pelvis
**Why:** Most pelvic fracture bleeding is venous and from the cancellous fracture surfaces, and it tamponades once the ring is closed and the retroperitoneum intact. The minority that keeps bleeding has an arterial source. The team's sequence is a binder, exclusion of chest and abdominal sources, then embolisation, packing or both, depending on what the hospital can deliver in minutes.
**Anaesthesia:** This is resuscitation, not orthopaedic anaesthesia. Check that the binder is over the greater trochanters and not the iliac crests, and leave it on. Activate the massive transfusion protocol early, give tranexamic acid if within 3 h of injury, keep the calcium and temperature up, and do not chase a normal pressure before haemorrhage control. Where the patient goes is not settled by evidence: in the pooled series, packing and embolisation could not be separated on mortality once like was compared with like, and more than a quarter of packed patients still needed embolisation. You may be giving this anaesthetic in an angiography suite, away from help, with a patient who is not yet intubated: take the rapid infuser, the blood and a second pair of hands. Induction in a hypovolaemic patient with a tamponading haematoma is the moment of arrest; reduced doses, blood running and the surgeon or radiologist ready. See [polytrauma sequencing](polytrauma-sequencing.html).

### 2. Emergency skeletal stabilisation
**Why:** An anterior external fixator (pins in the iliac crests or above the acetabula) or a posterior clamp holds the volume reduction the binder achieved and allows access to the abdomen and groins. It is a 20-30 min procedure, often combined with packing through a low midline incision or a laparotomy.
**Anaesthesia:** The binder is cut or shifted for access and the pelvis can re-open as it comes off: have the blood ahead. Packing opens the preperitoneal space and releases tamponade before it restores it. A urethral catheter may have been deferred for blood at the meatus; a suprapubic one placed by the urologist sits exactly where the symphyseal plate will later go, which the pelvic surgeon will want to know. This is [damage control](open-fractures-damage-control.html): stop when the physiology says so.

### 3. Before definitive surgery
**Why:** Definitive fixation waits until the patient is resuscitated and the surgeon has CT reconstructions and a plan, usually day 2 to 10. Earlier, the pelvis bleeds heavily when opened; later than two to three weeks, reduction is fought against callus.
**Anaesthesia:** See the patient as a trauma patient a week on: lungs after rib fractures and transfusion, a haemoglobin that has drifted down, a week of bed rest. Three specific questions. What prophylaxis has been given and when was the last dose: a patient who has had none for a week has a real chance of an existing deep vein thrombosis, and units vary between duplex screening and a filter. What do the sciatic and femoral nerves do now: document ankle dorsiflexion and plantarflexion and quadriceps function, because about 5% of acetabular fractures arrive with a sciatic palsy and the operation adds to that. And is there a urological injury, a Morel-Lavallee lesion over the flank or a closed head injury that changes the position or the plan.

### 4. Anaesthesia, lines and blood
**Why:** The surgeon needs complete muscle relaxation for hours, since reduction is done against the largest muscles in the body, and a table that takes fluoroscopy in several oblique planes.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with a tracheal tube and continuous neuromuscular block monitored with a nerve stimulator; a request for "more relaxation" during reduction is literal. Arterial line and two large cannulae for any open acetabular approach, urinary catheter, temperature probe, forced-air warming and a fluid warmer. Cross-match four units for an anterior or combined approach. On cell salvage and tranexamic acid the evidence is less supportive than habit: two retrospective series returned a mean of 345 and 484 mL of salvaged blood, with no reduction in allogeneic transfusion in the larger one and more benefit in complex fracture types in the other; and two randomised trials and a meta-analysis of tranexamic acid in acetabular fixation found no reduction in loss or transfusion. The author's practice is to set up salvage in collect-only mode for anterior, combined and complex cases and process if the reservoir fills, and to give tranexamic acid 1 g IV on the argument of low cost, while planning as though neither will change the transfusion. Pre-operative anaemia and operating time were the predictors of transfusion in the trial.

### 5. Positioning
**Why:** Supine for the ring and for anterior acetabular approaches, with the leg draped free so the hip can be flexed to relax iliopsoas and the femoral vessels. Prone or lateral for Kocher-Langenbeck, with the knee flexed to at least 60-90 degrees and the hip extended throughout to take tension off the sciatic nerve.
**Anaesthesia:** Prone positioning of a trauma patient with a pelvic fracture, rib fractures and perhaps a cleared-but-sore cervical spine needs enough people and a moment of real blood loss as the pelvis is moved. The abdomen must hang free, the eyes and face are checked after every table movement, and arms are positioned around any upper limb injuries. In a series of 644 posterior approaches the rate of iatrogenic sciatic palsy was the same prone and lateral (about 3%), so the position is the surgeon's preference and the knee flexion is the thing to protect: if the leg holder slips and the knee extends, say so.

### 6. Percutaneous iliosacral screws
**Why:** A cannulated screw is passed from the outer ilium across the sacroiliac joint into the body of S1 or S2 over a guide wire, steered on inlet, outlet and lateral fluoroscopy through a corridor a few millimetres wide between the sacral foramina, the L5 root in front and the spinal canal behind.
**Anaesthesia:** A short, nearly bloodless procedure with a great deal of radiation and a surgeon whose view depends on the image. Bowel gas and contrast in the bladder or bowel obscure the sacrum, so mention any recent contrast study. Some surgeons run neuromonitoring of the L5 and S1 roots, which means no relaxant after intubation and a total intravenous anaesthetic; ask before induction. Pain afterwards is modest and no block is needed.

### 7. Symphyseal plating
**Why:** Through a Pfannenstiel incision the recti are split in the midline or partly released, the bladder is held back with a malleable retractor, the symphysis is closed with a clamp and a plate is laid across the top of the pubic bodies.
**Anaesthesia:** Deep relaxation so the recti can be retracted. The bladder is directly under the surgeon's hand; a catheter that stops draining or drains blood is reported at once. The retropubic venous plexus bleeds briskly and briefly. Loss is 300-500 mL.

### 8. Kocher-Langenbeck approach
**Why:** A curved incision over the greater trochanter, gluteus maximus split in line with its fibres, piriformis and the obturator internus tendon divided and reflected to protect the sciatic nerve behind them, and the posterior wall and column exposed. The hip may be distracted or dislocated to clear fragments from the joint. The wall fragments are reduced and held with a contoured plate and screws that must not enter the joint.
**Anaesthesia:** The sciatic nerve is in the field for the whole operation, under a retractor, in a patient whose nerve may already be bruised by the dislocation. Pooled incidence of iatrogenic palsy is about 1-3%, with roughly three in four recovering fully; the peroneal division is the vulnerable part. Your contributions are the knee flexion, a mean pressure that perfuses the nerve, and no long-acting sciatic or sacral plexus block that would hide a deficit in recovery. The superior gluteal vessels at the sciatic notch are the source of sudden bleeding in this approach, and they retract into the pelvis when cut. Loss is typically 500-800 mL.

### 9. Ilioinguinal and anterior intrapelvic approaches
**Why:** Ilioinguinal: an incision from the iliac crest to the midline above the symphysis, the abdominal muscles and iliacus lifted off the crest and the inner pelvis, and the inguinal canal opened so that the iliopsoas with the femoral nerve, and the external iliac vessels, can each be slung and moved aside to work through three windows. Anterior intrapelvic: a midline or Pfannenstiel entry behind the rectus, then along the pelvic brim from inside, with the surgeon standing on the opposite side. Both give access to the anterior column and the quadrilateral plate without opening the hip.
**Anaesthesia:** These are the long cases and the wet ones. The external iliac vein is retracted for hours and can tear or thrombose. On the back of the superior pubic ramus an anastomosis between the obturator and the external iliac or inferior epigastric vessels (the corona mortis) crosses the field in a substantial minority of patients and must be found and tied before it is torn, because the cut end retracts into the obturator canal. A sudden, large venous bleed in the middle of an otherwise steady case is one of these. The femoral nerve and the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve are stretched by the retractors, and thigh numbness afterwards is the rule. Keep the hip flexed on a bolster as the surgeon asks, keep the relaxant on, and keep a running tally of the suction, the swabs and the salvage reservoir against an hourly blood gas.

### 10. Reduction and fixation: the long middle
**Why:** Reduction of an acetabulum is done with clamps, ball spikes and traction through small windows, checked by feel and fluoroscopy, and may take longer than everything else together. Long plates are contoured along the pelvic brim or the posterior column, with screws aimed down narrow safe corridors.
**Anaesthesia:** The pattern is steady loss rather than a single event: 100-300 mL an hour from bone and muscle for three or four hours. Transfuse to the trend, not to a number reached after the fact; correct the calcium and the temperature; repeat the antibiotic at four hours. The frequent table tilts for oblique views move your lines and your pressure transducer. A fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide with hypotension during work near the iliac veins is air or thrombus until shown otherwise.

### 11. Closure, imaging and what goes home with the patient
**Why:** Final fluoroscopic views confirm that no screw is in the joint. The muscle is reattached to the crest or the trochanter, drains are common, and the abdominal wall is repaired in layers after an anterior approach to prevent a hernia.
**Anaesthesia:** The abdominal wall closure needs relaxation to the end, so plan the reversal accordingly. If a regional technique is being used, this is when it is placed: the surgeon can lay a catheter under the iliac fascia against the femoral nerve before closing an anterior approach, with no extra procedure, or you can place a fascia iliaca or quadratus lumborum catheter under ultrasound once the dressings are on. Start the ketamine and the PCA before emergence. Extubate the isolated injury; keep the polytrauma patient with a 2 L loss and a long prone time intubated without apology.

### 12. Recovery and the first week
**Why:** The surgeon wants a documented nerve examination, a check radiograph or CT, the patient sitting out within a day or two and touch weight-bearing on the injured side for 8 to 12 weeks. After a posterior approach many give indomethacin or a single dose of radiotherapy to prevent heterotopic bone in the abductors.
**Anaesthesia:** Examine and record sciatic function (ankle and toes, up and down) and femoral function as soon as the patient can cooperate; a new foot drop is the surgeon's business that evening. High dependency care for a combined approach, a large transfusion or other injuries. Thromboprophylaxis restarts that night or the next morning by agreement with the surgeon and is the single most important drug chart entry of the week; any neuraxial or deep catheter has to fit around it. If indomethacin is prescribed for heterotopic ossification it is also the patient's NSAID, and a second one is not added.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is muscle and periosteum over a large area, loaded for weeks, so by the logic of this book a continuous technique has a strong claim. The evidence does not yet support one. A case-control study of posterior wall fixation found no difference in pain scores, time to mobilisation or length of stay when an epidural was added to general anaesthesia. A retrospective cohort of 205 pelvic and acetabular fixations found that regional anaesthesia was associated with no less, and at some time points more, opioid use, which probably says more about who was selected for a block than about blocks. For anterior approaches, a surgeon-placed catheter under the iliac fascia gave three-day morphine use similar to an epidural (5.0 and 6.0 mg) and less than half that of no regional technique (12.7 mg), a difference that did not reach significance in 85 patients; and a small randomised trial found a quadratus lumborum block noninferior to a fascia iliaca block. That is the whole comparative literature.

The defensible default is therefore systemic and multimodal, with regional analgesia added where the approach makes it easy and safe. For every patient: regular paracetamol; an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor once bleeding and renal function allow, or the surgeon's indomethacin doing that job; ketamine at induction and by low-dose infusion for 24-48 h; an opioid PCA converted to oral modified-release and immediate-release opioid by day 2 or 3, with a written taper, because this is a population in which a discharge prescription easily becomes a habit. Gabapentinoids are reserved for established neuropathic symptoms from a nerve that was injured or stretched.

Regional additions, as author's judgement. After an ilioinguinal or anterior intrapelvic approach: a fascia iliaca catheter (surgeon-placed or ultrasound-guided) or a quadratus lumborum catheter for 48-72 h, covering the iliac fossa, the groin and the anterior hip; the femoral nerve has already been stretched, so its function is documented first and a dilute infusion is used. After a Kocher-Langenbeck approach: nothing in the sciatic distribution on the day; a lumbar or sacral erector spinae plane block is a low-risk option supported only by case reports; surgeon infiltration of the gluteal wound is simple and reasonable. An epidural gives the most complete analgesia for any approach and is the right choice for the occasional isolated injury in an opioid-tolerant patient, but it masks the sciatic examination, drops the pressure in a patient who has bled, and has to be placed and removed around the anticoagulant that matters most this week. For percutaneous screws alone, no block.

Escalation: buttock and posterior thigh pain with a new foot drop is the sciatic nerve or a gluteal haematoma compressing it; a swollen leg is a thrombosis; burning anterolateral thigh pain is the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve and is usually temporary; groin pain on any hip movement with a clunk is a screw in the joint or a lost reduction. Pain still severe at two weeks, with allodynia, is the start of a chronic pain trajectory and merits referral before discharge.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The elderly acetabular fracture. A low-energy fall onto the hip in an osteoporotic patient is now a common presentation: the same approaches, a frailer patient, sometimes an acute total hip replacement at the same sitting, which adds [arthroplasty](tha-posterior.html) blood loss and a possible cement step to a fracture operation.
- Combined and extended approaches. A both-column fracture needing front and back exposure is two operations, sometimes with a turn in the middle; six hours and 2 L are realistic, and staging over two days is a reasonable request to make of the surgeon when the physiology is drifting.
- Fracture-dislocation of the hip. The dislocated head is reduced as an emergency, under a brief deep anaesthetic with full relaxation, to protect the head's blood supply and the sciatic nerve; definitive fixation follows days later.
- Urological and bowel injury. A bladder or urethral injury changes the catheter, the antibiotic and the infection risk of anterior plates; an open pelvic fracture with a rectal or perineal wound needs a diverting colostomy and has a far higher mortality.
- Morel-Lavallee lesion. A closed degloving over the flank or trochanter, full of haematoma and fat necrosis, lies under the planned incision or under a block site and is colonised more often than it looks.
- Anterior subcutaneous internal fixator. Pedicle screws above the acetabula joined by a subcutaneous rod: quick and well tolerated, with compression of the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve as the common complaint and of the femoral nerve as the rare serious one.
- Sacral fractures with root injury. Neurological deficits in the L5 and S1 roots and the bladder are part of the injury; document them before any neuraxial technique is considered.
- Thromboembolism. Proximal thrombosis in the pelvic veins is not seen by a leg duplex. A fall in saturation in the first fortnight is a pulmonary embolus until imaged.
- Obesity. Every approach is deeper, the fluoroscopy is poorer, wound complications are much more frequent, and prone positioning for hours becomes the main anaesthetic risk.
- The anticoagulated older patient. A minor-looking pubic ramus fracture can bleed slowly to shock over hours; reverse early and image with contrast.

## Key sources

The evidence here is better for what does not work than for what does. Tranexamic acid has two randomised trials and a meta-analysis in acetabular fixation, all negative. Cell salvage has retrospective series that disagree on whether it saves allogeneic blood. Packing against embolisation for the unstable pelvis has a meta-analysis that concludes the cohorts cannot be compared. The sciatic nerve figures come from a meta-analysis and a large single-centre series. Analgesia has a negative case-control study of epidurals, a confounded cohort, a small retrospective comparison of surgeon-placed catheters and one small randomised trial comparing two blocks with each other. The pain score of 5 and the regional recommendations are the author's judgement from the anatomy and should be revised when a proper trial exists.
