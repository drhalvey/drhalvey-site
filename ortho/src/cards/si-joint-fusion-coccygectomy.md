## What the surgeon is doing and why

These two operations share a card because they share a patient and a position. Both treat a pain generator at the bottom of the axial skeleton, both are offered only after years of failed non-operative treatment, both are done prone through a small incision in under an hour, and both produce a recovery that surprises the patient. They are otherwise unrelated and the card treats them in turn.

Sacroiliac joint fusion. The sacroiliac joint is a large, irregular, almost immobile joint that transmits the whole load of the trunk to the pelvis. It moves a few degrees, enough to hurt when its ligaments are lax after pregnancy, when it is arthritic, or when a long lumbar fusion above it has made it the next mobile segment. The diagnosis is clinical and soft: provocation tests on examination and relief from an image-guided injection of local anaesthetic into the joint. The surgical idea is to stop the movement. The open operation, a large posterior or anterior exposure with plates and bone graft, has largely given way to a percutaneous lateral technique: through a 3 cm incision on the side of the buttock, a guide pin is driven across the ilium, through the joint and into the sacral ala, and over it two or three implants (triangular porous titanium rods in the original system, threaded screws or cages in others) are placed to bridge the joint. The implants hold the joint rigid at once and bone grows onto and around them over months.

What makes it safe or not is the corridor. The implants must stay within the sacral ala: in front of it lie the L5 root and the iliac vessels, behind and medial to it the sacral foramina and the S1 and S2 roots, and at the starting point on the outer ilium the superior gluteal artery leaves the greater sciatic notch. The surgeon finds the corridor on three fluoroscopic views (lateral, inlet and outlet) or with navigation, and most of the operating time is spent obtaining them.

The evidence deserves a plain statement, because the anaesthetist is often asked whether the operation works. Two randomised trials against non-surgical care, of 148 and 103 patients, found large and durable differences in favour of fusion: six-month success of 81% against 26% in one, back pain improved by 45 points against 11 at two years in the other, with opioid use falling from 56% to 33%. Both were unblinded and both had manufacturer authorship. The one double-blind trial against sham surgery, in 63 patients, found pain improved by 2.6 points after fusion and 1.7 after a sham incision, a difference of 1.0 point that did not reach significance. It was small, and it is the only blinded evidence there is. The safety review of 720 patients put procedural complications at 11%, mostly wound problems, implant malposition events at 3%, mostly nerve root impingement, and revision at 2.6%.

Coccygectomy. The coccyx is three to five fused segments hanging from the sacrum by a joint and a ring of ligaments, with the levator ani and the anococcygeal raphe attached to its tip and the rectum lying directly in front of it. After a fall onto the buttocks or a difficult delivery it can become unstable or subluxed and hurt on sitting and on rising from sitting; the patients are women four times out of five. Injections and cushions settle most. For the rest the operation is to remove it: a short midline incision over the bone above the natal cleft, a subperiosteal dissection that stays on the bone all the way round so that the rectum is never seen, disarticulation at the sacrococcygeal joint, and a careful layered closure of a wound that lies a few centimetres from the anus. In pooled series of 671 and 826 patients about three quarters had a good result and pain fell by about 5 points on a 10-point scale; the complication rate was 8-11%, nearly all of it wound infection and dehiscence.

The pain logic. Both are bone on this book's mechanism list. The sacroiliac implants are broached through cortical ilium and into cancellous sacrum via a track through gluteus medius, and the joint is loaded with body weight from the first step, which is why surgeons protect weight-bearing for three to six weeks. The coccygectomy removes a bone from its periosteal sleeve and leaves a wound that is compressed by sitting and stretched by defaecation. On tissue injury alone each would be a 2. They are scored 3 because the patients are not pain-naive: half the sacroiliac trial patients were taking opioids at baseline, and central sensitisation after years of pain is the rule. Coccygectomy has a further sting in that the patient was promised the removal of a painful bone and wakes to several weeks in which sitting hurts more than before.

## Steps

### 1. Sacroiliac fusion: the patient and the promise
**Why:** The surgeon has selected the patient by examination and by a diagnostic injection, and has usually told them the operation takes under an hour.
**Anaesthesia:** Take a pain history, not just a medical one: daily opioid dose, gabapentinoids, antidepressants, previous lumbar fusion, and what happened after the last operation. Continue all of it on the day. A patient on a modified-release opioid who is sent home on paracetamol after a day-case fusion will be back that night. Decide with the surgeon before the list whether this patient is a day case or stays.

### 2. Induction and prone positioning for imaging
**Why:** The surgeon needs true lateral, inlet and outlet views of the sacrum, which means a radiolucent table, nothing metallic under the pelvis and a C-arm that can arc from 40 degrees caudal to 40 degrees cranial.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with a tracheal tube is the default prone, though the case is short. Abdomen free, arms forward, eyes clear, the usual prone checks. Image quality is the limiting step in an obese patient or one with bowel gas over the sacrum, and a case booked for 45 min can take 90 min for that reason alone; if the surgeon cannot see the corridor they should not place the implant, and your part is not to hurry them. If the surgeon uses electromyographic monitoring of the L5 and S1 roots during drilling, give one intubating dose of relaxant and no more.

### 3. Incision and the guide pin
**Why:** A 3 cm incision is made on the lateral buttock in line with the sacrum on the lateral view. The gluteal fascia and muscle are split bluntly down to the outer table of the ilium, and a guide pin is advanced across the ilium and the joint into the sacral ala, checked on all three views at each centimetre.
**Anaesthesia:** A brief, stimulating start and then a long quiet period of imaging. Ask for the track to be infiltrated down to periosteum before the pin goes in; it is the main regional component. Movement with a pin in the ala risks the L5 root or the foramen, so keep the patient still without needing to be asked.

### 4. Drill, broach and implants
**Why:** A cannulated drill and then a triangular broach or tap are passed over the pin, and the implant is impacted or screwed across the joint. The sequence is repeated for a second and third implant caudal to the first, each needing its own corridor between the foramina.
**Anaesthesia:** Impaction is hammering through bone and is the most stimulating part of the case; the heart rate tells you if the infiltration was thorough. Blood loss is trivial unless the starting point has strayed towards the sciatic notch and the superior gluteal artery: brisk bleeding from the track that packing does not stop, or a buttock that is tense at the end of the case, is that vessel, and it may need embolisation rather than a bigger incision. It is rare and it is the one way this operation produces a transfusion.

### 5. Final views, closure and waking
**Why:** The surgeon confirms on inlet and outlet views that no implant breaches the anterior ala or a foramen, closes fascia and skin, and wants to know promptly if the patient has new leg pain.
**Anaesthesia:** Infiltrate the wound again at closure. On waking, ask specifically about new pain, numbness or weakness in the leg and foot on the operated side, because a malpositioned implant against the L5 or S1 root declares itself as radicular pain in recovery and is fixed by repositioning the same day after a CT. Buttock pain is expected; pain below the knee is not.

### 6. Sacroiliac fusion: recovery and discharge
**Why:** The implants are stable at once but rely on bone ongrowth, so the surgeon restricts weight-bearing on that side with crutches or a frame for three to six weeks.
**Anaesthesia:** Mobilise with the physiotherapist before discharge; a patient who cannot manage crutches is not a day case. Paracetamol and the NSAID given before leaving theatre, with the patient's usual opioid continued and a short supply of immediate-release opioid on top. Wound drainage in the first week is the commonest complication in the pooled safety data and is reported to the surgeon.

### 7. Coccygectomy: preparation
**Why:** The surgeon is planning a clean operation in a contaminated neighbourhood. Some ask for a bowel preparation or an enema beforehand so that the first bowel movement is delayed; others consider it unnecessary.
**Anaesthesia:** The same pain history as above. Antibiotics matter more here than in most small operations: cefazolin 2 g IV before incision, with the Gram-negative and anaerobic cover the surgeon requests. Decide the anaesthetic: general anaesthesia with a tracheal tube is the author's default for the jackknife position, and a low-dose spinal as used for perianal surgery is a reasonable alternative for a slim patient and a quick surgeon. Start a laxative plan before the operation, not after the first hard stool.

### 8. Prone jackknife
**Why:** Flexing the table at the hips opens the natal cleft and brings the coccyx to the surface; the buttocks are taped apart and the anus is sealed off from the field with an adhesive drape.
**Anaesthesia:** Jackknife is prone with the head down and the hips flexed: more venous return than flat prone, more pressure on the abdomen if the bolsters are wrong, and a face that swells in a long case, which this is not. Check that the tape has not been stuck across a cannula or a pressure area and that the genitalia are free.

### 9. Incision and subperiosteal excision
**Why:** A 4-6 cm midline incision is made over the sacrococcygeal joint, kept above the depth of the cleft. The periosteum is incised and peeled off the bone circumferentially, the sacrococcygeal joint is opened, and the coccyx is lifted out from proximal to distal with the anococcygeal attachments released from its tip. Staying inside the periosteum keeps the rectum, which is a few millimetres anterior, out of the wound. Sharp edges on the sacrum are smoothed.
**Anaesthesia:** Short and moderately stimulating, with almost no blood loss. Ask for the periosteal bed and the skin edges to be infiltrated with long-acting local anaesthetic; in a wound this small it covers most of the first evening. Some surgeons examine the rectum with a finger at this point to exclude a tear; a rectal injury is rare and converts the case into a general surgical problem with a different antibiotic course.

### 10. Closure
**Why:** The closure is the operation. The surgeon obliterates the dead space where the bone lay by suturing the periosteal sleeve and the deep fascia in layers, closes the skin so that it does not gape with sitting, and seals the dressing against soiling.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing is asked of you except not to rush it, and to keep the patient from coughing or bucking on the tube with the wound under tension as the table is flattened. Turn supine onto a soft mattress, not a slide board dragged under the wound.

### 11. Coccygectomy: the first week
**Why:** The surgeon wants the wound kept clean and dry, pressure kept off it and the bowels soft; most advise a wedge or ring cushion and sitting only for meals for two weeks or more.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the part of the operation the patient remembers. Pain on sitting is worse than before surgery for two to four weeks and the first bowel movement is dreaded: regular paracetamol and NSAID, a stool softener and osmotic laxative from the day of surgery, and as little opioid as the patient's baseline allows because constipation is a direct threat to the wound. Tell the patient in recovery, in those words, that the first weeks are harder than the size of the wound suggests and that this is expected. Increasing pain with redness or discharge at day 5-10 is the infection that complicated roughly one in twelve cases in the pooled series.

## Pain plan

Neither operation has a procedure-specific guideline, and neither has a nerve block that is clearly worth its trouble: the sacroiliac joint and the lateral ilium are supplied by posterior rami from L4 to S3 and by the superior gluteal nerve, and the coccyx by the lower sacral and coccygeal roots, none of which is neatly reached by a single peripheral block. The mechanism is bone under a small wound, so the plan is infiltration, non-opioid analgesia and an honest allowance for the patient's history. All of this is the author's judgement.

Sacroiliac fusion. Surgeon's infiltration of each implant track to periosteum before the pin and of the wound at closure; paracetamol 1 g IV, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor and dexamethasone 8 mg IV in theatre; ketamine 0.25-0.5 mg/kg IV at induction for the patient on long-term opioids. Fascial plane blocks around the sacrum are described and may help; there are no trials for this operation, and the author does not use them routinely. Catheter: no.

Coccygectomy. Infiltration of the periosteal bed and skin; the same systemic drugs. A spinal provides excellent early analgesia if it was the anaesthetic. A caudal injection is anatomically tempting and puts a needle through the sacral hiatus a few centimetres from the incision; with infection as the main complication the author avoids it. Catheter: no.

Oral regimen shape for both: regular paracetamol and an NSAID for one to two weeks; the patient's own long-term analgesics unchanged; a small quantity of immediate-release opioid for the first three to five days, less for coccygectomy and with laxatives co-prescribed; no new gabapentinoid started for the operation. Agree before discharge who the patient contacts about pain, because this population makes heavier use of that number than any other day-case group.

Rebound is moderate: infiltration wears off in the evening, and the rebound study's risk factors (bone surgery, female sex, younger age) describe this patient exactly, while intravenous dexamethasone was protective. Escalation: after sacroiliac fusion, new radicular leg pain means an implant against a root and needs imaging, and a tense, painful, enlarging buttock is a gluteal haematoma; after coccygectomy, pain that rises after the first week with a wet wound is infection, and neither is managed by raising the opioid.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Bilateral sacroiliac fusion. Done in about one patient in seven in the pooled series, in one sitting or staged. In one sitting it doubles the imaging time and leaves no leg on which to protect weight-bearing; plan a night in hospital.
- Open and posterior sacroiliac fusion. A posterior midline or paramedian approach with bone graft, or a posterior allograft dowel placed into the joint: more muscle dissection, more blood, a 4 on the scale for the open operation.
- Sacroiliac screws for trauma. The same corridor and the same imaging in an unstable pelvic ring belongs to the [pelvic and acetabular card](pelvic-acetabular-fixation.html), with that patient's bleeding and physiology.
- Fusion below a long construct. Sacroiliac fixation added to a deformity operation uses iliac or sacral-alar-iliac screws and is part of the [deformity card](adult-deformity-scoliosis.html).
- Navigation and intra-operative CT. Better views in the obese patient, at the price of a reference pin in the iliac crest, a spin with everyone out of the room and a longer anaesthetic.
- The obese patient. Poor imaging, a long track, a deeper wound and higher wound complication rates for both operations; for coccygectomy a deep natal cleft makes the wound harder to keep dry.
- Partial coccygectomy. Removal of the mobile distal segments only; a smaller wound, the same position and aftercare.
- Pilonidal disease or perianal sepsis. A reason for the surgeon to postpone a coccygectomy; an active skin problem in the cleft is not a minor finding.
- The unconvinced reader. The sham-controlled trial is small and negative; the patient on the table has already made the decision with the surgeon, and the anaesthetic room is not the place to reopen it.
- Spinal anaesthesia prone. Suitable for coccygectomy, with the usual jackknife cautions; unsuitable for sacroiliac fusion in most hands because of the length and discomfort of the imaging and the need for a still patient.

## Key sources

The efficacy evidence for sacroiliac fusion is two unblinded, manufacturer-supported randomised trials with large effects and one small blinded sham-controlled trial without a significant one; the safety figures come from pooled case series. Coccygectomy has no randomised evidence at all, only pooled retrospective series that agree on a good result in about three quarters of patients and wound trouble in about one in ten. Nothing has been published on analgesia specific to either operation; the plan here is reasoned from the anatomy, the wound and the chronic pain history, and the rebound pain cohort is cited only for its risk factors.
