## What the surgeon is doing and why

A child's femur heals fast and remodels generously, so the question is never whether it will unite but how to hold length, rotation and alignment for the few weeks that takes, at the lowest cost to the child and the family. The answer changes with age because the forces change. An infant's thigh muscles are weak and the periosteum is a thick sleeve that holds the fragments; a twelve-year-old's quadriceps and hamstrings will shorten an unsupported fracture by several centimetres.

So treatment is stratified:

```
age / weight            usual treatment              your case
0-6 months              Pavlik harness or spica      often none
6 months - 5 years      early hip spica              30-45 min GA, or sedation in ED
5 - 11 years, < 50 kg   flexible nails (two)         45-90 min GA
length-unstable         submuscular bridge plate     90-150 min GA
> 11 years or > 50 kg   rigid lateral-entry nail     adult-style nailing
```

The hip spica is a plaster or fibreglass shell from the nipple line to the ankle on the injured side (often to the knee on the other), with hips and knees flexed so the cast itself resists shortening. Up to 2 cm of overlap is accepted under 5 years because the fracture stimulates growth. Flexible nails are two pre-bent titanium or steel rods introduced through the distal femoral metaphysis, medial and lateral, 2 cm above the physis, and driven up the canal so that their opposing curves give three-point fixation inside the bone. They are load-sharing, which is why they fail in heavy children and in long spiral or comminuted fractures: complications more than doubled at 50 kg and above in one series. Those fractures get a long plate slid under vastus lateralis through two small incisions, or in the adolescent a rigid nail entered through the lateral trochanter (never the piriformis fossa, whose vessels feed the femoral head until the physis closes).

In the child under walking age the surgeon has a second job. A femur does not break in an infant who cannot yet generate the energy to break it: abuse was found in 17-35% of femoral fractures under 12 months across 24 studies, against 2-6% at 12 months and over. The fracture is treated the same way; the admission is not.

Pain logic: bone and muscle. The unfixed fracture is a 7 or 8, driven by spasm, and this is the pain a fascia iliaca block in the emergency department treats. After fixation or casting the bone is quiet within a day. What persists for two or three days is quadriceps spasm, which opioids treat poorly and a femoral block or diazepam treat well, plus the nail ends at the knee. A spica adds little nociception and a great deal of distress in a toddler who cannot move.

## Steps

### 1. Before theatre: age, mechanism, other injuries
**Why:** The surgeon confirms an isolated injury and a mechanism that fits the child's development. Under 12 months, or in any child not yet walking, a skeletal survey and a child protection assessment run alongside the orthopaedic plan.
**Anaesthesia:** An isolated closed femoral fracture does not produce shock in a child: in 111 such children nobody was unstable and the haematocrit did not move. Tachycardia beyond pain, hypotension or a falling haemoglobin means spleen, liver, pelvis or head, and the femur can wait; see [polytrauma sequencing](polytrauma-sequencing.html). In the infant, examine the whole child under anaesthesia as you would anyway and record objectively what is there: bruises, torn frenulum, rib crepitus, fontanelle. You may be asked to keep the child asleep for the skeletal survey or an ophthalmology examination; that is a reasonable request. The differential that matters to you is osteogenesis imperfecta (blue sclerae, previous fractures): gentle cuff inflation and positioning, and expect a rise in temperature under anaesthesia that is not malignant hyperthermia.

### 2. Analgesia before induction and the block decision
**Why:** The fracture hurts with every transfer until it is held, and thigh spasm fights the reduction.
**Anaesthesia:** Most children arrive with intranasal fentanyl 1.5 microg/kg or intravenous morphine 0.05-0.1 mg/kg on board and many with a fascia iliaca block from the emergency department, which in the one randomised trial lasted a median of 313 minutes against 60 for morphine. Ask what volume and when: the local anaesthetic budget is shared, at ropivacaine 3 mg/kg or bupivacaine 2.5 mg/kg in total (2 mg/kg under 6 months). If no block is in place, do one after induction: fascia iliaca or femoral, ropivacaine 0.2% 0.5 mL/kg (max 30 mL). For a spica in a child under about 20 kg a caudal (ropivacaine 0.2% 1 mL/kg, max 20 mL) is the alternative; it also relaxes the hamstrings and adductors for the moulding, at the price of both legs and the bladder for four to six hours.

### 3. Induction and airway
**Why:** The surgeon needs a relaxed thigh and, for a spica, a child who can be lifted and suspended without the airway becoming the problem.
**Anaesthesia:** Fasting is as for any injured child: count from the meal to the injury. For the semi-elective spica next morning an inhalational or intravenous induction and a supraglottic airway work for flexible nails on a flat table. For the spica table the author's default is a tracheal tube in the child under 2 years or under about 12 kg: the child will be lifted twice, the head rests on a platform the size of a book, plaster-covered hands are everywhere, and regaining a lost airway means cutting off a half-set cast. Tube size and depth are checked after the lift, not before it.

### 4. Spica: onto the table
**Why:** The spica table suspends the pelvis and legs in air so plaster can pass all the way round the trunk and both thighs. The trunk rests on a narrow box, the sacrum on a small support, the perineum against a padded post, and two assistants hold the legs in the chosen position, usually hips flexed 60-90 degrees and abducted 30 degrees, knees flexed.
**Anaesthesia:** Before the lift, a folded towel 3-5 cm thick is placed on the abdomen under the stockinette; it comes out when the cast is hard and leaves room for a meal and for the diaphragm. Confirm it is there. The child is now fully exposed on a metal frame with wet plaster going on: forced-air warming is impossible, so the room is at 24-26 degrees C before the child arrives, an overhead radiant heater or a warm mattress under the trunk box is on, and temperature is measured. An infant of 8 kg loses a degree in the time the cast takes to apply. Eyes taped, tube and circuit supported from above the head so that the assistants do not lean on them, cannula in a hand (the feet are being cast).

### 5. Spica: reduction, plaster and the moulding
**Why:** Padding first, then plaster or fibreglass in layers, with the surgeon moulding a valgus push over the lateral thigh to resist the usual varus drift, and holding traction through the thigh, not through a short leg cast: pulling on a below-knee cast and then incorporating it drove the nine reported cases of calf compartment syndrome and Volkmann contracture after 90/90 spicas.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing here is stimulating once the block works; the risk is the opposite, a child who lightens and moves. Plaster sets hard enough to move in 10-15 minutes and fibreglass in 5: keep the depth until the surgeon has trimmed the perineal opening, pulled the towel and taken a radiograph. Plaster warms as it sets; a temperature that rises a little at this point is the cast. Check that the chest wall moves freely and that peak pressures have not climbed with the cast on.

### 6. Flexible nails: position and entry
**Why:** Supine with the leg free on a radiolucent table, or on a traction table for the older, more muscular child so that length is held while the nails pass. Two 2 cm incisions at the distal thigh, medial and lateral; the cortex is opened with an awl or drill 2 cm above the physis.
**Anaesthesia:** On a traction table the perineal post sits against a small pelvis: check its padding, the genitalia and the well leg, and keep traction time in mind, because the pudendal and sciatic neurapraxias described in adults are a function of force over time and children are not exempt. Opening the metaphysis and the first advance of the nail are the stimulating moments; fentanyl 1 microg/kg IV if the block is patchy at the medial entry site, which is obturator and saphenous territory at its edge. Cefazolin 30 mg/kg IV (max 2 g) before incision.

### 7. Passing the nails across the fracture
**Why:** Each nail is rotated and tapped up to the fracture, the fracture is reduced by traction and manipulation, and the bent tip is steered into the proximal fragment under screening, one nail then the other, finishing in the neck and the trochanter. The ends are cut short and left just proud of the cortex for later removal.
**Anaesthesia:** Reduction needs a slack thigh: the block provides most of it, and a small dose of relaxant (rocuronium 0.3-0.5 mg/kg IV) is a fair request if the surgeon is fighting spasm in a ten-year-old. Screening time is long; the lead goes on before the drapes. Blood loss is a few mL/kg. Fat embolism from unreamed flexible nails in a child's fatty marrow is not a practical concern, unlike the reamed [adult femoral nail](femoral-shaft-nailing.html).

### 8. Plate or rigid nail in the heavier child
**Why:** A comminuted or long spiral fracture will telescope over flexible nails, and a child over 50 kg bends them. A submuscular plate bridges the fracture with screws at each end through small incisions; a rigid lateral-entry nail is reamed and locked as in an adult.
**Anaesthesia:** These are 90-150 minute operations with 5-10 mL/kg of blood loss, vastus lateralis elevated off the bone and a pain course a point higher than nails for two days. Above about 50 kg, dose as an adult: cefazolin 2 g IV, tranexamic acid 1 g IV if open, and the block volume capped at the adult figure. The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve covers the incisions, which favours fascia iliaca over femoral block here.

### 9. Emergence
**Why:** A fresh spica is at its weakest at the hip, and nails rely on the child not thrashing against a fracture that is only splinted from inside.
**Anaesthesia:** Lift the child off the spica table as a unit, supporting the cast under the buttocks and never by the bar between the legs. Extubate awake but calm: clonidine 1 microg/kg IV or dexmedetomidine 0.3-0.5 microg/kg IV near the end in the 1-5 year group reduces emergence delirium, and the block should mean no pain to drive it. A child in a new spica who is tachypnoeic or whose saturation drifts on waking has a cast that is too tight on the abdomen until it has been checked with two fingers under the edge.

### 10. The first night
**Why:** The surgeon wants toes that are pink and moving, a cast that does not press on the heel or the back of the knee, and a family who can lift and nappy the child.
**Anaesthesia:** Regular paracetamol 15 mg/kg orally every 6 h (max 60 mg/kg/day; max 1 g per dose) and ibuprofen 10 mg/kg every 8 h (max 400 mg) from 3 months of age; oxycodone 0.1 mg/kg orally (max 5 mg) every 4 h as required. Spasm is the usual reason for a call at 2 am when the block has gone: sudden screaming with a visibly jumping thigh, relieved by diazepam 0.1 mg/kg orally (max 5 mg) every 8 h as required, not by more opioid. Pain in the calf, pain on passive toe movement, or a child who cannot be settled in a 90/90 spica is the cast and the compartment, and the cast is split.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is bone held still plus muscle in spasm, a score of 3 with nails or a plate and nearer 2 in a spica. A single-shot femoral or fascia iliaca block matches it: it covers the periosteum of the shaft, the quadriceps and the lateral incisions, it lasts 8-12 h at 0.2%, and by the time it recedes the fixed fracture is no longer the problem. Ropivacaine 0.2% 0.5 mL/kg (max 30 mL) is enough; a higher concentration buys a weak quadriceps in a child who is about to be taught to stand on crutches. Intravenous dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg (max 8 mg) extends the block and covers nausea in the child over 2 years.

Caudal is the alternative when the child is small and both legs are going into plaster. Its attraction is hamstring and adductor relaxation for the moulding; its costs are a shorter duration (4-6 h plain, 8-12 h with clonidine 1 microg/kg, which is avoided under 6 months for its sedation and apnoea), bilateral motor block, and a bladder that may not empty into a nappy sealed inside a cast. The author's practice is a fascia iliaca block for every age that has one leg injured and a caudal only for the infant in whom the groin will be under plaster and ultrasound access is poor.

A catheter is rarely justified. Rebound is moderate: the peak comes on the first night as spasm, and the answer is the benzodiazepine already charted. The compartment question is smaller than in the forearm or tibia. A femoral or fascia iliaca block does not anaesthetise the calf (sciatic territory), so calf pain in a spica is still reported; a caudal does for a few hours, and the spica cases in the literature presented later than that. In the child with an ipsilateral tibial fracture or a crushed limb, skip the neuraxial option.

Oral regimen shape: paracetamol and ibuprofen regular for five days, an oral opioid for the first two or three nights, diazepam as required for spasm for 48 h. Itch under the cast and constipation from immobility and opioid are the next problems; a laxative goes home with the opioid.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Sedation in the emergency department. Many units apply spicas there under ketamine or nitrous oxide, with reduction and complication rates equal to theatre and less delay. The child who reaches you is the one who is small, unfasted, or failed that attempt.
- The non-walking infant. The injury is treated in a harness or spica, usually without you; your role is the examination under anaesthesia if one happens and an accurate record. Under 6 months halve local anaesthetic infusion rates and cap single doses at ropivacaine 2 mg/kg.
- Bilateral fractures or a fracture plus head injury. Early stabilisation with nails or an external fixator makes nursing and imaging possible; blood loss is then real and from elsewhere.
- Open fractures and external fixation. Cefazolin 30 mg/kg IV (max 2 g) as soon as possible; pin sites hurt for the duration of the frame, as described on the [limb lengthening card](limb-lengthening-guided-growth.html).
- Pathological fractures. Through a unicameral cyst, fibrous dysplasia or osteogenesis imperfecta: low energy, less swelling, same anaesthetic, and sometimes telescoping rods instead of nails.
- Neuromuscular children. Osteopenic bones break during transfers and physiotherapy; these fractures are often managed in a soft cast, and when they need theatre the considerations are those on the [cerebral palsy card](cerebral-palsy-multilevel.html).
- Nail removal. At 6-12 months, a 20-minute day case under a supraglottic airway with local infiltration; occasionally a long struggle if the ends are buried in bone.
- Cast syndrome. Vomiting days after a tight spica from duodenal compression by the superior mesenteric artery; rare, and the reason for the towel.
- Leg length. Overgrowth of about 1 cm after a fracture between 2 and 10 years is expected and is why shortening is tolerated; it has no anaesthetic consequence.

## Key sources

The haemodynamic and abuse-prevalence statements rest on retrospective cohorts and a systematic review that are consistent with each other. The fascia iliaca trial is small and unblinded but is the only randomised analgesic evidence in this injury; there is no trial of caudal against peripheral block for spica application, and the choice between them here is the author's judgement. Treatment-by-age thresholds vary between surgeons by a year or 5 kg either way. General paediatric considerations are in [the child having orthopaedic surgery](../a/a15-the-child.html).
