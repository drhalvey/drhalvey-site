## What the surgeon is doing and why

An ankle fracture and a pilon fracture both break the distal tibia and fibula at the joint, and they are different operations because of the direction of the force. The malleolar fracture is a rotational injury: the talus twists in the mortise, the fibula breaks at or above the joint line, the medial malleolus or the deltoid ligament fails, and sometimes the posterior malleolus and the syndesmosis go too. The joint surface of the tibia is intact; the mortise is simply unstable, and the surgeon's job is to put the talus back under the tibia and hold it there with a fibular plate, a medial screw or two, a posterior malleolar screw if the fragment is large, and a syndesmosis screw or suture-button if the fibula will not stay reduced. The soft tissues are bruised but the envelope is usually intact, and the operation is a day case in a fit patient.

The pilon is an axial injury: a fall from a height or a foot on a brake pedal drives the talus up into the tibial plafond and shatters it. The articular surface is impacted and comminuted (Ruedi-Allgower III), the metaphysis is crushed, the fibula is usually broken, and the skin over the front of the ankle, with the thinnest envelope in the lower limb, has taken the same energy. Early open reduction through that skin produced wound breakdown, deep infection and amputation at rates the surgeons of the 1990s called unacceptable, and the answer was staging: a spanning external fixator and a fibular plate on day 0 to restore length and let the swelling go, a CT once the fracture is out to length, and definitive fixation at 7-14 days when the skin wrinkles when pinched and the fracture blisters have healed. The definitive operation is then an anteromedial or anterolateral approach chosen from the CT exit of the fracture line, elevation of the impacted articular fragments, bone graft into the metaphyseal void, and a plate; the worst fractures get a circular frame instead and a year of rehabilitation.

```
   rotational injury                    axial injury
   (talus twists in mortise)            (talus driven into plafond)
          |                                     |
   malleolar fracture                    pilon fracture
   joint surface intact                  joint surface impacted
   envelope bruised                      envelope injured
          |                                     |
   ORIF when swelling allows             day 0: spanning fixator
   (same day to 7 days),                        +/- fibular plate
   day case, block as anaesthetic        day 7-14: CT, wrinkle sign,
                                                 ORIF or frame
```

The pain logic is bone and periosteum under a thin skin, and the block question dominates. The fibular plate sits on subcutaneous bone with a strip of periosteum lifted; the medial malleolar screws cross a fracture that is now compressed; the pilon adds an elevated, grafted articular surface and a metaphysis full of bone substitute. All of this is well covered by a popliteal sciatic block with a saphenous block for the medial skin, which is why ankle surgery is the lower-limb operation where the block is the whole anaesthetic. The cost is the rebound: in the AnAnkle trial, blocks beat spinal anaesthesia for pain and opioid use over 27 h and 99% of block patients would choose them again, but the authors noted intense pain as the blocks wore off; in the trial that tested dexamethasone for exactly this, rebound pain affected 44% of ankle ORIF patients with a plain block and 15% with intravenous dexamethasone, lasting about 3 h either way. The Gerbershagen cohort, in which blocks were rarely used, ranked open reduction of the calcaneus the single most painful procedure of 179 and foot arthrodesis seventh; the malleolar ankle is not in that company, but the pilon and the high-energy hindfoot are its neighbours. The score is 3 for the malleolar ankle: a block is expected, opioids are needed on the first two days, and the pain falls quickly because the mechanism is bone. The pilon sits a point higher on duration and the second operation, and the card says so where it matters.

## Steps

### 1. Presentation: reduce, splint, and decide the day
**Why:** A dislocated ankle is reduced in the emergency department before the radiograph, because the skin over the medial malleolus tents and dies within hours; a swollen ankle is elevated in a backslab and fixed within a day if the skin allows or at 5-10 days if it does not. A pilon gets a CT after the spanning fixator, not before, because the fracture is only readable at length.
**Anaesthesia:** The reduction in the department is a sedation or a block; a popliteal block for a fracture-dislocation is the kindest reduction and does not hide a compartment syndrome in a malleolar injury, which is not the at-risk pattern the 2021 guideline describes. The fasting state is counted from the injury for the same-day case. The decision between day-of-injury surgery and a delay belongs to the surgeon's reading of the skin; for the anaesthetist the delayed case is the easier one, fasted and on a planned list, and the day-case plan applies to it.

### 2. Block, airway and the night plan
**Why:** The surgeon needs a still foot, a bloodless field under tourniquet, and a patient who will go home that evening with the leg elevated.
**Anaesthesia:** A popliteal sciatic block (20-30 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% or 0.75%) and a saphenous block in the adductor canal (10 mL) with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, under sedation or a light general anaesthetic with a supraglottic airway; the block is the anaesthetic, and a general anaesthetic that is only there to stop the patient feeling the tourniquet after 45 min is a small one. The saphenous block is not optional: the medial malleolar incision and the skin over the medial ankle are saphenous territory, and a block that misses it hurts on the medial side the moment the surgeon starts there. Spinal anaesthesia is a legitimate alternative, inferior in the trial for the first 27 h and the wrong choice for a day case. Cefazolin 2 g IV before the tourniquet goes up. The written night plan (what to take at bedtime, what to take when the tingling starts, what the opioid is for) is written now and handed over in recovery; the trial data say the rebound comes in the small hours in a third to a half of patients without dexamethasone.

### 3. Positioning and the tourniquet
**Why:** Supine with a bump under the ipsilateral buttock internally rotates the leg so the lateral malleolus faces up; the bump comes out for the medial side; a large posterior malleolar fragment needs a posterolateral approach with the patient prone or lateral, or a figure-of-four position. A thigh tourniquet at 250-300 mmHg.
**Anaesthesia:** The prone posterior malleolus is a different anaesthetic: a tracheal tube, a turn, pressure areas, and a case that cannot be a day case in an older patient. Tourniquet time for a trimalleolar fixation with a syndesmosis runs to 90 min, and the tourniquet pain that a popliteal block does not cover (the thigh cuff is above it) is what the sedation or general anaesthetic is for; see [the tourniquet](../a/a01-tourniquet.html). The swollen leg with fracture blisters, the diabetic foot and the leg with peripheral vascular disease are the ones the surgeon may not tourniquet, and the field bleeds instead.

### 4. Lateral malleolus: fibular plate
**Why:** Through a lateral incision the surgeon reduces the fibular fracture to length and rotation, because the fibula is the key to the mortise, and fixes it with a lag screw and a neutralisation plate, or a locking plate in osteoporotic bone, or a posterolateral antiglide plate. The superficial peroneal nerve crosses the field above the joint line and the sural nerve lies behind it.
**Anaesthesia:** The plate on subcutaneous bone with a strip of periosteum lifted is the periosteal mechanism and the pain the patient feels on day 1-2; there is no deep muscle to dissect. Nothing to do at this step but note the tourniquet time.

### 5. Medial malleolus and deltoid
**Why:** The medial malleolus is reduced through a small medial incision and fixed with two partially threaded screws or a tension band; a deltoid ligament rupture with an intact medial malleolus is usually left to heal once the fibula is out to length. The saphenous vein and nerve run across the incision.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the saphenous territory, and the step that tells you whether the adductor canal block worked: a patient under sedation who flinches as the medial incision is made has a popliteal block and no saphenous block.

### 6. Posterior malleolus and syndesmosis
**Why:** A posterior malleolar fragment over a quarter of the joint surface, or one that leaves the talus subluxed, is fixed from the front with screws or from behind through a posterolateral approach with a buttress plate. The syndesmosis is then stress-tested under the image intensifier (a hook test or external rotation); if the fibula moves, it is reduced by hand with the foot in neutral and held with one or two screws across both bones or a suture-button.
**Anaesthesia:** The syndesmosis reduction needs the foot held still and in neutral while the surgeon images and places the screw; a patient who moves under light sedation here costs a malreduction. The stress test and the imaging add 15-30 min to a case that felt finished.

### 7. Closure, splint and emergence
**Why:** Layered closure without tension over the fibular plate, a well-padded backslab or boot with the ankle at neutral, and elevation; the surgeon wants the foot above the heart for 48 h and non-weight-bearing for 6 weeks.
**Anaesthesia:** Tourniquet release before closure is the surgeon's call and the moment the field starts to bleed; the reperfusion changes are modest at 60-90 min. Emergence is unremarkable; the foot is numb. The splint goes on before the patient lightens, because a patient with a dense sciatic block will otherwise let the foot drop off the trolley without noticing.

### 8. Recovery, discharge and the first night
**Why:** The fixation is stable from the moment the screws are in; the surgeon wants elevation, the splint untouched and a clinic review at two weeks.
**Anaesthesia:** The first paracetamol and NSAID dose goes in before discharge. The patient goes home with a numb, insensate foot, crutches, a written night plan and someone at home; the discharge criteria are the same as for any day case, and a working block is not a reason to keep the bed. The rebound comes 12-24 h after the block with dexamethasone and 8-12 h without, usually at night, rises over an hour to its peak, lasts about 3 h and responds to the opioid that the patient should already have taken; the trial data make the dexamethasone and the plan the two interventions that decide whether the patient telephones. Day 1-2 is the peak; by day 3 the regular regimen is enough and the opioid is stopped.

### 9. The pilon on day 0: spanning fixator and fibular plate
**Why:** Two pins in the tibial shaft and one or two in the calcaneus or the talus, connected by bars across the ankle, pull the fracture out to length by ligamentotaxis; many surgeons plate the fibula at the same sitting through the lateral skin, which is usually the least injured side, to set the length. A CT follows.
**Anaesthesia:** A 30-60 min general anaesthetic, often at night, in a patient who may have fallen from a height and has a spine and a calcaneus to think about. A popliteal block is reasonable here (the pilon is not the compartment syndrome pattern that the tibial shaft is), but a short one, because the leg will be examined for the next 48 h. Analgesia for the fortnight in the frame is oral and the leg is elevated.

### 10. The pilon at 7-14 days: definitive fixation
**Why:** When the skin wrinkles and the blisters have epithelialised, the surgeon opens the ankle through the approach the CT dictates (anteromedial for a medially exiting fracture line, anterolateral for a lateral exit), reconstructs the joint surface from the key fragment outwards, elevates the impacted segments, fills the metaphyseal void with autograft, allograft or substitute, and applies a medial or anterolateral plate. The most comminuted fractures get a circular frame with fine wires instead, and a long rehabilitation.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the long, elective operation on this card and the one where the catheter has the strongest claim: 2-4 h, a tourniquet to its limit and released, a bone graft (an iliac crest donor site if autograft is used, which needs its own analgesia), and a patient who will be in more pain for longer than the malleolar patient and is not going home that night. A popliteal catheter with a dilute infusion, a saphenous single shot and the usual regimen is the author's preference; the compartment window of the first 48 h has passed. The haemoglobin after tourniquet release in a long case is worth checking in an older patient.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is bone and periosteum under thin skin, fully covered by a popliteal sciatic plus saphenous block, so the plan is built around the block and its rebound. There is no PROSPECT guideline for ankle fracture surgery; the trial evidence is good. AnAnkle randomised 150 ankle fracture patients to popliteal sciatic plus saphenous blocks or spinal anaesthesia, all with paracetamol, ibuprofen and PCA morphine, and found the blocks superior for pain and opioid use over 27 h, with the caveat that the pain as the block wore off was substantial and sometimes intense. The dexamethasone trial randomised 130 bimalleolar and trimalleolar ORIFs with the same blocks to intravenous dexamethasone or saline: rebound pain 15% against 44%, the block 8 h longer, the first analgesic request at 26 h rather than 17 h. The ambulatory rebound cohort of 972 patients adds the risk factors: bone surgery, younger age, female sex, and no intravenous dexamethasone.

The defensible default for malleolar ORIF is therefore a single-shot popliteal sciatic block and a saphenous block with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, under sedation or a light general anaesthetic, regular paracetamol and an NSAID started before discharge, oral opioid for the first two nights, and a written night plan that tells the patient to take the bedtime dose whether or not the foot hurts and the next dose when the tingling starts. The catheter is the right call for the pilon's definitive fixation, for the opioid-tolerant or chronic pain patient, for the patient with a previous catastrophic rebound night, and for the unit that admits its ankle fractures and can run an infusion; it is not needed for the malleolar day case and adds a dislodgement and a leaking dressing to a patient going home on crutches.

Escalation triggers: pain above 7 when the block has worn off, with the splint intact and the toes pink and moving, is rebound and is treated with the opioid already charted; pain that is severe inside the splint with a foot that is cold, pale or paraesthetic is a tight cast or a vascular problem and the splint is split; pain that persists beyond day 3 with a hot ankle is an infection. The malleolar ankle is not a compartment syndrome injury in the way the tibial shaft is, and the 2021 guideline's caution about dense blocks is aimed at the shaft and the high-energy proximal tibia, not at this card.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The elderly, osteoporotic, diabetic ankle. The fibular plate does not hold, the skin does not heal, and the surgeon may choose a fibular nail, a hindfoot nail through the calcaneus and talus, or a cast. A diabetic neuropathic foot (Charcot) is a different disease with a different operation and a long cast. Blocks are still the right anaesthetic.
- The open ankle fracture. Common because the medial skin is thin: antibiotics within an hour, debridement, often primary fixation if the wound is clean, and the [open fracture card](open-fractures-damage-control.html).
- The fracture-dislocation with skin at risk. Reduced in the department before anything else; a block in the department is the kindest way to do it.
- The posterior malleolus done prone. A tracheal tube, a prone turn with a fractured ankle, and no day case.
- The syndesmosis screw that will not hold the foot in neutral. A malreduced syndesmosis is the commonest technical error in ankle fixation and the surgeon's concern; for you it means a still foot during the imaging and a case that runs 20 min longer than the booking.
- The calcaneus in the same patient. A fall from a height that produces a pilon often produces a calcaneal fracture or a spine fracture; the calcaneus is the most painful operation in the Gerbershagen cohort and has its own staging and its own catheter decision.
- Day case criteria. A dense popliteal block is not a reason to delay discharge, but the patient needs crutches they can use, elevation at home, the night plan, the dexamethasone already given, and a telephone number; a patient who lives alone and cannot manage stairs stays the night.
- Rebound in the opioid-tolerant patient. The worst phenotype for this operation: a catheter or an admission with a planned regimen, not a single shot and a prescription.
- The patient who refuses the block. A general anaesthetic with surgeon's infiltration and a spinal both work; the first night is worse and the opioid requirement doubles, which the trial data make explicit.

## Key sources

The analgesia evidence for ankle fracture surgery is better than for most trauma operations because the operation is common, short and standardised. AnAnkle is the randomised comparison of blocks against spinal anaesthesia; the dexamethasone trial is the randomised answer to the rebound problem the AnAnkle authors flagged; the ambulatory rebound cohort gives the risk factors. The pilon evidence is surgical and observational, and the staging protocol rests on the wound complication history rather than a trial. The Gerbershagen cohort places the hindfoot and foot arthrodesis among the most painful operations in surgery, with the caveat that few of those patients had a block; it is cited here as context for the high-energy foot rather than as a ranking of the malleolar ankle, which it did not list. The catheter advice is the author's judgement.
