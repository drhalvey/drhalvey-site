## What the surgeon is doing and why

Two different diseases share this card because they share a hip, a table and a list, and little else.

Slipped upper femoral epiphysis is a failure of the growth plate under shear. In early adolescence (girls 10-13 years, boys 12-15) the proximal femoral physis is at its thickest and weakest, and in a heavy child body weight shears the neck forwards and outwards off the head, which stays in the socket. The risk rises steeply with body mass: 17-fold with severe obesity at age 11-12 in the Scottish cohort. A slip in a thin child, or one under 10 or over 16 years, suggests an endocrine cause (hypothyroidism, growth hormone deficiency or treatment, renal osteodystrophy, panhypopituitarism). The blood supply of the head runs in retinacular vessels on the back of the neck, stretched over the step of the slip. Everything the surgeon does is governed by not kinking or tearing them.

The classification that matters is functional. A child who can walk, even with crutches, has a stable slip: avascular necrosis is close to nil. A child who cannot bear weight at all has an unstable slip, which behaves like a displaced fracture through the physis, with avascular necrosis in a quarter to a half. The operation for both is the same in principle: accept the position, and stop it getting worse by passing one large cannulated screw from the anterolateral cortex up the neck, perpendicular across the physis, into the centre of the head. No reduction is attempted for a stable slip. For an unstable one the gentle repositioning that happens when the leg is laid on the table is accepted, a forceful manipulation is not, and many surgeons decompress the joint haematoma. Severe slips are increasingly treated by open subcapital realignment through a surgical hip dislocation (the modified Dunn procedure), which restores anatomy at the cost of a 3-4 hour operation and its own osteonecrosis rate of about one in four in the multicentre series.

Perthes disease is the other end of childhood (4-8 years, range 2-12, four boys to each girl): idiopathic loss of blood supply to the femoral head, which softens, collapses and re-forms over two to four years. The surgical idea is containment. A soft head moulded inside a round socket re-forms round; a head that extrudes laterally re-forms flat. So the surgeon either redirects the head into the socket (proximal femoral varus osteotomy with a blade plate) or the socket over the head (Salter innominate osteotomy or a shelf), usually in the child over 6-8 years with significant head involvement, and uses arthrograms, adductor tenotomy and abduction casts for the stiff irritable hip.

Pain logic. In situ pinning is the rare orthopaedic operation that hurts less than the disease: a stab incision and a drill track, bone, score 2, done in three days. The open operations are bone plus muscle plus capsule and score 4, and the reasoning for them is on the [developmental dysplasia](ddh-pelvic-osteotomy.html) and [periacetabular osteotomy](periacetabular-osteotomy.html) cards.

## Steps

### 1. Stable or unstable: the urgency
**Why:** A stable slip is pinned on the next available list, with the child admitted on crutches or bed rest so it cannot convert. An unstable slip is treated as urgent by most surgeons, on the reasoning that a tense haemarthrosis and kinked vessels are time-dependent.
**Anaesthesia:** The timing evidence does not give a clean rule: pooled avascular necrosis was 22% with surgery inside 24 hours, 42% between 24 hours and 7 days and 18% after a week, none of the differences significant. Surgeons read that as "inside 24 hours or wait", so the unstable slip arrives as an urgent case that should be done by a team that does them, in daylight if that falls inside the window. The request to you is not speed at all costs but a gentle transfer: the child moves themselves across where possible, the leg is supported in the position it lies in (flexed and externally rotated), and nobody straightens it. A suprainguinal fascia iliaca block before the transfer (ropivacaine 0.2% 0.5 mL/kg, max 30 mL) is kind and stops the muscle guarding that makes the move forceful.

### 2. Assessing the obese adolescent
**Why:** The typical patient is above the 95th centile for weight, which is the cause of the slip and of most of your problems.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask about snoring, witnessed apnoea, daytime sleepiness, exercise tolerance before the limp, and reflux. Measure blood pressure with a cuff that fits, and glucose: type 2 diabetes and fatty liver are common and usually undiagnosed. If the child is thin, short, under 10 or over 16 years, thyroid function, renal function and a paediatric opinion are due, but not before an unstable slip is fixed. Hypothyroidism severe enough to matter under anaesthesia (bradycardia, slow emergence, hypothermia) is uncommon and is found by looking. Dose by the right weight: induction agents, opioids, non-depolarising relaxants, paracetamol (15 mg/kg, max 1 g) and local anaesthetics on lean or ideal body weight; suxamethonium, sugammadex and cefazolin on total body weight up to the adult maximum.

### 3. Induction and airway
**Why:** The surgeon needs a still pelvis for a guidewire that has about 5 mm of tolerance in the centre of the head.
**Anaesthesia:** For the slim child with a stable slip a supraglottic airway is fine. For the obese adolescent on a traction table the author's default is a tracheal tube: the abdomen splints the diaphragm supine, access to the head is limited once the image intensifier is in, and reflux is frequent. Pre-oxygenate head-up, induce on the bed or the table top with the leg supported by an assistant, and have a second pair of hands for mask ventilation. Venous access can be the hardest part of the case; ultrasound it early. Spinal anaesthesia is an option in the mature adolescent, but sitting or turning with an unstable slip is the very movement to avoid.

### 4. Positioning: a traction table used without traction
**Why:** The traction table holds the leg still (in neutral or slight internal rotation for a stable slip, in the position it lies for an unstable one) and lets the image intensifier swing between anteroposterior and lateral views without moving the hip. The surgeon does not want longitudinal pull, which can tear the retinacular vessels of an unstable slip. On a flat radiolucent table the lateral view is obtained by frog-legging the hip, acceptable for a stable slip and forbidden for an unstable one.
**Anaesthesia:** Watch the set-up. The perineal post presses on a large perineum, the well leg is abducted and flexed in a holder (check the common peroneal nerve and the calf), and the boot is applied to a foot that should be resting, not pulled. A 100 kg adolescent on a narrow traction table needs the arms secured across the chest or out on boards with care for the brachial plexus, and the table's weight limit known. The image intensifier coming in over the pelvis is the last moment of access to the abdomen and groin.

### 5. Guidewire and screw
**Why:** Because the head has slipped backwards relative to the neck, the correct screw starts on the anterior surface of the neck or the anterolateral cortex and aims posteriorly, to end perpendicular to the physis in the centre of the head and short of the joint. A screw that penetrates the joint causes chondrolysis; one in the superolateral head endangers the vessels. The wire is placed and checked in two planes, then drilled over and the screw inserted through a 1 cm incision.
**Anaesthesia:** Stimulation is brief: skin, fascia lata, a drill through cortex. Fentanyl 1 microg/kg IV, paracetamol and infiltration by the surgeon cover it. Ask for the local anaesthetic to be placed down to periosteum along the track before the drill, not into the skin at the end. Screening time is longer than the surgery in a heavy patient, and image quality through a large thigh is the usual cause of delay.

### 6. Capsular decompression in the unstable slip
**Why:** An acute slip bleeds into an intact capsule, and intracapsular pressure can exceed the perfusion pressure of the retinacular vessels. Some surgeons aspirate the joint with a wide needle or make a small anterior capsulotomy after fixation; others do not.
**Anaesthesia:** No physiological consequence, but it is a reason not to run the blood pressure low: perfusion of the head depends on the gradient between arterial pressure and a tense joint. Keep the mean pressure at the awake value throughout an unstable slip. It is an easy, costless contribution to the one outcome that matters.

### 7. Pinning the other hip
**Why:** The second hip slips later in a substantial minority, more often in the young child, the endocrine case (61% bilateral in one series) and where follow-up is unreliable. The decision analyses disagree: one favours prophylactic pinning on long-term outcome, the other favours observation unless the risk of a second slip exceeds 27%. Surgeons therefore vary, and the plan may be made on the day.
**Anaesthesia:** A second screw adds a re-position, a re-drape and 30-40 minutes, and a second infiltration that shares the local anaesthetic budget: ropivacaine 3 mg/kg of lean body weight in total across both sides and any block. Ask before induction whether both hips are being done, because it changes the airway choice in a borderline patient and the day-case plan.

### 8. Open realignment by surgical dislocation
**Why:** For a severe or unstable slip some surgeons restore the anatomy. Lateral position, trochanteric flip osteotomy to lift the abductors forwards, Z-capsulotomy, dislocation of the hip, elevation of a retinacular flap carrying the vessels off the neck, removal of callus, reduction of the head onto a shortened neck without tension on the flap, screw or wire fixation, and re-attachment of the trochanter.
**Anaesthesia:** This is a different anaesthetic: 3-4 hours in the lateral position, an arterial line in the high-risk adolescent, 300-800 mL of blood loss, tranexamic acid 15 mg/kg IV (max 1 g), active warming and a urinary catheter. The surgeon checks head perfusion by drilling it and watching for bleeding, sometimes with a pressure probe: again a mean arterial pressure at the awake value, not controlled hypotension. Analgesia is an epidural or a fascia iliaca or lumbar plexus catheter; see the pain plan.

### 9. Perthes: arthrogram, tenotomy and containment osteotomy
**Why:** An arthrogram under anaesthesia shows whether the soft head is round and whether it sits in the socket in abduction. A stiff hip gets an adductor tenotomy and abduction casts (broomstick or Petrie casts) for six weeks. Containment surgery is a proximal femoral varus osteotomy fixed with a blade plate, a Salter innominate osteotomy, or a shelf.
**Anaesthesia:** A 20-30 kg child, usually well, small for age and active. The arthrogram and casts are a supraglottic airway, a caudal (ropivacaine 0.2% 1 mL/kg, max 20 mL) and 30 minutes; allow the casts to set before waking. The osteotomies run 90-120 minutes with 5-10 mL/kg of blood loss, enough to need a second cannula and a known starting haemoglobin in a 20 kg child whose blood volume is 1.5 L. The steps and the spica that follows are described on the [developmental dysplasia card](ddh-pelvic-osteotomy.html).

### 10. Emergence and afterwards
**Why:** After a stable slip the surgeon allows touch or partial weight-bearing on crutches; after an unstable slip or realignment, non-weight-bearing for six to eight weeks. The physis fuses over months and the screw usually stays.
**Anaesthesia:** Extubate the obese adolescent awake and sitting up, and decide in recovery, not on the booking form, whether sleep apnoea and opioid make this an overnight stay. Regular paracetamol 15 mg/kg (max 1 g) every 6 h and ibuprofen 10 mg/kg (max 400 mg) every 8 h, with oxycodone 0.1 mg/kg of ideal body weight orally (max 5-10 mg) as required for two or three nights. A heavy teenager on crutches for the first time is a falls risk on a slip that is held by one screw; physiotherapy clearance is part of discharge.

## Pain plan

For in situ pinning the mechanism is a drill track through bone and a 1 cm incision, score 2, and the plan is infiltration by the surgeon down to periosteum (ropivacaine 0.2% up to 0.5 mL/kg per side, max 20 mL per side), paracetamol, an NSAID, dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV (max 8 mg) and a small opioid dose. A fascia iliaca block adds a quiet transfer for the unstable slip and several hours of comfort; it is not needed for the stable slip and its quadriceps weakness is unwelcome in a heavy child learning crutches. Rebound is low because there is little to rebound from. Pain that is worse on day 2 than day 0, or groin pain with a locked, stiff hip in the following weeks, is screw penetration, further slip or early chondrolysis and goes back to the surgeon.

For open realignment and for Perthes osteotomies the mechanism is the full hip set: osteotomy surfaces, a capsulotomy, abductors lifted on the trochanter or adductors divided, and muscle spasm across a cut bone. Score 4, worst on days 1 and 2. A continuous technique for 48 hours is the defensible default. In the small Perthes child that is a caudal or lumbar epidural catheter (ropivacaine 0.1-0.2% at 0.2-0.4 mg/kg/h); in the adolescent, a lumbar epidural at the same hourly ceiling or a suprainguinal fascia iliaca catheter at up to 0.3 mg/kg/h, capped at adult rates over 50 kg. The only paediatric comparison, retrospective and small, found pain scores about two points lower over 24 hours with caudal epidural analgesia than with lumbar plexus block and no difference in opioid use, so the choice is driven by the bladder, the cast and the ward rather than by efficacy. Add diazepam 0.1 mg/kg orally (max 5 mg) every 8 h as required for adductor and quadriceps spasm, which opioids do not treat.

The obese adolescent with sleep apnoea is the patient in whom an opioid-sparing plan matters most, and the reason to push infiltration, regular non-opioids and, for open surgery, a regional catheter, with any opioid dosed on ideal body weight and oximetry overnight.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The missed diagnosis. Knee or thigh pain with a normal knee in an overweight 12-year-old is a slip; some present for a knee arthroscopy list.
- Unstable slip handling. No traction, no internal rotation to "improve the view", no frog-leg lateral. If the image is poor, the image intensifier moves, not the leg.
- Screw penetration. Checked by screening through a range of rotation at the end; a child with unexpectedly severe pain in recovery after a routine pinning needs that question asked.
- Endocrine slips. Hypothyroid patients have often not been diagnosed; growth hormone and renal patients are known. Renal osteodystrophy brings its own list: potassium, fluid state, access and bone that does not hold a screw well.
- Bilateral simultaneous slips. Both hips unstable is rare and is two urgent operations in one sitting; one fascia iliaca block per side with the dose split.
- Late deformity surgery. Intertrochanteric flexion osteotomy and open or arthroscopic osteochondroplasty for the healed slip that impinges: elective, adolescent or young adult, planned as [hip arthroscopy](hip-arthroscopy.html) or as an osteotomy with a block.
- Screw removal. Usually left in; removal years later can be long and bloody because bone has overgrown the head of the screw.
- Perthes casts. Two long-leg casts joined by a bar: the child cannot roll, so recover them on the side they were cast and send them to the ward awake.
- Avascular necrosis. Declares itself at 3-12 months with a painful stiff hip; these adolescents return for realignment, joint distraction or, eventually, arthroplasty.

## Key sources

Stability as the predictor of avascular necrosis rests on a 55-hip series that has been repeatedly confirmed in direction if not in size. The timing meta-analysis is inconclusive by its own account, the two decision analyses on prophylactic pinning reach opposite conclusions from the same literature, and the modified Dunn data are a small multicentre series. There are no trials of analgesic technique for pinning in situ; the plan here is the author's judgement from the mechanism. Obesity dosing rules are consensus tier.
