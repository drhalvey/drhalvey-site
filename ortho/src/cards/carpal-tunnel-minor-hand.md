## What the surgeon is doing and why

These are the operations that fill a hand list and that, in most of the world, now happen without an anaesthetist in the room. They are grouped here because they share a mechanism (a structure divided or excised, nothing repaired), a pain score (the Part B anchor for a 1 is carpal tunnel release), and an anaesthetic default that has changed in the last fifteen years. The card exists so that the anaesthetist who is asked to be involved knows what the surgeon is doing with the adrenaline, when the WALANT default breaks, and why the one thing that goes wrong is the discharge analgesia.

Carpal tunnel syndrome is the median nerve compressed under the transverse carpal ligament, a thick band between the hook of the hamate and the pisiform on one side and the scaphoid tubercle and trapezium on the other. The surgeon divides the ligament along its ulnar side (to stay away from the recurrent motor branch, which usually comes off the radial side of the nerve), either through a 2-3 cm palmar incision or endoscopically through a portal at the wrist crease, and that is the operation; the nerve is not touched, the tunnel springs open, and the numbness of a recently compressed nerve often improves overnight. A nerve that has been compressed for years with thenar wasting does not recover its muscle and may keep its numbness, and the patient needs to know that beforehand. The palmar cutaneous branch of the median nerve and the superficial palmar arch are the structures the surgeon avoids; pillar pain (an ache at the heel of the hand on either side of the incision, from the divided ligament and the shifted carpal arch) is what the patient complains of at six weeks and is the only pain that outlasts the first days.

Trigger finger is a flexor tendon with a nodule that catches on the A1 pulley at the base of the finger. The surgeon makes a 1 cm incision over the pulley, divides it longitudinally, and asks the patient to flex and extend the finger to confirm it no longer catches, which is the reason the patient is awake: the test is the operation's end point. The digital nerves run either side of the pulley and are a few millimetres from the blade; the radial digital nerve of the thumb crosses the pulley and is the one that gets cut.

De Quervain's is the abductor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis brevis tendons inflamed in the first dorsal compartment at the radial styloid. The surgeon opens the compartment's roof and, because the extensor pollicis brevis often lies in its own sub-compartment, looks for and opens that too, with the superficial radial nerve branches lying directly in the subcutaneous fat over the incision and giving the painful neuroma if cut.

A ganglion is a mucin-filled cyst arising from a joint capsule or tendon sheath, most often the dorsal scapholunate ligament of the wrist. Excision is the only one of these operations with a dissection: the cyst is traced to its stalk and the stalk excised with a cuff of capsule, which means the wrist joint is opened and a little periosteum disturbed; a volar wrist ganglion sits against the radial artery and takes longer. Recurrence is common and aspiration is a reasonable first treatment; surgeons vary on both.

WALANT (wide awake local anaesthesia, no tourniquet) is the surgeon's infiltration of lidocaine with adrenaline, buffered with bicarbonate so it does not sting, in volumes of 20 mL for a carpal tunnel release and 4 mL for a trigger finger, injected at least 25-30 min before the incision so that the adrenaline has produced a dry field. The adrenaline is what removes the tourniquet, and the tourniquet is the only part of a 15 min hand operation that an awake patient cannot tolerate. The old prohibition on adrenaline in the fingers came from early procaine and cocaine preparations; the modern data are a prospective project of 3,110 consecutive cases of adrenaline in the fingers and hand with no digital infarction, and the reported rare ischaemic events involve either a patient with an undiagnosed vasospastic or occlusive disease (one in about 5,000 in a single surgeon's series, in a patient with severe Raynaud's and occluded ulnar arteries who also had a digital tourniquet) or an accidental high-dose injection, and phentolamine infiltration reverses the vasoconstriction. The surgeon's screening question is whether the patient gets cold, pale fingertips; if yes, plain local anaesthetic is used. The technique needs no fasting, no sedation, no cannula, no anaesthetist and no recovery bay, doubles throughput at a quarter of the cost, and lets the surgeon test a tendon or a trigger with the patient's own muscles. It is the default for every operation on this card.

The pain logic is surface: a short incision, a ligament or pulley divided, no bone except the stalk of a ganglion, nothing tensioned, and a pre-operative pain generator removed. The score is a 1 and the trajectory peaks at a 3 on day 1 with simple analgesia. The only reason to write a pain plan at all is the finding in the 179-procedure cohort that patients after minor operations typically received little or no opioid yet reported high pain scores on day 1, with small hand and foot operations ranking among the most painful of all, and the plan on this card is what prevents that: a non-opioid regimen started before the local anaesthetic wears off, and a small opioid supply for the few.

## Steps

### 1. Where this happens and who is in the room
**Why:** WALANT hand surgery is done in a procedure room or an office, with the patient walking in and out, in most units; the main theatre and an anaesthetist are kept for the patient who needs a block, sedation or a general anaesthetic.
**Anaesthesia:** If you are involved, the question is why. The reasons are an anxious patient who wants sedation, a patient who cannot lie still or communicate (dementia, a tremor, a child), a surgeon who uses a tourniquet and wants a block or a Bier's block for it, a combined or bilateral list, or a list in a hospital whose rules put an anaesthetist in every theatre. For the sedated patient the aim is a patient who is calm and can still move the finger on request for the trigger finger test; for the patient who needs a general anaesthetic a supraglottic airway with spontaneous breathing is the whole plan. No fasting is needed for WALANT alone; sedation brings the fasting rules back.

### 2. Surgeon infiltration and the 25-30 min wait
**Why:** Lidocaine 1% with adrenaline 1:100,000, buffered 10:1 with 8.4% bicarbonate and warmed, is injected slowly through a fine needle, proximal to distal, 20 mL for a carpal tunnel release (10 mL between the median and ulnar nerves and 10 mL under the incision line), 4 mL for a trigger finger, 2 mL for a digital block; then the surgeon waits for the field to blanch, which takes 25-30 min, and the next patient is injected in the meantime.
**Anaesthesia:** The dose is well within the 7 mg/kg that the technique's authors stay under, and for a trigger finger it is trivial. The wait is the step that gets skipped in a hurry, and a field that is injected and cut at 10 min bleeds. The first-dose paracetamol and NSAID can go in during this wait, which is the simplest way to make the discharge analgesia start on time.

### 3. The carpal tunnel: open or endoscopic
**Why:** Open: a 2-3 cm incision in line with the ring finger, through the palmar fascia, the transverse carpal ligament divided under direct vision along its ulnar border from distal to proximal, with care at the superficial palmar arch distally and the nerve's recurrent motor branch radially. Endoscopic: a 1 cm portal at the wrist crease, a cannula passed under the ligament, and the ligament cut from beneath with a blade on the endoscope, which spares the palmar skin and gives a quicker return to work at the cost of a blind cut.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient feels pushing in the palm and nothing else if the infiltration was adequate; a patient who flinches when the ligament is divided had too little volume in the tunnel, and the surgeon tops it up through the wound. Endoscopic release in an awake patient is tolerable and common. There is no tourniquet pain, no sedation and no airway, and the operation is 10-15 min.

### 4. The trigger finger and the awake test
**Why:** A 1 cm incision over the metacarpal head, the A1 pulley exposed between the neurovascular bundles and divided along its length, and then the patient is asked to make a fist and open the hand while the surgeon watches the tendon glide. If it still catches, a little more pulley is divided; if the patient cannot flex, the surgeon looks for a reason.
**Anaesthesia:** The test is why this is done awake and why a brachial plexus block or a general anaesthetic is the wrong default: a paralysed hand cannot show the surgeon that the trigger is gone. The digital nerve is millimetres from the blade and a numb fingertip the next day is a cut nerve, not a residual local anaesthetic; the volume used is too small to last.

### 5. De Quervain's and the superficial radial nerve
**Why:** A short transverse or longitudinal incision over the radial styloid, the superficial radial nerve branches found in the fat and held aside, the roof of the first dorsal compartment opened along its dorsal edge (to leave a volar lip that stops the tendons subluxing), and the sub-compartment for extensor pollicis brevis sought and opened. The patient is asked to extend and abduct the thumb to confirm both tendons glide.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing to do; the same awake test as the trigger finger. The painful neuroma of a cut superficial radial nerve branch is the one complication, and it is the surgeon's.

### 6. The ganglion and the opened joint
**Why:** A dorsal ganglion is traced between the extensor tendons to its stalk on the scapholunate ligament and excised with a cuff of capsule, which opens the wrist joint; a volar ganglion is dissected off the radial artery, which lies against it and is sometimes damaged. The capsule is left open or loosely closed and the wrist splinted for a few days by surgeons who think it reduces recurrence.
**Anaesthesia:** The one operation on the card with a real dissection and a joint opened, so the infiltration volume is larger (20-30 mL in the dorsal wrist) and the case runs 20-45 min; a volar ganglion in a patient with a thin radial artery is the one where the surgeon may ask for a tourniquet and a block. The opened capsule and the disturbed periosteum give this the sorest day 1 of the four operations, which is still a 3 on simple analgesia.

### 7. Dressing, hand elevation and the walk out
**Why:** A bulky dressing, the fingers free to move, the hand elevated for two days, and no splint for a carpal tunnel or trigger finger. The patient walks out.
**Anaesthesia:** Under WALANT there is no recovery stay and no discharge criteria beyond a dressing that is not tight and a patient who has eaten. A sedated patient needs the usual escort. The adrenaline field stays pale for an hour or two and the fingers are checked to be pink before the patient leaves; a finger that is white and cold at the end of the case is infiltrated with phentolamine and watched, and this is rare enough that most hand surgeons have never done it.

### 8. The discharge analgesia that is the whole plan
**Why:** The surgeon's interest after a minor hand operation is in the hand moving, the dressing staying dry and the patient not telephoning.
**Anaesthesia:** The local anaesthetic lasts two to four hours, and the patient who was told this was nothing goes home with no plan and finds the hand hurts at 9 pm. Paracetamol and an NSAID, started before the local wears off and continued for two or three days, is the regimen, and the randomised trial after carpal tunnel release found that ten tablets of paracetamol or ibuprofen gave the same or lower pain scores than ten tablets of oxycodone (mean worst daily pain 2.3-2.8 against 2.8-3.4), with patients using three to five tablets in total over five days and most of the adverse events in the oxycodone group. The authors' advice is no more than five to ten tablets of anything. A small supply of an oral opioid for the ganglion patient or the patient who has had a hard time before is the author's practice; a routine opioid prescription for a carpal tunnel or trigger finger release is not.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is surface, the score is a 1, and the honest statement is that the anaesthetic is chosen for the surgeon's needs and the patient's tolerance, not for the pain. WALANT is the default because it removes the tourniquet, the sedation, the fasting, the cannula and the anaesthetist, and because the awake test is part of the operation for a trigger finger and a de Quervain's. There is no PROSPECT guideline for any of these operations and there does not need to be; the randomised evidence after carpal tunnel release says non-opioids are as good as oxycodone and the authors of the trial ask for no more than five to ten tablets of whatever is prescribed.

The defensible default is surgeon infiltration with buffered lidocaine and adrenaline, no sedation, no tourniquet, paracetamol and an NSAID started in the waiting room or during the vasoconstriction wait and continued for two to three days, and a written line telling the patient that the hand will hurt when the numbness goes and that the tablets are for that. For the patient who cannot be awake, a general anaesthetic with a supraglottic airway and the same infiltration; for the surgeon who wants a tourniquet, a Bier's block or a distal nerve block at the forearm, or an axillary block for a bilateral list. A brachial plexus block is never for the pain on this card and should not be sold as such; it is for the anaesthetic, and it costs the patient a numb arm for twelve hours after a 15 min operation.

Rebound pain is not the problem it is after a bone operation under a dense plexus block, because the local anaesthetic wears off in the afternoon with the patient awake and the regimen already started. The escalation triggers are the ones that are not pain: a white, cold finger at the end of a trigger finger release is the adrenaline and is treated with phentolamine; numbness in the distribution of a digital nerve or the palmar cutaneous branch that persists past the local anaesthetic is a cut nerve; pain and swelling out of proportion at a week in a carpal tunnel patient is the start of complex regional pain syndrome, which this group of operations does produce occasionally; and pillar pain at the heel of the hand at six weeks is expected and treated with time.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The patient who gets cold, pale fingers. Raynaud's, scleroderma, Buerger's, a previous digital replant or a known occluded ulnar artery: the surgeon uses plain lidocaine and a tourniquet, and this is the one group in whom the adrenaline prohibition still stands.
- The patient on beta-blockers or with ischaemic heart disease. The adrenaline dose in 20 mL of 1:100,000 is 0.2 mg, infiltrated slowly; a brief tachycardia is common and clinically trivial, and the author's judgement is that it changes nothing.
- The anxious patient. Sedation brings fasting, a cannula, an escort and a recovery stay; a conversation and a screen often do the same job. Where sedation is used, keep it light enough for the awake test.
- The bilateral or combined list. Two carpal tunnels under WALANT are two injections and no extra time; a carpal tunnel with a trapeziectomy is the trapeziectomy's anaesthetic.
- Endoscopic release. Same anaesthetic, a blind cut, and an incomplete release or a cut arch that the surgeon converts to open.
- The tourniquet habit. A surgeon who uses a forearm tourniquet for 10-15 min can still do the operation awake; one who wants it for 30 min needs a Bier's block or a sedated patient.
- Steroid injection before surgery. Many patients have had one or more; it does not change the anaesthetic, and it raises the infection risk a little for a trigger finger release in the weeks afterwards.
- Dementia, tremor and the patient who cannot lie still. The general anaesthetic group, with a supraglottic airway and the same infiltration; the trigger finger test is lost and the surgeon accepts that.
- The diabetic trigger finger. Commoner, more often multiple, and more likely to recur; the anaesthetic is the same and the NSAID question is renal.
- Complex regional pain syndrome. Rare, more after a carpal tunnel release than after the others, and referred early rather than reassured.

## Key sources

The anaesthetic default on this card rests on the WALANT literature, which is large, consistent and mostly descriptive: the instructional lecture that sets out the recipe, volumes and dose ceiling, and the 3,110-case prospective project it cites for the safety of adrenaline in the fingers, against which stands a single surgeon's report of one ischaemic digit in about 5,000 blocks in a patient with undiagnosed vascular disease and a digital tourniquet. The analgesia evidence is a randomised trial after carpal tunnel release showing non-opioids match oxycodone, and the 179-procedure cohort's finding that minor surgery is undertreated. The pain score is the Part B anchor; nothing on the card is sourced beyond that except the numbers quoted, and the sedation, general anaesthetic and tourniquet alternatives are author's judgement.
