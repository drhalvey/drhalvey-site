## What the surgeon is doing and why

A primary hip replacement fails in one of five ways and the way it failed is the operation. A loose cup or stem (aseptic loosening, the commonest) has to be dug out of bone eaten away by wear debris and replaced by something that will hold in what is left. An unstable hip has a malpositioned component, a worn liner, a weak abductor mechanism or all three, and the surgeon changes the orientation, the head size, the liner constraint or the whole construct. A periprosthetic fracture around a loose stem needs the stem out and a long stem past the fracture, which is this card with a trauma pathway attached (see [periprosthetic fracture](periprosthetic-fracture.html)). An infected hip has its own card ([periprosthetic joint infection](periprosthetic-joint-infection.html)), but the second-stage reimplantation of a two-stage exchange is a revision in every physical sense and belongs here. And a metal-on-metal bearing with a pseudotumour needs a soft-tissue debridement and a bearing exchange, with the abductors sometimes destroyed by the reaction.

The common problem is that the surgeon is working in scar, through an old approach, against implants that are either loose in bone that has gone or fixed in bone that must be cut to release them, with less bone to build on than the primary surgeon had. The sequence:

1. In through the old scar by the posterior approach, the most extensile; scar dissected off the trochanter, the sciatic nerve (now in scar and possibly displaced) and the thickened capsule; dislocate and assess what is loose and what is fixed.
2. Remove the femoral component. A loose stem comes out with a tap. A fixed uncemented stem or a long cement mantle does not, and the surgeon makes an extended trochanteric osteotomy, a 12-20 cm longitudinal cut hinging a third of the femoral circumference open on its vastus lateralis attachment, so the stem and the cement can be removed under vision; it unites in 98% once cabled back.
3. Remove the acetabular component, classify what is left (Paprosky: contained defects with an intact rim at one end, pelvic discontinuity at the other) and rebuild it with a larger cup and screws, porous metal augments, a cup-cage or antiprotrusio cage, impaction grafting with mesh, or a custom triflange. Each option up that list adds an hour, several hundred millilitres, and screws placed blind into a pelvis with the external iliac and obturator vessels on the far side.
4. Ream the femur for its length to bleeding bone and fix a new stem in the diaphysis below the damage: a modular tapered uncemented stem, a long cemented stem, or impaction grafting with a cemented stem.
5. Trial, reduce, test stability (a constrained liner or a dual-mobility bearing if the abductors are gone), cable the osteotomy, repair what soft tissue there is, close over drains.

The anaesthetic reading: everything the primary card says about reaming, cement and the pelvic clamps is still true, and three things are new. The dissection is long, bloody and unpredictable: 800 mL is a quiet revision and 2 L is not unusual, and in a six-centre cohort on tranexamic acid one revision in six was transfused, predicted by pre-operative haemoglobin, INR and operative hours rather than by the type of revision. The patient is older and frailer than the primary patient was, and may be anaemic, anticoagulated and opioid-tolerant before you meet them. And a long cemented stem in a canal just reamed to bleeding bone for its full length is the highest-risk bone cement implantation syndrome step in elective orthopaedics: hypotension in 38% and desaturation in 25% of one long-stem series, against 2% and 17% in elective primaries.

The pain logic is different in kind. The dominant mechanism is periosteum: a long femoral osteotomy or a stripped canal, cabled back and loaded by every contraction of the abductors hanging off the trochanteric fragment, and a pelvis cut and screwed into. Beneath it are bone (two reamed surfaces), muscle (a re-opened and extended dissection) and, where the abductors have been detached, a repair under tension. The operation has created a fracture-like injury along the femur that the patient feels for a week and that is re-provoked by the partial weight bearing the surgeon prescribes. A 4 on the Part B scale, the only hip arthroplasty card to earn one, and the only one where a catheter is routine rather than selective.

## Steps

### 1. The plan: what is coming out, what is going in, how long, and which anaesthetic
**Why:** The surgeon has planned this from radiographs, a CT and the old operation notes, and knows whether the stem needs an osteotomy, whether the cup needs augments or a cage, whether cement is going in, and roughly how long it will take. None of that is on the theatre list, and the surgeon needs 2-5 hours of a still, relaxed patient on their side and a patient who will tolerate partial weight bearing on a cabled osteotomy from day 1.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask, and plan from the answer. A liner exchange is 90 minutes and 300 mL; a both-component revision with an osteotomy and a cup-cage is four hours and two litres. The pre-operative haemoglobin is the strongest predictor of transfusion and the one thing correctable in advance, the INR the second; a crossmatch of two to four units, cell salvage from the start, a large-bore cannula and an arterial line are the author's defaults for anything beyond a liner exchange. The neuraxial-versus-general evidence for primary hips is extrapolated here, reasonably, because these patients are older and sicker than the primary cohort, but a single-shot spinal does not cover a four-hour revision. The options are a combined spinal-epidural with the epidural run during the case and for two or three days after, or a general anaesthetic with a tracheal tube and a perineural catheter (lumbar plexus or supra-inguinal fascia iliaca) placed before induction. The author's default is the combined spinal-epidural for a planned cup-and-stem revision where anticoagulation and platelets allow, with the arterial line placed awake, and the general anaesthetic with a catheter where an epidural is impossible or the case is on the fracture pathway. Under general anaesthesia the end-tidal carbon dioxide is the right heart monitor for the cement step, an argument for the tube in the frail patient having a long cemented stem.

### 2. Lateral decubitus for a long case
**Why:** The posterior approach is the most extensile and the one that can be carried down the femur for an osteotomy; a few surgeons use a direct lateral or anterolateral approach for a cup-only revision.
**Anaesthesia:** Everything on the [posterior primary](tha-posterior.html) card, multiplied by the duration. The dependent arm, axilla, ear, eye and peroneal nerve are at risk for four hours rather than one; the clamps compress the abdomen and the dependent lung for the same period, so the ventilated patient needs a ventilator set for lower compliance and the sedated spinal patient needs their saturation watched. Pressure areas on the dependent hip, knee, ankle and breast need padding that would be excessive in a primary. Warming is a problem from the first hour: a long wound, cell salvage, a cold theatre and litres of irrigation during cement removal all cool the patient, and the forced-air blanket goes on from the start. The pelvis shifts in the clamps as the patient relaxes and the surgeon levers; the surgeon notices it on the cup orientation, you notice it on the airway and the arm.

### 3. Antibiotic, tranexamic acid, cell salvage and the baseline
**Why:** A re-operated joint, a long operation and large implants: infection is the complication that converts a revision into a two-stage exchange. In a suspected infection, deep tissue samples are the diagnosis and the antibiotic must not reach them first.
**Anaesthesia:** Cefazolin 2 g IV (3 g over 120 kg) before the knife in an aseptic revision, re-dosed at 4 hours and after each 1.5 L of blood loss; in a suspected infection or a second-stage reimplantation the antibiotic is held until the surgeon has taken samples, by explicit agreement, and then given. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV before incision with a second dose at 3 hours or an infusion: in the revision meta-analysis it cut the transfusion odds to about a quarter and the haemoglobin fall by more than 1 g/dL with no excess thromboembolism, and the one in six transfused in the prospective cohort were all on it. Cell salvage runs skin to skin in anything beyond a liner exchange; in a suspected infection the salvaged blood is not returned. Record the pre-induction pressure for the cement target later.

### 4. Exposure through scar, the sciatic nerve and dislocation
**Why:** The old scar is extended, gluteus maximus split again through adhesions, and scar dissected off the posterior trochanter and whatever remains of the rotators to reach the capsule. The sciatic nerve is tethered in scar and may be closer to the field than it was; many surgeons find and protect it first. The hip is then dislocated, which in a stiff revision may need the head taken off the trunnion or the neck of a loose stem cut, and the surgeon assesses which components are loose, which are fixed, and whether the plan survives contact with the hip.
**Anaesthesia:** Dissection through scar bleeds continuously and diffusely in a way a primary approach does not: a few hundred millilitres before the joint is open. The sciatic nerve is at risk from retraction now and from lengthening later, and a pre-operative examination of foot dorsiflexion and sensation, written down, is the only way to separate a new injury from an old one; under a combined spinal-epidural it is done before the block. Forcible dislocation of a scarred hip in an osteoporotic patient is where the intra-operative periprosthetic fracture happens, converting a cup revision into a both-component revision with an osteotomy; the surgeon's language changes and so does your estimate of the next three hours. The femoral vein is kinked from here until reduction, which may be hours away.

### 5. Femoral component removal: the extended trochanteric osteotomy and cement removal
**Why:** A loose stem comes out easily. A well-fixed uncemented stem (removed for infection, instability or a damaged trunnion) or a long cement mantle does not, and attempts to remove it from above perforate the femur. The extended trochanteric osteotomy cuts the lateral femur longitudinally for 12-20 cm and hinges the lateral third of the shaft open on its vastus lateralis attachment, exposing the whole stem and the cement for removal under vision with osteotomes, burrs and ultrasonic tools. The osteotomy's length is set by the implant and the cement to be removed.
**Anaesthesia:** The step that gives the operation its pain score and most of its blood loss. The femur is opened along a fifth of its length, and periosteal and endosteal bleeding from the osteotomy, the cancellous bed the cement is chipped from and the perforations cement removal produces runs continuously inside the canal where it cannot be seen. Cell salvage earns its place here. Ultrasonic cement removal heats the bone and the irrigation that goes with it cools the patient. The fragment can fracture (the commonest complication, with the trochanter fracturing in about 9%), which adds cables and time. Nothing pharmacological changes except the vigilance: a haemoglobin on a gas every hour, the pressure supported, and a second unit checked before the cup is started.

### 6. Acetabular component removal and reconstruction
**Why:** A loose cup is levered out; a fixed cup is cut out with curved osteotomes around its rim to preserve bone. What is left is classified and rebuilt: a larger hemispherical cup with screws for a contained defect, porous metal augments for a missing rim, a cup-cage or antiprotrusio cage for a discontinuity, impaction grafting with mesh and a cemented cup, or a custom triflange. Each is more surgery than the last, and all put screws into the ilium and ischium with the external iliac vessels, the obturator vessels and the sciatic nerve on the far side.
**Anaesthesia:** Reaming a damaged acetabulum to bleeding bone and placing screws blind into a pelvis is the second bleeding focus and the one with catastrophic potential: a screw or reamer through the medial wall into the external iliac vein presents as a pressure that falls and keeps falling with nothing to see in the wound, and the response is to say so, to have blood in the room, and to expect a vascular surgeon rather than more vasopressor. A cage or a triflange adds an hour, and antibiotic re-dosing falls due about now. The pelvis's contribution to the pain is bone and periosteum where the ilium has been stripped for a cage, which is why the revision patient hurts in the buttock as well as the thigh.

### 7. Femoral reconstruction: the long stem and the cement decision
**Why:** The new stem has to bypass the damaged proximal femur and the osteotomy and fix in the diaphysis below. Most surgeons use a modular tapered uncemented stem reamed to a tight fit in the isthmus; some use a long cemented stem, cement-in-cement revision into a sound existing mantle, or impaction grafting (morselised allograft hammered into the canal) with a cemented polished stem. The canal is reamed for its full length to bleeding bone before any of these.
**Anaesthesia:** Reaming a long canal vents fat and marrow into the circulation for longer and from a larger surface than any primary; transient desaturation and hypotension are the rule and the right ventricle is the organ under load. If a cemented stem follows, this is the highest-risk cement step in elective orthopaedics: a long column of cement pressurised into a long, freshly reamed, bleeding canal in a patient who has been on their side for three hours, has lost a litre or more, and is older and frailer than any primary patient. The hip fracture cement precautions apply in full, with an arterial line: the surgeon's warning before mixing, a washed and dried canal, a retrograde gun on a plug and no heavy pressurisation; the pressure within 20% of baseline before the cement goes in, a vasopressor infusion running rather than boluses, fluid up to date, metaraminol and adrenaline drawn up, and the end-tidal carbon dioxide watched for the fall that means the right heart has met an embolised pulmonary bed. Grade 2 and 3 events are rare in primaries and are not rare here; the treatment is right heart failure's: oxygen, vasopressor, adrenaline early, fluid with judgement, resuscitation if it comes to that. An uncemented tapered stem avoids the cement column but not the reaming, and its impaction down a long canal is the hardest hammering of the case.

### 8. Trial, reduction, stability and the constrained or dual-mobility bearing
**Why:** The hip is reduced and tested through a range of positions; with a long stem, a rebuilt cup and often a weak or absent abductor mechanism, stability is the surgeon's main worry and the reason the revision dislocation rate is several times the primary's. A larger head, a lipped or constrained liner, or a dual-mobility bearing is chosen accordingly. Leg length is harder to judge because the landmarks are gone.
**Anaesthesia:** Each trial reduction is a hard pull on a long construct in a frail patient, the pressure dips with it, and after a cemented stem each reduction is a further embolic moment. The leg is commonly lengthened in a revision, because the surgeon is tensioning soft tissue that has lost the structures that held the primary, and the sciatic nerve pays for it: this is the hip operation with the highest nerve injury rate and lengthening is the mechanism. Nothing to add pharmacologically; the examination in recovery is what matters.

### 9. Osteotomy repair, soft-tissue repair and closure
**Why:** The osteotomy fragment is cabled back around the new stem with two or three cables; what remains of the capsule and rotators is repaired; detached abductors are reattached; and the wound is closed over drains, which most revision surgeons still use.
**Anaesthesia:** Give the antiemetic and the second tranexamic acid dose if due. The cabled osteotomy is what the patient will feel for a week: a long bone, cut and cabled, that moves a little with every step and every abductor contraction. Start the epidural infusion or confirm the perineural catheter is running now, so the patient wakes with it working. Drains and the thigh are the first night's blood loss; the haemoglobin at six hours and the next morning decide the transfusion.

### 10. Recovery, the first days and the frail patient's week
**Why:** The surgeon wants partial or toe-touch weight bearing on the osteotomy and the ingrowth stem for six weeks or more, hip precautions where the abductors are compromised, and a patient out of bed on day 1 regardless.
**Anaesthesia:** The revision patient is the one the primary pathway was not written for. The epidural or catheter runs for two to three days with a low-dose local anaesthetic that leaves the quadriceps working enough to transfer; the oral regimen starts before it stops; the opioid-tolerant patient keeps their baseline and has the pain service involved from day 0; and the haemoglobin, renal function and delirium screen are checked daily, because the frail elderly revision patient becomes delirious, hypotensive and anaemic in the first 48 hours more often than any other elective orthopaedic patient. A foot drop is the sciatic nerve and the surgeon sees it in recovery; a thigh that swells with a falling haemoglobin is bleeding at the osteotomy; new severe pain on standing with a short leg is a dislocation of a construct already known to be unstable. Chemoprophylaxis starts when the epidural timing allows and the bleeding has settled, usually the next morning.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a long femoral osteotomy or a stripped canal, cabled back and loaded by every step, plus a pelvis cut into, in a patient who is frail, often opioid-tolerant, and expected to mobilise on a construct that hurts when it moves. That is a periosteal, movement-evoked, several-day injury, and it is the hip operation where a continuous technique has its strongest claim.

PROSPECT's basic regimen carries over: paracetamol with an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor where the kidneys and the age allow (in this population often not, so the NSAID is a daily decision rather than a standing order), a single intravenous dexamethasone dose, and opioids as rescue. The single-shot blocks PROSPECT recommends for primary hips do not address revision, and a single shot does not cover a four-hour operation or three days of osteotomy pain; the guideline's silence is the honest position and the catheter advice here is the author's judgement from the mechanism and the comparative trials in hip arthroplasty generally. The author's default is a combined spinal-epidural for the planned cup-and-stem revision, the epidural run as a low-dose local anaesthetic infusion for two to three days and stopped when the patient is transferring and the oral regimen has been in for a day. Where an epidural is impossible (anticoagulation that cannot be paused, an instrumented spine, the fracture pathway's timing) the alternative is a perineural catheter placed before a general anaesthetic: a posterior lumbar plexus catheter, or a supra-inguinal fascia iliaca catheter as the motor-sparing compromise. The randomised comparison of continuous femoral against lumbar plexus techniques after hip arthroplasty gave the same pain scores, with the lumbar plexus patients walking five times as far on the first morning, which is the argument against a femoral catheter in any hip. Surgeon-delivered infiltration is a supplement to any of these and never the plan. Intrathecal morphine up to 0.1 mg with the spinal is reasonable where no catheter is placed and the patient will be an inpatient for a week anyway.

For the liner-only or head-only exchange the mechanism is the primary's and so is the plan: a spinal, infiltration, and a single-shot PENG or supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block if infiltration is not offered.

Rebound risk is high: when a single-shot block wears off at 2 am on a cabled osteotomy the patient goes from comfortable to immobile, and a revision patient sent to the ward on a single shot without a written rescue plan is the commonest pain service referral of the week. Escalation triggers: rising thigh pain with a tense swollen thigh and a falling haemoglobin is bleeding at the osteotomy; buttock and leg pain with a foot drop is the sciatic nerve; new pain on movement with a short externally rotated leg is a dislocation; pain with a fever on day 3 after a reimplantation is the infection that was not cleared. The opioid-tolerant patient's baseline is never stopped on the day of surgery and the rescue doses are scaled to it; a ketamine infusion for the first 48 hours is the author's practice in that patient, with the usual low-grade evidence behind it.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Scope. The liner exchange, the head and liner exchange for wear or trunnion corrosion, the cup-only revision, the stem-only revision, the both-component revision with osteotomy and the pelvic discontinuity are different operations under one name; the pre-operative plan, not the list, tells you which.
- The frail elderly patient. Anaemic, on a diuretic, cardiopulmonary disease, the BCIS risk group, delirium-prone, and the one in whom the extrapolated mortality benefit of neuraxial anaesthesia is most likely to be real. An arterial line, a catheter technique, a monitored bed overnight and an orthogeriatrician are the standard.
- Anticoagulation. Warfarin for atrial fibrillation or a mechanical valve is common here, the INR predicts transfusion, and the epidural timing rules decide whether the catheter is neuraxial or perineural; plan it a week ahead.
- The opioid-tolerant patient. Years of a painful loose hip produce a patient on long-term opioids; the baseline continues, the catheter is routine, and the pain service is involved from the start. See [opioid-tolerant and chronic pain patients](../a/a12-opioid-tolerant.html).
- Vascular injury. Acetabular screws, reamers and cages near the medial wall, and a pelvic discontinuity: the external iliac and obturator vessels are the hidden bleed, and the sign is a pressure that falls with nothing in the wound.
- Impaction grafting. Morselised allograft hammered into the canal with a cemented stem to follow: prolonged hard impaction, a large embolic load, and a full cement step at the end of a long case.
- Reimplantation for infection. The antibiotic is held until samples are taken, dexamethasone is discussed with the surgeon, salvaged blood is not returned, and the patient has had a spacer and weeks of immobility and intravenous antibiotics: anaemia, renal impairment and deconditioning are the baseline. See [periprosthetic joint infection](periprosthetic-joint-infection.html).
- Metal-on-metal revision for a pseudotumour. The implant exchange is the easy part; the soft-tissue debridement, the destroyed abductors and the dual-mobility or constrained bearing that follow are the operation, and the dislocation rate afterwards is high.
- Periprosthetic fracture around a loose stem. This card's femoral reconstruction on the fracture pathway's timing, with the fracture patient's physiology: see [periprosthetic fracture](periprosthetic-fracture.html).

## Key sources

The evidence for revision hip arthroplasty is weakest in the areas that matter to the anaesthetist. The blood loss and transfusion data are good (a six-centre prospective cohort and a tranexamic acid meta-analysis); the cement data are extrapolated from hemiarthroplasty and the long-stem series in the Donaldson review; the neuraxial-versus-general evidence is extrapolated from primaries; and there is no procedure-specific analgesic guideline and no randomised comparison of epidural against perineural catheter in revision hips. The catheter recommendation is the author's judgement from the mechanism, the one hip arthroplasty catheter trial, and the demands of mobilising a frail patient on an osteotomy, and it is labelled as such.
