## What the surgeon is doing and why

The ankle rarely wears out on its own. Most end-stage ankle arthritis is post-traumatic: a malleolar or pilon fracture years earlier, or a decade of ligament instability, has left the talus tilted or shifted in the mortise and the cartilage gone. The patient is therefore younger on average than a hip or knee replacement patient, often has old scars and metalwork around the joint, and has a stiff, painful ankle fixed in a few degrees of equinus with a tight calf behind it.

There are two answers. Fusion removes the joint and has been the standard for a century (see [ankle, subtalar and triple fusion](ankle-hindfoot-fusion.html)). Replacement keeps motion at the ankle, which protects the subtalar and midfoot joints from the extra load that a fused ankle transfers to them. In the one large randomised comparison, 303 patients aged 50-85, the two gave similar walking and standing scores at a year, with fixed-bearing implants ahead of fusion in a post hoc analysis. The price of replacement in that trial was the front of the ankle: wound-healing problems in 13.4% against 5.7%, and nerve injury in 4.2% against under 1%.

That price follows from the approach. The standard operation is anterior: a 12-15 cm incision down the front of the ankle, through the extensor retinaculum, between tibialis anterior and extensor hallucis longus, with the deep peroneal nerve and anterior tibial artery lifted off the capsule and held laterally. The skin here has no muscle beneath it and a watershed blood supply, and it is retracted for an hour and a half. Through that window the surgeon:

1. Removes anterior osteophytes and any old metalwork in the way.
2. Pins an alignment jig to the tibia (or a patient-specific guide made from a CT) and makes a flat cut across the distal tibia at right angles to its mechanical axis, staying clear of both malleoli.
3. Cuts the talar dome flat or with chamfers, taking as little bone as the implant allows.
4. Inserts trial components and tests whether the ankle reaches 10 degrees of dorsiflexion and sits level. If it does not, the gastrocnemius aponeurosis is divided through a separate medial calf incision or the Achilles tendon is lengthened percutaneously; a varus ankle gets a deltoid release, a valgus or unstable one a lateral ligament repair, and a malaligned heel a calcaneal osteotomy.
5. Impacts a metal tibial tray and a metal talar dome, usually uncemented, with a polyethylene bearing between them that is either locked to the tibia (fixed-bearing) or free (mobile-bearing).

The pain logic is bone first. Two sawn cancellous surfaces with press-fit implants hurt like any arthroplasty for two to three days and then fade, because the pre-operative pain generator has been removed. Around that sit a muscle mechanism (the calf lengthening, which patients feel as a cramp behind the leg) and the swollen anterior soft tissues. Foot arthrodesis ranked among the most painful of 179 procedures for day 1 pain in a cohort that rarely used blocks, and ankle replacement, which was not listed separately, involves the same bone and more dissection. The whole territory is sciatic apart from the medial side, so a popliteal catheter with a saphenous block covers it; the score with that plan in place is 4, and the worst day is the one on which the catheter comes out.

## Steps

### 1. Before the day: the post-traumatic ankle
**Why:** The surgeon plans around previous incisions, retained plates, deformity above and below the joint, and the state of the skin. Smoking, diabetes and inflammatory arthritis on immunosuppression are the wound-risk factors that make some surgeons decline the operation.
**Anaesthesia:** The same history shapes your plan. Previous ankle operations mean previous anaesthetics and often a patient who can tell you exactly how the last block wore off. Document sensation and toe movement before any block, because a nerve deficit afterwards has three candidate causes (retraction of the deep peroneal nerve, the saw near the tibial nerve behind the medial malleolus, and the block) and the trial rate was 4.2%. Rheumatoid patients bring the cervical spine and steroid cover; the anticoagulated patient brings the neuraxial timing question.

### 2. Anaesthetic, catheter and saphenous block
**Why:** The surgeon needs a still leg, a thigh tourniquet for up to 2 h, and relaxed calf muscles when balancing is assessed.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway or a spinal, either with regional analgesia. Place the popliteal sciatic catheter before induction, with the tip a few centimetres proximal to the bifurcation (a randomised trial found better analgesia there than at the bifurcation), bolus 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%, and add a single-shot saphenous block in the adductor canal with 10 mL: the medial end of the incision, the medial gutter and the medial malleolus are saphenous. Secure the catheter laterally, well away from the thigh cuff and the bump. Dexamethasone 8 mg IV, cefazolin 2 g IV and TXA 1 g IV all go in before inflation. A spinal covers the cuff without a general anaesthetic but tourniquet pain can break through beyond 90 min ([the tourniquet](../a/a01-tourniquet.html)), and this operation regularly passes that mark. Where the surgeon gives prophylactic enoxaparin and a spinal has been used, the first post-operative dose is given at least 12 h after the needle, and a direct oral anticoagulant at least 6 h after it ([venous thromboembolism](../a/a05-vte.html)); the popliteal catheter is a superficial, compressible site and needs no special timing.

### 3. Position and tourniquet
**Why:** Supine, a bump under the buttock until the foot points straight up, because every cut is referenced to the tibial axis and a rotated leg means a rotated implant. The image intensifier comes in from the other side for true anteroposterior and lateral views.
**Anaesthesia:** Set the cuff from the limb occlusion pressure and write the inflation time where the surgeon can see it. The bump tilts the pelvis; pad the opposite hip and heel. Call the tourniquet time at 60 and 90 min and agree the plan for 120 min beforehand: most surgeons release before closure anyway.

### 4. Anterior approach
**Why:** The incision runs lateral to tibialis anterior, the superficial peroneal nerve branches are protected distally, the extensor retinaculum is opened over extensor hallucis longus so it can be repaired, and the neurovascular bundle is mobilised laterally. The capsule is excised.
**Anaesthesia:** No physiological event, but this is the step that decides the wound. Retraction is meant to be gentle and intermittent, and a surgeon working without self-retaining retractors is doing it deliberately. Blood pressure extremes do not help a marginal skin flap; aim for the patient's normal range.

### 5. Clearing the joint and old metalwork
**Why:** Osteophytes are removed until the joint line can be seen and the talus moves; a medial malleolar screw or fibular plate from an old fracture is taken out if it obstructs the jig or the cuts, sometimes through its original incision.
**Anaesthesia:** A second incision over the fibula or medial malleolus is still within the block, the medial one only if the saphenous block was done. Stripped screw heads and broken plates add unplanned time under tourniquet.

### 6. Alignment jig and tibial cut
**Why:** The jig is pinned to the tibial crest and the tubercle, adjusted on fluoroscopy in both planes, and an oscillating saw makes the tibial cut with the malleoli protected by pins or guards.
**Anaesthesia:** Movement now ruins the alignment, so depth and stillness matter more than at any other step. The complications here are surgical and you see them on the screen: a saw cut that notches or fractures the medial malleolus (treated with one or two screws and a slower rehabilitation), and a saw that passes the posterior cortex towards the posteromedial tendons and the tibial nerve.

### 7. Talar preparation
**Why:** With the foot held at neutral the talar dome is resected or chamfered with a guide referenced from the tibial cut, and a fin or peg slot is prepared. Bone stock is precious because revision options are limited.
**Anaesthesia:** More sawn cancellous bone and the second surface of the pain mechanism. Bleeding is masked by the tourniquet until release.

### 8. Trials and balancing: calf lengthening, ligaments, heel
**Why:** With trials in place the surgeon tests dorsiflexion and coronal balance. A tight calf is dealt with by gastrocnemius recession through a short posteromedial calf incision or by percutaneous triple-cut Achilles lengthening; a varus or valgus ankle by ligament release or repair; a heel that is not under the leg by a calcaneal osteotomy fixed with screws.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon assesses equinus with the knee extended and flexed and needs the calf relaxed; under a dense sciatic block it already is, and under a spinal it is too, so neuromuscular block is rarely the answer to a tight ankle. The calf incision is partly saphenous skin. Each adjunct is more bone or more muscle, 15-30 min more tourniquet, and a stronger case for the catheter having been placed.

### 9. Definitive implants
**Why:** The tibial tray and talar component are impacted onto the cut surfaces, the polyethylene insert is selected and locked or seated, and stability and range are checked on fluoroscopy. Prophylactic or therapeutic malleolar screws go in now.
**Anaesthesia:** Impaction is loud under a spinal; warn or sedate. Where cement is used the volume is a few millilitres on a small cancellous surface with the tourniquet up, and no cement reaction is expected; see [cement and BCIS](../a/a02-cement-bcis.html) for the contrast with the hip.

### 10. Tourniquet release, haemostasis and closure
**Why:** The cuff is released, bleeding points are controlled, the retinaculum is repaired over the tendons so they do not bowstring against the wound, and the skin is closed without tension, sometimes over a drain. A well-padded backslab holds the ankle at neutral.
**Anaesthesia:** Most of the 300-450 mL blood loss happens from here; pooled comparative studies of tranexamic acid show less of it and roughly half the wound complications. Reperfusion after close to 2 h gives the usual carbon dioxide and potassium peak and a fall in pressure for some minutes. The splint should not press on the incision and the foot must not be left in equinus, which blanches the anterior skin; say so if the dressing looks tight.

### 11. Recovery and the first 72 h
**Why:** The surgeon wants strict elevation and an undisturbed wound for two weeks, then a boot and movement.
**Anaesthesia:** Start ropivacaine 0.2% at 5-8 mL/h with or without a patient bolus, and start regular paracetamol and, if the surgeon and the kidneys allow, an NSAID on day 0. Reinforce the saphenous single shot's limits: when it fades at 18-24 h the patient may report medial ankle pain with a working sciatic catheter, which is expected and treated orally. Leakage at the catheter site is the commonest problem (about one in seven in the pooled trials) and is managed by reinforcing the dressing. Plan the removal for a morning with oral opioid given an hour beforehand; the hours after removal are this operation's rebound. An inpatient catheter run for 48-72 h therefore means two or three nights in hospital. Where an ambulatory pathway sends the patient home with an elastomeric pump after one or two nights, the removal happens at home: the patient or a carer takes the opioid dose, removes the catheter on the appointed morning and is telephoned that day, and the written plan says so. The patient is non-weight-bearing with an insensate foot throughout, so heel protection and falls precautions are part of the infusion order.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is two bone surfaces and a large anterior dissection, with a muscle lengthening behind, in a limb whose nerve supply can be covered almost completely from the popliteal fossa. The pain lasts longer than a single-shot block even with dexamethasone, which is the argument for a catheter and the reason the [block menu](../a/a07-block-menu.html) lists ankle replacement with hindfoot fusion and calcaneal fixation as catheter operations.

The evidence for that is real and modest. In the original home-catheter trial, a ropivacaine infusion after moderately painful lower limb surgery took day 1 rest pain from a median of 4 to 0 and opioid tablets from 8 to 0. The pooled randomised trials in foot and ankle surgery show lower pain at 24 and 48 h with a catheter than with a single injection, no infections or neuropathic sequelae, and leakage in 13.9%. Against that, the one trial confined to major ankle and hindfoot surgery found a statistically significant benefit from a 72-h infusion but low pain scores in both arms, and its authors doubted the cost was justified. None of these trials used intravenous dexamethasone with the single shot. As in the shoulder, the catheter's margin over a dexamethasone-extended single shot is unmeasured and probably smaller than its margin over a bare one.

The defensible default is therefore a popliteal catheter for 48-72 h with a saphenous single shot, intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, regular paracetamol, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor where the surgeon agrees (there is no fusion to protect, though some surgeons restrict NSAIDs for bone ingrowth), and oral opioid for breakthrough and for the removal day. Where a catheter cannot be run, give the same blocks as single shots with dexamethasone and plan the second night in hospital, not at home. This is the author's judgement, consistent with the block menu and not a guideline statement; no PROSPECT guideline covers the operation.

Escalation triggers: medial ankle pain with a working catheter is the saphenous territory. Severe pain under the splint with a working catheter is a tight dressing or a wound problem until the splint has been opened and the skin seen. Pain that returns in the calf at one to three weeks is a thrombosis until excluded. Rebound after catheter removal peaks within a few hours and is treated with the opioid already charted.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Wound breakdown. The defining complication. Smokers, diabetics and patients on immunosuppressants are over-represented; avoid hypotension and vasoconstriction at closure, keep the patient warm, and keep the splint off the incision.
- Nerve injury. The deep peroneal nerve is retracted, the superficial peroneal crosses the incision, the tibial nerve sits behind the posterior saw cut. A catheter left unloaded until the foot has been examined in recovery is a reasonable request from a surgeon and costs you twenty minutes of analgesia.
- Malleolar fracture. Intra-operative, fixed with screws, changes the weight-bearing plan and not the anaesthetic.
- Lateral-approach designs. A fibular osteotomy and a lateral decubitus position, with an external alignment frame; the fibula is plated at the end. More bone, similar plan.
- Staged or simultaneous hindfoot fusion. A subtalar or triple fusion with the replacement makes this a 3-h operation that belongs with [ankle, subtalar and triple fusion](ankle-hindfoot-fusion.html) for pain and tourniquet planning.
- Revision and conversion of a fusion. Longer, bloodier after release, bone graft likely, and the strongest catheter indication on the card.
- Day-case replacement. Reported in case series with catheters and structured follow-up; it depends on the pathway, a carer, and a patient who can manage a pump and crutches with one insensate leg.
- The haemophilic or inflammatory ankle. Factor cover or biologic timing is settled before the day; the anaesthetic follows it.
- Diabetes. A neuropathic ankle is usually a contraindication to replacement; where it is done, block duration is prolonged, as described in [diabetic foot surgery](diabetic-foot-surgery.html).

## Key sources

TARVA is the only large randomised trial of ankle replacement against fusion and supplies the complication rates used here; it reports 52-week outcomes only. The tranexamic acid evidence is two systematic reviews of small comparative studies, consistent in direction. The catheter evidence comes from mixed foot and ankle populations, mostly outpatient and mostly before routine dexamethasone, and the single trial restricted to major ankle and hindfoot surgery is equivocal about clinical importance. Day-case replacement rests on level IV series. No trial has compared anaesthetic techniques for ankle replacement specifically, and the pain score is inferred from the mechanism and from the cohort ranking of foot arthrodesis.
