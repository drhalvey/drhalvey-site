## What the surgeon is doing and why

The clavicle is the only bony strut between the arm and the trunk: a subcutaneous S-shaped bone that holds the shoulder girdle away from the chest, with the subclavian vessels, the brachial plexus and the apex of the lung running under its middle third and nothing but skin and platysma over it. It breaks in the middle third when a cyclist or a rugby player lands on the point of the shoulder, and the acromioclavicular joint at its lateral end dislocates when the same fall drives the acromion down while the clavicle stays up, tearing the acromioclavicular capsule first (Rockwood I-II) and then the coracoclavicular ligaments (III and above), so that the clavicle rides high and the arm hangs away from it. The surgeon's problem in both injuries is the same: a displaced bone with no muscle envelope to hold it, under thin skin, that heals adequately without surgery in most people and badly in some, and that has the great vessels and the lung immediately beneath it.

For the midshaft fracture, a sling unites most fractures and operative fixation reduces nonunion and shortens the time to function at the cost of a plate under the skin that many patients want removed. For the displaced distal fracture, the one randomised trial found the same function at a year with fixation but union in 95% against 64% without, and 44% of the plated patients had a second operation to take the metal out. For the acromioclavicular joint, grades I and II are left alone, grade III is argued over, and IV and above are reconstructed with a hook plate, a suture button construct or a ligament reconstruction with graft, depending on the surgeon and the chronicity.

What the surgeon does to tissue:

1. Midshaft and distal clavicle plating. An incision along the clavicle, platysma and the supraclavicular nerves (which cross the bone and are preserved or sacrificed, giving the numb patch below the scar that most patients have), the periosteum stripped from the fracture ends, the fracture reduced with clamps, and a precontoured plate laid on the superior or anterior surface and fixed with screws drilled from top to bottom, with the drill tip emerging a few millimetres from the subclavian vein. Some surgeons use an intramedullary device, with less stripping and a smaller scar.
2. Hook plate for the acromioclavicular joint or the distal fracture. A plate along the distal clavicle with a hook that passes under the acromion and levers the clavicle down into reduction. Quick and strong, but the hook sits in the subacromial space pushing on the bursa and the cuff with every elevation, erodes the acromion, and is removed at three to six months in a second operation; it bleeds less than a button construct and hurts more while it is in.
3. Suture button construct. Open or arthroscopically assisted, a drill hole from the top of the clavicle through the base of the coracoid, a button under the coracoid and one on the clavicle with a high-strength suture loop between them, tensioned to pull the clavicle down. Nothing is left under the acromion. The drill passes 30-40 mm with the plexus and vessels medial to the coracoid base. The pooled comparisons give the button lower late pain scores and the hook plate less bleeding, with no clear winner in function.
4. Ligament reconstruction. For chronic dislocations, a tendon graft (hamstring autograft or allograft) is looped under the coracoid and through the clavicle to rebuild the coracoclavicular ligaments; a graft harvest adds a second site.

The pain logic. The dominant mechanism is periosteum: a subcutaneous bone stripped along its length and plated, or drilled through from top to bottom, under skin with almost nothing between the metal and the surface. It is raw, incisional and deep at once, peaks on day 1 and settles over three days as a fracture does, but the skin over the plate stays tender for weeks and the hook plate adds an irritation under the acromion that lasts until the plate comes out. No tendon is under tension and no joint re-tensioned, so the tail is short. That makes a 3: a block is expected, opioids are needed for two or three days, and the fall is quick.

The anaesthetic logic is about anatomy rather than intensity, and it is why this card exists separately. The clavicle is not in the brachial plexus. Its skin and periosteum are supplied by the supraclavicular nerves, which come off the cervical plexus at C3 and C4 and descend over the bone; the acromioclavicular joint and the lateral end receive a C5 contribution through the suprascapular and lateral pectoral nerves, and the inferior surface from the nerve to subclavius. An interscalene block at C5-C6 covers the joint and the lateral end but leaves the C3-C4 skin and periosteum of the shaft untouched, which is why clavicle fixation under interscalene block alone produces a patient who flinches at the incision and a first night the block did not cover. The combined approach, a superficial or intermediate cervical plexus block for C3-C4 plus an interscalene or superior trunk block for C5-C6, is the technique the scoping review of 44 studies names as reliable surgical anaesthesia; the motor-sparing alternatives (clavipectoral fascial plane block plus supraclavicular nerve block, or a selective C3-C5 root block) are the subject of small randomised trials described in the pain plan.

## Steps

### 1. The patient, the injury and which nerves to block
**Why:** The surgeon is fixing a fracture in a cyclist who fell last week, reconstructing a joint in a rugby player, or removing a hook plate from either; the elective nonunion and the chronic dislocation with graft are the long versions. The injury that matters to you is the one next to the clavicle: rib fractures, a pneumothorax or a scapular fracture on the same side are common in the cyclist and the motorcyclist, and the polytrauma patient's clavicle is fixed when the rest allows.
**Anaesthesia:** Decide the block by territory. The cervical plexus component (superficial or intermediate) covers the C3-C4 skin and periosteum of the shaft; the interscalene or superior trunk component covers the C5-C6 contribution to the lateral end and the joint. For a midshaft plate the cervical plexus block does most of the work and a small-volume superior trunk block completes it; for an acromioclavicular reconstruction the interscalene component matters more. Intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg with the block. The combined block carries one phrenic palsy, and a patient with same-side rib fractures and a chest drain cannot afford it: use a low-volume superior trunk block, or the clavipectoral plus supraclavicular nerve combination, which spared the diaphragm in the trials. The block site is the surgical field, so the cervical plexus injection goes in before the skin preparation and is noted to the surgeon. Airway: a tracheal tube taped to the opposite side, because the surgeon's hands, the drill and the retractor are all at the root of the neck; a supraglottic device is reasonable for a quick hook plate removal.

### 2. Positioning
**Why:** Beach chair at 30-45 degrees with the head turned slightly away brings the clavicle into the surgeon's line and lets the arm be moved to reduce the fracture or the joint; some surgeons work supine with a bump under the scapula to push the clavicle forward.
**Anaesthesia:** The usual beach chair arithmetic at a lower incline: a smaller hydrostatic gradient, but the same strokes in the same literature and the same vasopressor logic. The head turned away puts the tube and the circuit on the far side and the neck in slight rotation; check the ear and the eye on the dependent side. The cervical plexus block site is now in the field and the drapes will be over the face and the tube; secure both before the surgeon starts. The arm is draped free so that it can be lifted to reduce the fracture, which moves the shoulder girdle and the neck with it.

### 3. Incision, platysma and the supraclavicular nerves
**Why:** A longitudinal incision along the clavicle (or a transverse one in some hands), platysma divided, and the supraclavicular nerve branches crossing the bone identified and preserved or sacrificed, which the surgeon decides by whether they can be swept aside; most patients have some numbness below the scar whatever is done.
**Anaesthesia:** The skin incision is the test of the cervical plexus component: a patient under light general anaesthesia who responds to the knife here has a working interscalene block and no cervical plexus block, and the lesson is for next time. Cefazolin 2 g IV finished before the knife, 3 g over 120 kg; the skin is thin and infection means exposed metal.

### 4. Exposure, periosteal stripping and reduction
**Why:** The periosteum is elevated from the fracture ends and far enough along the shaft to seat the plate, the haematoma and interposed soft tissue are cleared, and the fracture is reduced with pointed clamps, often with a lag screw across an oblique fracture line first. The reduction of a week-old displaced fracture in a muscular patient takes force, and the arm is lifted and pushed by the assistant to bring the lateral fragment up.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the mechanism of the card being created: a stripped bone, raw along its length. It bleeds from the periosteal surface and the fracture ends, which is the 100-250 mL of the operation. The reduction manoeuvre moves the shoulder girdle and the neck, and the pressure on the neck from a retractor levering the medial fragment up is pressure on the structures beneath the clavicle. A pneumothorax on the same side, if present, is at its most relevant now: positive pressure ventilation and a surgeon leaning on the chest wall.

### 5. Plating, drilling and the structures beneath
**Why:** A precontoured plate is laid on the superior (or anterior) surface and fixed with screws, three bicortical screws each side of the fracture. Each superior-to-inferior drill hole passes through the inferior cortex a few millimetres above the subclavian vein, the subclavian artery, the brachial plexus and the pleura; the surgeon protects them with a retractor or a finger under the bone and drills with a stop, and the anteriorly placed plate exists partly because its screws point away from the vessels.
**Anaesthesia:** The one catastrophe of the operation happens here: a drill or a long screw into the subclavian vein or artery, which presents as sudden, uncontrolled bleeding from the depths of a wound the surgeon cannot see into, and a pneumothorax from the pleura, which presents as a rise in airway pressure and a fall in saturation during or after the drilling. The second is commoner and is diagnosed on the recovery room radiograph. Note the time of each drill hole in a difficult reduction; if the surgeon says "that one went deep", the post-operative chest radiograph is not optional.

### 6. Acromioclavicular reconstruction: hook, button or graft
**Why:** For the hook plate, the plate is laid along the distal clavicle and the hook slid under the acromion from the posterior edge, then the plate is pushed down to reduce the joint and screwed. For the button construct, a drill passes from the top of the clavicle through the base of the coracoid, which is found by palpation or by arthroscopy from the joint, the button is flipped under the coracoid, the suture loop is tensioned and the clavicle is pulled down and held. For the graft reconstruction the tendon is passed under the coracoid and through tunnels in the clavicle.
**Anaesthesia:** The coracoid drill is aimed at the base of a bone that has the plexus and the axillary vessels on its medial side; a drill that skids off the coracoid medially is the complication, and the arthroscopically assisted techniques exist to watch the drill emerge. The hook under the acromion sits on the subacromial bursa and the cuff and is the reason hook plate patients hurt more in the weeks after than button patients, a difference the pooled data put at half a point on the pain scale and a plate removal later. A graft harvest from the hamstring is a second site in a different block territory, and it needs its own infiltration or block.

### 7. Closure, sling and the skin
**Why:** Platysma is closed over the plate as the only soft-tissue cover, then skin, and a sling supports the arm for two to six weeks depending on the fixation; the patient is told not to lift the arm above shoulder height until the surgeon allows, and a hook plate patient is told the hook will catch.
**Anaesthesia:** Give the antiemetic. The sling before the patient lightens. The skin over a clavicular plate is the thinnest cover of any orthopaedic implant and the surgeon will want the dressing left alone; the numb patch below the scar from the sacrificed supraclavicular nerves is permanent in many and is not the block.

### 8. Recovery, the chest radiograph and the first night
**Why:** Day case or one night. The surgeon wants a radiograph of the fixation and, after any difficult drilling, of the chest, and a patient who moves the elbow and hand from the first day.
**Anaesthesia:** A chest radiograph in recovery after any midshaft plate where the drilling was difficult or the patient had rib fractures; a pneumothorax that was not there before the operation is a surgical complication and presents late if it is not looked for. Regular paracetamol and an NSAID start in recovery, and the block recedes over 10-15 hours depending on the technique, with the cervical plexus component usually wearing off before the interscalene. The first night is the periosteal ache along the bone plus the pre-existing fracture pain, treated with oral opioid for two or three nights. A dense arm block from the interscalene component is not a reason to keep the patient; a weak diaphragm in a patient with rib fractures is.

### 9. Hook plate removal and the second operation
**Why:** The hook plate is removed at three to six months, once the joint or the fracture has healed, because the hook erodes the acromion and irritates the cuff for as long as it stays; the button construct and the simple plate are removed only if they bother the patient, which under thin skin they often do.
**Anaesthesia:** A short operation through the old scar: the plate comes out, the hook is slid back out from under the acromion, and the wound is closed. It hurts less than the insertion and a cervical plexus block alone, with surgeon infiltration, is often enough; a patient who has had a bad first night the first time will tell you. Day case.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is periosteal and fracture pain along a subcutaneous bone that settles over three days, so the plan is a single-shot combined block that covers the operation and the first night, a regular oral regimen started before it recedes, and no catheter; the complexity is anatomical, not pharmacological. There is no PROSPECT guideline for clavicle surgery; the scoping review of 44 studies is the nearest synthesis and names the intermediate cervical plexus block as the technique of choice, reliable for surgical anaesthesia when combined with an interscalene block, and the rotator cuff repair guideline's skeleton of intravenous dexamethasone, paracetamol and NSAID applies.

The defensible default is a superficial or intermediate cervical plexus block with 5-10 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% for the C3-C4 skin and periosteum, plus a superior trunk block with 10-15 mL for the C5-C6 contribution to the lateral clavicle and the joint, with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, regular paracetamol and an NSAID started in recovery, and oral opioid for two or three nights. The superior trunk rather than the interscalene for the second component because the diaphragm matters more here than in any other shoulder group: the clavicle patient often has rib fractures on the same side. Where the diaphragm cannot be touched at all, the clavipectoral fascial plane block plus a supraclavicular nerve block is the phrenic-sparing combination; in one randomised comparison of 60 fixations it gave longer analgesia (14.6 against 10.3 hours) and less 24 h analgesic use than cervical plexus plus interscalene, with hemidiaphragmatic paralysis only in the interscalene group, while a second trial found the upper trunk combination gave two hours more analgesia than the clavipectoral combination at the cost of motor block and diaphragmatic excursion. Both are small single-centre trials; the honest reading is that any combination covering both territories works, and the chest decides which.

The catheter is rarely justified. The pain is periosteal and short, and no single catheter position covers both the C3-C4 skin and the C5-C6 bone. The exception is the nonunion revision with bone graft, the chronic reconstruction with a hamstring harvest and the patient with chronic pain or opioid tolerance, where an interscalene catheter plus a single-shot cervical plexus block is the author's compromise and the harvest site gets its own infiltration.

Rebound is moderate: bone surgery in a young population, but a plate rather than an osteotomy, and the cervical plexus component wears off earlier and more gently than the interscalene. Escalation: pain above 7 after the block has receded with the hand warm and moving is treated with the charted opioid; pain on the top of the shoulder with a numb arm is a working interscalene block and a missed cervical plexus block, fixed with a superficial cervical plexus block in recovery; new dyspnoea or a fall in saturation after a midshaft plate is a pneumothorax until the radiograph says otherwise; a swelling wound with a rising pulse is a vascular injury and the surgeon is called before anything else.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The missed territory. An interscalene block alone is the commonest error on this list: the patient is awake to the incision and sore on the top of the shoulder all night, and the fix is a five-minute superficial cervical plexus block that was not placed. Place it first, before the skin preparation, and tell the surgeon it is there.
- Rib fractures and pneumothorax on the same side. The cyclist and the motorcyclist with a clavicle fracture often have both; a phrenic palsy from an interscalene block on top of a flail segment is the combination to avoid. Superior trunk, clavipectoral plus supraclavicular nerve, or a selective C3-C5 root block in hands that do it.
- The polytrauma clavicle. Fixed when the rest allows, under the trauma pathway's anaesthetic, with the block decision made on the chest and the head injury, not on the clavicle.
- Hook plate versus button. The hook plate bleeds less, is quicker, hurts more while it is in and comes out in a second operation; the button construct is longer, drills next to the plexus, and is left in. The pooled comparisons find no clear winner in function; the pain difference favours the button by half a point.
- Nonunion and the chronic dislocation. Scar, a plate to remove, bone graft from the iliac crest or a hamstring harvest, and a second painful site in a different block territory that needs its own plan; the one setting where a catheter is reasonable.
- Intramedullary fixation. Less stripping, a smaller scar and less pain than a plate, with the same territory to block.
- The subclavian vessels and the lung. A drill depth below the inferior cortex. A sudden bleed from the depths or a rising airway pressure during drilling is the surgeon's catastrophe presenting to you; the chest radiograph in recovery after difficult drilling is not optional.
- Adolescents. Most displaced midshaft fractures in adolescents are treated without surgery on the pooled data; the ones that reach theatre are the open, the shortened and the athlete's, and the combined block works under general anaesthesia as it does in an adult.
- Plate removal. Common, elective, short, and under-blocked because it looks minor; the cervical plexus block alone with surgeon infiltration is usually enough.

## Key sources

The surgical evidence on operative versus non-operative treatment and on hook plate versus button is reasonable and is cited for the variants, not for pain. The anaesthetic evidence is the point of this card and it is thin but consistent: a scoping review of 44 heterogeneous studies establishes that no single block covers the clavicle and that cervical plexus plus interscalene is the combination that reliably works, and the small randomised trials of 2025-2026 compare motor-sparing combinations with each other and with interscalene, mostly in single centres and with analgesia duration rather than pain trajectory as the outcome. The innervation argument is standard anatomy. The pain score, the trajectory and the catheter advice are the author's calibration against the Part B scale and the mechanism.
