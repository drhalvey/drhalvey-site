## What the surgeon is doing and why

This card is written as a set of differences from the [posterior approach](tha-posterior.html). The disease, the implant, the cup and stem steps, the cemented or uncemented decision, the cement precautions, the tranexamic acid, the neuraxial-versus-general evidence, the analgesic plan and the day-case pathway are the same and are not repeated. What changes is the way in, and the way in decides the position, the nerve at risk, the muscle that hurts afterwards, which step is the dangerous one, and what the patient is allowed to do on day 1.

There are three honest ways into a hip and every surgeon has been trained to trust one of them. The posterior approach comes in behind the joint through the short rotators and spares the abductors. The direct anterior approach comes in from the front between tensor fasciae latae and sartorius (the Hueter interval), then between rectus femoris and gluteus medius: an internervous, intermuscular plane in which no muscle is cut and no tendon detached. The direct lateral approach comes in from the side through the abductors, splitting gluteus medius and vastus lateralis in continuity, peeling the anterior part off the trochanter and repairing it at the end. The three dislocate at comparable rates once the posterior capsule is repaired, and the Australian registry puts all-cause revision at about 2% for all of them, with the difference for any single complication at most about 0.2%. The choice is about what the surgeon can see, what they are used to, and which complication they would rather have.

The direct anterior approach's bargain: because nothing is cut there is no posterior capsule to protect, so there are no hip precautions, the patient can sit, bend and cross their legs from the first day, early function and pain scores in the small randomised trials are better, and the stay is about half a day shorter. It is the approach most often chosen for a day-case pathway. The costs are on the femur and the skin. The femur lies behind the surgeon's line of sight, so the leg has to be dropped into extension, adduction and external rotation with the pelvis fixed, the capsule released off the trochanter, and the proximal femur levered up into the wound. That is the weakest exposure of any approach, it is where the calcar cracks and the trochanter fractures, and it is why the registry found more early femoral complications and early major revisions after the anterior approach despite less dislocation and infection. The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve crosses the interval and is stretched or cut in most patients: 81% in the Ottawa series, higher after resurfacing, mild, without effect on function, and rarely fully resolving. The groin-crease wound under an obese abdomen is the wound-complication penalty. And the fluoroscopy most surgeons use for cup position and leg length means a C-arm over the pelvis and a radiation question for whoever stands at the head.

The direct lateral approach's bargain is the opposite. The surgeon sees both the acetabulum and the femur well, the dislocation rate is the lowest of the three, and no fluoroscope or traction table is needed. The cost is the abductor mechanism: gluteus medius is split and repaired, the superior gluteal nerve crosses the split about 5 cm above the trochanter and is cut if the split is carried too high, and the pooled comparisons show more limping and more stem malposition than the posterior approach, with heterotopic ossification trending higher. A limp that lasts weeks is a muscle repair under load; a limp that never resolves is a superior gluteal nerve palsy or a failed repair.

The pain logic follows. For the anterior approach the mechanism is pure bone, reaming and broaching with no muscle divided and no capsule under tension, so the pain is the posterior approach's or a little less and the PROSPECT regimen fits it unchanged. For the lateral approach the bone mechanism is joined by a muscle one, a split and reattached gluteus medius loaded by every step, which does not change the first two nights but does change the second week. Both are a 2 on the Part B scale with a spinal and infiltration in place, the lateral at the top of that band and the anterior at the bottom. Neither needs a catheter.

## Steps

### 1. Position: supine, with or without a traction table
**Why:** The anterior approach is done supine because the interval opens best with the hip extended and the surgeon standing on the operative side. A traction table (the feet in boots on telescoping spars with a perineal post) lets one surgeon control extension, adduction, external rotation and traction with a lever and a pedal; a standard table with the operative leg draped free and the contralateral leg in a holder lets the assistant do it by hand and the leg be dropped off the side of the table for the femoral step. Surgeons vary and both are common.
**Anaesthesia:** Supine is the one real advantage of this approach from your side: the airway is in front of you, a spinal with sedation is comfortable, the dependent lung problem of lateral decubitus does not exist, and the pressure is steadier. The traction table brings its own list. The perineal post is a pudendal nerve and perineal skin hazard if traction is heavy or prolonged, as on the [hip arthroscopy](hip-arthroscopy.html) card, though the pulls here are brief; the boots are an ankle and foot hazard, and the contralateral leg left in traction is a peroneal nerve and compartment hazard. The operative-side arm is often placed across the chest on a rest to clear the surgeon's elbow, so the drip and the cuff go on the other side. The C-arm comes in over the pelvis from the opposite side, so the machine and the lines need to be where it is not going.

### 2. Skin, the interval and the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve
**Why:** An oblique or longitudinal incision over tensor fasciae latae, a few centimetres lateral to the anterior superior iliac spine, deliberately placed lateral to where the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve usually runs. The fascia over tensor is opened and the muscle belly swept laterally to find the interval with sartorius and rectus femoris. The nerve is not sought out; the approach stays lateral of it and accepts that its branches cross the field.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing to do except know what the patient will report. Numbness or tingling over the anterolateral thigh after this approach is the rule, not the exception, is usually mild, does not limit function in the series that measured it, and mostly does not fully resolve. Document pre-operative sensation if the patient already has meralgia paraesthetica, because they will blame the operation for it, and have the surgeon consent for it explicitly. A lateral femoral cutaneous nerve block has no analgesic role here and is listed by PROSPECT as not recommended.

### 3. Lateral femoral circumflex vessels, capsulotomy and neck cut
**Why:** The ascending branches of the lateral femoral circumflex artery and their veins cross the lower end of the interval and are ligated or sealed before the capsule is reached; this is a step peculiar to the anterior approach and a known bleeding point. The anterior capsule is opened as a flap or excised, the neck exposed, and the neck cut made in situ (the head cannot be dislocated out of a small anterior window) before the head is removed with a corkscrew. Many surgeons use the fluoroscope now to check the neck cut level.
**Anaesthesia:** The circumflex vessels bleed briskly if a tie slips; it is the one early arterial bleeding point of the case and the surgeon will say so. Blood loss over the case is similar to the posterior approach in the pooled trials. The in situ neck cut means no forcible dislocation and no shifting of the patient's mass against clamps, so the pressure is steadier here than at the equivalent posterior step.

### 4. Acetabular exposure, reaming and the fluoroscope
**Why:** The acetabulum is the easy part of this approach: it faces the surgeon, the pelvis is supine and level, and fluoroscopy gives a true anteroposterior view, so cup inclination and anteversion are checked against the image rather than the surgeon's estimate of where the floor is. Reaming and cup impaction are as for the posterior approach.
**Anaesthesia:** Reaming is the largest single bleed of the case and the first bone pain mechanism, exactly as behind. The C-arm swings in over the pelvis, often several times; stand behind the shield or step back, because the scatter from a hip at the head end over a two-hour list is not trivial, and keep the lines, the sedation pump and the warming blanket clear of the arc. In a sedated patient a spinal that is wearing thin becomes apparent at cup impaction, which is the first hard mallet work of the case.

### 5. Femoral exposure: extension, external rotation and capsular release
**Why:** This is the step the anterior approach is judged by. The femur lies posterior to the acetabulum with the patient supine, so the proximal femur has to be brought up into the wound: the leg is extended (dropped below table level, or the traction table spar lowered), adducted under the other leg, externally rotated through 90 degrees or more, and the capsule and the conjoint tendon released off the posterior trochanter until a hook under the calcar can lever the femur up and out. The amount of release is the surgeon's judgement and the source of the approach's reputation: too little and the femur cannot be prepared safely, too much and the posterior stability the approach is supposed to preserve is lost.
**Anaesthesia:** The leg goes into a position that stretches the femoral nerve and vessels over the front of the hip; on a traction table the pelvis is fixed and the force goes through the femur. A stiff hip, an osteoporotic femur or a surgeon early in their experience makes this the longest step of the case and the one where things break: a calcar crack during broaching or a trochanteric fracture during levering is the mechanism behind the registry's higher early femoral revision rate, and if it happens the surgeon needs a cable, a different stem or a longer exposure, and the case doubles. The surgeon needs relaxation here more than at any other step: neuromuscular block under general anaesthesia, a dense enough block and adequate sedation under a spinal, because a patient who tenses the quadriceps and hip flexors makes the femur impossible to deliver. The pressure falls as the leg is dropped and rotated and recovers.

### 6. Broaching and stem insertion with the leg in extension
**Why:** Broaches go down the canal with the femur levered up and the leg in extension and external rotation. Most surgeons use short or collared stems designed for this approach because a long straight broach cannot be lined up with the canal through an anterior window, and the collar is a safeguard against subsidence when the fit is less certain. Cementing is possible but awkward, because the gun has to be delivered down a canal that is pointing away from the surgeon.
**Anaesthesia:** The canal is vented with each blow as for any femur, and the cement precautions, if a stem is cemented, are unchanged from the posterior card. The exposure is worse and a stem at the wrong version or subsiding is harder to see, so the fluoroscope comes back to check the stem and the leg length. A collared uncemented stem is the usual choice, which makes this the approach where cement is least often used: a BCIS advantage worth noticing in the frail patient.

### 7. Trial reduction, leg length by fluoroscopy, and the absence of posterior precautions
**Why:** With the leg brought back to neutral the trial head is reduced by traction and internal rotation (the traction table makes this a pedal and a lever). Leg length and offset are read off the fluoroscopy image against the other side rather than by palpating heels, which is the second real advantage of the supine position. Because no posterior structure was cut, the surgeon tests the hip in extension and external rotation for anterior stability rather than in flexion and internal rotation, and writes no precautions.
**Anaesthesia:** Reduction on a traction table is a sharp pull on the leg with the pelvis fixed against the post, the moment of greatest traction on the pudendal nerve and the perineum, and brief. The femoral nerve is the one at risk in this approach, from the anterior retractors and the extended leg, and a quadriceps that will not fire in recovery is that rather than a block. The surgeon's ward instruction is usually "no precautions", which changes the physiotherapy and the discharge timing and is part of why this approach suits a day-case pathway.

### 8. The direct lateral approach: the abductor split
**Why:** In lateral decubitus (or supine in some hands) the surgeon goes straight down onto the trochanter, splits gluteus medius in the line of its fibres from about 3-4 cm above the trochanter down into vastus lateralis, and peels the anterior third of the abductor insertion and vastus lateralis forward off the bone in one flap. The hip is dislocated anteriorly by external rotation. The approach gives a direct view of both the acetabulum and the femur and a dislocation rate at the low end of the three, and it needs no table or fluoroscope. The split must not be carried more than about 5 cm above the trochanter because the superior gluteal nerve runs across gluteus medius there, and cutting it denervates the anterior abductors permanently.
**Anaesthesia:** The positioning and the pelvic clamp physiology are as for the posterior card. What changes is the tissue: a split and repaired gluteus medius is a muscle under tension loaded by every step, which is the muscle mechanism of the Part B table and the reason this approach aches for longer. The dislocation is anterior and the sciatic nerve is further from the field; the femoral nerve and vessels are at risk from an anterior acetabular retractor placed too deep. Heterotopic ossification is more common after this approach in the pooled comparisons, which is the surgeon's reason for asking for an NSAID to be continued, and that fits the analgesic plan anyway.

### 9. Abductor repair and the limp
**Why:** The gluteus medius and vastus lateralis flap is sutured back to the trochanter through bone or to its tendon stump under some tension, and the patient walks on it the next day with crutches. Weight bearing is usually unrestricted but abduction exercises are deferred for six weeks to protect the repair.
**Anaesthesia:** The repair is what the patient feels at day 7 and what the trajectory's final number reflects: a Trendelenburg gait for several weeks is expected, and a limp that persists past three months is a repair that has pulled off or a superior gluteal nerve that was cut, both of which belong to the surgeon. Infiltration into the split muscle is where the surgeon's local anaesthetic earns its place in this approach, and the regular NSAID does double duty for pain and for heterotopic bone.

### 10. Recovery and the first day
**Why:** All three approaches want the patient standing on the day of surgery. The anterior approach wants no precautions and a same-day discharge if the patient qualifies; the lateral approach wants weight bearing on a protected abductor repair.
**Anaesthesia:** The analgesic and day-case logic is the posterior card's. Two approach-specific findings present to you first. After the anterior approach, numbness over the anterolateral thigh is expected and a quadriceps that will not fire is a femoral nerve retraction injury until proven otherwise; after the lateral approach, a hip that will not abduct against gravity in recovery is a superior gluteal nerve injury or a repair that has already failed, and a sedated patient's inability to lift the leg is the spinal, so the examination waits until the block has gone. A wound in the groin crease of an obese patient after the anterior approach is the one to look at before discharge.

## Pain plan

The plan is the posterior card's and the PROSPECT regimen is approach-independent: paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started before or during surgery, a single intravenous dexamethasone dose up to 10 mg, opioids for rescue, and a spinal where there is no contraindication. The block question is answered the same way, with the one note that the PROSPECT recommendation for supra-inguinal fascia iliaca block was derived from trials in the posterolateral approach and the PENG block, which is motor sparing and equivalent in the pooled data, is the author's preference where a block is used after an anterior approach on a day-case pathway, because a quadriceps that works at four o'clock is the whole point of that approach. A lateral femoral cutaneous nerve block is not an analgesic intervention for this operation and PROSPECT lists it as not recommended; the nerve injury is a surgical event and the numbness is not pain.

The direct lateral approach earns the only adjustment. Its muscle mechanism makes the second week ache more than the anterior or posterior hip does, so the oral NSAID is continued longer where the kidneys allow (which the surgeon will want anyway for heterotopic ossification), infiltration into the abductor split is asked for, and the patient is told in advance that the limp and the lateral ache are the repair healing rather than something going wrong. No catheter for any of the three.

Escalation triggers are the posterior card's with the approach-specific substitutions above: anterolateral thigh numbness after the anterior approach is expected and is not an indication for anything; a quadriceps that will not fire is the femoral nerve; a hip that will not abduct after the lateral approach is the superior gluteal nerve or the repair; and a groin-crease wound that is red and wet on day 2 after the anterior approach in an obese patient is the approach's known infection route.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The learning curve. The anterior approach's femoral complications are concentrated early in a surgeon's experience, and every guideline that addresses it says so; a surgeon in their first year of the approach brings longer cases, more blood, and more intra-operative fractures, and a cable or a change of stem mid-case is the sign.
- The traction table versus the free leg. A traction table fixes the pelvis against a post and makes the femoral step a lever and a pedal; a free leg on a standard table avoids the post and the boots but needs a strong assistant and a longer femoral release. Neither changes the pain; both change what you check at the end (perineum and feet with the table, the contralateral hip and knee with the free leg).
- Obesity. The anterior approach's incision sits under the panniculus, the fat is the thickest of any approach and the wound complication rate rises with BMI; many surgeons will not do the anterior approach above a BMI of around 40 and use the posterior instead, so a change of approach on the day for a large patient is not unusual.
- The muscular young man and the stiff hip. The femoral release has to be larger, the lever harder and the fracture risk higher; the surgeon may ask for full neuromuscular block under general anaesthesia and will be grateful for a spinal that is still dense at the one-hour mark.
- Fluoroscopy. Lead for everyone at the head, the C-arm's arc kept clear of the lines and the pump, and a word with the radiographer about where the machine will park, before the drapes go on.
- Resurfacing through the anterior approach. The exposure is larger, the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve injury rate higher (91% in the Ottawa series), and the case longer; see [hip resurfacing](hip-resurfacing.html).
- The anterolateral (Watson-Jones) approach. Between tensor fasciae latae and gluteus medius without detaching the abductors, in lateral decubitus or supine: a middle path with less muscle damage than the direct lateral and a harder femoral exposure than the posterior. The anaesthetic implications are those of whichever position is used.
- Cement through the anterior approach. Possible, awkward, and avoided by many anterior-approach surgeons; if the surgeon does cement, the whole of the posterior card's BCIS step applies with a worse view of the canal.
- Converting to another approach. Rare, but an anterior approach that cannot deliver the femur, or a lateral approach with a fractured trochanter, becomes a longer case through a second window; the blood loss and the duration go up and the plan follows the [revision](revision-tha.html) card's physiology.

## Key sources

The comparative evidence between approaches is large in numbers and poor in quality: registry cohorts confounded by surgeon and patient selection, and small randomised trials with short follow-up. The Australian registry analysis of 122,345 hips is the best single statement of the trade-off (femoral complications against dislocation and infection, all-cause revision the same) and the Dutch guideline's reading of the randomised trials is the fairest summary of the early-function claim. The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve figure comes from a single centre that asked; most series reporting a lower number did not. The analgesic guidance is PROSPECT's and approach-independent; the preference for a PENG block after an anterior approach on a day-case pathway is the author's judgement about motor sparing, not an evidence statement.
