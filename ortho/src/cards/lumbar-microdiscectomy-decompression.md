## What the surgeon is doing and why

A lumbar disc prolapse is nucleus pulposus that has pushed through a tear in the annulus and is sitting on a nerve root in the lateral recess or the foramen. The root is compressed, inflamed and ischaemic, and the patient's pain is in the leg, in the territory of that root, not in the back. Most prolapses shrink on their own over weeks to months, which is why the operation is offered for pain that has not settled, for progressive weakness, or urgently for cauda equina compression. The surgeon's aim is narrow: take the fragment off the root and leave everything else alone. The annulus is not repaired, the segment is not fused, and the back pain the patient also has, from the degenerate disc and the facets, is not treated and will still be there afterwards. That sentence is the pain logic of the card and the content of the pre-operative conversation.

Lumbar canal stenosis is the other indication. A degenerate bulging disc, hypertrophied ligamentum flavum and overgrown facet joints have narrowed the canal and the lateral recesses, usually at several levels in an older patient, and the symptom is neurogenic claudication rather than a single root. The operation is a decompression: a laminectomy or bilateral laminotomy with undercutting of the medial facets and removal of the flavum until the dura and the roots are free. It is a bigger bone-and-muscle operation on an older patient, and the version of this card that stays in hospital.

The microdiscectomy has a fixed sequence: localise the level on fluoroscopy; a 2-4 cm midline incision with the paraspinal muscles stripped subperiosteally off the lamina on one side and held with a self-retaining retractor (tubular systems dilate through the muscle instead, endoscopic systems go smaller still); a laminotomy of a few millimetres and excision of the ligamentum flavum, which is the only bone work and it is small; medial retraction of the dura and the traversing root to expose the prolapse; removal of the fragment through the annular defect; haemostasis and closure. Surgeons vary on how much disc they take: a fragmentectomy that removes the loose piece only gives a faster recovery and a higher recurrence; a subtotal discectomy that cleans out the disc space gives more back pain and a lower recurrence.

The far lateral prolapse, which compresses the exiting root outside the foramen, is reached through a paramedian muscle-splitting (Wiltse) approach; no laminotomy is needed, and the step that hurts is the dorsal root ganglion being handled.

The pain follows from what has been done to tissue. The dominant mechanism is muscle: multifidus has been stripped off bone and crushed under a retractor blade, and in a laminectomy across two or three levels that stripping is bilateral and long. Underneath it is a small bone window (the raw cancellous edge of a laminotomy, or the whole posterior arch gone in a laminectomy) and a nerve root that has been retracted for ten or twenty minutes, which produces the transient dysaesthesia and residual leg symptoms that settle over days as the root recovers. Tubular and endoscopic techniques remove most of the muscle mechanism, which is why they are day cases with less analgesia. The pre-operative pain generator, the compressed root, has been removed, so the patient's dominant symptom is usually gone or much better in recovery. The residual is back and wound pain, worst on day 1, which fades over a week and responds to paracetamol and an NSAID. In the 179-procedure cohort the major spinal operations (fusion and scoliosis) sat at ranks 2, 3 and 6 of all surgery for worst pain on day 1; the discectomy is not among them, and the difference is that between a muscle window and an instrumented, grafted, re-tensioned segment, which has its own card ([posterior lumbar fusion](posterior-lumbar-fusion.html)).

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative neurology, the scan and the plan for prone
**Why:** The surgeon operates on the level the MRI shows and the dermatome the patient describes, and will want them to match. A foot drop, a dense sensory loss or any sphincter symptom changes the urgency and the consent.
**Anaesthesia:** Document the neurology yourself before anything is given: ankle dorsiflexion and great toe extension (L5), plantar flexion (S1), knee extension (L4), the dermatomal sensory loss, and whether the patient has perineal numbness or retention. A deficit that is new in recovery can only be called new if the baseline is written down. Cauda equina compression (bilateral sciatica, saddle anaesthesia, retention, loss of anal tone) is an emergency decompression on the next available list, with a catheter in and the retention volume recorded. Ask about the shoulders and neck before you plan the arm position. A patient with severe radicular pain cannot lie flat and needs analgesia before the induction, or an induction in the lateral position.

### 2. Induction, airway and the turn
**Why:** The surgeon needs the patient prone, flexed at the hips to open the interlaminar space, motionless, and with epidural venous pressure as low as it can be, because the field is a 2 cm window at the bottom of a muscle well and every vein that bleeds obscures it.
**Anaesthesia:** A reinforced or well-secured tracheal tube is the author's default for any prone case; a supraglottic airway is used by some for short microdiscectomies in slim patients and is a decision made with the surgeon and the frame in mind rather than a habit. Secure the tube and the eyes before the turn, disconnect for the log roll, and reconnect and check bilateral air entry and capnography before anyone touches the drapes. Then the checks, in order: eyes free of any pressure in the head support and visible through its mirror or cut-out; nose and chin clear; neck neutral; arms on boards with the shoulders below 90 degrees of abduction and the elbows flexed and padded, so the plexus is not stretched over the humeral head and the ulnar nerve is not on an edge; abdomen hanging free, so the inferior vena cava is not compressed and the epidural veins do not fill; breasts and genitals clear of the frame edges; knees padded; feet off the end of the table. Recheck the eyes and the arms after the surgeon has adjusted the flexion of the frame, because that is when the head moves. There is no neuromonitoring in a routine microdiscectomy, so the choice between volatile and total intravenous anaesthesia, and the use of relaxant, are matters of habit.

### 3. Localisation and the skin incision
**Why:** A needle or marker is placed over the intended interlaminar space and a lateral fluoroscopic image confirms the level against the sacrum. Then a short midline incision over that space, or a 1 cm stab for a tube.
**Anaesthesia:** Cefazolin finished before the knife. Lead aprons on for the localisation film. The surgeon may ask for a pause while the image is read; a wrong-level operation is the disaster the pause prevents. Heavy patients and transitional lumbosacral anatomy are the reasons the film takes longer than expected.

### 4. Muscle stripping and retraction, or tubular dilatation
**Why:** The paraspinal muscles are taken off the spinous process and lamina with cautery and a periosteal elevator, one side only for a microdiscectomy, both sides for a laminectomy, and a self-retaining retractor holds them out of the way. Tubular systems pass serial dilators through the muscle to a tube fixed to the table, with the microscope looking down it.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the mechanism of the post-operative back pain and the moment at which a light patient moves. Muscle stripping is the most stimulating step before the root is touched; deepen or give opioid in anticipation rather than in response. Bleeding is from muscle and is controlled with cautery; a bilateral multilevel exposure in an anticoagulated patient is where the laminectomy blood loss starts. The retractor crushes the muscle it holds, and the longer it stays the more the back hurts tomorrow; a surgeon who releases it periodically is doing the pain plan's work.

### 5. Laminotomy, flavectomy and the bone window
**Why:** A high-speed burr or Kerrison punch takes the inferior edge of the upper lamina and the medial facet, and the ligamentum flavum is lifted off the dura and excised. In stenosis surgery this is the operation: a full laminectomy, or bilateral laminotomies from one side, until the dura pulsates and the roots are free in their recesses.
**Anaesthesia:** The bone work is brief and does not bleed much; the first epidural vein bleeds when the flavum comes off. The surgeon now wants a still field with low venous pressure, and the things that raise it are an abdomen on the frame, coughing or straining, high positive end-expiratory pressure, and a head-down tilt. A modest reverse Trendelenburg tilt and a tidal volume that does not drive the abdomen into the bolsters are the levers. Deliberate hypotension buys nothing and adds risk to a prone patient's eyes and spinal cord.

### 6. Root retraction and discectomy
**Why:** The traversing root and the dural sac are retracted medially with a root retractor, which exposes the prolapse and the posterior longitudinal ligament, and the fragment is removed with pituitary rongeurs. Surgeons vary between removing the loose fragment only and clearing the disc space.
**Anaesthesia:** The root under retraction is the nerve mechanism. A lightly anaesthetised patient will move a leg when the root is pulled, which in a 2 cm window under a microscope is dangerous. The epidural veins around the root bleed into the field and the surgeon's requests for lower pressure usually come now; the honest reply is that venous pressure, not arterial, fills the field and the position and ventilation have already been optimised. Pituitary rongeurs placed deep into the disc space can go through the anterior annulus into the retroperitoneum, where the aorta and the iliac vessels lie: sudden unexplained hypotension or tachycardia during the discectomy, with little blood in the wound, is an anterior vascular injury until proven otherwise. It is rare, often unrecognised until the patient collapses in recovery, and the treatment is a supine patient, a vascular surgeon and blood, not a vasopressor.

### 7. Dural tear and repair
**Why:** The dura is thin, adherent to a prolapse or to scar, and is torn in about 3% of primary microdiscectomies and a fifth of revisions. The surgeon repairs it with a fine suture if it can be reached, adds a sealant, and may ask for a Valsalva to test the repair.
**Anaesthesia:** A tear changes the rest of the day. The surgeon will want a still field for a fiddly repair under the microscope, often with a slight head-up tilt to lower the cerebrospinal fluid pressure at the tear; a sustained inflation to 30-40 cmH2O when asked, to show whether the repair leaks; and a smooth emergence with no coughing or bucking, because every strain tests the repair. The post-operative consequences are a positional headache from cerebrospinal fluid loss, which behaves like a post-dural-puncture headache and is treated as one, and a period of flat bed rest that surgeons vary on: the review evidence is that flat rest beyond 24 h after a watertight repair adds medical complications and length of stay without reducing reoperation, but the instruction is the surgeon's. A tear in a day-case patient means an admission. Document it on the anaesthetic record for the next anaesthetist who considers a spinal or epidural.

### 8. Haemostasis, infiltration and closure
**Why:** Epidural veins are controlled with bipolar diathermy and a haemostatic agent, the root is checked to be free, and the wound is closed in layers. Many surgeons infiltrate the muscle and skin with long-acting local anaesthetic; some place a steroid on the root, and a few leave a drain after a multilevel decompression.
**Anaesthesia:** The infiltration is most of the pain plan and the moment to confirm it has happened. Reverse any relaxant well before the skin closes. A drain after a microdiscectomy tells you the surgeon was worried about bleeding; a laminectomy in an anticoagulated patient is the typical case.

### 9. The turn back and emergence
**Why:** Nothing surgical remains, but the wound sits over an opened epidural space and a cauterised venous plexus; an epidural haematoma is the complication of the first hours.
**Anaesthesia:** Turn supine with the tube secured and the eyes still protected, then inspect the face, the eyes and the arms before the patient wakes: pressure marks on the forehead and cheeks are common and fade, a swollen eye or a complaint of visual change needs an ophthalmological assessment at once. Aim for an emergence without coughing or straining, which raises epidural venous pressure in a fresh field; a dural repair makes this a priority rather than a preference. Extubate when the patient obeys commands and will move both feet to order.

### 10. Recovery, the neurological check and the day-case decision
**Why:** The surgeon wants the leg pain gone, the neurology at baseline or better, and the patient walking within hours; mobilisation is the treatment for the back pain and the prevention of thrombosis.
**Anaesthesia:** Test ankle dorsiflexion, plantar flexion and knee extension against your pre-operative note; a new deficit, or leg pain that is worse than before with a tense wound or increasing back pain, is a haematoma or a retained fragment and the surgeon is called before any opioid is given, because the opioid will mask the progression. Most patients say the leg pain is gone or changed in character; a residual ache or tingling in the same dermatome is the retracted root and settles over days. Retention is common on the first evening after a prone case with opioids and an inflamed root; a bladder scan at four hours prevents the 2 am catheter. Day-case discharge after a single-level microdiscectomy is established practice: the patient walks, passes urine, has pain controlled on oral analgesia, has someone at home and a written note on the red flags (worsening leg pain, new weakness, saddle numbness, inability to pass urine, a leaking wound or a positional headache). A multilevel laminectomy in an older patient stays one to three nights; a drain, an anticoagulant to restart, a dural tear and a bladder are what keep them in.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is stripped and retracted muscle over a small bone window, with the pre-operative pain generator removed, so the plan is a short one: regular paracetamol and an NSAID (or a COX-2 inhibitor) from before the infiltration wears off, a surgeon-infiltrated wound, intravenous dexamethasone at induction for its antiemetic and modest analgesic effect, and oral opioid as rescue for day 0 and 1. The defensible default needs no regional block. Most microdiscectomy patients need a few doses of opioid on the evening of surgery and the following day and then nothing stronger than the oral regimen, and the day-case model depends on exactly that.

The erector spinae plane block is the regional technique with evidence in lumbar surgery, and the evidence is mixed in quality. A 2022 meta-analysis of 15 randomised trials in 980 patients, across discectomy, decompression and fusion, found that a bilateral block reduced opioid consumption at 24 h, lowered pain at rest through the first day and roughly halved nausea and vomiting, with heterogeneity wide enough that no milligram figure can be quoted. PROSPECT, writing for complex spine surgery rather than for discectomy, does not recommend the block for lack of procedure-specific evidence. The author's reconciliation: a bilateral erector spinae plane block at the operative level, placed after induction and before the turn, is worth its ten minutes for a multilevel laminectomy, for a patient who must be opioid-sparing (obstructive sleep apnoea, the frail, the opioid-tolerant) and for the day-case patient whose surgeon does not infiltrate. For a single-level microdiscectomy with a surgeon who infiltrates, it adds little. The catheter question does not arise.

Ketamine, which PROSPECT recommends intra-operatively for complex spine surgery, has no place in a routine microdiscectomy and a sensible one in the opioid-tolerant patient, who is common in this population after months of sciatica on tapentadol or oxycodone and whose plan is in the [opioid-tolerant chapter](../a/a12-opioid-tolerant.html). Gabapentinoids are not recommended by PROSPECT because of their adverse effects; a patient already taking one for radicular pain continues it rather than starting it.

Rebound pain in the usual sense does not happen, because no dense block wears off. What does happen on the first night is back pain and muscle spasm when the infiltration recedes, worse on movement and relieved by walking, and the patient who has been told to expect it does not mistake it for failure. Escalation triggers: pain out of proportion with a tense wound, any new neurology, leg pain that is worse rather than better, or retention are surgical, and the surgeon is called first. A positional headache is a dural leak, declared or not. Pain unchanged from before surgery, in the same leg and distribution, is a root that has not yet recovered or a fragment that was missed: a conversation for the surgeon, not a reason for a stronger opioid.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Microdiscectomy versus laminectomy. The single-level microdiscectomy in a 40-year-old is a 60 minute day case with 100 mL of blood loss. The three-level laminectomy in a 78-year-old on apixaban is a different operation wearing the same name: two hours prone, bilateral stripping, 400 mL from epidural veins, a drain, a night or three in hospital, and a positioning risk that rises with every decade. Plan the second as a fusion without the instrumentation.
- Tubular and endoscopic techniques. The muscle is dilated rather than stripped, the wound is 1-2 cm, the pain is less and the day case is routine. Endoscopic transforaminal discectomy is often done under local anaesthesia and sedation, with the patient able to report leg pain when the exiting root is touched, which is the surgeon's neuromonitoring; oversedation removes that safeguard.
- Spinal anaesthesia. A single-level discectomy or decompression under spinal with sedation, prone on a frame, is a well-described alternative; its limits are the duration of the block, the patient's tolerance of the position awake, and the surgeon's willingness to have a patient who can talk.
- Revision discectomy. Scar between muscle, dura and root; a durotomy rate of nearly one in five; a longer case; more bleeding; more residual leg pain. Not a day case in most hands.
- Cauda equina syndrome. Out of hours, a patient in retention, a surgeon in a hurry, and a decompression that may be wider than planned. The urgency is real and the positioning checks are the thing that gets skipped.
- The obese patient. The frame may not keep the abdomen free, ventilation pressures rise when prone, the epidural veins fill, the field bleeds, and the surgeon asks for hypotension. The answer is the position, not the pressure: a table with the abdomen hanging, reverse Trendelenburg, and a surgeon who accepts a slower dissection.
- The anterior vascular injury. Rongeurs through the anterior annulus into the iliac vessels or the aorta: rare, under-recognised, and presenting as unexplained hypotension with little blood in the wound, or as collapse in recovery.
- Postoperative visual loss. Rare after a short prone case, but the risk factors are those of the long fusions (see [spine essentials](../a/a14-spine-essentials.html)): pressure on the globe, a long case, large blood loss, anaemia, hypotension and a head-down position. A single-level microdiscectomy carries almost none of them, which is why the eye check at the turn is the one that matters.
- Position-related nerve injury. Brachial plexus from arms abducted beyond 90 degrees, ulnar nerve on the board edge, lateral femoral cutaneous nerve on the iliac crest bolster, and a face or breast pressure mark; all checked at the turn and again after the surgeon adjusts the frame.

## Key sources

The evidence base for this operation's analgesia is thin because the operation does not hurt much, and the spine literature has concentrated on fusions. PROSPECT's complex spine guideline is cited for what it excludes as much as for what it recommends: single-level discectomy is outside its definition, and its advice on paracetamol, NSAID and opioid-as-rescue transfers downwards while its advice on ketamine and epidural catheters does not. The erector spinae plane block meta-analysis pools heterogeneous trials and shows a real but modest opioid-sparing effect rather than the size of it. The durotomy figures come from a narrative review of largely retrospective series. The Gerbershagen cohort is cited for the contrast between discectomy and fusion, not for a discectomy rank.
