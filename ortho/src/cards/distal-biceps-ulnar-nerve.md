## What the surgeon is doing and why

Two operations on the front and the inside of the elbow, paired because they share a position, a tourniquet and a day-case pathway, and because they sit at opposite ends of the question this book keeps asking: whether a block helps, and whether it hides something the surgeon needs to see.

The distal biceps tendon inserts on the radial tuberosity, a bump on the medial side of the radius 2 cm below the radial head, and it ruptures in one setting: a man in his forties or fifties, often heavily built or a weight trainer, takes an eccentric load on a flexed elbow and feels a pop. The tendon retracts into the arm, the muscle belly rides up, and the forearm loses much of its supination strength and some flexion strength if nothing is done. Non-operative treatment is reasonable in a sedentary patient; everyone else is repaired within two or three weeks, before the tendon shortens, scars to the brachialis, and can no longer reach the tuberosity without a graft.

The surgeon's problem is that the tuberosity faces posteriorly and medially and the only ways to it pass the radial nerve. The single anterior incision goes through the antecubital fossa, between brachioradialis and pronator teres, retracts the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve laterally (it is injured, usually transiently, in about 9% of repairs, and more often through a small incision than an extensile one), finds the recurrent radial vessels and ties them, and reaches the tuberosity with the forearm fully supinated to roll the bone towards the surgeon and the posterior interosseous nerve away. The nerve wraps around the radial neck inside the supinator a finger's breadth from the tuberosity, and a retractor on the lateral side of the neck, a drill that exits the far cortex, or a cortical button flipped on the wrong side of it, are the mechanisms of a palsy; it happens in about 1.6-2.7% of repairs and mostly resolves. The double-incision technique, which the trial of 91 men found gave a 10% advantage in flexion strength for the same function scores, retrieves the tendon through a small anterior incision, passes it between radius and ulna, and fixes it through a second posterolateral incision with a muscle split of the extensor mass and bone tunnels in the tuberosity; it trades the nerve risk (lateral antebrachial cutaneous neuropraxia 2.2% versus 9.8%, posterior interosseous palsy 0.2% versus 2.7%) for heterotopic ossification and, rarely, a radioulnar synostosis (ossification or synostosis 9.8% versus 3.2%), because the ulnar periosteum and the interosseous membrane are disturbed on the way through.

Whichever route, the fixation is the same idea: the tuberosity is decorticated or a socket drilled into it, the tendon end is whip-stitched, and it is pulled down and fixed with a cortical button, an interference screw, suture anchors, or sutures through bone tunnels. The arm is flexed and supinated to get the tendon to reach; a tendon that has retracted for weeks has to be dragged, and a chronic rupture over about six weeks gets a graft because it will not reach at all. After the repair the forearm is immobilised in flexion and supination, then moved early but unloaded for six weeks, with a no-lifting rule that the patient population finds hard to keep: re-rupture (1.4-2.5%) clusters in the first three weeks and is attributed to non-compliance.

Ulnar neuropathy at the elbow is a nerve compressed or stretched in a tunnel that narrows with flexion. The nerve runs behind the medial epicondyle under the cubital tunnel retinaculum, between the two heads of flexor carpi ulnaris, and is tethered proximally by the arcade of Struthers and the medial intermuscular septum. The surgeon has three choices and the randomised evidence says they are equivalent: simple in situ decompression (open the roof of the tunnel and the fascia of flexor carpi ulnaris and leave the nerve where it is), anterior subcutaneous transposition (free the nerve over 15 cm, divide the septum, move it in front of the epicondyle into a fat pocket), or submuscular transposition (the same, with the flexor-pronator origin detached from the epicondyle and repaired over the nerve). In the 152-patient randomised trial decompression and subcutaneous transposition gave the same proportion of good results at a year (49 of 75 versus 54 of 77), with complications in 9.6% after decompression and 31.1% after transposition, and the meta-analyses agree that there is no difference in outcome. Decompression is therefore the modern default, with transposition kept for a nerve that subluxes over the epicondyle, a revision, or a nerve that has to be moved for an elbow operation.

The pain logic follows. A biceps repair is tendon-tension in its purest form: a strong, retracted tendon fixed to drilled bone under tension in a young muscular patient, then loaded by every attempt to supinate or flex for weeks. The early peak is a bone-and-tendon peak and the course is prolonged by movement, the shape of a cuff repair but in a joint that is easier to rest, so it earns a 3 rather than a 5; the patient demographic (young, male, opioid-naive) puts rebound risk high. Ulnar nerve decompression is a nerve mechanism: a neuropathic ache and dysaesthesia often better the next morning than the day before surgery, with an incision that hurts for a few days. A transposition adds muscle (the flexor-pronator origin taken down and repaired, in the submuscular version) and a bigger wound, and sits at a 2. The card carries the biceps score because the biceps is the operation that needs a plan.

## Steps

### 1. Documenting the nerves and deciding the block
**Why:** The surgeon is about to work within a centimetre of the posterior interosseous nerve, retract the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve for an hour, and in the ulnar case operate on the nerve itself; they will want to know afterwards which deficit is new.
**Anaesthesia:** Before any drug, examine and write down wrist and finger extension (posterior interosseous), sensation over the lateral forearm (lateral antebrachial cutaneous), and for the ulnar case little finger sensation, first dorsal interosseous power and a Froment's sign. For a biceps repair, the author's practice is a single-shot infraclavicular or supraclavicular block with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, placed awake before induction, after the surgeon has agreed that the nerve check will wait for the block to recede. For an ulnar nerve decompression or a subcutaneous transposition, no block: the operation is short, the pain is a 2 that infiltration covers, and the surgeon wants the little finger examined in recovery. A submuscular transposition is the author's exception, where a block is reasonable if the surgeon accepts the delay.

### 2. Position, tourniquet and the airway
**Why:** Supine with the arm on a table, the forearm supinated for the anterior approach. The tourniquet goes high on the arm. For the ulnar nerve, the shoulder is externally rotated and the elbow flexed on the table to present the medial side; some surgeons prefer the arm across the chest.
**Anaesthesia:** A supraglottic airway and spontaneous ventilation are reasonable for every operation on this card; a tracheal tube for the reflux-prone or the heavily built biceps patient. Cefazolin 2 g IV before inflation for the implant case. The external rotation needed for the medial elbow strains a stiff shoulder and is the position to check after the surgeon has set it. Tourniquet time runs 45-90 min for a biceps repair and under 45 min for a decompression; a submuscular transposition is longer.

### 3. Anterior incision and the lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve
**Why:** A transverse or S-shaped incision in the antecubital crease, then the interval between brachioradialis and pronator teres. The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve runs in the subcutaneous fat lateral to the biceps tendon and is retracted laterally for the rest of the case; the recurrent radial vessels cross the field and are ligated.
**Anaesthesia:** The nerve retracted here is the commonest complication of the operation (9.2% in 3,091 repairs), and it is a sensory nerve: a numb patch over the lateral forearm that usually resolves. It is also the nerve whose distribution a patient will describe the next day as "the block has not worn off", which is why the pre-operative sensory map matters. Nothing to do at this step but note the time.

### 4. Retrieving the tendon
**Why:** The tendon end is found, often retracted into the arm and scarred to the brachialis; the surgeon milks it distally with the elbow flexed, excises the frayed end, and whip-stitches it. A tendon more than a few weeks old may need a separate proximal incision or a graft.
**Anaesthesia:** The elbow is flexed hard and the muscle belly pulled on; in an unblocked patient this is a stimulating moment, and in a blocked patient it is the moment the surgeon finds out whether the tendon will reach. If it will not, the case lengthens by an hour for a graft and the pain course shifts up.

### 5. Exposing the tuberosity and protecting the posterior interosseous nerve
**Why:** With the forearm in maximal supination, the radial tuberosity rotates into the wound and the posterior interosseous nerve, in the supinator on the far side of the neck, rotates away. A retractor is placed carefully, never levered against the lateral side of the neck. In the double-incision technique the surgeon instead passes a clamp between radius and ulna from the anterior wound to the posterolateral skin and makes the second incision there, splitting the extensor mass with the forearm pronated and staying off the ulna.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step the trap is about. A posterior interosseous palsy presents as a wrist drop with intact wrist extension in radial deviation (extensor carpi radialis longus is spared) and no sensory loss; the mechanisms are retraction, a drill exiting the far cortex, and a cortical button that flips into the nerve. The surgeon will want the forearm held in full supination and still; a request for stillness is a request for depth or for a hand on the forearm, not neuromuscular block. In the double-incision technique the pronation and supination of the forearm between the two wounds is where the arm board and the tourniquet get dislodged.

### 6. Preparing the tuberosity and fixing the tendon under tension
**Why:** The tuberosity is decorticated with a burr, or a socket drilled to the far cortex for a button or a screw, or bone tunnels made for a double-incision repair. The tendon is pulled down with the elbow flexed and the forearm supinated and fixed with a cortical button through the far cortex, an interference screw into the socket, suture anchors, or sutures tied over a bone bridge. Some surgeons let the tourniquet down before the final tensioning so that the muscle is at its resting length and the repair is not set too tight.
**Anaesthesia:** The mechanism this card is built around exists from the moment the tendon is fixed: bone drilled and a tendon tied to it under tension in a young patient. The bone component (socket, decortication) is the reason the rebound-pain odds are those of bone surgery. If the surgeon drops the tourniquet before tensioning, expect the reperfusion dip and a bleeding antecubital fossa in the middle of the critical step rather than at the end.

### 7. Closure, the splint and the position of the forearm
**Why:** Haemostasis after tourniquet release (the recurrent radial vessels and the brachial artery are in the field), layered closure, and a posterior splint with the elbow at about 90 degrees and the forearm supinated so the repair is unloaded. Early protected motion starts at one to two weeks; no lifting for six.
**Anaesthesia:** Give the antiemetic. The splint goes on before the patient lightens; a young man with a dense block will otherwise flex and supinate the arm with the other hand to look at it. A tense antecubital fossa in recovery is a haematoma from a recurrent branch and is surgical; it compresses the median nerve and the brachial artery, so a hand that is cooler than the other or a new numb thumb is examined rather than observed.

### 8. Ulnar nerve: the medial approach and in situ decompression
**Why:** A 4-8 cm incision behind the medial epicondyle, protecting the branches of the medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve that cross it (the usual cause of a painful scar), then division of the cubital tunnel retinaculum, the fascia between the two heads of flexor carpi ulnaris and, proximally, the arcade of Struthers. The nerve is left in its bed and the elbow is flexed on the table to check that it does not subluxate forward over the epicondyle; if it does, the surgeon moves to a transposition.
**Anaesthesia:** Surgeon infiltration with 10-15 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% or bupivacaine 0.25% along the wound and a general anaesthetic or sedation; the operation is 20-45 min. The nerve itself should not be stimulated in a decompression, but a tug on it in a lightly sedated patient produces a jolt into the little finger that the patient will remember. A medial antebrachial cutaneous branch cut here gives a numb or painful patch on the medial forearm that outlasts the nerve symptoms.

### 9. Ulnar nerve: anterior transposition
**Why:** When the nerve subluxes, or at revision, the nerve is freed over about 15 cm with its vessels where possible, the medial intermuscular septum is excised so the nerve does not kink over it, and it is moved in front of the epicondyle. Subcutaneous: a fascial sling or a fat pocket holds it. Submuscular: the flexor-pronator origin is lifted off the epicondyle, the nerve laid on the muscle bed next to the median nerve, and the origin repaired over it. A medial epicondylectomy is a third option that removes the bone the nerve was riding over.
**Anaesthesia:** The submuscular version is the one with a muscle mechanism, a repaired flexor origin protected for a few weeks, and a 90 min tourniquet; it is the one ulnar operation where a block is reasonable, and even here the surgeon usually wants the hand examined in recovery because a nerve devascularised in the move declares itself early. Complications were three times more frequent after transposition than decompression in the randomised trial (31.1% versus 9.6%); a transposition patient in recovery with a new dense ulnar deficit is examined by the surgeon, not attributed to infiltration.

### 10. Recovery, the first night and discharge
**Why:** The surgeon wants the biceps repair protected and the nerve patient's hand working, and both patients home the same day.
**Anaesthesia:** For the biceps patient, the oral regimen starts in recovery: paracetamol and an NSAID, and an oral opioid for the first three nights. The block recedes at 12-24 h and this patient is the rebound-pain phenotype of the 972-patient cohort (bone surgery OR 1.82, younger age, and the dexamethasone given to blunt it); the pain rises over an hour or two to its day 1 level, the worst of the course, and the written night plan is what keeps him from the emergency department. The nerve check happens when the block is gone, at the agreed time, and a wrist drop then is a call to the surgeon. For the ulnar patient, paracetamol and an NSAID with a few opioid doses for two nights; the little finger is examined before discharge and the patient told that the tingling may be better tomorrow or may take months, depending on how long the nerve was compressed.

## Pain plan

Two mechanisms, two plans. The biceps repair is tendon-tension on a drilled bone in the patient most likely to have rebound pain, and the plan has to cover the first night and expect a hard day 1 and 2. The ulnar nerve operation is a nerve mechanism with a small wound, where the plan has to not hide the hand.

For the biceps repair the defensible default is a single-shot infraclavicular or supraclavicular block with 20-25 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%, intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, paracetamol and an NSAID started before the block recedes and continued for a week, and an oral opioid for the first three nights, with a written night plan. The patient is young, male and will be at home with a flail arm; he needs the splint explained, the no-lifting rule explained with the re-rupture figure attached to it, and someone with him on the first night. A catheter is rarely justified: the operation is a day case, the joint is splinted and rested rather than moved, and a flail arm for two days in a patient who will not keep still outweighs the gain. The exceptions are author's judgement: a chronic reconstruction with a graft, a bilateral or revision case, and a patient who has had a catastrophic rebound night before. There is no procedure-specific guideline and no randomised analgesia trial for distal biceps repair; the plan is the mechanism and the general ambulatory block data.

For the ulnar nerve the defensible default is surgeon infiltration, general anaesthesia or sedation, paracetamol and an NSAID for a few days, and a couple of opioid doses for the first two nights, with no block, because the operation's only serious complication is a nerve that is worse, and that is found by examining the hand in recovery. A submuscular transposition is the exception where a block is reasonable if the surgeon agrees. A patient already on a gabapentinoid or an antidepressant for neuropathic pain continues it.

Escalation for the biceps patient: pain above 7 on the first night with a soft antecubital fossa, warm fingers and the splint on is the block wearing off and is treated with the charted opioid; pain with a tense fossa, a cool hand or a new numb thumb is a haematoma on the median nerve and brachial artery and is surgical; a wrist drop after the block has gone is a posterior interosseous palsy and is the surgeon's to follow. For the ulnar patient, pain is rarely the problem; a dense new motor deficit is.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Single versus double incision. Same function; the double incision gives more flexion strength, fewer nerve injuries and more heterotopic ossification, and adds a posterolateral muscle split and the pronation-supination shuffle during the case. The anaesthetic is the same.
- Chronic rupture and graft. Over about six weeks the tendon will not reach; an allograft or autograft is woven in, the case runs to 150 min, the tourniquet is let down and re-inflated, and this is the patient for whom the catheter question is worth asking.
- Heterotopic ossification. Mostly after double-incision repairs; some surgeons give an NSAID for prophylaxis, which is the regular NSAID you were charting anyway.
- The weight trainer. Anabolic steroid use is over-represented in distal biceps ruptures; ask, because it changes the airway, the cardiovascular risk and the opioid tolerance.
- The lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve. Numb lateral forearm in 9% of repairs, more with small incisions; it looks like a residual block to the patient and the ward, and the pre-operative sensory map is what separates them.
- Posterior interosseous palsy. 1.6-2.7%; retraction, drill and button. Document before, examine after the block, and treat a persisting palsy as the surgeon's to image and explore.
- Ulnar nerve decompression versus transposition. Equivalent outcomes, a third of the complications with decompression; transposition for a subluxing nerve or a revision, submuscular for the thin patient whose subcutaneous nerve would sit on the desk edge.
- The nerve decompressed as part of an elbow operation. Transposition is bundled with total elbow arthroplasty, distal humerus fixation and some releases; there the nerve plan belongs to the main operation's card.
- Pre-existing neuropathic pain. A long-compressed nerve with wasting does not recover its motor function and may not lose its pain; set the expectation before surgery and continue the neuropathic agents.

## Key sources

The complication data for distal biceps repair are consistent across two systematic reviews and the randomised single-versus-double-incision trial, and they are what the trap and the nerve documentation rest on. The ulnar nerve evidence is better still: one adequately sized randomised trial and two meta-analyses agreeing that decompression and transposition give the same result and that transposition carries more complications. There is no analgesia trial for either operation and no PROSPECT guideline; the block and catheter advice is author's judgement from the mechanism and the general ambulatory block literature, and the score of 3 for a biceps repair is calibrated against the Part B anchors rather than against measured pain data, which do not exist for this operation.
