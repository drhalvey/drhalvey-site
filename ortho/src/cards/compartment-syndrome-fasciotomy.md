## What the surgeon is doing and why

A muscle compartment is a closed box: bone on one side, inelastic fascia on the others, with muscle, nerves and the vessels that feed them inside. After a fracture, a crush, a reperfusion or a tight cast the contents swell or bleed, and because the box cannot expand the pressure rises. The arterial pulse is not the casualty. Capillary flow depends on the gradient between arteriolar pressure and tissue pressure, and once tissue pressure comes within about 30 mmHg of the diastolic pressure the capillaries empty, the muscle becomes ischaemic, ischaemic muscle swells further, and the loop closes on itself. Nerve conduction fails within an hour or two; muscle is dead after six to eight hours at body temperature. The foot stays pink and the pulse stays palpable throughout, which is why waiting for pallor and pulselessness is waiting for an amputation.

The surgeon's only treatment is to open the box. A fasciotomy divides the skin and the fascia along the full length of every involved compartment, lets the muscle bulge out, and leaves the wound open because the muscle will keep swelling for 24 to 48 hours and a closed skin is as tight an envelope as the fascia was. Timing is the whole outcome: in the classic series normal function followed 68% of releases done within 12 h of onset and 8% of those done later, with complications in 4.5% and 54%. The British standard is that decompression follows the diagnosis immediately, and that the wound is re-explored within 72 h.

The diagnosis is the difficult part, and it is the part you share. The textbook signs are pain out of proportion to the injury, pain on passive stretch of the muscles in the compartment (the toes dorsiflexed for the deep posterior and flexed for the anterior), a tense compartment, and paraesthesia in the nerve that runs through it. Their test characteristics are poor in one direction: across four prospective studies each sign had a sensitivity of 13-19% and a specificity of 97%, so their absence is reassuring and any one of them alone gives a probability of only about 25%, rising to 93% with three. Pressure measurement fills the gap. The threshold that survived is the differential pressure, diastolic minus compartment pressure, below 30 mmHg; it outperformed absolute pressures in the original 116-fracture study, and with continuous monitoring in 850 tibial fractures it had a sensitivity of 94% and a specificity of 98%. Note what the threshold contains: the diastolic pressure is yours. A hypotensive patient under anaesthesia has a lower differential pressure for the same compartment, and a single reading taken at a diastolic of 45 mmHg on the table overcalls the diagnosis, which is why surgeons who measure in theatre ask for the pre-induction diastolic.

Who gets it: the young man with a tibial shaft fracture above all, then high-energy tibial plateau fracture-dislocations, forearm fractures, crush injuries, the revascularised limb, the limb that lay under an unconscious body, and occasionally the well leg after hours in lithotomy. An open fracture is not exempt (see [open fractures](open-fractures-damage-control.html)), and the tibial risk shapes the block on the [tibial card](tibial-nailing-plateau.html).

The regional anaesthesia controversy needs stating exactly, because it is argued louder than the evidence allows. The surgical fear is that a block removes the one symptom that prompts the examination. The evidence for and against is almost entirely case reports. The 2009 systematic review found 28 reports and series, 23 of them about epidurals: in 32 of 35 patients the classic signs were present despite the epidural, 18 with documented breakthrough pain, and the authors found no convincing evidence that patient-controlled opioid or regional analgesia delays diagnosis when the patient is properly watched. A 2016 review of 34 articles reached the same reading. A 2020 surgical review of peripheral blocks in long bone fractures found six single-patient reports, two with a delayed diagnosis and four without, and concluded the opposite way: that no conclusion is possible and blocks belong in research settings. Both readings are honest descriptions of the same thin literature. The newer comparative data are larger and reassuring without being decisive: among 791 operative tibial fractures the rate of missed compartment syndrome was 0.7% with a nerve block and 1.7% without, not different, but all four missed cases in the block group had a catheter; and a 2026 systematic review of five comparative studies and 105,139 trauma patients found no study in which regional anaesthesia raised the incidence or delayed the diagnosis. The Association of Anaesthetists turned this into a workable rule in 2021: avoid dense blocks that far outlast the surgery, accept dilute blocks without adjuncts when the surveillance is real, chart the observations, document the patient's consent, and neither specialty holds a veto. The British orthopaedic standard says the same from the other side: a joint decision and an agreed monitoring policy. The Part A chapter on [nerve injury and compartment syndrome](../a/a11-nerve-injury-compartment.html) carries the full argument; this card is about the limb in which the diagnosis has been made.

The pain logic changes at the moment of release. Before it, the pain is ischaemic muscle, unresponsive to opioid, and it is the diagnostic signal. After it, the mechanism is muscle in the Part B sense: a large soft tissue wound with swollen muscle exposed over 20-30 cm on each side of the limb, re-traumatised at every dressing change and second look, then closed under tension or grafted. Add a neuropathic element in any patient whose nerves were ischaemic for hours, and usually a fresh fracture and its fixation. The score is 4: no single day is as bad as a knee replacement's first, but the plateau runs for a fortnight because the operation is repeated.

## Steps

### 1. The call: surveillance, examination and the pressure
**Why:** The surgeon is trying to make a diagnosis from soft signs in the first 48 h after an injury or operation. The standard is an hourly documented assessment of every at-risk limb, release of all circumferential dressings and re-evaluation within 30 min when symptoms appear, and a pressure measurement when the picture is unclear or the patient cannot report.
**Anaesthesia:** You are often the person who generates the first signal. A patient whose opioid requirement climbs through the night, who has breakthrough pain through a working epidural or block, or whose pain is not touched by a bolus that worked 4 h earlier has the pattern that 18 of the 35 reported epidural cases showed before anyone acted. Treat it as a surgical referral, not a pain round problem. Stop or reduce an infusion only if the surgeon wants an examination without it, and do not top up. For the sedated, intubated or head-injured patient there is no symptom to mask and no examination to trust, and continuous pressure monitoring is the only surveillance; say so when the polytrauma patient leaves theatre.

### 2. The decision and the trip to theatre
**Why:** Once the diagnosis is made the muscle has hours. The surgeon wants the next available theatre, not the next list.
**Anaesthesia:** This is a true emergency in which fasting does not earn a delay; rapid sequence induction. The patient is commonly a young man in severe pain who has had large opioid doses, with a full stomach from the injury. Take three results into theatre if they exist: potassium, creatinine kinase and creatinine. A limb that has been ischaemic for many hours, a crush, or a revascularisation makes this a reperfusion case. Avoid suxamethonium in the established crush or the limb more than several hours ischaemic with a raised potassium; rocuronium is the default. Maintain the diastolic pressure near the patient's normal from induction: every 10 mmHg you lose is 10 mmHg off the perfusion gradient of a muscle that has none to spare.

### 3. No block, and why
**Why:** After the release the surgeon needs to know what the nerves are doing. A foot drop found the next morning is either ischaemic injury that will declare its prognosis over days, an incompletely released compartment that needs to go back today, or your block, and only the last is avoidable.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with opioid, ketamine 0.25-0.5 mg/kg IV and paracetamol 1 g IV, and no regional technique at the index operation: the author's practice, on the reasoning above rather than on trial evidence. A neuraxial technique is wrong for a second reason, since it lowers the diastolic pressure in a patient whose perfusion gradient is the pathology. An NSAID waits for the creatinine and the urine colour.

### 4. Position, preparation and the uninflated tourniquet
**Why:** The whole limb is in the field so that both sides of the leg (or the volar and dorsal forearm) can be reached and the incisions extended. Any cast, backslab or bandage has been split to skin already.
**Anaesthesia:** Supine, a bolster under the calf, the limb at heart level rather than elevated, because elevation lowers arterial inflow without lowering compartment pressure. No tourniquet inflation. Warm the patient; cefazolin 2 g IV before the knife. If pressures are to be measured on the table, give the surgeon the pre-induction diastolic, not the one on the monitor.

### 5. The release: two incisions, four compartments
**Why:** For the leg, an anterolateral incision centred between the tibial crest and the fibula opens the anterior and lateral compartments either side of the intermuscular septum, with the superficial peroneal nerve at risk in the distal third. A posteromedial incision 2 cm behind the tibial border opens the superficial posterior compartment, and then soleus is taken off the tibia to reach the deep posterior compartment, the one most often missed. Both incisions run nearly the length of the leg, because a short skin incision is a persisting constriction. The forearm is released through a long volar incision that includes the carpal tunnel and usually a dorsal one; the thigh usually through a single lateral incision.

```
   +-----tibia-----+

   |------| DEEP   |   ANT + LAT
   | LAT  | POST   |
   |fibula|--------|   medial incision:
   |  SUPERFICIAL  |   SUP POST + DEEP POST
   |   POSTERIOR   |
   +---------------+

```

**Anaesthesia:** Reperfusion happens here, in proportion to how much muscle was ischaemic and for how long. After a few hours in one leg it is a non-event. After a crush, a revascularised limb or a late presentation, expect a fall in pressure, a rise in end-tidal carbon dioxide, and potassium, lactate and myoglobin delivered to the circulation over minutes: have calcium chloride 10 mL of 10% IV to hand, a recent potassium, and the fluid running before the fascia is opened. Skin edges and bulging muscle bleed steadily, and without a cuff every millilitre is real.

### 6. Muscle assessment and debridement
**Why:** The surgeon judges each muscle by colour, consistency, contractility to a pinch or the diathermy, and capacity to bleed. Frankly dead muscle is excised now; doubtful muscle is left for the second look, because muscle that was dusky at the index operation is often alive at 48 h.
**Anaesthesia:** Contractility is tested on unparalysed muscle, so let the relaxant wear off after intubation and do not redose. The amount of dead muscle is your forecast for the kidneys: a creatinine kinase in the tens of thousands, dark urine and a debrided compartment mean a urinary catheter, crystalloid to a urine output of 1-2 mL/kg/h and potassium rechecked within the hour. A late presentation with a whole dead compartment is the case in which the surgeon may reasonably not operate at all, because opening dead muscle converts a contracture into an infected wound; the standard asks for two consultants and renal protection for that decision.

### 7. Fracture stabilisation at the same sitting
**Why:** If the cause is a fracture it is stabilised now, because an unstable fracture keeps bleeding into the open compartments: an external fixator in most hands, a nail if the patient and the wound allow.
**Anaesthesia:** This doubles or triples the operating time and moves the case onto the [tibial](tibial-nailing-plateau.html) or [open fracture](open-fractures-damage-control.html) card. The second-hit logic of the [polytrauma card](polytrauma-sequencing.html) applies if the patient has other injuries; a fasciotomy with a fixator is the damage control version.

### 8. Dressing the open wounds
**Why:** The wounds are left open. Most surgeons place staples along the skin edges with elastic vessel loops laced across them (the shoelace), which stops the skin retracting and lets them tighten it every day or two, under a negative pressure dressing or a bulky absorbent one. Plastic surgery is told within 24 h.
**Anaesthesia:** The loss does not stop with the dressing: a negative pressure canister on two leg wounds collects several hundred millilitres of serosanguinous fluid over the first day, with protein and haemoglobin that the fluid chart does not count. Extubate awake. Write three things for the ward: the neurological examination to be done once the patient is awake (now the baseline), the haemoglobin and potassium times, and an analgesic plan that assumes an opioid-experienced patient with a large wound.

### 9. The second look at 48-72 h
**Why:** Muscle that was doubtful has declared itself and is debrided or kept. The swelling has peaked; the shoelace is tightened or the wound partly closed. A second and third return follow at the same interval until the skin meets or a graft is planned.
**Anaesthesia:** A short general anaesthetic in a patient who is now fasted, anaemic and on regular opioid, and the first sensible moment for regional anaesthesia: all compartments are open, the baseline deficit (if any) is recorded, and the block cannot hide what has already been treated. A popliteal sciatic catheter with a saphenous block makes the dressing changes and the next two operations tolerable and is the author's choice for the patient facing a graft; a single shot at each sitting is the alternative. Recheck the haemoglobin before each return, because transfusion at the third sitting is common after an uncounted fortnight of ooze.

### 10. Closure or split skin graft
**Why:** Delayed primary closure is the aim, usually the medial leg wound first, by progressive dermatotraction; in the pooled series most wounds closed this way within an average of under 10 days. The lateral wound often will not close and is covered with a split skin graft from the thigh.
**Anaesthesia:** Closure under tension is the most painful of the later sittings and a skin graft adds a donor site that hurts more than the grafted wound. A block that covers the leg does not cover the lateral thigh: add a lateral femoral cutaneous or fascia iliaca block, or ask for the donor site to be infiltrated. A wound closed too tightly can recreate the problem, so the first night after closure is a surveillance night again and a dense new block is the wrong choice on that day; run the existing catheter dilute or stop it.

### 11. After the wounds
**Why:** The scars are large and the limb is changed. In a 60-patient follow-up, 77% had altered sensation at the wound margins, 26% tethered scars, 25% a swollen limb, 13% recurrent ulceration, 13% muscle herniation, 10% persistent wound pain, and 12% changed occupation.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient who had a long ischaemic time may have a neuropathic pain picture by the second week: burning in the nerve territory, allodynia over the graft. That is the point for the pain service and an antineuropathic agent, not a continuing rise in opioid.

## Pain plan

The plan has two halves divided by the release. Before decompression, in any limb at risk, the aim is analgesia that leaves the warning intact: the dilute, adjunct-free, surveillance-backed approach of the Association of Anaesthetists guideline, described on the [tibial card](tibial-nailing-plateau.html). Nothing in the comparative evidence shows that a block causes a missed diagnosis, and the one signal in the 791-fracture series is that every missed case with a block had a catheter, so the catheter is the device to hold back.

At the index fasciotomy: no block. Opioid, ketamine and paracetamol under general anaesthesia, then a patient-controlled opioid with a low-dose ketamine infusion (0.1-0.2 mg/kg/h) for the first 48 h in a patient who has usually had two days of escalating opioid already. The reason is diagnostic rather than analgesic: the post-release neurological examination distinguishes an incomplete release from an ischaemic injury, and it needs unblocked nerves. This is the author's judgement; a surgeon who has released all four compartments under direct vision and is content may reasonably accept a block at the index operation, and no evidence says they are wrong.

From the second look: regional anaesthesia has its strongest claim in the whole trauma section, because the mechanism is now a large soft tissue wound operated on repeatedly and the contraindication has been surgically removed. For the leg, a popliteal sciatic catheter with ropivacaine 0.2% and a single-shot saphenous or adductor canal block; for the forearm, an infraclavicular or axillary catheter. Catheter decision: selective. It is worth placing when the surgeon expects three or more returns or a graft, and not for a limb closed at the first second look. A catheter in an open, colonised limb is taken out at closure or at seven days, whichever is first.

Oral regimen shape: regular paracetamol; an NSAID only once the creatinine and creatinine kinase are falling; a regular modified-release or scheduled immediate-release opioid with a weaning plan written at the start, since this is a two-week inpatient course in a young patient; and a gabapentinoid or tricyclic for a neuropathic picture rather than by routine. Rebound when a single-shot block wears off after a second look is moderate: a wound pain that the regular regimen holds.

Escalation: pain rising after the release is not expected and has three causes worth naming. An incompletely released compartment (usually the deep posterior) presents with the original pain and a tense calf medially and goes back to theatre. Infection of dead muscle presents at day 3-5 with fever and a wound that smells. A wound closed too tightly presents the night after closure. None of them is treated with a bolus through the catheter.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The forearm. Volar release including the carpal tunnel and lacertus fibrosus, often a dorsal incision as well; supine with an arm table. The median nerve is the structure at stake and the hand is examined afterwards for the same reason the foot is. A brachial plexus catheter from the second look on the same terms as the leg.
- The thigh. Rare, nearly always blunt high-energy trauma, a femoral fracture in about half; a single lateral incision releases anterior and posterior compartments in most series. The blood loss is that of the femoral fracture and the patient is usually a polytrauma patient.
- The foot and the hand. Small compartments, contested indications; the British standard records no consensus on the foot and asks only for a documented justification.
- The well leg. Compartment syndrome in the uninjured leg after prolonged lithotomy or hemilithotomy on a traction table, with calf supports and hypotension as the mechanism. The patient wakes with calf pain nobody expects, often under a spinal or epidural; the breakthrough pain rule applies.
- Crush syndrome and the late presentation. Hyperkalaemia, myoglobinuria and acute kidney injury are the anaesthetic; the reperfusion at release can arrest a patient with a potassium already at 6 mmol/L. A dead compartment more than a day old may be left closed by a two-consultant decision.
- The revascularised limb. Prophylactic fasciotomy at the same sitting as the arterial repair is routine after more than 4-6 h of ischaemia; the sequence is on the [open fracture card](open-fractures-damage-control.html).
- The patient who cannot report. Sedated, intubated, head-injured, spinal cord injured, a child, or under a dense block placed elsewhere: examination is worthless and pressure monitoring is the surveillance.
- Hypotension as a cause. Deliberate or tolerated hypotension in a patient with a swollen limb lowers the differential pressure directly. The fasciotomy rate in a ventilated polytrauma patient is partly a function of the diastolic pressure you keep.
- Anticoagulated patients and the coagulopathic. A spontaneous or minor-trauma compartment syndrome in a patient on an anticoagulant bleeds briskly once opened; reverse before the incision where time allows and have blood in the room.
- Chronic exertional compartment syndrome. An elective, limited fasciotomy in an athlete with closed wounds: a day case with a score of 2, and none of this card applies.

## Key sources

The diagnostic evidence is better than its reputation: clinical signs with poor sensitivity and good specificity, and a differential pressure threshold validated in one unit's 850 monitored tibial fractures. The timing evidence is a 1976 series that no one will repeat. The regional anaesthesia question has no randomised evidence and never will at this incidence; the two systematic reviews of case reports disagree in their conclusions while describing the same reports, the 2025 cohort and 2026 review are reassuring, and the Association of Anaesthetists guideline is consensus. There is no procedure-specific analgesia guideline for fasciotomy. The split used here (no block until the release and the first examination, then a catheter for the repeated operations) is the author's reasoning from the mechanism.
