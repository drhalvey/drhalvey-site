## What the surgeon is doing and why

An infected joint replacement is a biofilm problem. Bacteria on the surface of the implant and the cement live in a matrix that antibiotics do not penetrate at any dose the kidneys will tolerate, so the infection is cured by removing the biofilm physically, and the surgeon's options are arranged by how much implant has to come out to do that. The diagnosis itself is made by criteria (a sinus to the joint, the same organism in two samples, or a combination of inflammatory markers, synovial cell count and histology), and the patient has usually had an aspiration that names the organism before the operation; when it does not, the operation is also the diagnostic test, which is the antibiotic-timing problem on this card.

The choice of operation is a question of time. An infection that is less than a few weeks old, either early after the index arthroplasty or acute haematogenous from a bacteraemia onto an old implant, has an immature biofilm, and the surgeon can open the joint, strip the synovium, exchange the modular parts, wash out with litres of fluid and leave the fixed components in: debridement, antibiotics and implant retention (DAIR). In the pooled literature DAIR fails in about a third of early post-operative and acute haematogenous infections and in three quarters of late chronic ones, which is why it is not offered to the chronic infection; knees fail more often than hips, Staphylococcus aureus more often than anything else, exchanging the modular parts has not been shown to help, and a planned second DAIR does as well as a single one (about 70% success). A chronic infection needs the implant out. The surgeon then either reimplants in the same anaesthetic (one-stage or single-stage exchange, with a complete re-preparation, re-draping and new instruments between the two halves) or leaves an antibiotic-loaded cement spacer in the joint, treats with systemic antibiotics for six to twelve weeks, and reimplants at a second operation when the markers have settled (two-stage exchange). The two-stage has been the standard for decades on the grounds of a lower reinfection rate; the one randomised trial, 140 infected hips, found the same patient-reported outcome at 18 months, a better outcome at three months and fewer intra-operative complications (8% against 27%) with the single stage, and the same proportion with markers of possible ongoing infection, so the single stage is gaining ground for the patient with a known organism and a reasonable soft-tissue envelope. The knee follows the hip in this, with less trial evidence.

The explantation is a [revision](revision-tka.html) in every mechanical sense: the old scar, a stiff joint needing a quadriceps snip or a tubercle osteotomy (an extended trochanteric osteotomy for a well-fixed hip stem), well-fixed components and every fragment of cement levered and burred out of bone, and then something the aseptic revision does not have: a radical debridement of all infected synovium, capsule, sinus track and bone, because tissue left behind is biofilm left behind. The spacer that follows is cement with grams of antibiotic mixed in, hand-formed into a block that holds the joint space (static) or moulded into a crude articulating knee or a stemmed hip that lets the patient move (articulating), eluting antibiotic locally at concentrations no intravenous dose could reach. The reimplantation, weeks later, is the second stage: the spacer comes out of a joint that has been operated on twice, more scar is released, the bone loss is now worse, and a stemmed, often constrained implant is cemented in with antibiotic-loaded cement at a lower dose. In the hip the same logic applies through a lateral position, an extended trochanteric osteotomy and a stem-type spacer or cemented stem that is a full canal cement step.

The patient is the other half of this card. Chronic infection patients arrive anaemic, hypoalbuminaemic, often diabetic or immunosuppressed, usually on opioids for months of a painful joint, and sometimes already with the renal impairment that the spacer will test. The acute haematogenous patient may arrive septic, and the operation is then source control under resuscitation.

The pain logic: the infected joint is an inflammatory pain generator before anything is cut, with rest pain and night pain, and the debridement removes much of that. What the operation adds is bone (components and cement out, canals reamed, a spacer or stems cemented in), muscle and soft tissue from the radical debridement, and at reimplantation the retension of a constrained implant in a scarred envelope, the aseptic revision's mechanism. The score is 4: a catheter or a robust plan for three days, movement-evoked pain that limits physiotherapy, and rebound when a single shot wears off in a patient who was already opioid-exposed. It is not the aseptic revision's 5 because the physiotherapy after a spacer is cautious by design (a static spacer is not bent at all) and the inflammatory component falls as the infection clears; the second stage, taken alone, sits on the revision card's curve.

## Steps

### 1. Classification, the organism and the patient before the list
**Why:** The surgeon decides DAIR or exchange on the age of the infection, the fixation of the components, the soft-tissue envelope and the organism; the aspirate result, the sinus, the inflammatory markers and the radiographs are the inputs, and the plan determines the case length, the blood loss and the cement.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask which operation, whether the organism is known, and whether the patient is bacteraemic. Then assess the host: haemoglobin (usually low from chronic inflammation, and cell salvage is off the table), albumin, glucose control, renal function with the spacer in mind, the immunosuppressant or biologic, the steroid needing cover, the anticoagulant, and the opioid history, which in a chronic infection is months long. Crossmatch for any exchange. The two-stage patient returning for reimplantation has had six to twelve weeks of antibiotics and a spacer: the creatinine, the haemoglobin, the vancomycin level if still running, and the deconditioning are the baseline, not an abnormality.

### 2. The septic patient
**Why:** An acute haematogenous infection onto an old implant can present as sepsis; the surgeon's job is source control within hours, and the operation does not wait for the markers to be perfect.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the one patient on the card for whom the antibiotic is never held: blood cultures and the joint aspirate are taken and the antibiotic goes in now, before the patient reaches theatre. Resuscitate with fluid and a vasopressor, place an arterial line before induction and a central line if the pressor is running, and induce for a general anaesthetic in a patient with a compromised circulation. Neuraxial anaesthesia in a bacteraemic patient carries the risk of seeding the epidural space, and the author does not do it; the peripheral block decision is made on the same grounds, a single shot being reasonable and a catheter through or near cellulitis not. The lactate and the urine output are the monitors of the next 12 hours, and the renal protection that the spacer will need starts with not being hypovolaemic now. A septic patient with a DAIR planned can turn into an explantation on the table if the components are found loose; the blood and the time are planned for that.

### 3. Antibiotic timing and the tissue samples
**Why:** The surgeon takes three to five deep tissue samples with separate clean instruments from the interfaces, the synovium and the sinus, and sends the implant for sonication where available; these cultures choose the antibiotic for the next six to twelve weeks. Surgeons worry that a dose of cefazolin before the knife will sterilise the samples.
**Anaesthesia:** The evidence is more settled than the folklore. In a small randomised trial and in a 576-patient multicentre cohort, giving the prophylactic dose before incision did not reduce culture positivity (63% with or without it in the cohort), and in the subgroup whose pre-operative aspirate was negative, withholding raised the yield from 29% to 49%. That gives the rule: when the organism is already known from the aspiration, the antibiotic goes in before the knife as for any arthroplasty, because a new implant or a retained one is about to be exposed; when the aspirate was negative or never done, the dose is held until the samples are in the pots, by agreement made before the patient is asleep, and given the moment the surgeon says so. In the septic patient it is never held. The organism-directed agent (vancomycin for a resistant staphylococcus, which needs an hour's infusion) replaces or joins cefazolin, and the re-dosing interval for a four-hour case with a litre of loss is written on the chart, not remembered.

### 4. DAIR: arthrotomy, synovectomy, modular exchange and lavage
**Why:** Through the old incision the joint is opened, pus evacuated, the synovium stripped radically from the gutters, the pouch and the posterior capsule, the polyethylene insert (and for a hip the head and liner) exchanged to reach the surfaces beneath, the fixed components checked for loosening, and the joint washed with several litres of fluid, often with an antiseptic soak, before closure over drains. Some surgeons add antibiotic beads or powder.
**Anaesthesia:** A 60-120 minute case that bleeds from inflamed synovium throughout, with or without a tourniquet, and that is more painful than its booking suggests because the whole synovial lining has been stripped. The single-shot adductor canal block with the surgeon's infiltration is the author's default, with the oral regimen and the opioid planned for an inflamed knee that was painful before. If the components are found loose the case becomes an explantation; that possibility is why the crossmatch exists even for a DAIR.

### 5. Explantation and radical debridement
**Why:** The revision exposure (snip or tubercle osteotomy for the knee, extended trochanteric osteotomy for the hip) gives access; the components are freed at their interfaces and removed, every fragment of cement is chased out of the metaphysis and canals, and then all infected tissue is excised, including bone that is soft or sequestered. Nothing that could carry biofilm is left.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the bloody half of the operation, 1-2 L measured with more hidden, through a bed that the surgeon deliberately leaves bleeding; there is no cell salvage, so the bank blood, the two doses of tranexamic acid and a haemoglobin check at the end of the debridement are the plan. A tourniquet, where used, comes down before the spacer and the reperfusion is of a leg that has been inflamed for weeks. The canal reaming and cement removal embolise marrow as in any revision, and the warming has to match a four-hour open wound. An arterial line and a large-bore cannula are the author's defaults for any explantation; a central line for the patient who arrived septic.

### 6. The spacer: antibiotic-loaded cement and what it does to the kidneys
**Why:** Cement is mixed with high doses of heat-stable antibiotic, commonly vancomycin and an aminoglycoside (gentamicin or tobramycin) at several grams of each per 40 g pack, and up to 8 g per pack in some units, and formed into a spacer that fills the space, holds the soft tissues out to length, and elutes antibiotic into the joint for weeks. An articulating knee spacer or a stemmed hip spacer allows some movement; a static block does not. The cement cures with the joint held to length.
**Anaesthesia:** Two things. First, cement physiology: a knee spacer is a surface cement load and a stemmed hip spacer is a canal cement load, with the full bone cement implantation syndrome mechanism of the [cement chapter](../a/a02-cement-bcis.html) in a patient who may be anaemic, hypovolaemic and on a vasopressor already. Second, the kidneys. The antibiotic in the spacer is absorbed systemically and in the largest knee series acute kidney injury followed in 14% of patients with normal baseline renal function and 45% of those with chronic kidney disease, with more than 3.6 g of either antibiotic per batch nearly doubling the odds; in the hip series the figures were 7% and 28%. In both series the independent predictors were things the anaesthetist controls: peri-operative hypovolaemia, hypertension, acute atrial fibrillation, intensive care admission and fluid depletion afterwards. A unit using very high doses reported acute kidney injury in 6.6% after the first stage, mostly resolving within a month. The practical rules: no NSAID in the spacer patient, no hypovolaemia at the end of a four-hour case, an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or receptor blocker held, the creatinine checked daily for the first days, and the systemic aminoglycoside or vancomycin dosed by a team that knows what is in the cement.

### 7. One-stage exchange: the second half in the same anaesthetic
**Why:** After the debridement the surgeon changes gloves and gowns, re-prepares and re-drapes, opens new instrument trays, and reimplants definitive components with antibiotic-loaded cement in the same sitting; the patient leaves theatre with a new joint rather than a spacer. The trial evidence for the hip supports it for the patient with a known organism, and the surgeon's willingness depends on the soft tissues and the bone loss found at step 5.
**Anaesthesia:** This is a 4-5 hour case: the explantation's blood loss plus a cemented stemmed reimplantation's, a second cement step with a canal in the hip, a second tranexamic acid dose, antibiotic re-dosing, and a spinal that will not last; general anaesthesia with a catheter placed beforehand is the practical answer, or a combined spinal-epidural in the afebrile patient in a unit that runs them. The haemoglobin and the temperature at the half-way re-drape decide whether the second half proceeds comfortably.

### 8. The interval and the second stage
**Why:** With a spacer in, the patient has six to twelve weeks of intravenous then oral antibiotics, partial or no weight-bearing on a static spacer, and serial markers; when they fall and the wound is quiet, the second stage removes the spacer, releases the scar of two operations, grades the bone loss, and reimplants stemmed, often constrained components with cones or augments, in antibiotic-loaded cement at a lower dose. Repeat tissue samples are taken.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the [revision knee](revision-tka.html) or [revision hip](revision-tha.html) card's operation with a worse starting point: anaemia from weeks of infection and antibiotics, renal function that may not have recovered from the first spacer, deconditioning, a vancomycin or aminoglycoside level that may still be relevant, and an opioid requirement that has grown. The antibiotic question returns: the prophylactic dose is usually given, because a new implant is going in and the organism is known, but some surgeons hold it for the repeat samples, and the conversation is had again. The catheter is routine here as it is for any revision, and the dexamethasone discussion is repeated, with the surgeon's view carrying more weight in a patient whose infection may not be cleared.

### 9. Recovery, the ward and the weeks
**Why:** The surgeon wants a dry wound, the drains out when they slow, the antibiotics running, the markers falling and the physiotherapy at the pace the construct allows: full after a DAIR, protected after an articulating spacer, none through the knee after a static one.
**Anaesthesia:** The trajectory on this card is 4 on the first evening with the block or catheter working, 6 on day 1, 5 on day 2, 4 on day 3 and 3 at a week, on top of a baseline that was painful before surgery. Regular paracetamol and the opioid the patient was already on, with rescue on top; the NSAID is the drug this patient cannot have for the duration of the spacer and its systemic antibiotics, and the catheter and paracetamol carry more as a result. The ketamine infusion is the author's practice for the opioid-tolerant two-stage patient, with the usual low-grade evidence. The first-morning haemoglobin decides the transfusion; the creatinine on days 1-3 is the spacer's report card; the wound that keeps draining after a week is the infection that was not cleared, and the surgeon sees it first.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is an inflamed joint cleared of its infection and then subjected to a revision's bone work, a radical soft-tissue debridement and, at reimplantation, the retension of a constrained implant in scar; the patient is opioid-exposed, often anaemic and renally fragile, and the NSAID is unavailable for as long as the spacer and its systemic antibiotics are in play. PROSPECT's arthroplasty guideline is extrapolated for the skeleton (paracetamol, adductor canal block, infiltration, intravenous dexamethasone, opioid as rescue, no femoral or sciatic block) and everything beyond it is the author's judgement, because no guideline covers infection.

For DAIR a single-shot adductor canal block, the surgeon's infiltration where the tissue will take it, and a written night plan with the pre-existing opioid continued are enough; the operation is a synovectomy and the pain is inflammatory and short. For the explantation and the reimplantation an adductor canal catheter is the author's default, as for any revision, with three qualifications. In the bacteraemic patient a single shot replaces it, because a catheter is a foreign body left in an infected patient for days; in 1,416 ward catheters the tip was colonised in 28.7%, local inflammation occurred in 3% and a psoas abscess in one patient, with duration beyond 48 h and absence of antibiotic prophylaxis as the risk factors, and every patient on this card is on antibiotics, so the risk is real and small. A catheter does not pass through cellulitis or a sinus. And a neuraxial catheter is reserved for the afebrile chronic reimplantation in a unit that runs epidurals on a mobilising patient; the author does not site one in a patient with a positive blood culture that week.

Dexamethasone is the honest uncertainty: it prolongs the block and reduces rebound, it raises glucose in the diabetic patient for a day, and in active infection some surgeons would rather it were not given. There is no trial. The author gives it for a DAIR in a non-septic patient and asks the surgeon for an explantation.

Rebound and escalation: rebound is high because the single shot recedes onto an inflamed joint in an opioid-exposed patient; stepping the catheter infusion down rather than off and having the oral regimen running for a day beforehand is the practical answer. Escalation triggers: pain above 7 with the catheter running is a migrated catheter before it is a prescription problem; a tense, swollen joint on day 1 is a haematoma in a patient with no cell salvage and two doses of tranexamic acid; new fever, rigors and hypotension on day 2 are the infection that was not cleared or a line; a creatinine that climbs on day 2 is the spacer, and the fluid balance and the nephrotoxins are reviewed that morning, not at the end of the week.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The hip. Lateral position, an extended trochanteric osteotomy for a fixed stem, a stem-type spacer or a cemented reimplantation stem with the full cement precautions, spacer dislocation as the interval complication, and the [revision hip card](revision-tha.html)'s catheter choices (supra-inguinal fascia iliaca or lumbar plexus) in place of the adductor canal.
- Chronic kidney disease. The spacer's acute kidney injury risk is five times higher; the antibiotic dose in the cement is a conversation with the surgeon and the infectious diseases team before the day, the NSAID is out, and the fluid balance of a four-hour case is managed as if the kidneys were the operation.
- Diabetes and immunosuppression. Glucose control through the dexamethasone question; biologics usually paused; steroid cover; wound healing and reinfection both worse.
- Vancomycin. An hour's infusion that has to start in time to be in before incision when the organism demands it; vancomycin flushing reaction with fast infusion; a level before the second stage.
- Aminoglycoside in cement plus systemic aminoglycoside: the nephrotoxic combination, and ototoxicity in the elderly.
- Fungal infection. Amphotericin in the cement, a longer interval, and a sicker host.
- Culture-negative infection. The antibiotic-timing conversation matters most here, because the samples are the only chance of a name.
- Cell salvage. Not used with infection in the field; the blood plan is bank blood, two tranexamic acid doses and a haemoglobin that was corrected beforehand where time allowed.
- The static spacer. No knee movement for weeks, a high venous thromboembolism risk, deconditioning, and a second stage that starts from a stiffer joint.
- The opioid-tolerant patient. Most chronic infections; baseline opioid continued, the catheter as default, ketamine and a pain service plan, and a long conversation before the first stage about what the two operations will feel like. See [opioid-tolerant and chronic pain patients](../a/a12-opioid-tolerant.html).
- Anticoagulation. Common in this age group; affects the neuraxial choice and the haematoma risk in a bed that is left bleeding on purpose.
- Delirium. Elderly, infected, anaemic, long anaesthetic, opioids, and days in bed: the commonest medical complication of the week after an explantation.
- Infection prophylaxis for the next implant. Cefazolin before incision at the second stage; the [antibiotics chapter](../a/a13-antibiotics-infection.html) for the principles.

## Key sources

The surgical evidence is uneven: DAIR outcomes rest on large systematic reviews of observational series, the single-stage versus two-stage question has one randomised trial in hips, and the spacer nephrotoxicity data are large single-institution cohorts whose predictors are consistent between hip and knee. The antibiotic-timing evidence is a small randomised trial and a recent large cohort that agree. The catheter infection figures are a prospective multicentre cohort from 2005. There is no analgesia guideline for infected arthroplasty, no trial of dexamethasone in active infection, and no trial of neuraxial technique in bacteraemia; the block, catheter and dexamethasone advice is the author's judgement from the mechanism and the physiology, and the score is calibrated against the aseptic revision cards.
