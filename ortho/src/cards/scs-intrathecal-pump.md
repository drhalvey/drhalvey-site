## What the surgeon is doing and why

Both devices on this card are answers to the same clinical failure: a patient whose pain has outlasted every operation and every drug, who is usually on a long-acting opioid, and in whom the pain service has decided that a piece of hardware in the spinal canal is the next move. The hardware is different, the surgery is small in both cases, and the anaesthetic problems are almost entirely about the patient rather than the wound.

A spinal cord stimulator delivers current to the dorsal columns through an electrode lying in the posterior epidural space, usually at T8-T10 for leg and back pain and at C2-C5 for the arm. Conventional stimulation produces a paraesthesia that must overlap the painful area, which is why the classic technique is done awake: the patient reports where they feel the tingling and the lead is moved until it matches. The newer paradigms (high frequency, burst, dorsal root ganglion stimulation) are paraesthesia-free or nearly so, and that has changed the anaesthetic, because a lead that does not need the patient's report can be placed under general anaesthesia with the midline confirmed by electromyography of the legs. There are two kinds of lead. Percutaneous cylindrical leads go in through a Tuohy needle with loss of resistance, exactly as an epidural catheter would, and can migrate. Paddle leads are flat, cover more of the cord, migrate less and need a laminotomy: the surgeon removes the upper part of the lower spinous process and enough lamina to slide a plate into the epidural space under direct vision. The lead is anchored to the fascia, a strain relief loop is left, and it is tunnelled to a generator in a pocket in the upper buttock, the flank or the abdominal wall. Every patient has a trial first, with externalised percutaneous leads for a week or so, and goes on to the implant only if the pain falls by half or more.

An intrathecal pump bypasses the dorsal columns and puts the drug where the receptors are. A catheter is placed through a paramedian lumbar puncture, threaded to the dermatome of the pain, tunnelled around the flank and connected to a programmable pump with a 20-40 mL reservoir in a pocket in the abdominal wall, refilled through the skin every few weeks to months. Morphine and ziconotide are the first-line single agents in the consensus algorithms, with bupivacaine, hydromorphone, fentanyl and clonidine as admixtures; baclofen pumps for spasticity are the same hardware with a different drug and a different withdrawal syndrome. The dose ratio is the point: a milligram of intrathecal morphine does the work of hundreds of milligrams by mouth, which is also why the first days after implantation have killed people.

The complication profile explains most of the anaesthetic planning. In the pooled literature 30-40% of stimulator patients have at least one complication; lead migration is the commonest, infection and pain over the implant the commonest biological ones, and neurological injury is rare. The modern infection rate in a multicentre series of 2,737 implants and revisions was 2.45%, with no independent effect of diabetes, smoking or obesity, no difference between paddle and cylindrical leads or between antibiotics, and fewer infections with an occlusive dressing. An infected stimulator or pump is explanted; the device is lost, and so is the months of work that led to it.

The pain logic is unusual for this book because the wound is not the problem. A one-level laminotomy and a midline dissection are minor bone and muscle; the pocket is a subcutaneous dissection that aches for a week or two and is the thing patients complain about; the needle tracks of a percutaneous system are nothing. All of it lands on a patient whose baseline pain is a 6 or 7, who takes a long-acting opioid, often a gabapentinoid and sometimes a benzodiazepine, and who has had many operations before. The score is a 2 on the scale because the plan is paracetamol, an NSAID and a few rescue doses; the day 1 figure of 4 is the pocket, and the honest statement is that the surgical pain will be hard to distinguish from the pain the device was implanted for.

## Steps

### 1. Selection, the trial decision and the opioid plan
**Why:** The pain service has excluded a treatable cause, screened the patient psychologically, and decided between a stimulator (focal neuropathic pain, failed back surgery, complex regional pain syndrome, limb ischaemia) and a pump (diffuse or cancer pain, spasticity, or a patient already on high-dose opioid who has failed everything else). The consensus guidelines describe who needs a trial and who does not.
**Anaesthesia:** Take the drug history in milligrams and times: the long-acting opioid, the gabapentinoid, any benzodiazepine, any buprenorphine, and the pain service's plan for all of them around the implant, which is written before the day and not improvised in recovery (see the [opioid-tolerant chapter](../a/a12-opioid-tolerant.html)). Anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs are managed as for a neuraxial catheter, because a lead in the epidural space is one, and the surgeon's interval is written down. Diabetes control, MRSA screening and decolonisation are the infection measures with the guideline behind them. A patient who already has a stimulator for a revision needs its programmer in the room. For a pump patient, the systemic opioid dose on the day and the planned starting intrathecal dose are both on the chart.

### 2. Trial lead placement under local anaesthetic and sedation
**Why:** One or two percutaneous leads are placed through Tuohy needles at T12-L1 or L1-L2 and advanced up the posterior epidural space under fluoroscopy to T8-T10, then stimulated while the patient reports whether the paraesthesia covers the pain. The leads are anchored at the skin, connected to an external generator and left for five to ten days.
**Anaesthesia:** The patient must be able to lie prone, lie still for the needle and answer questions during the mapping, and the sedation is titrated to exactly that: enough to tolerate the needle, little enough to describe a tingling in the left calf. Propofol in small increments or a dexmedetomidine infusion both work; a patient who cannot answer is a patient whose lead is in the wrong place. Local anaesthetic by the operator at the needle entry and down to the ligament is the analgesic. Dural puncture happens, presents as a wet needle and a headache, and usually means the trial is deferred. A paraesthesia-free system (high frequency or burst) allows deeper sedation because the lead is placed to anatomical landmarks rather than to the patient's report; find out which system it is before choosing the sedative.

### 3. Permanent implant: awake or asleep
**Why:** A successful trial leads to a permanent system within a few weeks. Percutaneous leads are replaced in the same way and tunnelled to a generator; paddle leads need a laminotomy and have traditionally been placed awake for mapping, which is uncomfortable, slow and unreliable.
**Anaesthesia:** The best evidence on the anaesthetic is a prospective, non-randomised, multicentre comparison of 30 paddle lead implants: 19 placed asleep with electromyographic guidance and 11 awake with verbal mapping. Asleep placement took 89 min against 125, gave 83.5% pain-paraesthesia overlap at six weeks against 46.6%, produced fewer stray paraesthesiae, matched the awake group for pain relief at 24 weeks, and had two adverse events against six. The author's default for a paddle lead is therefore a general anaesthetic with neuromonitoring where the unit can provide it, and awake placement only where it cannot or where the surgeon insists. The neuromonitoring rules apply: a propofol and remifentanil infusion, no neuromuscular block after the intubating dose (sugammadex if the laminotomy needs a relaxed patient early), and a bite block for the motor evoked potentials if those are run as well as electromyography.

### 4. Prone positioning
**Why:** Prone on bolsters with the thoracolumbar spine flexed to open the interlaminar spaces, the arms up for a thoracic lead or down for a cervical one, a fluoroscopy arc that can swing to lateral, and the pocket site on the buttock or flank prepared in the same field.
**Anaesthesia:** For the asleep patient the prone rules apply: abdomen free, eyes checked after the turn and after every fluoroscopy swing, pressure points padded, arms below 90 degrees. For the awake patient the position is the anaesthetic: a prone pillow with a clear view of the mouth, nasal oxygen with capnography, a chest bolster that leaves the abdomen free, a pillow under the shins, an antisialagogue if the case will be long, and a frank conversation beforehand about lying face-down for an hour. A patient with a hip or knee that cannot be extended, a chest that cannot be loaded or a neck that cannot be turned is a general anaesthetic, decided before the day.

### 5. Lead placement and intra-operative testing
**Why:** Percutaneous leads are steered up the posterior epidural space to the target level and the midline is confirmed on the anteroposterior image. For a paddle, the surgeon removes the superior edge of the lower spinous process and a window of lamina, opens the ligamentum flavum and slides the plate cranially into the epidural space, where it sits against the dura and can compress the cord if the canal is narrow or the paddle too wide. Testing follows: verbal mapping awake, or electromyography asleep, where symmetrical responses in both legs at the same threshold mean the paddle is midline.
**Anaesthesia:** Keep the pressure at the patient's normal through the paddle insertion, because a cord pressed by a plate in a tight canal is an ischaemic cord. A fall in somatosensory potentials or asymmetric motor responses during insertion is the paddle in the wrong place; the response is to tell the surgeon, who repositions it. Under sedation, the lead passage and the stimulation test are tolerable but the laminotomy is not, so the awake technique is really an awake-mapping technique with the bone work under heavy local and the deepest sedation the patient's airway will allow face-down. Cervical leads bring the airway into it: a cervical epidural haematoma or an oedematous cord presents as a weak arm and then a weak diaphragm.

### 6. Anchoring, tunnelling and the generator pocket
**Why:** The lead is anchored to the fascia with a strain relief loop so that spinal flexion does not pull it, tunnelled subcutaneously, and connected to the generator in a pocket sized to the device and placed where the patient will not lie or sit on it.
**Anaesthesia:** Tunnelling is the most stimulating step for the sedated patient and the one the operator under-infiltrates; ask for the tunnel track to be infiltrated before the rod goes through. The pocket is the post-operative pain generator: a wide subcutaneous dissection over fascia in a thin patient, a deep one in a heavy patient, and the site most likely to produce pain over the implant for weeks. Surgeon infiltration of the pocket with long-acting local anaesthetic before closure is the one request worth making by name. Blood pressure at the patient's normal keeps the pocket dry; a pocket haematoma is an infection waiting to happen.

### 7. Closure, dressing, programming and the wake-up check
**Why:** Layered closure, an occlusive dressing (the one measure associated with fewer infections in the multicentre series), and initial programming of the device before the patient leaves theatre or in recovery.
**Anaesthesia:** Wake the asleep patient to a neurological examination of the legs (or the arms for a cervical lead) before they leave the room; new weakness is a haematoma or a misplaced paddle and the imaging is immediate. Dural puncture headache after a paddle implant is treated conservatively first; a blood patch near a fresh lead is the surgeon's decision. The device is left on or off according to the programmer's plan, and the patient's existing analgesia continues unchanged.

### 8. Intrathecal pump: trial and drug
**Why:** A single-shot intrathecal dose, a short epidural or intrathecal catheter infusion or, in the terminally ill, no trial at all, as the consensus trialling guidance allows. The drug follows the algorithm: morphine or ziconotide first line, admixtures with bupivacaine or clonidine for mixed pain, baclofen for spasticity.
**Anaesthesia:** A single-shot morphine trial in an opioid-tolerant outpatient is a neuraxial opioid in a patient also taking systemic opioid, monitored for 24 h with the same respiratory vigilance as any intrathecal morphine; the consensus documents say what to do with the systemic dose during the trial and the pain service writes it down. Ziconotide trials bring psychiatric and cognitive effects rather than respiratory ones. A baclofen trial in a spastic patient can drop the tone enough to unmask weakness and compromise transfers.

### 9. Pump implantation
**Why:** Lateral decubitus, pocket side up. A paramedian intrathecal puncture at L2-L3 or below, the catheter threaded to the target level under fluoroscopy and anchored to the lumbodorsal fascia, tunnelled around the flank to an abdominal pocket, and connected to the pump, which is filled, primed with a bridging bolus to fill the catheter dead space, and secured to the fascia, subfascially in a thin patient.
**Anaesthesia:** A general anaesthetic with a tracheal tube is usual; the lateral position has the standard dependent-side padding and the airway on its side for an hour or more. Cerebrospinal fluid runs freely from a 15-gauge needle and the puncture is the source of the post-dural puncture headache that follows a proportion of these operations, treated conservatively because a blood patch beside a fresh catheter is a surgical decision. The priming bolus matters to you: the catheter dead space is filled with drug at the end of the case and the pump then runs at its programmed rate, so the first intrathecal dose arrives in theatre or recovery, not in a week. Anticoagulant timing is the neuraxial rule again, with a catheter that stays in for life.

### 10. The first nights on an intrathecal opioid
**Why:** The pump is started at a conservative dose and titrated over days to weeks while the systemic opioid is reduced; the pain service owns both numbers.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the lethal step and the reason the card exists. Device registration data put mortality after intrathecal opioid pump implantation for non-cancer pain at 0.088% at three days, 0.39% at one month and 3.89% at one year, higher than after stimulator implantation or lumbar discectomy; in all nine index cases respiratory arrest caused or contributed to death and no device over-infusion was found, which leaves the drug, the systemic opioid still on board, and the interaction. The defensible plan: the starting dose from the consensus guidance rather than from the trial response, continuous oximetry and capnography where available for at least the first night, a sedation score hourly, naloxone at the bedside, no additional sedatives, a written schedule for reducing the oral opioid rather than a stop, and the patient's family told what to watch for at home. The pocket and the back hurt more than the patient expected in the first week; treat that with the plan below and not with the pump.

### 11. Recovery and discharge for both devices
**Why:** The surgeon wants a dry wound, a device that works, and a patient who understands the restrictions (no bending, twisting or lifting for several weeks after a lead, no lying on the pocket) that keep the lead from migrating.
**Anaesthesia:** Day case for a trial, a percutaneous implant or a generator change; one night for a paddle lead; longer for a pump. The pain the patient reports in recovery is three things at once (the pocket, the back, and the chronic pain the device will or will not treat), and separating them is the first conversation. Infection presents over the following weeks as pocket erythema, a fluctuant pocket, a wound that weeps, fever, or new back pain and meningism; any of them is an urgent review, because an epidural abscess around a lead is a cord compression. Lead migration presents as a change in the paraesthesia pattern or loss of effect and is a surgical revision rather than an anaesthetic problem, but the revision patient is the same patient with another operation on the list.

## Pain plan

The wound is minor and the patient is not, so the plan is built on the pain service's opioid plan with surgical analgesia added rather than the other way around. Regular paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor where the kidneys allow, started before the block of sedation or the general anaesthetic wears off, cover the laminotomy and the pocket for most patients. The baseline long-acting opioid continues at its usual dose and time on the day of surgery, including buprenorphine; an immediate-release rescue dose is charted at a size scaled to the baseline, which for a patient on 200 mg of oral morphine equivalent a day is not 5 mg. Gabapentinoids and benzodiazepines continue because stopping them produces withdrawal, not analgesia. For the paddle lead under general anaesthesia, a low-dose ketamine infusion through the case is the author's practice in the opioid-tolerant patient, on the strength of the opioid-dependent lumbar surgery trial (0.5 mg/kg at induction then 10 microg/kg/min to closure, which reduced opioid use to 48 h and pain at six weeks), and an erector spinae plane block at the laminotomy level is a reasonable adjunct without procedure-specific evidence. A transversus abdominis plane block on the pocket side for an abdominal pump pocket is the same category of judgement. Surgeon infiltration of the pocket and the tunnel track is asked for in every case.

For the pump patient the plan inverts: the intrathecal opioid starts low, the systemic opioid is reduced on a written schedule, no sedative is added, and the surgical pain of the first week is treated with paracetamol, an NSAID and the reduced systemic opioid rather than with a faster pump titration. A patient who is sedated on day 1 after a pump has too much opioid in total and the pump is the first thing to question.

Rebound is not a feature. Escalation: new leg weakness, retention or back pain out of proportion after a stimulator is an epidural haematoma or a misplaced paddle until imaged; a pump patient with a falling respiratory rate has an opioid problem and gets naloxone and a pump interrogation, not a sleep; a hot, fluctuant pocket is an infection and goes to the surgeon the same day.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs. A lead or a catheter in the canal is a neuraxial catheter with no removal date; the neuraxial intervals for stopping and restarting apply, and a patient who cannot stop them is a patient who may not be a candidate.
- The sedated prone airway. The awake-mapping technique fails when the sedation deepens to keep the patient still; a face-down patient with an obstructed airway and a needle in the epidural space has no good options. Choose the general anaesthetic beforehand for the patient with sleep apnoea, a difficult airway or a body habitus that will not lie prone.
- Buprenorphine. Continue it; the modern advice is in the [opioid-tolerant chapter](../a/a12-opioid-tolerant.html), and a patient taken off buprenorphine for a stimulator implant is in withdrawal with a sore pocket.
- Opioid use disorder. The pump is rarely the answer and the stimulator sometimes is; the plan is agreed with addiction medicine, the baseline agonist continues, and the rescue dose is scaled honestly.
- The existing stimulator at later surgery. Turn it off for the operation, bipolar diathermy or a monopolar return pad placed so that the current path avoids the generator and leads, and MRI only under the device's conditional rules; the programmer and the representative's number go in the notes.
- The baclofen pump patient. A pump that fails, a catheter that fractures or a refill that is missed produces baclofen withdrawal within hours: hyperthermia, rigidity, rhabdomyolysis, seizures and death. Treat as an emergency with oral or enteral baclofen, benzodiazepines and the pump team.
- Refills and pocket fills. A refill needle that misses the port puts weeks of intrathecal opioid into the subcutaneous pocket at once; the presentation is an overdose within an hour of a clinic visit and the treatment is naloxone, an infusion and observation.
- Catheter-tip granuloma. An inflammatory mass at the tip of a catheter delivering high-concentration opioid, presenting as loss of effect then new neurological signs; the safety consensus describes it, and it is the differential for a pump patient with new leg symptoms.
- Cervical leads. The epidural haematoma and the swollen cord present as arm weakness and then a diaphragm; the wake-up check includes the arms and the breathing, and the first night is a monitored one.
- Dorsal root ganglion stimulation. Leads through the foramen onto the ganglion itself, done awake in most hands because the mapping is precise; the same sedation problem with a longer case.
- Obesity. The pocket is deep, the tunnelling long, the prone position worse, the lead more likely to migrate and the infection rate, in the large series, not higher. The general anaesthetic is chosen more often.
- Pain over the implant. Pocket pain for weeks is common enough to be listed among the main biological complications and is not an indication for more opioid; a repositioned generator is the surgical answer when it persists.

## Key sources

The infection guidance is consensus with a large retrospective series behind it; the two disagree on post-operative antibiotics and the card says so. The awake-versus-asleep evidence is one small prospective non-randomised comparison and a technique video from the same group, which is thin, but the direction is consistent with the physiology and the author follows it. The intrathecal pump guidance is three consensus documents from one conference, and the mortality figures come from manufacturer registration data cross-referenced with death records, with the mechanism inferred rather than proven. Nothing on this card has a procedure-specific analgesia trial behind it; the pain plan is the opioid-tolerant plan with a laminotomy attached.
