## What the surgeon is doing and why

Tumours of the sacrum and the pelvic bones are large before they are found, because the pelvis has room for them, and they are cured only by removal in one piece with a clear margin. Chordoma and chondrosarcoma do not respond to chemotherapy or ordinary radiotherapy, so the surgeon cannot compromise the margin and make it up later. The same operations are used for other sarcomas, for giant cell tumour that cannot be curetted, and for rectal cancer that has recurred onto the sacrum. What has to be sacrificed is whatever the tumour touches: nerve roots, the hip joint, pelvic viscera, or the limb.

Sacrectomy is defined by the level of the cut, and the level decides the patient's future. Keeping both S2 roots and at least one S3 root generally preserves bladder, bowel and sexual function; a cut above S2 on both sides abolishes them; a cut through S1 or above also disconnects the spine from the pelvis and needs spinopelvic fixation with lumbar pedicle screws, iliac screws and rods. A low sacrectomy (below S2-3) is done from behind in one stage. A high or total sacrectomy needs the front of the sacrum cleared first: through a laparotomy the rectum is mobilised or taken with the specimen, the internal iliac and median sacral vessels are ligated, the ureters are identified (often over stents), an anterior osteotomy is started, and a rectus abdominis flap or a sheet of protective material is left in the pelvis. The patient is then turned prone, the same day or a few days later, for laminectomy, ligation of the dural sac below the last roots to be kept, division of the sacrificed roots, osteotomy through the sacrum or the sacroiliac joints, delivery of the specimen, fixation, and flap closure of a cavity the size of a fist or larger.

Hemipelvectomy removes part or all of one innominate bone. Internal hemipelvectomy keeps the leg: the ilium, the acetabulum, the pubis or a combination is resected with the surrounding muscle, the femoral vessels and the femoral and sciatic nerves are preserved, and the defect is reconstructed with a custom implant, an allograft or a saddle, or left to form a flail hip. External hemipelvectomy (hindquarter amputation) is for tumours that involve the sciatic nerve, the femoral vessels and the hip joint together: the symphysis and the sacroiliac joint are divided, the iliac vessels are ligated, the femoral, obturator and lumbosacral nerves are cut, and the wound is closed with a posterior gluteal flap or, if the buttock is involved, an anterior thigh flap raised on the femoral vessels.

Three facts shape the anaesthetic. The pelvis is a venous sponge and bleeds from surfaces that cannot be clamped, so these are massive transfusion cases by design: the pooled median loss for sacrectomy with exenteration was 3.7 L over 12 hours. The wounds are enormous, lie under the patient's weight and next to the anus, and break down or become infected in a large minority. And the nerves are cut at the root, close to the cord, which is the worst place for phantom and neuropathic pain: up to 90% of hindquarter amputees report phantom limb pain.

The pain logic: nerve is the dominant mechanism, with muscle and bone in large amounts beneath it. Pain is severe from the start, persists far beyond 72 hours, is re-provoked by sitting, turning and dressing changes, and merges into chronic neuropathic pain in many. Most patients arrive on opioid for sciatic or sacral tumour pain. That is a 5 on every criterion in the scale.

## Steps

### 1. Planning with the whole team
**Why:** These operations involve orthopaedic oncology or spinal surgery, colorectal, urology, vascular and plastic surgeons in sequence, and are planned at a multidisciplinary meeting.
**Anaesthesia:** Attend it or read its outcome. You need the level of resection, whether it is one stage or two, which vessels and nerves will be sacrificed, whether a stoma is planned, and which flap will close the wound, because the flap dictates post-operative position. Pre-operative embolisation of the tumour or the internal iliac territory is used for vascular lesions. Assess the patient as for major cancer surgery, with attention to nutritional state, baseline opioid dose and haemoglobin; correct iron deficiency if time allows. An intensive care bed is booked. Explain the first week honestly, including the possibility of waking ventilated.

### 2. Lines, monitoring and the transfusion setup
**Why:** Everything that will be needed at the worst moment must already be running.
**Anaesthesia:** Arterial line, a large-bore central sheath or dialysis-calibre line plus two wide peripheral cannulae in the arms (never in the leg being removed, and not below a pelvis whose veins will be tied), a rapid infuser primed, fluid and forced-air warming, urinary catheter, temperature probe, and point-of-care haemoglobin, gases and viscoelastic testing. Six units crossmatched as a starting stock with the blood bank warned. Tranexamic acid before incision. Cell salvage in tumour and bowel surgery is a unit decision. A second anaesthetist for the resection phase is normal staffing, not a luxury. Place the lumbar epidural now for a hemipelvectomy, awake, with normal clotting; for a sacrectomy the surgeon places it at the end.

### 3. Anterior stage of a high sacrectomy
**Why:** Working through the abdomen, the surgeons separate or resect the rectum, tie the internal iliac arteries and veins and the middle and lateral sacral vessels, free the ureters and lumbosacral trunks, and begin the bone cut from the front so that the posterior stage does not have to work blind towards the great vessels.
**Anaesthesia:** A long laparotomy in steep head-down lithotomy: protect the calves and peroneal nerves, limit the tilt time, and expect facial oedema. Bleeding at this stage is from the presacral veins and the internal iliac vein branches, which tear and retract into the sacral foramina. Ligating the internal iliac arteries reduces arterial inflow for the next stage and does not stop venous bleeding. Keep the patient warm and the calcium, fibrinogen and platelets corrected as you go, because the second stage starts where this one finishes. If the stages are separated, the interval is spent in intensive care being made ready again.

### 4. The turn prone, or the roll in floppy lateral
**Why:** The posterior approach gives access to the canal, the roots and the sacroiliac joints. For hemipelvectomy the lateral position allows the surgeon to work at the front and back of the pelvis without redraping.
**Anaesthesia:** A patient who has already had six hours of surgery and several litres of fluid is turned onto a frame: secure the tube and lines yourself, check that the abdomen is free and the eyes are clear, and reposition the head regularly. Long prone time with large blood loss is the profile for ischaemic optic neuropathy (see [spine essentials](../a/a14-spine-essentials.html)); keep the head at or above the heart, avoid profound anaemia and hypotension, and record that the risk was discussed beforehand. In floppy lateral the dependent shoulder, brachial plexus and the sound leg carry the load for hours.

### 5. Dural sac ligation and nerve root division
**Why:** After laminectomy the surgeon identifies the roots to be kept, ties and divides the dural sac below them, and cuts the sacral roots that enter the tumour. In a hindquarter amputation the femoral nerve, obturator nerve and lumbosacral trunk are divided high in the pelvis.
**Anaesthesia:** Division of large nerve trunks under general anaesthesia produces a brief pressor and heart-rate response; some surgeons infiltrate each nerve with local anaesthetic before cutting, which is reasonable and unproven as prevention. If intrathecal morphine is part of the plan the surgeon injects it before the sac is tied. Ask the surgeon to consider targeted muscle reinnervation or to bury the nerve ends, and to place catheters in the nerve sheaths at hindquarter amputation, while the nerves are exposed. After a cut above S2 the bladder and bowel are denervated from this moment.

### 6. Osteotomies and delivery of the specimen
**Why:** The sacrum is cut across at the planned level and through or beside the sacroiliac joints; in hemipelvectomy the symphysis and the sacroiliac joint or sacral ala are divided. The specimen is then levered out and the last soft-tissue attachments cut.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the trap. Cancellous sacral bone and the venous plexus on its anterior surface bleed faster than any other surface in orthopaedic surgery for several minutes, the surgeon's only control is to finish and pack, and they will not stop. Before the first osteotomy: blood checked and hanging, the infuser running, noradrenaline connected, the second anaesthetist at the infuser. Transfuse red cells, plasma and platelets in balanced ratio from the outset and correct by viscoelastic testing, with fibrinogen and calcium given early. Watch for air embolism from open pelvic veins in the prone, head-up patient. In a hindquarter amputation, the limb leaves with its own blood volume and a large share of body mass: the target circulating volume afterwards is smaller than at the start, and it is easy to overshoot.

### 7. Haemostasis, reconstruction and fixation
**Why:** The cavity is packed and then dried. After a total sacrectomy the lumbar spine is fixed to the ilia with screws and rods, sometimes with a fibular strut or cage between them; after internal hemipelvectomy the hip may be reconstructed with an implant.
**Anaesthesia:** A quieter period in which to repair the physiology: temperature, pH, ionised calcium, potassium, clotting, urine output. Dilutional and consumptive coagulopathy peaks now. If an implant with a cemented stem or cup is used the ordinary cement precautions apply in a patient with no reserve left. Reconstruction adds hours; if the patient is cold, acidotic and coagulopathic, say so, because the surgeon can pack, close temporarily and come back.

### 8. Flap closure
**Why:** The dead space is filled and the skin closed with a gluteal advancement or rotation flap, a rectus abdominis flap passed through the pelvis, or the planned posterior or anterior amputation flap. Drains are left for a week or more.
**Anaesthesia:** Flap survival needs perfusion pressure, haemoglobin, warmth and no pressure on the pedicle. Keep the mean pressure up with volume and modest vasopressor; high-dose noradrenaline in a cold patient threatens the flap. The surgeon-placed epidural for a sacrectomy goes in now, threaded cephalad from the top of the laminectomy and tunnelled out through clean skin away from the perineum. Agree the nursing position (prone, side to side, or an air-fluidised bed) before leaving theatre.

### 9. Intensive care and extubation
**Why:** The patient has had a massive transfusion, a long prone or head-down period, and often bowel and urinary diversion.
**Anaesthesia:** Leave the tube in where the face and tongue are swollen, the transfusion has exceeded a blood volume, or the patient is cold and acidotic; extubate the next day when the cuff leak has returned. Do not start the epidural infusion or remove any catheter until the clotting and platelet count have been measured and corrected, and time prophylactic heparin by the guideline intervals. Expect ileus, a falling albumin, and second-day fluid shifts.

### 10. The first weeks
**Why:** The wound is the surgeon's main concern; infection and flap breakdown are the commonest complications, and return to theatre for washout is frequent.
**Anaesthesia:** Repeat anaesthetics for debridement and dressing changes in an opioid-tolerant, malnourished, anaemic patient who must not lie on the flap. The epidural or perineural catheters are kept for five to seven days if the sites stay clean; phantom sensation and pain are asked about by name from day 1.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is nerve division near the cord under very large wounds, in a patient usually opioid tolerant, so the plan is continuous neuraxial or perineural local anaesthetic plus ketamine, on top of the baseline opioid. For hemipelvectomy the defensible default is a lumbar epidural placed before induction and run for five to seven days with ropivacaine 0.1-0.2% and fentanyl, dilute enough to keep the remaining leg strong; at hindquarter amputation, surgeon-placed catheters beside the cut lumbosacral trunk and femoral nerve are the alternative or the supplement. For sacrectomy the dural sac has been tied in the field, so the catheter is laid by the surgeon above it at the end, which is the technique PROSPECT recommends after complex spine surgery. A ketamine bolus of 0.5 mg/kg IV at induction and an infusion of 0.1-0.25 mg/kg/h continued for three to five days is used as analgesia for the opioid-tolerant patient, where the spinal surgery trial showed reduced opioid consumption; it should not be presented as prevention of phantom pain, since the amputation trial that tested that was negative.

The same applies to the epidural. In the randomised trial of lower limb amputation, an epidural started before surgery did not reduce phantom pain at a week or a year. It is used here because it is the most effective acute analgesia available for the pelvis and buttock. Start a gabapentinoid when neuropathic features appear, which in most patients is within days; there is no evidence that starting it earlier prevents them. Regular paracetamol throughout. NSAIDs wait until bleeding, renal function and any bowel anastomosis allow. Targeted muscle reinnervation at the time of resection is a surgical measure with early supportive data for hindquarter amputation and randomised evidence in established limb amputation pain (see [amputation](amputation.html)).

Every element competes with the coagulopathy. The fallback, which should be written before the case starts, is ketamine and lidocaine infusions with an opioid patient-controlled device set for tolerance, managed by the pain service daily (see [the opioid-tolerant patient](../a/a12-opioid-tolerant.html)).

Escalation: perineal and saddle numbness is expected after sacral root division and is not epidural toxicity. Rising pain with fever and a boggy flap is collection or infection. New weakness in the preserved leg under an epidural is haematoma until imaged, and after a massive transfusion the threshold for imaging is low. Refer to the chronic pain service before discharge, not after the first failed outpatient visit.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Low sacrectomy. Posterior only, three to four hours, 1-2 L of loss, sphincters usually preserved; still a flap closure next to the anus and still root division.
- Recurrent rectal cancer. Previous radiotherapy and surgery make every plane bleed; a stoma, a urinary conduit and the sacrectomy are done together, and the 12-hour median belongs to this group.
- Internal hemipelvectomy with reconstruction. The longest operations on the card with the highest infection rates; the limb survives, the femoral and sciatic nerves are stretched and may not work for months, and the pain plan must allow their examination.
- Hindquarter amputation. Faster and often drier than limb-sparing resection once the vessels are tied. The psychological injury is large and starts in recovery; phantom sensation should have been explained beforehand.
- Staged surgery. The second anaesthetic begins with a patient who is oedematous, has an ileus and a tracheal tube that should be changed only if there is a reason.
- Ureter and bowel. Stents make the ureters palpable but not safe; an unrecognised injury presents as high drain output or sepsis in the first week. A defunctioning colostomy protects the wound and is sometimes formed before the main operation.
- Aortic balloon occlusion and tourniquet-like techniques. Used in some centres to reduce pelvic bleeding during the osteotomies; they add an ischaemia-reperfusion event and a femoral arterial sheath, and are a local decision.
- Hyperkalaemia and citrate. Rapid transfusion through a central line in a cold, acidotic patient: give calcium by the measured ionised level and treat potassium early.

## Key sources

Numbers for blood loss and duration come from a systematic review of retrospective series of sacrectomy with exenteration; primary sacral tumour and hemipelvectomy series report similar ranges with wide variation, and the description of bleeding at the osteotomy is clinical experience, not measured data. Phantom pain prevalence after hindquarter amputation comes from a review. The negative prevention trials of epidural and ketamine were done in vascular amputees and are extrapolated. No analgesic trial exists for sacrectomy or hemipelvectomy; the plan is author's judgement built on the mechanism, the amputation literature and PROSPECT's complex spine guidance.
