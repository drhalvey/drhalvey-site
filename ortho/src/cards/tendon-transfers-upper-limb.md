## What the surgeon is doing and why

A tendon transfer takes a working muscle that the hand can spare, detaches its tendon at the far end, reroutes it and sews it into the tendon of a muscle that no longer works. Nothing new is created: a function is moved. The indications are a nerve that will not recover (radial, median, ulnar, or the late stage of a [brachial plexus injury](brachial-plexus-nerve-transfer.html)), a tendon that has ruptured and cannot be repaired end to end (extensor pollicis longus after a distal radius fracture, the ulnar finger extensors in the [rheumatoid wrist](rheumatoid-hand-small-joint.html), flexor pollicis longus on a plate edge), and the tetraplegic hand, where elbow extension, a key pinch and a grasp can be rebuilt from the few muscles still under voluntary control.

The common sets:

- Radial nerve palsy (no wrist, finger or thumb extension): pronator teres to extensor carpi radialis brevis for the wrist, flexor carpi radialis or ulnaris to extensor digitorum communis for the fingers, palmaris longus to extensor pollicis longus for the thumb. Three donors, three weaves, incisions on both surfaces of the forearm.
- Extensor pollicis longus rupture: extensor indicis proprius moved across to the thumb. Two or three small incisions, half an hour.
- Low median palsy (no thumb opposition): an opponensplasty using the ring finger superficialis, extensor indicis or palmaris longus, routed around a pulley at the pisiform.
- Ulnar palsy (clawing, weak pinch): a superficialis slip looped through the A1 or A2 pulleys (the lasso), or a wrist extensor lengthened with graft and passed to the lateral bands, plus an adductorplasty.
- Tetraplegia: posterior deltoid or biceps to triceps, brachioradialis to flexor pollicis longus, extensor carpi radialis longus to the finger flexors, with a thumb joint fused.

Every transfer has the same four acts. Harvest: the donor tendon is divided distally and the muscle belly freed proximally until it glides in a straight line. Route: a wide subcutaneous tunnel is made to the recipient, avoiding raw bone and scar. Weave: the donor is passed three or four times through the recipient tendon (a Pulvertaft weave) or sewn side to side. And tension, which is the whole operation. A muscle generates force over a narrow range of length. Sewn too loose, the transfer contracts and nothing moves; too tight, and the finger is held extended and cannot be flexed. There is no gauge. Under a block or general anaesthetic the surgeon sets the joints in a standard posture, pulls the donor to a judged length, sutures, and then moves the wrist to see whether the fingers open and close passively (the tenodesis effect). Under wide-awake local anaesthesia the surgeon places one suture, asks the patient to move, looks at what happens, and adjusts before finishing the weave.

That is why the anaesthetic is a surgical decision before it is yours. The case for the awake method is direct: the patient shows the result while it can still be changed, and sees that the hand works, which is said to shorten the relearning. The evidence is technique papers, case series and one small comparison in which pain and opioid use were no different and early motor function and satisfaction favoured the awake group; a review by its advocates states that level 1 comparisons do not exist. Many experienced surgeons get reliable results with tenodesis under a block and prefer a bloodless field.

The pain logic: this is soft tissue surgery. The mechanism is muscle and fascia from the harvest and the tunnel, the transfer is immobilised and unloaded for four weeks, and nothing is done to bone. It is a 2, with the first night after a dense block as the worst moment.

## Steps

### 1. Agree the method with the surgeon and the patient
**Why:** The surgeon has chosen between tenodesis tensioning in a bloodless field and active tensioning in an awake patient with no tourniquet. The two need opposite anaesthetics.
**Anaesthesia:** For the wide-awake case the patient must be cooperative, able to lie still for up to two hours without sedation and able to follow instructions; anxiety, a language barrier without an interpreter, movement disorders and young children are the exclusions. For tetraplegia reconstruction read the level: a lesion at or above T6 brings autonomic dysreflexia with any stimulus below the level (a full bladder more often than the surgery, since the arm is partly or fully sensate), impaired cough, postural hypotension and difficult temperature control.

### 2. Anaesthesia for the conventional method
**Why:** The surgeon wants a still, bloodless forearm for 90-150 min and a tourniquet that is tolerated throughout.
**Anaesthesia:** Axillary or infraclavicular block with ropivacaine 0.5% 20-30 mL and dexamethasone 8 mg IV; add the intercostobrachial and medial cutaneous nerve of arm for the cuff. This is sufficient as the sole technique with light sedation, and is combined with a supraglottic airway when the patient prefers to be asleep. Record what the nerve palsy has already taken before the block, so that the deficit is not later attributed to the needle.

### 3. Anaesthesia for the wide-awake method
**Why:** The surgeon infiltrates the whole operative field with dilute lidocaine and adrenaline, waits for vasoconstriction, and operates without a tourniquet so that the muscles stay perfused and under the patient's control.
**Anaesthesia:** The local anaesthetic is the surgeon's and the dose is yours to add up. The published triple-transfer recipe is up to 200 mL of lidocaine 0.25% with adrenaline 1:400,000, buffered with bicarbonate: 500 mg of lidocaine, which is 7 mg/kg in a 70 kg patient and over that in a smaller one. Work out the ceiling in mL for this patient before the first syringe and say it. Infiltration is done 25-30 min before incision, proximal to distal through already numb skin, ideally outside theatre. Monitoring, a cannula and lipid emulsion in the room. No sedation is the method; if the patient needs something, small doses of midazolam or a low-dose remifentanil infusion that is off 10 min before tensioning, never a propofol infusion that leaves a patient who cannot follow a command. Do not add a block. Phentolamine reverses adrenaline-induced digital ischaemia and is rarely needed.

### 4. Tourniquet and exposure
**Why:** Under a block the limb is exsanguinated and the cuff inflated; the recipient tendons are exposed first to prove they glide and that the joints are passively mobile, because a transfer cannot move a stiff joint.
**Anaesthesia:** Note the inflation time and agree a limit of 120 min; a triple transfer with a slow weave reaches it (see [the tourniquet](../a/a01-tourniquet.html)). Antibiotic before inflation.

### 5. Donor harvest and mobilisation
**Why:** The donor tendon is cut at its insertion and the muscle freed from surrounding fascia well up the forearm so that it pulls in a straight line. Pronator teres is taken with a strip of periosteum from the radius to give it length.
**Anaesthesia:** The most stimulating part: proximal forearm dissection is at the upper edge of the infiltrated field in an awake case, and the patient reports it. More dilute local from the surgeon is the answer, within the ceiling. Under a block there is nothing to do.

### 6. Tunnelling and routing
**Why:** A blunt instrument makes a subcutaneous tunnel around the border of the forearm, wide enough for the muscle belly to glide, and the donor is passed to the recipient site.
**Anaesthesia:** Brief and blunt. The tunnel crosses skin supplied by the lateral and medial cutaneous nerves of forearm, the usual site of a patchy block; a few millilitres from the surgeon is quicker than conversion.

### 7. Setting tension
**Why:** One suture joins donor to recipient with the joints in the chosen posture. Asleep or blocked, the surgeon checks tenodesis: wrist flexed, fingers should open; wrist extended, fingers should close into the palm. Awake, the patient is asked to make a fist, then open the hand, and the suture is moved until full flexion and full extension are both possible.
**Anaesthesia:** In the wide-awake case this is the moment the patient must be alert, comfortable and not looking at a bleeding wound with alarm: lower the drape or use a mirror only if the patient wants to watch, coach the movement (after a pronator teres transfer the instruction is "turn your palm down" to extend the wrist), and keep the room quiet. If a tourniquet has been used under a block and the surgeon now wants active movement, it cannot be had: say so at the briefing, not here. Under general anaesthesia, no neuromuscular blocker is needed at any stage, and full relaxation alters resting muscle tone enough that some surgeons ask for it to be avoided.

### 8. Completing the weave, haemostasis and closure
**Why:** The remaining passes of the weave are sutured, the tourniquet is released and bleeding controlled, skin is closed.
**Anaesthesia:** Reactive hyperaemia after a two-hour cuff; nothing systemic of note in an adult. If the block was the sole anaesthetic, the surgeon's wound infiltration is unnecessary and uses up dose.

### 9. Splint
**Why:** The cast holds the joints so that the transfer is slack: wrist extended 30-45 degrees and fingers extended for radial palsy transfers, thumb opposed for an opponensplasty. It stays for three to four weeks unless an early active motion protocol is used.
**Anaesthesia:** The splint goes on before the patient moves. A blocked patient cannot protect the transfer and a waking patient under general anaesthesia will flex against it; either way the hand is held by the surgeon until the plaster is hard.

### 10. Recovery and discharge
**Why:** The surgeon wants elevation, a splint that is not tight, and a patient who will not test the transfer.
**Anaesthesia:** After WALANT the patient walks out with a working shoulder and elbow and a numb forearm for three to five hours; oral analgesia starts before discharge. After a long-acting block the arm is flail until the evening: sling, written advice about the insensate limb, and the first doses of paracetamol and NSAID taken on a schedule and not when pain starts.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is muscle and fascia in a cast, so the plan is simple analgesia with a single anaesthetic technique that also covers the first evening. Paracetamol and an NSAID regularly for three to five days, elevation, and a few doses of oral opioid for the first two nights after a triple transfer; a single small transfer in the hand often needs no opioid.

Under the conventional method the block is the anaesthetic and the analgesia. The choice between a long-acting agent with dexamethasone (12-18 hours, then a rebound at night) and a short-acting agent (a working arm by discharge, pain arriving in daylight) follows the reasoning in [hand trauma](hand-trauma.html); for a soft tissue operation with a pain score of 2 the author's practice is the shorter agent, or a short-acting plexus block with long-acting forearm nerve blocks as described in [the block menu](../a/a07-block-menu.html). Rebound is less of a problem than after bone surgery. Under WALANT the lidocaine wears off in the afternoon and the transition is gentle; the one comparative study found no difference in post-operative pain or opioid dose between wide-awake and general anaesthesia.

No catheter. If a joint fusion is added (a thumb interphalangeal or carpometacarpal fusion in tetraplegia or ulnar palsy), the pain moves to a 3 with a bone mechanism for 48 hours and a long-acting block with dexamethasone is the better choice.

Escalation: increasing pain in a cast is a tight cast, and it is split to skin. Sudden loss of the new movement with a snap is a ruptured weave. Pain and paraesthesia in the median distribution after an opponensplasty or a flexor-side tunnel is carpal tunnel compression from swelling.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The surgeon who wants both. A bloodless dissection under tourniquet and an awake test of tension are incompatible within one block. Workable compromises are WALANT throughout, or tenodesis throughout. A forearm tourniquet released 15-20 min before tensioning with distal sensory blocks does not reliably restore strength in time.
- Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity. A large-volume forearm infiltration plus a top-up plus skin infiltration at closure is how the ceiling is passed. One person keeps the tally.
- Tetraplegia. Autonomic dysreflexia, a blood pressure that falls on sitting, no thermoregulation, pressure areas, and a patient whose remaining arm function is being operated on, so both arms may be unavailable for cannulation and the patient cannot press a call bell afterwards. General anaesthesia is common; suxamethonium is avoided after the first days of injury.
- Leprosy and long-standing neuropathy. Insensate hands and feet, sometimes autonomic involvement; a block is still used, and what cannot be examined afterwards is documented before it.
- Rheumatoid extensor ruptures. Usually combined with an ulnar head excision and synovectomy, which is bone and synovium, in a patient with the problems set out in [rheumatoid hand surgery](rheumatoid-hand-small-joint.html).
- Transfers at the shoulder (latissimus dorsi or lower trapezius for an irreparable cuff) are a different operation in beach chair or lateral with an interscalene block; see [rotator cuff repair](rotator-cuff-repair.html).
- Combined with a nerve repair or transfer. If the surgeon is also stimulating nerves, the relaxant rules of the [brachial plexus](brachial-plexus-nerve-transfer.html) card apply.

## Key sources

The comparative evidence is thin. Wide-awake tendon transfer is supported by technique descriptions, case series and one small comparison with general anaesthesia; conventional tensioning is supported by decades of series without a control. No trial has asked whether active tensioning improves long-term function, and the choice remains the surgeon's. The anaesthetic content here is consensus and author's judgement, anchored to the published local anaesthetic volumes and to the general day-case hand block literature.
