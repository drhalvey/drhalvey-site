## What the surgeon is doing and why

The knee is the joint that arthroscopy was built for: two large compartments and a patellofemoral space that a 4 mm scope through an anterolateral portal can see in full, with instruments through an anteromedial portal and the joint kept open by saline. Everything on this card goes through those portals, and that is why a meniscectomy is a 1 and the anchor of the scale. What varies is what the surgeon does inside, and the range runs from trimming a flap of meniscus in twenty minutes to drilling holes in subchondral bone or lifting plugs of cartilage out of the trochlea, which are bone operations wearing arthroscopy's clothes.

The meniscus is a fibrocartilage ring that spreads load across the tibial plateau and is vascular only in its outer third. A tear in the avascular inner two-thirds will not heal, so the surgeon removes the unstable fragment back to a stable rim (partial meniscectomy): a fast, painless-looking operation whose consequence is a smaller meniscus and more contact stress for the rest of the knee's life. That consequence is why the operation's numbers are falling. In the randomised sham-controlled trial in middle-aged patients with a degenerative medial tear and no arthritis, meniscectomy did no better than a skin incision and the sound of a shaver at twelve months. The meniscectomy that remains is for the mechanical tear in a younger knee, the locked knee and the bucket-handle that will not reduce, and the surgeon is increasingly trying to keep the tissue. A tear in the vascular outer third, a root avulsion, or a bucket-handle in a young patient gets a repair: all-inside anchors fired through the meniscus into the capsule, inside-out sutures passed through the meniscus and tied over the capsule through a small posteromedial or posterolateral incision, or a transtibial pull-out suture for a root. The sutures are through capsule and the meniscus has to heal to its rim over weeks, which is why the repaired knee goes into a brace and onto crutches: partial weight-bearing in the first week and full weight-bearing at four to six weeks in most protocols, though the accelerated ones did no worse in the pooled series.

Cartilage is the other reason to be inside. A loose body or a flap of cartilage is removed and the edge trimmed (chondroplasty). A contained full-thickness defect in a young knee is treated with one of three things, in ascending order of what it does to bone. Microfracture perforates the subchondral plate with an awl so that marrow bleeds into the defect and forms a fibrocartilage clot; it is a few minutes of bone work with six weeks of protected weight-bearing to let the clot mature. Osteochondral autograft transfer (OATS, mosaicplasty) harvests cylinders of cartilage and bone from the non-loading margin of the trochlea and presses them into the defect: two bone surfaces, a donor site that gives patellofemoral symptoms in about 6% of knees, and the same weeks of protection. Matrix-induced autologous chondrocyte implantation (MACI) is two operations: a biopsy of cartilage at a first arthroscopy, then weeks later an arthrotomy to glue the cultured cells on a membrane into the prepared defect, which is a mini-open knee with a long rehabilitation and the pain of an arthrotomy, not of a scope. Synovectomy, plica excision and lateral retinacular release are the soft-tissue extras, and the release is the one that bleeds.

The pain logic follows. For a meniscectomy, a loose body or a chondroplasty, the only mechanism is surface: portals, a capsule distended by litres of saline, and fluid in the fat pad and the subcutaneous tissues that makes the knee feel tight for two days. Nothing has been cut that is loaded afterwards, and the patient walks on it that evening. Meniscal repair adds a capsule mechanism (sutures or anchors through the capsule, pulled by every flexion) and a brace, and the small incision of an inside-out repair runs beside the saphenous nerve medially or the peroneal nerve laterally. Microfracture and OATS add bone: a bleeding subchondral plate or a trochlear donor site, which behave like any small osteotomy for two or three days and are then protected by the crutches rather than loaded. None of it reaches the tendon-tension or retension mechanisms that make the [ACL reconstruction](acl-reconstruction.html) a 3 or the [tibial tubercle osteotomy](patellofemoral-mpfl-tto.html) a 4. The card is a 2 because of the repairs and the chondral work; a straight meniscectomy is the 1 the scale was anchored on.

## Steps

### 1. Pre-operative plan: the booking, the MRI and the day-case default
**Why:** The surgeon's list says "arthroscopy, right knee"; the MRI report and the surgeon's letter say whether it is a meniscectomy, a repair or a chondral procedure, and the surgeon will often not decide until the scope is in. Everything they want (a relaxed leg to lever into valgus, a view, and a patient who goes home that afternoon) is the same for all of them.
**Anaesthesia:** A general anaesthetic with a second-generation supraglottic airway is the default for every procedure on this card in a day-case patient; a unilateral spinal is reasonable when a general anaesthetic is undesirable, and the pooled trials show it costs about forty minutes to discharge readiness and over an hour to voiding against a peripheral block. Decide the block question now, against the worst case the surgeon has described (see the pain plan): no block for a meniscectomy, an adductor canal block offered for a repair or a chondral procedure. Paracetamol and an NSAID before induction. The patient who cannot take an NSAID is the one for whom the block has the most to offer.

### 2. Positioning, the leg holder and the tourniquet question
**Why:** The thigh is clamped in a holder with the foot of the table dropped, or the leg lies flat against a lateral post, so that the surgeon can apply valgus with the knee near extension to open the medial compartment and the figure-of-four to open the lateral. A thigh tourniquet is applied by habit.
**Anaesthesia:** The leg holder is a vice around the thigh above the cuff and the valgus stress is applied through it; a medial collateral ligament sprain from an enthusiastic surgeon in a stiff knee is the positioning injury of this operation, and the contralateral leg needs its own support and padding. The tourniquet is the honest conversation: in sixteen randomised trials and over a thousand patients, no cuff meant less blood loss and less analgesic use, with no difference in the view, the time, quadriceps strength or complications, and in the one trial that randomised inflation the surgeon needed the cuff up in four patients of the non-inflated group. A cuff applied and left down costs nothing; a cuff inflated at 300 mmHg for a 30-minute meniscectomy adds the one mechanism (ischaemia and cuff pain) that the operation otherwise does not have. If a block is planned and the cuff will be inflated, the block goes in before the cuff or at the end, not between the two: an adductor canal block followed immediately by cuff inflation produced quadriceps weakness in eight of a group where the other sequences produced none.

### 3. Portals, irrigation and the diagnostic round
**Why:** Anterolateral and anteromedial portals beside the patellar tendon, sometimes a superomedial outflow or a posteromedial portal for the back of the joint. Saline runs in by gravity or a pump at 40-80 mmHg to distend the capsule and clear the view; the surgeon inspects the suprapatellar pouch, both compartments, the notch and the cruciates before touching anything, and the diagnosis is made here.
**Anaesthesia:** The capsule distending is the first stimulus and a light patient flexes the hip against the holder; the portal incisions are small. The irrigation goes somewhere: into the joint, out of the portals onto the floor, and into the soft tissues around the knee, and over a long case the thigh and calf swell. Extravasation into the calf under a pump is the rare mechanism of a compartment syndrome after a knee scope, and a tense calf at the end of a long case under a working block is examined, not reassured. Adrenaline in the bags is the surgeon's answer to bleeding when the cuff is down; the transient tachycardia when a new bag goes up is that.

### 4. Partial meniscectomy, loose bodies and chondroplasty
**Why:** The unstable fragment is resected with a punch and a shaver back to a stable, contoured rim, keeping as much meniscus as possible because every millimetre removed raises contact pressure; a loose body is found and retrieved; an unstable cartilage flap is trimmed to a stable edge. Ten to twenty minutes of work.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing to do but keep the patient still for the surgeon's lever and note that the booking has resolved into the simplest version of the card. The patient will weight-bear as tolerated from the recovery room, and the pain for the next two days is the swollen joint and the fat pad, not the meniscus, which has no nerve supply in the part that was removed. This is the operation for which a nerve block would be given for nothing.

### 5. Meniscal repair
**Why:** A peripheral or root tear in a knee worth saving is reduced and fixed. All-inside devices fire a suture with anchors through the meniscus into the capsule from the front; inside-out repair passes long needles through the meniscus and out through the capsule, where the sutures are retrieved and tied over the capsule through a 3 cm posteromedial or posterolateral incision with a retractor protecting the structures behind the knee; a root is fixed with a suture pulled through a tibial tunnel. The surgeon abrades the tear edges and the synovium to bleed, because the repair heals from the vascular rim.
**Anaesthesia:** The case lengthens by half an hour, the capsule is now sutured and will be loaded by every flexion for six weeks, and the brace and crutches follow; this is the version that earns the 2 and the adductor canal block. The posteromedial incision sits on the saphenous nerve and its infrapatellar branch, the posterolateral one on the peroneal nerve, and the popliteal vessels lie behind the retractor during inside-out needle passage: a numb medial calf or a foot drop in recovery is surgical anatomy, not the block, which does not reach the peroneal nerve at all. The plan for discharge changes from "walk out" to "crutches, brace and the rehabilitation protocol", and the patient hears it from the surgeon before leaving.

### 6. Chondral procedures: microfracture, osteochondral autograft and the MACI stages
**Why:** Microfracture: the defect is debrided to stable walls, the calcified layer removed, and the subchondral plate perforated every 3-4 mm with an awl until marrow bleeds. OATS: cylinders of cartilage and bone are harvested from the trochlear margin and press-fitted into drilled sockets in the defect, usually through a small arthrotomy. MACI: a biopsy of a few hundred milligrams of cartilage from the notch at a first scope, then an arthrotomy weeks later to fix the cultured membrane into the prepared defect with fibrin glue.
**Anaesthesia:** Bone, for the first time on this card: a bleeding subchondral plate or two sets of cancellous sockets, which hurt like a small osteotomy for two or three days and then fall quiet under the protection of crutches and, for microfracture, six weeks of restricted weight-bearing. An OATS or a MACI implantation through an arthrotomy is a mini-open knee with a haemarthrosis and an incision through the retinaculum; the adductor canal block covers the medial arthrotomy and the oral regimen has to carry the first two nights. Remember the donor site: the trochlear harvest gives patellofemoral symptoms in about 6% of knees in the pooled series and is the sore spot the patient points to at a week.

### 7. Synovectomy, plica, lateral release and the bleeding extras
**Why:** An inflamed synovium or a thickened medial plica is shaved; a tight lateral retinaculum is divided from inside the joint with a radiofrequency device to let the patella track medially. The release cuts the lateral genicular vessels and a raw retinacular edge is left to bleed.
**Anaesthesia:** These are the procedures that produce the tense haemarthrosis at 6 pm: a raw synovial bed or a lateral release that bled only after the cuff came down and the portals closed. A drain is sometimes left; a request for tranexamic acid before the release is reasonable. The pain of a haemarthrosis is pressure and does not respond to opioid as well as it does to the surgeon's needle.

### 8. Intra-articular injection, portal closure and the chondrotoxicity question
**Why:** The surgeon injects local anaesthetic, often with adrenaline and sometimes a corticosteroid or morphine, into the joint and the portals, and closes the portals with a stitch or a strip. A compressive dressing goes on, and a brace if the meniscus has been repaired.
**Anaesthesia:** The injection is the analgesic plan for a meniscectomy, and it works: in the randomised comparison the surgeon's intra-articular local anaesthetic beat an adductor canal block for pain at one and two hours and matched it after that. Ropivacaine is the agent to prefer; bupivacaine, lidocaine and most corticosteroids are chondrotoxic to cartilage in a dose- and exposure-dependent way, and the chondrolysis literature was built on intra-articular infusion pumps, which have no place in any joint. A single 20 mL dose at the end of the case is a different exposure from an infusion, and a young knee with a fresh cartilage repair is the one where the surgeon's recipe deserves a glance. Count the total dose if a block has also been given.

### 9. Recovery, discharge and the first night at home
**Why:** The surgeon wants the meniscectomy walking that afternoon with no restriction; the repair in a brace, partial weight-bearing and range-of-motion exercises from the first week; the microfracture and the chondral graft on crutches with the knee moving but not loaded.
**Anaesthesia:** Paracetamol and an NSAID started before the patient leaves, with the first dose at home timed before bed, and a small number of oral opioid doses for the two nights after a repair or a chondral procedure; a meniscectomy patient does not usually need them. The local anaesthetic in the joint lasts into the evening and the knee is at its sorest the next morning, swollen and stiff rather than painful, and ice and elevation are the treatment. A patient going home with an adductor canal block has a leg that works and a numb medial calf that will recover overnight, and is told so. The calls that come back are the haemarthrosis after a release, the calf that is tense rather than sore, and the patient who was told "scope" and sent home with crutches without understanding why.

## Pain plan

The mechanism for most of this card is surface, so the plan is the surgeon's intra-articular and portal infiltration, paracetamol and an NSAID started before induction, and no block. The evidence for adding an adductor canal block to minor arthroscopic knee surgery is real and small: in the pooled trials it lowered resting pain by 1.5 cm at the moment the patient woke and by about half a centimetre at 6 and 8 h, and saved about 7 mg of oral morphine equivalent over 24 h. Against a surgeon's intra-articular injection it lost at one and two hours and matched it afterwards, with no difference in opioid use. A block that buys half a centimetre on a scale of ten, for an operation whose patients are going home on paracetamol, is the block the scale's first line describes: for the anaesthetic, not for pain. A landmark-based saphenous injection by the surgeon at the medial condyle, without ultrasound, did nothing at all.

The block earns its place when the operation changes. A meniscal repair, a microfracture, an osteochondral autograft and the implantation stage of MACI all add a mechanism (capsule under suture, or bone) and a night or two of real pain to a patient who is young, cannot bear weight and will not sleep; the author's practice is to offer a single-shot adductor canal block for these, placed in the femoral triangle rather than the true canal (the femoral triangle injection gave lower pain scores at 12 h with the same quadriceps strength), with 15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.375-0.5% and intravenous dexamethasone, and to write the oral regimen so that the first doses are in before the block fades the next morning. A femoral nerve block gives the same analgesia and a quadriceps that does not fire, which in a day-case patient on crutches is a fall waiting to happen; the trial that compared the two after meniscectomy found the same pain relief and a better knee score at a month with the adductor canal block. The opioid-tolerant patient, the patient who has had a miserable night after a previous scope, and the patient who cannot take an NSAID are the other three for whom the block is offered regardless of what is found.

No catheter, for anything. Rebound pain is a small problem because the block is small and the surgery is mostly surface; after a chondral procedure in a young patient it is the usual pattern of a sore morning rather than a catastrophic night, and the oral regimen started in recovery covers it. Escalation: pain out of proportion with a tense knee in the first 24 h is a haemarthrosis, usually after a release or a synovectomy, and the surgeon aspirates it; a tense, painful calf is extravasation or a compartment syndrome, rare and surgical; pain with fever and a hot joint from day 3 onwards is a septic arthritis until the aspirate says otherwise.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The surgeon who inflates the cuff for the whole case. The randomised evidence is against it; the cost is cuff pain and quadriceps weakness in a day case that had neither. Raise it once, then put the cuff on and leave the decision to them.
- The leg holder and the valgus lever. Medial collateral sprain and, in the osteoporotic, a femoral fracture through the holder have both been described; the surgeon's force, not yours, but the padding and the contralateral leg are.
- Inside-out repair. A posteromedial or posterolateral incision and long needles passed towards the back of the knee: the saphenous nerve, the peroneal nerve and the popliteal vessels are the things that present to you as numbness, a foot drop or a swelling in recovery.
- Extravasation and compartment syndrome. Pump pressures above 80 mmHg, long cases, a capsular breach: the calf swells. A tense calf under a working block is examined by pressure measurement, not by waiting for pain.
- Intra-articular drugs. Ropivacaine rather than bupivacaine, a single dose rather than an infusion, no pain pumps, and a question about the corticosteroid in a knee with fresh cartilage work; the surgeon's recipe is the surgeon's, but you count the dose.
- Local anaesthetic arthroscopy. A 20 mL intra-articular dose with portal infiltration and light sedation is enough for a simple meniscectomy in a slim, calm patient with a quick surgeon; the pump pressure and the lever hurt, and conversion to general anaesthesia with the leg in a holder is awkward.
- The degenerative meniscus in the middle-aged knee. The sham-controlled trial found no benefit; the operation is still done, the patient's expectations are the surgeon's problem, and the pain afterwards is the pain of the arthritis that was already there.
- Two-stage MACI. The biopsy is a 20-minute scope; the implantation weeks later is an arthrotomy with a long rehabilitation, booked on the same list as a scope and anaesthetised like a mini-open knee, with the block and the opioid plan that implies.
- Paediatric and adolescent patients. Discoid meniscus saucerisation, osteochondritis dissecans drilling and fixation: the same portals, often a longer non-weight-bearing period, and a parent who needs the night plan.
- Anticoagulated patients and venous thromboembolism. The operation itself is low risk; the six weeks on crutches after a repair or a chondral procedure is not, and the unit's protocol decides.

## Key sources

The block evidence is a meta-analysis of ten randomised trials and a handful of later trials, consistent with each other: an adductor canal block gives a small, early, motor-sparing benefit after minor arthroscopy, is beaten by the surgeon's intra-articular injection in the first two hours, and is wasted when done by landmark. The tourniquet evidence is sixteen randomised trials pointing the same way. The rehabilitation statements after meniscal repair come from two systematic reviews that agree there is no consensus; the chondrotoxicity statements rest on the shoulder pain-pump literature and a scoping review of intra-articular drugs, extrapolated to the knee by mechanism rather than by trial. The sham-controlled meniscectomy trial is cited for why the operation is changing, not for anything about its pain.
