## What the surgeon is doing and why

The forearm is a joint, not two bones. The radius is bowed and rotates around a straight ulna, hinged at the proximal and distal radioulnar joints and tied along its length by the interosseous membrane. Pronation and supination depend on the exact length of both bones and on the curve of the radius; a few degrees of malunion or a millimetre of shortening costs rotation. In an adult a displaced shaft fracture is therefore treated like an articular fracture: opened, reduced anatomically and held with rigid compression plates. Casts and nails do not control rotation well enough, and the operation is close to universal.

The injuries:

- Both bones, from a fall or a direct blow, sometimes high energy.
- Isolated ulna (the nightstick fracture, a direct blow to a raised forearm).
- Monteggia: an ulnar fracture with the radial head dislocated at the elbow. Fix the ulna to length and the radial head reduces; if it does not, something is in the joint.
- Galeazzi: a radial shaft fracture with the distal radioulnar joint disrupted. Fix the radius and then test the joint, pinning or repairing it if unstable.

The surgeon uses two separate incisions, on purpose, because a single exposure of both bones invites a bridge of bone between them (synostosis) that abolishes rotation. The radius is reached from the front by the Henry approach: between brachioradialis and flexor carpi radialis, the radial artery taken medially, the superficial radial nerve laterally, and the deep muscles (pronator teres, flexor pollicis longus, pronator quadratus, and supinator proximally, with the posterior interosseous nerve inside it) lifted off the bone. Some use the dorsal Thompson approach for the proximal radius. The ulna is subcutaneous along its border and is exposed between flexor and extensor carpi ulnaris. Each bone gets a 3.5 mm plate with at least three screws either side of the fracture, applied in compression. The deep fascia is not closed.

That last detail is the surgical acknowledgement of the problem this card is organised around. The forearm has three tight compartments, and after the tibia it is the commonest site of acute compartment syndrome. The fracture bleeds into them; the operation adds retraction, a tourniquet and reperfusion swelling. Risk rises with energy: in the systematic review the fracture and the degree of soft tissue injury were the main predictors, and a series of gunshot forearm fractures recorded compartment syndrome in 12%. In a low-energy closed fracture the risk is low but not zero. The diagnosis is clinical: pain out of proportion, rising when it should be falling, and pain on passive stretch of the fingers, hours before any change in pulse or sensation.

The pain logic: periosteum stripped along two bones through muscle, a 3 with a sharp day 1 and a quick fall, in a young population prone to rebound. The mechanism argues for a block. The complication argues for care about which block. The argument is set out in [nerve injury and compartment syndrome](../a/a11-nerve-injury-compartment.html) and applied here.

## Steps

### 1. Stratify the limb before choosing the analgesia
**Why:** The surgeon knows the mechanism and has examined the forearm; some have a fixed view on blocks in forearm fractures and some do not.
**Anaesthesia:** Sort the patient into one of two groups and say which aloud at the briefing. Lower risk: a fall from standing, a closed fracture, a soft forearm, surgery a day or more after injury. Higher risk: a crush, a fall from height or road traffic injury, a gunshot, an open fracture with a small wound (the compartments are not decompressed by it), a floating elbow, a tense or very swollen forearm, a young muscular man, anticoagulation, a vascular injury or repair, or an obtunded patient who cannot report pain whatever you do. Document sensation and motor function for median, ulnar, radial and anterior and posterior interosseous nerves before any block; the posterior interosseous nerve is at risk from the injury (Monteggia) and from the approach.

### 2. The analgesic decision
**Why:** The surgeon needs a patient whose forearm can be assessed overnight by someone who is not the surgeon.
**Anaesthesia:** For the lower-risk limb the author's practice is general anaesthesia with a single-shot plexus block (ropivacaine 0.375-0.5% 20-25 mL) and intravenous dexamethasone, exactly as for [distal radius fixation](distal-radius-orif.html), with compartment observations charted. For the higher-risk limb the defensible options are no block (opioid, paracetamol, NSAID if renal function and bleeding allow, low-dose ketamine and surgeon wound infiltration) or a low-concentration block without adjuncts (ropivacaine 0.2%), which blunts incisional pain and lets ischaemic pain break through. The Association of Anaesthetists guideline for the lower leg supports the second option provided surveillance is real, and a 2026 systematic review found no included study in which regional anaesthesia raised the incidence or delayed the diagnosis; both rest on case series and observational data and neither is specific to the forearm. Whichever is chosen, the decision is made jointly and written down with the block's expected duration. The guideline is explicit that neither specialty holds a veto.

### 3. Induction, position and tourniquet
**Why:** Supine with the arm out on a radiolucent table; the surgeon will supinate for the radius and then flex the elbow and bring the forearm up or across for the ulna.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway or tube by the usual criteria; a block alone is possible for an isolated ulna but two approaches and two hours of forearm traction under a tourniquet are long for an awake patient. Antibiotic before inflation. Ask whether the cuff will be inflated: in a swollen high-energy forearm some surgeons leave it down, which means 150-300 mL of blood loss into the drapes and a slower, wetter operation.

### 4. Volar approach to the radius
**Why:** The interval between brachioradialis and flexor carpi radialis is opened, the radial artery and its branches to brachioradialis are controlled, and the forearm is fully supinated to move the posterior interosseous nerve away before supinator is elevated from the proximal radius.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing physiological under tourniquet. The superficial radial nerve under brachioradialis is retracted for an hour and is the usual source of a numb patch on the back of the thumb afterwards.

### 5. Reduction and plating of the radius
**Why:** Fragments are cleaned, keyed together and held with clamps, restoring the radial bow; the plate is contoured, applied in compression and checked on fluoroscopy.
**Anaesthesia:** Reduction needs traction against muscle; a relaxed patient makes it easier, and a single dose of relaxant at this point is reasonable when the airway allows. Lead for the screen.

### 6. Approach and plating of the ulna
**Why:** The subcutaneous border is exposed with little muscle to lift, the fracture reduced and plated. In comminuted both-bone fractures the simpler bone is fixed first to give length for the other.
**Anaesthesia:** The arm is repositioned: check that the shoulder is not forced, the tourniquet tubing is not kinked and the cannula in the other arm still runs. Tourniquet time passes 60 min here.

### 7. Radial head or distal radioulnar joint assessment
**Why:** In a Monteggia injury the radial head must be reduced on screening after the ulna is fixed; in a Galeazzi injury the distal radioulnar joint is stressed and, if unstable, pinned in supination or the triangular fibrocartilage repaired.
**Anaesthesia:** Adds 15-45 min. A radial head that will not reduce means an elbow arthrotomy and a different post-operative splint. Note the tourniquet time and plan release at 120 min.

### 8. Tourniquet release, haemostasis and closure of skin only
**Why:** The cuff is let down before closure to find bleeding and to see the muscle. Fascia is left open; skin is closed, or left open if it will not come together without tension.
**Anaesthesia:** Look at the forearm with the surgeon. Muscle that bulges and a wound that cannot be closed are compartment findings, and the surgeon may extend to a formal [fasciotomy](compartment-syndrome-fasciotomy.html) there and then. Maintain normal blood pressure from here on: perfusion pressure across the compartment is diastolic pressure minus compartment pressure, and a hypotensive, vasoconstricted recovery narrows it.

### 9. Dressing and splint
**Why:** A padded backslab or nothing rigid at all; fixation is stable and early movement is the aim. Circumferential casts are avoided.
**Anaesthesia:** Dressings loose enough to admit a finger and split-able. The hand must be visible and reachable for examination.

### 10. Recovery and the first night
**Why:** The surgeon wants elevation at heart level (not above it, which lowers perfusion pressure) and hourly neurovascular and pain observations.
**Anaesthesia:** Write three things in the notes and say them to the recovery and ward nurses: what block was given and when it should wear off; that pain scores should fall with analgesia, and a score that rises, or pain on passive finger extension, or new paraesthesia, triggers a call to the surgical team and not another dose; and that breakthrough pain under a working block is treated as compartment syndrome until examined. Patient-controlled opioid does not exempt anyone from this: escalating demand is the same sign. Start oral paracetamol in recovery.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is periosteal and muscular, peaks on day 1 and falls by day 3. Paracetamol regularly; an NSAID for three to five days in the author's practice unless the surgeon objects on union grounds or renal function and bleeding argue against; oral opioid for three to five days. With a block in the lower-risk limb, intravenous dexamethasone and the first oral doses before the block recedes are the measures against rebound, which this young, bone-surgery population is prone to.

The block question, in one place. There is no trial. The physiological concern is that ischaemic muscle pain, the earliest and most sensitive sign of compartment syndrome, is abolished by a dense block. The counter-arguments are that case reports of delayed diagnosis mostly involve dense, prolonged blocks or epidurals with poor surveillance; that ischaemic pain has been reported to break through low-concentration blocks; that opioid analgesia also masks pain; and that untreated pain is itself harm. The lower-leg guideline resolves this by avoiding dense blocks that far outlast surgery, permitting low-concentration blocks without adjuncts, and putting the weight on surveillance. Applied to the forearm, the defensible default is: a standard block for the low-energy closed fracture, a low-concentration block or none for the high-risk limb, and no long-acting adjunct-laden block for a limb that worries the surgeon. This is author's judgement by extrapolation, and a surgeon who prefers no block in any forearm fracture holds a position that the evidence cannot refute.

A next-morning block, once the forearm has been examined and is soft, is a good answer for the high-risk patient who had a bad night.

No catheter as a routine.

Escalation is the content of step 10. Do not treat rising pain by re-blocking.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The obtunded or intubated patient. Pain is unavailable as a sign, so the block debate is moot and the surgeon relies on examination and pressure measurement; say this at handover.
- Open fractures. Debridement first, sometimes temporary stabilisation and a second operation; a small wound does not decompress the compartments. See [open fractures](open-fractures-damage-control.html).
- Anticoagulated patients and those with a vascular repair have the highest risk of a tense forearm overnight.
- Posterior interosseous nerve palsy. From the Monteggia injury, the proximal radial approach or a retractor. Finger and thumb extension documented before surgery, and before a block, is what distinguishes them.
- Tourniquet pain and hypertension after 60-75 min under general anaesthesia without a block: expected, and it disappears with deflation.
- Synostosis and refracture after plate removal are the late surgical problems; [removal of metalwork](removal-of-metalwork.html) from the forearm is not a trivial operation and repeats the nerve risks.
- Children. Forearm shaft fractures in children are treated with casts or flexible nails and belong to [paediatric supracondylar and forearm fractures](paediatric-supracondylar-forearm.html).
- Polytrauma. The forearm is fixed late in the sequence; the block decision is then also about which other limbs are at risk ([polytrauma sequencing](polytrauma-sequencing.html)).

## Key sources

The surgical evidence for plating is old and observational, with a pooled adverse event rate of about a quarter. The block debate has no randomised evidence and will not get any, because compartment syndrome is too rare to power a trial. What exists is a consensus guideline for the lower leg and systematic reviews of observational studies that find no signal of harm from regional anaesthesia with surveillance. The recommendations on this card extrapolate both to the forearm and are labelled as judgement.
