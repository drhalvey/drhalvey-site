## What the surgeon is doing and why

A hip fracture is two different injuries that share a ward. The line that separates them is the capsule. An intracapsular fracture (subcapital or transcervical) breaks the neck inside the joint capsule and tears the retinacular vessels that run up the neck to feed the head, so a displaced intracapsular fracture in an old patient has a head that will die if it is fixed. The surgeon therefore removes the head and replaces it. An extracapsular fracture (intertrochanteric or subtrochanteric) breaks through the cancellous metaphysis outside the capsule, leaves the head alive, and bleeds into the thigh instead of into the joint. The surgeon fixes it and lets it heal, and never replaces it unless the fixation fails.

That one anatomical fact sets the two surgical pathways:

```
                     hip fracture
                          |
          +---------------+----------------+
     intracapsular                     extracapsular
   (head blood supply torn)         (head alive, bleeds into thigh)
          |                                |
   undisplaced  displaced           stable 2-part   unstable, reverse
       |            |               intertroch.     oblique, subtroch.
  cannulated   fit, independent,        |                 |
   screws      cognitively intact:     DHS       cephalomedullary nail
               THA                                  (traction table)
               everyone else:
               cemented hemiarthroplasty
```

For the displaced intracapsular fracture the replacement is a hemiarthroplasty in most patients: a stemmed femoral component with a large head that articulates with the native acetabulum, cemented in most units. The WHiTE 5 trial settled the cement argument for this group: in 1,225 patients the cemented stem gave better quality of life at four months, a quarter of the periprosthetic fracture rate (0.5% against 2.1%), and 12-month mortality of 23.9% against 27.8% with an uncemented stem, so the cement that frightens the anaesthetist does not cost lives overall and the uncemented stem that avoids the cement step fractures the osteoporotic femur it is hammered into. The fit, independent, cognitively intact patient who walks outdoors with at most a stick gets a total hip arthroplasty instead, because the native acetabulum wears under a metal head and a total hip gives better function in someone who will use it for years; the price is a longer operation, more blood loss and a higher dislocation rate, which is why it is reserved for the patient who can follow precautions. The undisplaced intracapsular fracture in which the head is probably still alive is fixed with cannulated screws through a small lateral incision, the shortest and least painful operation on this card.

For the extracapsular fracture, the device depends on whether the lateral wall of the femur will hold a plate. A stable two-part intertrochanteric fracture with an intact lateral wall gets a dynamic hip screw: a large lag screw up the neck into the head, sliding in a barrel attached to a plate on the lateral femoral shaft, so that the fracture compresses as the patient weight-bears. An unstable, reverse oblique or subtrochanteric fracture gets a cephalomedullary nail: an intramedullary nail down the canal from the tip of the greater trochanter, with a lag screw or blade through it up the neck, which takes the load through the centre of the bone rather than the side. Both are done on a traction table under image intensification, with the fracture reduced closed by traction and rotation before the skin is cut.

The frail patient is the operation. The mean age in the big trials is about 80, a fifth are delirious at some point, and roughly a quarter are dead within a year of the fracture, mostly from what they already had. The surgery is the treatment for the pain, the immobility and the pneumonia and pressure injury that immobility causes, which is why both the Australian clinical care standard and the UK guideline set a 36-hour limit from presentation to surgery and treat a delay as a harm that needs a justification. HIP ATTACK tested whether faster is better still, with a median of 6 h against 24 h in 2,970 patients: 90-day mortality (9% against 10%) and major complications (22% in both arms) did not change, but delirium, urinary infection, moderate-to-severe pain and length of stay all fell. The lesson is that the window is the standard, that hours within it buy comfort rather than survival, and that the pre-operative work has to be done inside the window rather than used as a reason to miss it.

The pain logic is bone throughout, with a twist. Before surgery the pain generator is a mobile fracture, and it hurts on movement far more than at rest, which is why positioning for a spinal without a block is cruel and why the block goes in before the patient is moved. After surgery the generator is the osteotomy and the cement interface (hemiarthroplasty), or the drill track and reamed canal through a fracture plus a lateral incision through vastus lateralis (DHS) or a nail entry through gluteus medius at the trochanteric tip (nail). None of these tissues is under tension and nothing is being re-tensioned, so the pain falls over two or three days the way bone pain does, and the operation has removed most of what hurt before. The score is 3: a block or neuraxial technique is expected, opioid is needed on day 0-2, and the fall is quick. The complication is that the patient often cannot tell you, so the pain plan has to be written for someone who will not ask.

## Steps

### 1. Emergency department: the block, the clock and the assessment
**Why:** The surgeon wants the patient optimised and on a trauma list within 36 h, with a decision already made between replacement and fixation, which needs the radiographs, the patient's pre-fracture walking and cognition, and the anticoagulant history.
**Anaesthesia:** The block goes in here, not in the anaesthetic room. Both the Australian standard and PROSPECT want a fascia iliaca or femoral nerve block as part of multimodal analgesia from presentation, and the Australian standard measures it before surgery and before any inter-hospital transfer. The supra-inguinal fascia iliaca approach is the PROSPECT preference; a PENG block is a reasonable alternative and matched it for dynamic pain in the one head-to-head trial, with no difference in resting pain or motor block to 48 h, although PROSPECT does not yet recommend it on the evidence available. Paracetamol starts now. An NSAID is a judgement in a patient with an eGFR that is usually unknown at this point and a kidney that has just been through a fall and a night on the floor. The admission bundle that matters to you: a 4AT for delirium, a frailty score, the Nottingham Hip Fracture Score or similar for risk, an ECG, haemoglobin, electrolytes, renal function, a group and hold, the time and dose of the last anticoagulant, and intravenous fluid, because almost every one of these patients is dry.

### 2. Optimisation and the reasons to delay
**Why:** A delayed operation is a longer time in pain and in bed. The surgeon will push for the next list and the orthogeriatrician will push for the patient to be fit for it, and both are right.
**Anaesthesia:** The UK guideline lists the reasons that justify delay and insists they are corrected inside the 36-h window rather than used to cancel: haemoglobin under 80 g/L, sodium under 120 or over 150 mmol/L, potassium under 2.8 or over 6.0 mmol/L, uncontrolled diabetes, acute left ventricular failure, an uncorrected rate over 120, chest sepsis and a reversible coagulopathy. A murmur without symptoms is not on the list, and an echocardiogram that costs a day is a worse trade than a careful anaesthetic with an arterial line and the assumption that the aortic valve is tight. Anticoagulants: warfarin is reversed with vitamin K 5 mg IV on admission, with surgery acceptable at an INR of 1.8 or below and a spinal at 1.5 or below; a factor Xa inhibitor is stopped and the operation planned 24 h after the last dose (48 h if the eGFR is under 60), or sooner if an anti-Xa level is under 50 ng/mL; dabigatran is planned for the afternoon of the next day with a normal thrombin time, or reversed with idarucizumab if the thrombin time is prolonged and the surgery cannot wait. A single antiplatelet agent, including clopidogrel, is not a bar to a spinal in the UK guidance; dual therapy pushes most anaesthetists towards a general anaesthetic with the block as the analgesic, although the guideline stops short of forbidding the spinal. Transfuse to a haemoglobin above 90 g/L, or above 100 g/L with ischaemic heart disease, before the patient reaches the table rather than during cementing.

### 3. Spinal or general: the decision and what it rests on
**Why:** The surgeon has no preference beyond a still patient and no delay; the choice is yours and the patient's.
**Anaesthesia:** Two large trials have taken the folk belief that spinal anaesthesia protects the brain and the heart and failed to confirm it. REGAIN randomised 1,600 patients with a mean age of 78 and found death or inability to walk at 60 days in 18.5% with spinal and 18.0% with general anaesthesia, with delirium in 20.5% and 19.7%, and with 15% of the spinal group converted to general anaesthesia on the day. RAGA randomised 950 patients over 65 and found delirium within a week in 6.2% with regional anaesthesia given without sedation and 5.1% with general anaesthesia. PROSPECT accepts either, with a note that spinal may give better pain relief in the first hours. So the decision rests on the individual: a spinal is the defensible default for the patient who can lie still on a blocked hip for the duration, with a low dose of hyperbaric bupivacaine (the UK guideline's specimen protocol uses under 2 mL of 0.5%) and no intrathecal opioid when a block is working, because the hypotension of a full-dose spinal in a dry 85-year-old is the preventable harm of the technique; a general anaesthetic is the right answer for the patient with dementia who cannot cooperate, dual antiplatelet therapy, a fixed cardiac output lesion where sympathectomy is dangerous, or a nail that will take 90 min on a traction table. Sedation for a spinal is propofol in small doses, not midazolam, and a patient who needs deep sedation to tolerate the spinal should have had the general anaesthetic. Target a mean arterial pressure above 70 mmHg with a vasopressor infusion started as the spinal takes rather than boluses after the fact; the UK guideline's quality target is less than five minutes in total below that figure.

### 4. The block before positioning, and positioning for the spinal
**Why:** The spinal has to be done with the patient on their side or sitting, and either position moves the fracture.
**Anaesthesia:** If the emergency department block was more than six hours ago, repeat it in the anaesthetic room before the patient is moved; the UK guideline asks for a block in the emergency department and a block at surgery, at least 6 h apart, and the second one is the one that makes the spinal civilised. Sit or lateral the patient with the fractured side down if lateral, because the fractured hip tolerates lying on it better than being lifted off the bed, with an assistant holding the leg in slight traction and the foot supported. Cefazolin 2 g IV goes in once the spinal is sited. Tranexamic acid is drawn up only after the spinal is in and never on the same tray as the spinal drugs; intrathecal tranexamic acid is a lethal drug error that the hip fracture list has produced more than once.

### 5. Positioning: lateral for the arthroplasty, traction table for the fixation
**Why:** Hemiarthroplasty and THA need a lateral position for the posterior or anterolateral approach (some surgeons use a supine direct lateral approach). DHS and nail need the patient supine on a traction table with the fractured leg in a boot, the pelvis against a padded perineal post, the other leg abducted or scissored out of the way of the image intensifier, and the arm on the operative side across the chest so the C-arm can swing from anteroposterior to lateral.
**Anaesthesia:** On the traction table the hazards are the post and the pull. The perineal post takes the counter-traction and presses on the pudendal nerve and the genitalia; a pudendal neuropraxia and scrotal or labial pressure injury follow a long pull against a poorly padded post, so look at the padding and ask for the traction to be released whenever the surgeon is not reducing. The pull on the leg stretches the sciatic nerve and, in a boot that is too tight or a foot that is plantar-flexed, the peroneal nerve. The well leg, abducted and elevated, is at risk of a compartment syndrome in a long case, which matters more for a femoral shaft than for a 50-minute DHS but is not zero here. Under a spinal, the patient's arms need positioning before the drapes make them unreachable, and the operative-side arm across the chest must not pull on the shoulder. In the lateral position for a hemiarthroplasty, the dependent eye, ear and brachial plexus need the usual care, the axillary roll goes under the chest wall not the axilla, and the blood pressure cuff on the dependent arm reads the pressure at the heart with the same hydrostatic honesty as a supine patient. A spinal patient on their side under a drape for 80 minutes is a patient whose face you cannot see; the capnography line on the oxygen mask is the monitor of sedation depth.

### 6. Hemiarthroplasty: approach, capsulotomy and removal of the head
**Why:** Through a posterior approach (short external rotators divided and the capsule opened at the back) or an anterolateral approach (the anterior third of gluteus medius lifted off the trochanter), the surgeon opens the capsule, removes the fractured head with a corkscrew, measures it, and cuts the neck to the planned level. The posterior approach is faster and spares the abductors at the cost of a higher dislocation rate; the anterolateral approach is more stable and leaves an abductor repair that limps for weeks.
**Anaesthesia:** Removing the head is where the blood starts, from the neck and from the intracapsular haematoma that has been sitting there since the fall; the 2015 safety guideline tells you to watch for cardiorespiratory compromise from this moment onwards, not from the cement. It is also where the surgeon confirms the implant plan and where you should hear the words "we will be cementing" and acknowledge them, because the guideline makes that exchange a formal requirement and the next ten minutes are the dangerous part of the day.

### 7. Hemiarthroplasty: canal preparation and cement
**Why:** The femoral canal is opened, broached to size, washed with pressurised lavage, dried, plugged distally, and filled retrogradely from a cement gun; the stem is inserted and held while the cement cures. The washing and drying and plugging are not fastidiousness: they are the surgical half of the bone cement implantation syndrome precautions, because cement pressurised into a canal full of fat and marrow drives that fat and marrow into the femoral veins.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step the 2015 AAGBI, BOA and BGS safety guideline was written for. Bone cement implantation syndrome is graded 1 (saturation below 94% or a systolic fall of more than 20%, in about 20% of cemented hemiarthroplasties), 2 (saturation below 88%, a systolic fall of more than 40% or loss of consciousness, about 3%) and 3 (cardiac arrest, about 1%); the risk factors are age, male sex, cardiopulmonary disease and diuretic use, which describes most of the list. The mechanism is embolic: fat, marrow and air into the right heart, pulmonary vasoconstriction, acute right ventricular failure and a fall in left-sided filling, so under a spinal the first sign is the patient becoming confused or restless and under a general anaesthetic it is an abrupt fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide before the pressure moves. The precautions are all before the event: a patient resuscitated with fluid before induction and again before cementing, a vasopressor infusion already running, the pressure within 20% of the pre-induction systolic, non-invasive pressure on stat cycling or an arterial line in the higher-risk patient, adrenaline drawn up and diluted, and the surgeon told that in this patient they should not pressurise with vigour. When it happens: 100% oxygen, head down, fluid, adrenaline in small boluses for the right heart, and the surgeon stops pressurising and waits. The collapse that is not treated in the first two minutes is the one that becomes an arrest in the lateral position under drapes, which is a resuscitation nobody does well. See [bone cement and BCIS](../a/a02-cement-bcis.html) for the mechanism and the uncemented alternative, which WHiTE 5 has shown is not the safer choice for the patient overall.

### 8. Hemiarthroplasty and THA: trial, reduction and closure
**Why:** The head size is chosen to match the one removed, the hip is reduced, stability is tested through the range that matters for the approach, and the capsule and rotators (posterior) or the abductors (anterolateral) are repaired. A THA adds acetabular reaming and a cup, 30-60 min and a further bleed from the acetabular bed.
**Anaesthesia:** Reduction needs a relaxed patient; under a spinal that is already the case, under a general anaesthetic a bolus of relaxant or a deeper plane is what the surgeon is asking for when they say the hip will not go in. Each trial reduction and dislocation is a tug on the femoral vein and a transient fall in pressure in a patient who has just been cemented. For the THA patient, who is fitter by definition, the plan follows the elective card for the approach ([posterior](tha-posterior.html) or [anterior and lateral](tha-anterior-lateral.html)) with the fracture's blood loss and the fracture's timing added.

### 9. Extracapsular fractures: closed reduction on the traction table
**Why:** Before any incision the surgeon pulls on the boot, internally rotates the leg to bring the neck into profile, and checks the reduction on anteroposterior and lateral images: the neck-shaft angle restored, the medial cortex in contact, the fragments not in varus. A reduction that cannot be achieved closed means a percutaneous or open reduction and a longer case.
**Anaesthesia:** Traction on an unblocked fracture is the most painful moment of the day and the one that makes the case for the second block before the spinal. Under a spinal the patient feels the pull as pressure in the groin from the post; under a general anaesthetic the heart rate tells you the traction has gone on. The image intensifier means lead for everyone and a C-arm that sweeps across the patient's chest and your lines; secure the arm across the chest and the lines before the drapes go on. The reduction time is dead time for the surgeon and the right time for you to check the post, the boot and the well leg.

### 10. Dynamic hip screw: lateral approach, guidewire, reaming and plate
**Why:** A lateral incision from the greater trochanter down the shaft, through fascia lata and vastus lateralis lifted off the bone, exposes the lateral femoral cortex. A guidewire is placed up the neck into the centre of the head under imaging, the track is reamed over it with a triple reamer, the lag screw is inserted to within 10 mm of the joint surface, the barrel plate slides over it and is screwed to the shaft, and the traction is released so the fracture compresses along the screw.
**Anaesthesia:** The bleeding is from vastus lateralis stripped off the bone and from the reamed cancellous track, which is why this short operation has a bigger haemoglobin fall than its 45 minutes suggest; the extracapsular fracture has also already lost a unit or more into the thigh before the patient reached you. The vastus lateralis split and the periosteal stripping along the shaft are the muscle and periosteal components of the pain, which the fascia iliaca block covers better than a PENG block because the lateral femoral cutaneous and femoral territories are what the incision crosses. Release of traction at the end is a moment of brief hypotension in a vasodilated spinal patient as the leg's venous return changes; it passes.

### 11. Cephalomedullary nail: entry point, reaming, lag screw and locking
**Why:** A short incision proximal to the greater trochanter, through gluteus medius at the trochanteric tip, admits a guidewire and an entry reamer into the canal; the canal is reamed to accept the nail (short nails need little reaming, long nails reaming to the isthmus), the nail is driven in, a guidewire is placed up the neck through the nail, the lag screw or helical blade is inserted into the head, and the nail is locked distally through a small lateral incision with the image intensifier.
**Anaesthesia:** Reaming the femoral canal through a fresh fracture in an elderly patient is a marrow embolisation step, smaller than a shaft nailing but the same physiology: a fall in saturation and end-tidal carbon dioxide and a rise in pulmonary pressure as the reamer passes, more with a long nail than a short one; see [fat embolism](../a/a03-fat-embolism.html). The entry through gluteus medius at the trochanteric tip is the pain the patient complains of on the stairs for months, and it is the muscle mechanism in this card. Distal locking needs the surgeon's hands in the beam and a still leg, and a long nail that needs distal locking by freehand technique is 15 minutes of fluoroscopy at the end of a case that already felt finished. Hypothermia is the quiet complication of the traction table: the patient is exposed, the room is cold for the surgeon, and the spinal has removed thermoregulation below the waist; forced-air warming on the upper body from the start.

### 12. Emergence, recovery and the first night
**Why:** The surgeon wants a check radiograph, full weight-bearing from the day after surgery, and no restrictions beyond the posterior-approach precautions for a hemiarthroplasty.
**Anaesthesia:** The haemoglobin on the morning after is lower than the one in recovery, because the thigh and the wound keep oozing and the fluids redistribute; transfuse to the thresholds above rather than waiting for symptoms the patient cannot report. Delirium is the common post-operative complication, a fifth of patients in REGAIN whichever anaesthetic they had, and its drivers are the ones you control in the first 24 h: pain that is unassessed because the patient cannot score it, hypoxia, hypotension, a full bladder, anticholinergic or benzodiazepine drugs, and a night without sleep. Regular paracetamol and, where the kidney allows, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor are the PROSPECT skeleton, with opioid as rescue only; in the patient with dementia a behavioural pain scale replaces the number and the first dose goes in on time rather than on request. The block recedes overnight; the rebound is moderate because the fracture is fixed and the movement that used to hurt no longer moves anything. Low molecular weight heparin restarts at least 4 h after the spinal and 12 h before any further neuraxial procedure, and continues for 28-35 days.

### 13. Day 1 onwards: mobilisation, orthogeriatrics and what you still own
**Why:** The implant is designed to be loaded from day 1; a DHS compresses as the patient weight-bears, a cemented stem is fixed from the moment the cement cures, and a nail carries load through the canal. The surgeon wants the patient out of bed with the physiotherapists on the day after surgery, which is the Australian standard's quality statement 5.
**Anaesthesia:** The things that stop a patient standing on day 1 are yours: a dense block that has not worn off (a femoral or fascia iliaca catheter is the usual reason, which is why both guidelines discourage them), postural hypotension from a vasodilated spinal patient who is still dry, a haemoglobin under 90 g/L, a delirium, and pain that was not treated regularly overnight. The orthogeriatric review covers the medications, the bone health and the discharge plan; your contribution is to have written a plan that a ward can give at 2 am to a patient who cannot ask, and to have documented the block and the neuraxial timing so that the heparin and any later procedure are safe.

## Pain plan

The dominant mechanism is bone with a muscle component, in a patient who often cannot report pain, so the plan is a single-shot block early, a neuraxial or general anaesthetic for the operation, and a regular oral regimen that does not depend on the patient asking. PROSPECT's hip fracture recommendations are the skeleton: a single-shot femoral or fascia iliaca block (supra-inguinal preferred) before surgery, paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor started pre-operatively and continued regularly if the kidney and the gut permit, spinal or general anaesthesia with spinal possibly giving better immediate relief, and opioid as rescue only. PROSPECT lists the PENG block as not recommended for lack of evidence rather than because of evidence against it; the one randomised comparison with supra-inguinal fascia iliaca found the same dynamic pain relief, the same resting pain and the same motor block, so the choice between them is yours. The author's view: the fascia iliaca block covers the lateral incision of a DHS and the femoral territory of the lateral approach better; the PENG block preserves quadriceps function and suits the patient who will be asked to stand next morning after a nail or a hemiarthroplasty. Either block is repeated in the anaesthetic room if the emergency department block is more than 6 h old, and placed before positioning for the spinal.

The catheter decision is close to settled in this population: PROSPECT advises against catheters except for an expected surgical delay, and the UK guideline notes that continuous blocks may delay remobilisation, which is the outcome the whole pathway exists to protect. A catheter is reasonable for the patient who cannot be operated on for more than a day (an anticoagulant that needs time, a chest infection being treated) and who cannot be given a repeated single shot; otherwise a repeated single shot is the better tool.

The oral regimen shape: regular paracetamol from the emergency department, an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor for the first few days where renal function, cardiac failure and gastrointestinal risk allow (which in this population is a minority, so the NSAID is a decision rather than a default), and a short-acting opioid at a low dose charted regularly for the first 48 h in the patient who cannot ask, with a behavioural pain score rather than a numeric one as the trigger. Dexamethasone 4-8 mg IV at induction prolongs the block and reduces nausea; the cost is a day of hyperglycaemia in the diabetic patient.

Escalation triggers and rebound: pain that rises as the block recedes on the first night is moderate here, because the fracture that hurt on movement is fixed, and it responds to the regular regimen started on time. Pain that is severe and does not respond, with a thigh that is tense, is a haematoma or a bleed in an anticoagulated patient and needs a haemoglobin and the surgeon. Pain in a hemiarthroplasty with a leg that is shortened and rotated is a dislocation. New confusion on night one is delirium until proved otherwise, and the first question is whether the analgesia was given, the second whether the saturation and pressure are adequate, and the third whether the bladder is full.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The traction table well leg. Abducted and elevated for a long nailing, the well leg can develop a compartment syndrome in the calf; it is rare in a 50-minute DHS and real in a 2-hour subtrochanteric nailing. Check the leg at the end and ask that it be lowered whenever imaging does not need it up.
- The pudendal post. Numbness of the perineum and genitalia after the traction table is a pudendal neuropraxia; it resolves over weeks and is prevented by padding, by releasing traction between reduction manoeuvres and by not using the post as the only counter-traction in a long case.
- Uncemented hemiarthroplasty. Chosen by some surgeons for the patient with severe cardiopulmonary disease to avoid the cement step; WHiTE 5 shows the trade is a four-fold periprosthetic fracture rate and no survival gain, so the honest version of the discussion is a cemented stem with the full precautions, not an uncemented stem with none.
- The dual antiplatelet patient. A general anaesthetic with the block as the analgesic is the common answer; the UK guideline does not forbid a spinal but asks for a good reason to avoid the general anaesthetic. The stent that was placed last month is a cardiology conversation inside the 36 h, not a reason to miss it.
- The direct oral anticoagulant. The 24-h or 48-h rule depends on the eGFR, which in these patients is often borderline; an anti-Xa level under 50 ng/mL is the way to operate sooner when the laboratory offers it. A spinal needs the same clearance as the surgery and the guideline's figures apply to both.
- Aortic stenosis found on the ward. A fixed cardiac output lesion and a spinal are not incompatible, but the spinal has to be low-dose and slow with an arterial line and a vasopressor infusion from the start, and a general anaesthetic with the same monitoring is at least as defensible. The echocardiogram that delays surgery by a day to confirm what the murmur and the ECG already say is the wrong trade.
- The patient with dementia and a spinal. Lying still on their side under a drape for 80 minutes is not possible for many of them, and a spinal that becomes a struggle, deep sedation and an unprotected airway in the lateral position is the worst of both techniques. Decide at the door, not in the anaesthetic room.
- The subtrochanteric fracture. The hardest reduction on the traction table, often needing a percutaneous clamp or an open reduction, a long nail with reaming to the isthmus, the biggest blood loss of the extracapsular group and the longest case; it belongs in the plan as a femoral shaft nailing with an old patient attached (see [femoral shaft nailing](femoral-shaft-nailing.html)).
- The periprosthetic fracture around a hip replacement that presents as a hip fracture. A different operation (see [periprosthetic fractures](periprosthetic-fracture.html)): longer, bloodier, and not a hemiarthroplasty.
- Pathological fracture. A lytic lesion in the neck or the subtrochanteric region in a patient with known or unknown malignancy means a long nail or a replacement with a long stem, more bleeding, a cement step that may be larger, and an oncological conversation about the rest of the skeleton before surgery.
- The patient from a nursing home who is not for resuscitation. The operation is still the treatment for pain and is still done; the conversation about ceilings of care happens before the anaesthetic, with the family, and is documented before the cement goes in rather than during the arrest. See [the orthopaedic patient](../a/a10-the-orthopaedic-patient.html).

## Key sources

The anaesthetic evidence for hip fracture is better than for any other trauma operation, because the patients are many and the outcome that matters (walking at 60 days, death at a year) is easy to measure. REGAIN and RAGA between them close the question of whether spinal anaesthesia protects the brain or improves survival: it does not, and the choice is individual. HIP ATTACK shows that speed within the 36-h window buys comfort and a shorter stay rather than survival. WHiTE 5 shows that the cemented stem is the better implant despite the cement step, which puts the burden on the anaesthetist to make the cement step safe rather than on the surgeon to avoid it; the 2015 AAGBI, BOA and BGS safety guideline is the document that says how. PROSPECT's 2024 hip fracture recommendations and the two national standards (the Australian 2023 clinical care standard and the UK 2020 guideline) agree on the block, the timing and the oral regimen. The PENG versus fascia iliaca choice rests on one 80-patient trial and is the author's judgement where the card says so.
