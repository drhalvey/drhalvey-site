## What the surgeon is doing and why

These two operations sit together because they are the two ends of the same motor unit. The calf muscles act on the foot through the Achilles tendon behind and are balanced by the posterior tibial tendon on the medial side, which holds up the arch. When the Achilles fails the patient cannot push off. When the posterior tibial tendon fails the arch collapses, and a tight calf is usually part of the reason.

Acute Achilles rupture is the sudden version: a middle-aged recreational athlete, a snap, a gap 3-6 cm above the heel where the tendon's blood supply is poorest. Surgery is one of three acceptable treatments. In the largest randomised trial, 554 patients were assigned to a functional brace alone, open repair or minimally invasive repair, and their tendon function scores at a year were the same. What differed was the complication each chose: re-rupture in 6.2% without surgery against 0.6% with either operation, and nerve injury in 5.2% after minimally invasive repair, 2.8% after open repair and 0.6% without surgery. So the patients who reach theatre are those for whom re-rupture matters most, and the operation is modest. Open repair uses a posteromedial incision, deliberately away from the sural nerve on the lateral side, opens the paratenon, brings the frayed ends together with locking sutures with the foot plantarflexed, and closes the paratenon over it. Minimally invasive repair passes the sutures through the proximal stump percutaneously with a jig through a 2-3 cm incision, which protects the skin and exposes the sural nerve to a blind needle.

The chronic versions are bigger. A neglected rupture with a gap needs a V-Y lengthening of the gastrocnemius aponeurosis or a turndown flap, usually with the flexor hallucis longus tendon harvested behind the ankle and fixed into the calcaneus as a new motor. Insertional tendinopathy needs the tendon lifted off the heel, the degenerate tissue and the prominent posterosuperior calcaneus (Haglund deformity) removed, and the tendon reattached to bone with suture anchors.

Adult flatfoot, now called progressive collapsing foot deformity, is the slow failure of the medial side. The posterior tibial tendon degenerates, the spring ligament stretches, the heel drifts into valgus, the forefoot swings outward and the arch flattens. While the foot is still flexible the surgeon rebuilds it without fusing it, by combining procedures chosen from the deformity:

1. Medialising calcaneal osteotomy: the heel is cut obliquely behind the subtalar joint through a lateral incision, slid about 10 mm medially to put it back under the leg, and fixed with one or two screws.
2. Flexor digitorum longus transfer: through a medial incision the diseased posterior tibial tendon is debrided or excised and the neighbouring toe flexor is cut distally, passed through a drill hole in the navicular and tied under tension to do its job. The spring ligament is repaired or reinforced.
3. Lateral column lengthening (Evans): a second calcaneal osteotomy near the calcaneocuboid joint is opened with a wedge of graft to swing the forefoot back in.
4. Cotton osteotomy: an opening wedge in the medial cuneiform to bring the first ray down.
5. Gastrocnemius recession: the aponeurosis is divided in the calf to relieve the equinus that drove the collapse.

A rigid flatfoot gets a [triple fusion](ankle-hindfoot-fusion.html) instead.

```
            calf (gastrocnemius-soleus)
                     |
        +------------+-------------+
        |                          |
   Achilles fails             calf too tight, medial side fails
   (rupture, insertion)       (posterior tibial tendon, spring ligament)
        |                          |
   repair / reattach /        heel osteotomy + FDL transfer
   FHL transfer               +/- lateral column, Cotton, recession
   prone, 30-120 min          supine, 120-180 min
   tendon, unloaded: 2-3      bone x 2-3 + tendon: 4
```

The pain logic differs as much as the operations do. An Achilles repair is a tendon mechanism, but the repair is immobilised in equinus with the tendon slack and nothing loads it, so it is sore rather than severe: a 2-3 that settles in days. An insertional reconstruction adds resected bone and anchors. A flatfoot reconstruction is two or three osteotomies, a tendon transfer under tension in a bone tunnel, and three incisions on both sides of the foot and the calf; it behaves like a hindfoot fusion without the joint preparation, and foot arthrodesis ranked among the most painful of 179 procedures for day 1 pain in the cohort without blocks. It sets the card's score of 4.

## Steps

### 1. Which operation is on the list
**Why:** "Achilles" may mean an acute repair, a chronic reconstruction or an insertional debridement. "Flatfoot" may mean three procedures or six.
**Anaesthesia:** For Achilles surgery establish acute or chronic, open or minimally invasive, and the position the surgeon uses. For flatfoot, count the osteotomies and ask about graft, because that sets duration, tourniquet strategy and the catheter decision. Fluoroquinolone or corticosteroid exposure is common in the history of a tendon rupture, and the corticosteroid matters to you.

### 2. Achilles: anaesthetic and block
**Why:** A short operation on the back of the leg in a patient who will go home in a boot.
**Anaesthesia:** Three workable plans. General anaesthesia with a single-shot popliteal sciatic block (15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%) and intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg is the author's default: the posterior incision is sural and medial calcaneal territory, both sciatic, and a saphenous block adds nothing for a midline or posteromedial incision unless it strays well medially. A short-acting spinal suits the prone position, covers the thigh cuff and leaves sensation back within hours. The spinal has a condition attached. The patient who presents days after the rupture has usually been in a boot on prophylactic low molecular weight heparin or a direct oral anticoagulant since the injury, and the time of the last dose decides whether a spinal is available: the needle goes in at least 12 h after a prophylactic dose of enoxaparin, the first post-operative dose is given at least 12 h after the needle, and a direct oral anticoagulant is not restarted until at least 6 h after it ([venous thromboembolism](../a/a05-vte.html)). Where the last dose is too recent or its time is not known, the general anaesthetic with a popliteal block is the plan, because a superficial, compressible block site carries no such interval. Surgeon's infiltration under general anaesthesia is adequate for a percutaneous repair. The block has a useful second effect: a paralysed calf cannot contract against the fresh repair during emergence or in recovery.

### 3. The turn: prone positioning and airway
**Why:** The surgeon needs the back of both ankles facing up, feet hanging free over the end of the table so the resting equinus of the uninjured side can be seen and matched.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the part of an Achilles repair that carries the anaesthetic risk. For a slim, fasted patient and a 40-min operation, many anaesthetists let the patient position themselves prone awake and then induce with a supraglottic airway, or use a spinal with light sedation; an obese patient, reflux, a long chronic reconstruction or any doubt means a tracheal tube, induction on the trolley and a formal turn. Whichever is chosen: chest and pelvic supports with the abdomen free, arms forward with less than 90 degrees at the shoulders, eyes and face checked after the turn and again after the surgeon has adjusted the legs, and the thigh cuff applied before turning. The principles are in [positioning](../a/a06-positioning.html).

### 4. The tourniquet question
**Why:** A bloodless field helps identify the sural nerve and the tendon ends. The skin over the Achilles has a poor blood supply and wound breakdown is the complication surgeons fear, so some operate without a cuff, and none can use a calf cuff because it squeezes the muscle whose resting length sets the repair tension.
**Anaesthesia:** A thigh cuff under a popliteal block alone is outside the block; with sedation it becomes uncomfortable at 30-45 min, which an acute repair may just fit inside and a reconstruction will not ([the tourniquet](../a/a01-tourniquet.html)). Antibiotic before inflation. If no cuff is used, expect a slower dissection and a wetter dressing, not meaningful blood loss.

### 5. Achilles: approach and the sural nerve
**Why:** A longitudinal incision just medial to the tendon, full-thickness flaps without undermining, paratenon opened in the midline. In the minimally invasive technique a short transverse or longitudinal incision is made over the gap and a jig guides needles through the skin and the proximal stump.
**Anaesthesia:** The sural nerve crosses the lateral border of the tendon about 10 cm above the heel and is the nerve injured in 2.8-5.2% of repairs in the randomised trial. A numb lateral border of the foot after surgery has an obvious surgical explanation; your block record and the pre-operative sensory note let it be attributed correctly.

### 6. Tendon repair and tension
**Why:** The ends are freshened, locking sutures are placed in each stump and tied with the ankle plantarflexed to match the other side. Too loose and push-off is weak for life; too tight and the ankle will not come up to neutral.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon compares the two feet and needs both hanging symmetrically, undisturbed. A patient who moves or coughs with an active calf during knot tying can pull sutures through soft tendon; depth of anaesthesia, or the block, prevents it.

### 7. Chronic and insertional variants
**Why:** For a gap, the gastrocnemius aponeurosis is lengthened in a V-Y or the flexor hallucis longus is harvested through the same incision, deep to the fascia beside the tibial nerve, and fixed into a calcaneal tunnel with an interference screw. For insertional disease the tendon is split or detached, the calcaneal prominence is sawn off, and the tendon is reattached with two to four anchors.
**Anaesthesia:** Now a 90-120 min prone operation with bone work: a tracheal tube, the full prone set-up, and pain a point higher than the acute repair. The flexor hallucis longus harvest is next to the tibial neurovascular bundle. These patients are older and heavier than the acute rupture group and are inpatients more often.

### 8. Achilles: closure, equinus splint, turning back
**Why:** Paratenon and skin are closed without tension and without forceps on the skin edge; a backslab or boot holds the ankle in 20-30 degrees of plantarflexion to keep the repair slack.
**Anaesthesia:** The splint goes on prone, before the turn. When turning supine, hold the leg by the splint and never let the foot be pushed up into dorsiflexion against the bed. Emergence should be smooth; this is a short case where a supraglottic airway removed deep or a spinal has a real advantage.

### 9. Flatfoot: anaesthetic, blocks and position
**Why:** Supine, bump under the buttock for the lateral heel, bump out for the medial side, thigh cuff, 2-3 h.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway or tube according to duration and patient. The incisions are lateral heel (sural), medial midfoot and ankle (saphenous and tibial) and often posteromedial calf, so both blocks are required: popliteal sciatic, as a catheter where the unit runs them, and a saphenous block in the adductor canal with 10 mL. Dexamethasone 8 mg IV. The patient is typically a woman in her fifties or sixties, often with obesity, diabetes or hypertension, which is the demographic of the disease.

### 10. Medialising calcaneal osteotomy
**Why:** Through an oblique lateral incision behind the peroneal tendons, the tuberosity is cut with a saw at 45 degrees to the sole, completed with an osteotome to protect the medial structures, translated medially and fixed with screws from the heel.
**Anaesthesia:** The first and largest bone cut, through the bone that produces the most painful operation in the 179-procedure cohort when it is fractured and fixed. The medial neurovascular bundle lies against the far cortex, so the surgeon finishes the cut by hand and a still patient matters. The sural nerve crosses the incision.

### 11. Medial side: tendon transfer, spring ligament, further osteotomies, recession
**Why:** The posterior tibial tendon is debrided, the flexor digitorum longus is divided in the midfoot, passed through a navicular drill hole from plantar to dorsal and sutured with the foot inverted; the spring ligament is plicated or reinforced. An Evans or Cotton osteotomy is opened and held with a graft wedge and a plate, and the gastrocnemius is released through a calf incision if the ankle does not reach 10 degrees of dorsiflexion with the knee straight.
**Anaesthesia:** The foot is held inverted while the transfer is tensioned and tied, and must stay there until the cast is on. The tourniquet clock is the constraint: three procedures fit in 120 min, five do not, and the deflation interval or the cuff-off finish is agreed at the start. A graft wedge from the iliac crest is a site outside the blocks and needs the surgeon's infiltration.

### 12. Flatfoot: closure, cast and the first nights
**Why:** Cuff down, haemostasis, closure, and a backslab in slight inversion and plantarflexion to protect the transfer; non-weight-bearing for six weeks.
**Anaesthesia:** Reperfusion after 2 h produces the usual fall in pressure and carbon dioxide peak. The slab goes on before emergence. With a catheter, run ropivacaine 0.2% at 5-8 mL/h for 48-72 h and have the oral regimen established before removal; with a single shot and intravenous dexamethasone the block lasts about 26 h in the randomised data (ropivacaine 0.375% with dexamethasone 10 mg IV) and recedes on the afternoon or evening of day 1 in hospital, with the peak that night and on day 2.

## Pain plan

Acute Achilles repair: a tendon repair that is not loaded, a short incision, no bone. Single-shot popliteal block with dexamethasone or a short spinal with infiltration (timed around the anticoagulant as in step 2), regular paracetamol and an NSAID, and a small supply of oral opioid for two nights. The rebound risk is lower than after bone surgery but the patient is the demographic for it (younger, day case), so the written night plan applies. No catheter. The important prescription is the anticoagulant: after Achilles rupture the pooled clinical VTE rate was 7% and the radiological rate 35.3%, treated surgically or not, and the same meta-analysis that argues against routine chemoprophylaxis for other foot surgery names this as the exception.

Insertional and chronic Achilles reconstruction: bone resection, anchors or a tunnel, and a tendon transfer. Single-shot popliteal block with dexamethasone and an overnight stay is the default; a catheter is reasonable for the large reconstruction in an opioid-tolerant patient.

Flatfoot reconstruction: a bone mechanism on the scale of a fusion. The defensible default is a popliteal catheter for 48-72 h with a saphenous single shot, as in [ankle, subtalar and triple fusion](ankle-hindfoot-fusion.html), on the same evidence: pooled randomised trials in foot and ankle surgery show lower pain at 24 and 48 h with a continuous block, with leakage in about one in seven. Where no catheter service exists, single-shot blocks with intravenous dexamethasone, regular paracetamol, an NSAID if the surgeon permits one around osteotomies and graft, a regular oral opioid for three to five days, and one or two nights in hospital. No trial has studied analgesia for flatfoot reconstruction specifically, and the score and the catheter advice are the author's judgement from the mechanism.

Escalation triggers: pain in a tight cast with swollen or dusky toes is the cast, which is split first. Calf pain or swelling in the following weeks, in either operation, is a deep vein thrombosis until an ultrasound says otherwise. Increasing wound pain at 7-14 days after Achilles surgery is the posterior skin failing. Sudden pain with a pop after an Achilles repair is a re-rupture.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Supine and lateral Achilles repair. Some surgeons repair acute ruptures supine with the leg externally rotated over the other shin, or semi-prone, to avoid the turn; the airway plan becomes that of any supine day case.
- Self-positioning prone. Quick and safe in the right patient; the block or spinal is placed first, and the patient confirms comfort of the neck, shoulders and chest before induction.
- Bilateral legs prepared. The surgeon may drape both; keep lines and the cuff clear of the uninjured leg.
- No tourniquet. A surgeon's choice for wound reasons, not an oversight.
- Wound-risk patients. Smokers, diabetics and patients on corticosteroids have the posterior wound complications; they are also the patients most likely to be offered non-operative treatment, which the randomised trial supports.
- The missed thrombosis. A swollen, aching calf in a boot three weeks after an Achilles repair is easy to attribute to the injury.
- Flatfoot with a spring ligament tape or deltoid reconstruction. More anchors, more time, same plan.
- Flatfoot converted to fusion. If the foot proves rigid under anaesthesia the surgeon may change to a triple fusion; the consent, the tourniquet plan and the catheter decision should already allow for it.
- Gastrocnemius recession alone. Done for plantar fasciitis, forefoot overload and diabetic forefoot ulcers: a 20-min operation through a calf incision, supine or prone, with a pain score of 2; see [minor foot procedures](minor-foot-procedures.html).
- Neurological baseline. Sural territory for Achilles surgery, saphenous and medial plantar territory for flatfoot surgery: record before blocking.

## Key sources

The Achilles decision is supported by a large three-arm randomised trial, which is why the surgical caseload has fallen and why the card treats the operation as modest. The VTE figures come from a meta-analysis that pooled surgically and conservatively treated ruptures. The flatfoot nomenclature is a consensus statement and is cited for the logic of combining procedures, not for outcomes. No randomised trial addresses anaesthesia or analgesia for either operation; the block advice is extrapolated from mixed foot and ankle trials, the dexamethasone duration from a single randomised study, and the pain score for flatfoot reconstruction from the mechanism and the cohort ranking of foot arthrodesis.
