## What the surgeon is doing and why

The wrist on this card is a young one. Scaphoid fractures and TFCC tears belong to men in their twenties and thirties who fell on an outstretched hand, and the anaesthetic problem is not comorbidity but the mismatch between a short operation and a long block in a patient who expects neither to hurt. The surgical logic differs for each of the three things grouped here, and the pain follows the logic.

Wrist arthroscopy is a 2.7 mm scope through dorsal portals named for the extensor compartments they pass between, with the hand hung in finger traps under 3-5 kg of traction to open joint spaces a few millimetres wide. Surgeons vary between wet arthroscopy with a pump and dry arthroscopy, which avoids fluid in the forearm. The scope stages ligament injuries, debrides a central TFCC tear or a synovitis, excises a ganglion from inside, and increasingly assists scaphoid fixation and grafting. Nothing done through the scope alone is under tension afterwards.

The triangular fibrocartilage complex is the load-bearing disc and ligament hammock between the ulnar head and the carpus, and the radioulnar ligaments that run through it into the fovea of the ulna are what hold the distal radioulnar joint together. A central disc tear is avascular and is debrided. A peripheral tear at the ulnar side (Palmer 1B) is vascular and is repaired: outside-in sutures through the capsule, and for a foveal avulsion an anchor or transosseous sutures into the ulna, so that the ligaments that stabilise the DRUJ are back on bone. The pooled series report residual pain around 2 out of 10 at follow-up, complications that are mostly self-limiting, and paraesthesia of the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve as the characteristic one. The repair is protected by an above-elbow splint that stops forearm rotation for four to six weeks, a bigger imposition than the operation. The tension on the repaired tissue is low.

The scaphoid has a retrograde blood supply entering at its distal end, so a waist or proximal pole fracture cuts the proximal fragment off from its vessels; that is why it fails to unite and why the proximal pole dies. Most undisplaced waist fractures are now cast rather than fixed: in the 439-patient SWIFFT trial, early screw fixation gave no better wrist score at a year than a cast with fixation of the few that failed to unite, and 14% of the surgery group had a potentially serious surgical complication against 1%. Fixation is for the displaced fracture, the proximal pole, the patient who cannot be in a cast, and the non-union. A percutaneous screw is a headless compression screw passed along the central axis of the bone over a guidewire, volar for waist fractures and dorsal for proximal pole fractures, under the image intensifier through a stab incision. The screw compresses the fracture; the pain afterwards is the drilling of a small bone and the fracture itself, which was already hurting.

Non-union is a different operation because the problem is now biological. The surgeon opens the wrist, excises the fibrous non-union and sclerotic bone back to bleeding cancellous bone, corrects the humpback collapse by opening the volar gap, and fills it with graft before fixing it. The graft is the variable that matters to you. Local distal radius bone comes through the same incision. Iliac crest gives a larger, stronger corticocancellous wedge at the price of a second wound that hurts more than the wrist. A pedicled vascularised graft (the 1,2 intercompartmental supraretinacular artery graft from the dorsal distal radius is the common one) brings its own blood supply for an avascular proximal pole and lengthens the case. The free medial femoral condyle graft is a microsurgical operation for the failed, avascular, collapsed non-union: a corticoperiosteal block from the knee on the descending genicular vessels, anastomosed to the radial artery at the wrist; in the series of 49 failed previous repairs with proximal pole necrosis it united 84% at a mean of 16 weeks. The pooled data say the graft source does not change the union rate (88.7% non-vascularised versus 87.5% vascularised across 7,671 patients, distal radius and iliac crest equal), while the comparative studies of pedicled grafts show a small advantage in union and a week or two in time to union with no functional gain. Surgeons choose by the state of the proximal pole and by habit.

The pain logic: the dominant mechanism is bone, because the scaphoid work sets the upper end of the card and it is a drilled bone with a compression screw across a fracture that was already the pain generator. Fixation removes most of the pre-operative pain, so the curve is the distal radius curve: little while the block works, a sharp rise when it recedes on the first night, and a quick fall over three days because nothing is loaded through a screw in a cast. Arthroscopy and TFCC repair sit below that. The iliac crest donor site is the exception that changes the score in practice: periosteum stripped from a muscle-bearing crest that hurts to walk on, with first-day pain averaging 6.3 out of 10 in the pooled craniofacial series and chronic donor-site pain reported anywhere from 3% to 14% depending on the series. A grafted non-union with crest harvest behaves like a 3, and the plan says so.

## Steps

### 1. Choose the anaesthetic for the operation booked, and for its tourniquet
**Why:** The surgeon needs a still arm, a bloodless field under a cuff, and an image intensifier for anything scaphoid; the patient needs an anaesthetic that matches 40 minutes of surgery rather than 14 hours of numb arm.
**Anaesthesia:** A supraclavicular or infraclavicular block covers the hand, the wrist and the upper arm cuff and is the single technique that does everything; an axillary block covers the wrist well and the cuff poorly unless the intercostobrachial nerve is added, and after 60 min the sedation is carrying the tourniquet whatever block was used. Intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg goes with the block, for duration and for the rebound odds. The honest alternative for a short arthroscopy or percutaneous screw is a general anaesthetic with a supraglottic airway and surgeon infiltration, which trades a worse first evening for an arm that works by dinner; a Bier's block suits a surgeon who accepts an exsanguinated field for under 45 min. For a free medial femoral condyle graft the decision is different: a tracheal tube for a 4-6 h case with two operative sites, plus a plexus block or catheter for the arm, because the block's sympathectomy dilates the recipient vessels in the hours after the anastomosis.

### 2. Position, traction tower and the image intensifier
**Why:** Supine with the arm on a radiolucent hand table; for arthroscopy the elbow is flexed in a traction tower and the fingers hung in traps so that the joint spaces open; for scaphoid work the image intensifier sits at the end of the table and the surgeon turns the wrist through the views that show the screw in all planes.
**Anaesthesia:** The shoulder goes to 90 degrees of abduction and no further, the ulnar nerve at the elbow is padded where the tower rests, and the traction (3-5 kg through finger traps for 30-60 min) is tolerated under a block but is a steady pull a sedated patient will fidget against if the block is marginal. Lead for everyone and a patient whose other arm and eyes are out of the beam. The cuff and the cannula go on the non-operative side.

### 3. Portals, traction and the dry or wet joint
**Why:** The dorsal portals pass between extensor tendons and within millimetres of the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve at 6R and the superficial radial nerve at 1-2; the surgeon marks the compartments and dissects bluntly to the capsule. Wet arthroscopy distends the joint and clears the view; dry arthroscopy avoids fluid in the forearm and is preferred by many for bone work.
**Anaesthesia:** Nothing physiological. A wet case extravasates saline into the forearm over time; a tight forearm at the end of a long wet arthroscopy is fluid, not a compartment syndrome, and it settles, but the distinction depends on a patient who can be examined, which a dense block prevents until morning. Note the traction time.

### 4. TFCC: debride the centre, repair the edge
**Why:** A central tear is avascular and is shaved to a stable rim; a peripheral tear is repaired with outside-in sutures through the capsule, and a foveal avulsion with an anchor or transosseous sutures into the ulna so that the radioulnar ligaments are back on bone and the DRUJ is stable. The surgeon tests DRUJ stability with the forearm in each rotation before and after, and the repair goes into an above-elbow splint that stops rotation.
**Anaesthesia:** The repair is small and at low tension; the sutures go through skin on the ulnar side of the wrist, where the dorsal sensory branch of the ulnar nerve is caught and where the patient will report numbness afterwards that is not your block. If a diagnostic arthroscopy has turned into a repair, the discharge changes from a bandage to a splint above the elbow and the patient needs to hear that before the block wears off.

### 5. Percutaneous scaphoid screw
**Why:** A guidewire is passed along the central axis of the scaphoid under the image intensifier (volar through a stab incision at the tubercle for a waist fracture, dorsal through the proximal pole for a proximal fracture with the wrist flexed), the length is measured, the track is drilled, and a headless compression screw is advanced until it compresses the fracture and sits below the cartilage at both ends. Arthroscopy is sometimes added to check the reduction and the screw position.
**Anaesthesia:** A 30-45 min case under a cuff, with the wrist flexed hard over a bolster for the dorsal approach, the position that stretches the median nerve at the carpal tunnel and the one a blocked patient will not report. The drilling is the only bone work and the only pain. The fracture that needed fixing was already hurting, and the day-1 pain after a screw is no worse than the pre-operative pain in a cast; the rebound is the issue, not the intensity.

### 6. Non-union: open, excise, correct and graft
**Why:** Through a volar approach the surgeon exposes the non-union, removes the fibrous tissue and sclerotic bone with a burr and curette until both fragments bleed, opens the volar gap to correct the humpback collapse, and packs or wedges graft into the defect before passing a screw; a dorsal approach is used for proximal pole non-unions and for the pedicled 1,2 ICSRA graft, whose vessel runs along the dorsal distal radius under the extensor retinaculum.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the version with real bone work: a curetted cavity in a small bone and a wedge of graft under compression. Tourniquet time runs to 90-120 min and the cuff is now the dominant discomfort for a sedated patient, so a general anaesthetic on top of the block is the usual pairing. For a pedicled graft the surgeon will ask for the cuff down before closure to confirm the pedicle bleeds, and will want the pressure normal and the patient warm for that check.

### 7. The iliac crest harvest
**Why:** A corticocancellous wedge from the anterior crest gives the strongest structural graft for a collapsed non-union; the surgeon strips periosteum and muscle origin off the crest, cuts a block with an osteotome or saw, and curettes cancellous bone from between the tables. Bone wax, gelatin sponge and a layered closure follow, and some surgeons leave a drain.
**Anaesthesia:** This is a second operation in its own right and the one the patient will complain about. Periosteum is stripped off a bone that bears the abdominal wall and the hip abductors, so every step and every cough loads it; the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve runs close to the anterior superior iliac spine and is the neuropraxia that follows; and the cancellous bed bleeds after closure, so the haematoma that tracks down the thigh is the common complication, more so on the anterior crest in the pooled data. First-day pain is above 6 out of 10 and chronic pain at the site is reported in 3-14% of patients. The plan that works is surgeon infiltration of the wound and periosteum with ropivacaine before closure, regular NSAID, and an opioid for three days charted for the hip rather than for the wrist. A transfusion is not expected.

### 8. The free medial femoral condyle graft
**Why:** For the failed or avascular non-union with collapse, a block of cortex and periosteum is raised from the medial femoral condyle on the descending genicular vessels through a medial thigh incision under a thigh tourniquet, shaped to fill the scaphoid defect and inset; the pedicle is anastomosed end-to-side to the radial artery and a vein in the anatomical snuffbox, and the graft is fixed with a screw or wires.
**Anaesthesia:** Treat it as a free flap, because it is one: a tracheal tube and a 4-6 h case, two tourniquets whose times are tracked separately, active warming from before the first incision with a core temperature above 36.5 degrees because the anastomosis is to a small artery that spasms when cold, euvolaemia, and no vasoconstrictor infusion while the anastomosis is being done or in the hour after it, which is where a plexus block earns its place by dilating the forearm arterial tree without drugs. The surgeon releases the arm cuff before the anastomosis and will not want it re-inflated. A catheter is the author's preference here: the sympathectomy lasts as long as the infusion runs, the knee is covered by infiltration and the oral regimen, and the patient stays a night or two anyway. The knee donor site is a periosteal wound over a weight-bearing condyle; it hurts to walk on for a few days.

### 9. Tourniquet release, immobilisation and emergence
**Why:** The cuff comes down before or after closure according to the surgeon's habit, the wrist goes into a plaster or a thermoplastic splint (a scaphoid cast including the thumb for a screw in poor bone, a sugar-tong splint above the elbow for a TFCC repair, a volar slab for a graft), and a sling for comfort.
**Anaesthesia:** Reperfusion after 90-120 min of ischaemia is a transient fall in pressure and a rise in end-tidal carbon dioxide that passes in minutes. The splint goes on while the arm is still blocked, with the fingers free and visible: the patient and the ward are told to watch for a tight cast as the swelling rises overnight, because the block hides the pain that would otherwise warn them. After a graft the patient wakes with a numb arm and a sore hip, which is confusing unless it has been explained.

### 10. Recovery, discharge and the night the block ends
**Why:** The surgeon wants the cast intact, the fingers moving, the arm elevated, and the patient back in two weeks for a wound check and in six for a radiograph or CT; union is the outcome and smoking is the modifiable enemy of it.
**Anaesthesia:** The first paracetamol and NSAID go in before discharge and are timed to the block, not to the first complaint; the block lasts 12-24 h with dexamethasone and the pain rises over an hour or two when it recedes, usually in the small hours. In the ambulatory cohort half of all block patients met the definition of rebound pain, and bone surgery (OR 1.82), younger age and the absence of intravenous dexamethasone raised the odds; nearly everyone on this card is young and has had bone drilled. A written night plan with a short opioid supply is the intervention, and the "minor" label is the trap: in the 179-procedure cohort small extremity operations were among the most painful on day 1 because so little was prescribed for them. The iliac crest patient stays a night and walks with a stick for a few days.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is bone for the scaphoid and surface for the rest, so the plan is a single-shot block chosen for the operation and the cuff, intravenous dexamethasone, and an oral regimen started before the block recedes. The defensible default for arthroscopy, TFCC repair and a percutaneous screw is a supraclavicular or infraclavicular block with 20-25 mL of ropivacaine 0.5% (an axillary block with an intercostobrachial supplement is equivalent for a cuff time under an hour), dexamethasone 8 mg IV, sedation rather than a general anaesthetic, regular paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor for three to five days, and an oral opioid for the first two nights. There is no PROSPECT guideline for any operation on this card; the shape is borrowed from the distal radius evidence and the ambulatory rebound cohort. For a short arthroscopy, a general anaesthetic with infiltration and no block is a reasonable choice that moves the worst pain from 2 am to the recovery room and gives the patient a working arm by the evening; say which trade the patient is making.

The catheter question has one clear answer and one judgement. The clear answer is the free medial femoral condyle graft, where an infraclavicular or axillary catheter run for 48-72 h gives a sympathectomy across the anastomosis and covers a long case and a hospital stay; that is the author's practice, not trial evidence. The judgement is everything else: an open graft with an iliac crest harvest has its pain at the hip, which an arm catheter does not reach, and a percutaneous screw or a TFCC repair does not need three days of ropivacaine for a night of rebound. A continuous block is reserved for the opioid-tolerant patient and the one who has had a catastrophic rebound night before.

The iliac crest needs its own plan on the chart: surgeon infiltration of the periosteum and wound before closure, regular NSAID unless the surgeon objects on union grounds (the evidence that a short course impairs scaphoid union is weak and most hand surgeons accept it), an opioid for three days, and early mobilisation with a stick. Escalation triggers: pain above 7 after the block has gone with warm, moving fingers and a cast that is not tight is treated with the charted opioid and a check that the regular doses were given; pain with tight, pale or tingling fingers is the cast and is split, not medicated; a tense, expanding thigh or flank after a crest harvest is a haematoma and needs the surgeon; and numbness over the lateral thigh after a harvest is the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve and is explained, not investigated.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The booking that grows. A diagnostic arthroscopy for ulnar-sided pain becomes a foveal repair with an above-elbow splint; a percutaneous screw becomes an open graft when the fracture will not reduce closed. The block already placed is right for all of them; the discharge plan and the splint are not.
- Tourniquet coverage. The axillary block that numbs the wrist perfectly leaves the upper arm cuff to the sedation after about an hour; an infraclavicular or supraclavicular block covers it. Match the block to the cuff time the surgeon states, then add 30 minutes.
- The dead-arm discharge. A patient with a dense plexus block goes home with a sling, written instructions, someone at home, oral analgesia already started and a telephone number. A dense block is a reason to counsel, not to delay discharge.
- The dorsal approach and the flexed wrist. A proximal pole screw needs the wrist held in full flexion for the guidewire; the median nerve is stretched at the carpal tunnel and the blocked patient cannot tell you.
- Ulnar shortening osteotomy. Some surgeons shorten the ulna rather than repair the TFCC for impaction: a plated osteotomy with a different pain profile (periosteum and bone, a 3 for a week), the same block, and a longer tourniquet.
- Bilateral or re-do scaphoid surgery. Longer, scarred, more graft, and the patient who has been through the rebound night before; the catheter conversation starts here.
- Children and adolescents. TFCC repair and scaphoid fixation in a 15-year-old are usually done under general anaesthesia with a block placed asleep; the rebound counselling goes to the parent.
- The free medial femoral condyle graft in a patient who cannot lie flat for six hours, who smokes, or who has peripheral vascular disease. All three are the surgeon's problems before they are yours, and all three are reasons the operation is uncommon.

## Key sources

The scaphoid evidence is strong for whether to operate (SWIFFT) and moderate for how to graft (the 7,671-patient meta-analysis and the smaller pooled comparisons of vascularised grafts), and silent on analgesia; the pain plan is borrowed from the distal radius trials and the ambulatory rebound cohort. The iliac crest figures come from systematic reviews across orthopaedic and craniofacial surgery, with the wide range for chronic pain reflecting how the question was asked. The TFCC evidence is level IV series pooled in systematic reviews, cited for the shape of recovery, not for anaesthetic technique. The catheter advice for the free medial femoral condyle graft and the NSAID position are the author's judgement.
