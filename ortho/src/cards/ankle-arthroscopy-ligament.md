## What the surgeon is doing and why

Two operations share this card because they share a patient: the young, active person whose ankle was sprained, did not recover, and now either hurts at the front, gives way on uneven ground, or both.

Ankle arthroscopy deals with what is inside the joint. The anterior joint line collects the consequences of repeated sprains and forced dorsiflexion: a rim of osteophyte on the front of the tibia and the neck of the talus that collide in dorsiflexion (anterior impingement), a tongue of scarred synovium in the anterolateral gutter, loose bodies, and osteochondral lesions of the talar dome, where a flake of cartilage and subchondral bone has separated. The surgeon distracts the joint a few millimetres, enters through an anteromedial portal beside tibialis anterior and an anterolateral portal beside peroneus tertius, shaves the synovium, burrs the spurs until the ankle dorsiflexes without contact, and treats a talar lesion by curetting the unstable cartilage to a stable rim and puncturing the subchondral plate (microfracture) so that marrow clot fills the defect. Posterior arthroscopy is the same idea from behind, with the patient prone and two portals either side of the Achilles tendon, for an os trigonum or a tethered flexor hallucis longus in a dancer.

Lateral ligament reconstruction deals with what holds the joint together. The anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) and the calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) have healed long and lax after the sprains. In the Brostrom repair the surgeon opens the anterolateral capsule along the front of the fibula, finds the attenuated ligament and capsule as a single sleeve, shortens it, and reattaches it to the fibula with suture anchors with the ankle held in eversion and neutral dorsiflexion. The Gould modification pulls the inferior extensor retinaculum over the top as a second layer. Where the tissue is poor, the patient hypermobile or heavy, or a previous repair has failed, the repair is reinforced with a suture tape between talus and fibula, or replaced by a tendon graft (usually gracilis or an allograft) routed through tunnels in the talus, fibula and calcaneus. Most surgeons scope the ankle first at the same sitting, because the unstable ankle usually has something inside it worth treating.

```
 symptom                   structure                 operation
 pain at the front   -->   spur, synovitis, OLT  --> arthroscopy     (score 1-2)
 giving way          -->   lax ATFL and CFL      --> Brostrom-Gould  (score 3)
 both                -->   both                  --> scope, then open repair
 failed repair       -->   no usable tissue      --> graft or tape   (score 3)
```

The pain logic separates them cleanly. Arthroscopy alone is a capsule mechanism: distension, synovectomy and two portals, sore and swollen for three or four days, with bone added when a spur is burred or a lesion microfractured. The ligament reconstruction adds a capsulotomy, drill holes in the fibula and a sleeve of capsule and ligament tied down under tension. Unlike a rotator cuff, that repair is then held still in a boot and not loaded, which is why it behaves as a 3 with a quick fall and not as a tendon-tension 5. In the 179-procedure cohort, where blocks were rarely used, open ankle ligament reconstruction ranked 54th for day 1 pain: clearly painful, and nowhere near the hindfoot fusions and calcaneal fixations at the top of the same list.

## Steps

### 1. Reading the booking
**Why:** "Ankle arthroscopy +/- proceed" and "Brostrom" tell you different things. The surgeon decides between debridement alone, microfracture, and repair from the examination under anaesthesia and the arthroscopic findings as much as from the MRI.
**Anaesthesia:** Establish before induction whether a ligament repair is definite, possible or not planned, because that is the block decision. If it is only possible, the kindest sequence is a general anaesthetic without a block, surgeon's infiltration at the end, and a popliteal block in recovery if the repair was done. Note whether the arthroscopy is anterior or posterior, since posterior means prone. Record sensation over the dorsum of the foot and the first web space now.

### 2. Anaesthetic and the block decision
**Why:** The surgeon needs a relaxed calf so the joint will distract, a bloodless field, and for the repair a foot that can be held in eversion while the anchors are tied.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with a supraglottic airway is the default for supine cases; the thigh tourniquet rules out a block-only technique beyond 30-45 min. For arthroscopy alone give no nerve block: paracetamol, an NSAID, dexamethasone 8 mg IV and the surgeon's portal and intra-articular local anaesthetic are enough, and a long-acting sciatic block converts a score 1-2 operation into a numb foot on crutches followed by rebound. For a planned ligament reconstruction give a single-shot popliteal sciatic block (20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%) with intravenous dexamethasone. The lateral incision, the fibula and the lateral capsule are all sciatic territory (sural, superficial peroneal and the articular branches of the tibial and deep peroneal nerves), so the saphenous block that an ankle fracture needs is optional here; the anteromedial portal is the only saphenous skin involved and the surgeon infiltrates it.

### 3. Position, thigh holder, distraction and tourniquet
**Why:** For anterior arthroscopy the thigh is flexed over a padded holder so the leg hangs, or the heel sits at the end of the table, and a strap around the hindfoot is tensioned against the surgeon's waist or a bar to open the joint by 4-5 mm. The thigh cuff goes on first.
**Anaesthesia:** Pad the thigh holder well clear of the popliteal fossa and the fibular neck: it sits exactly where the common peroneal nerve can be compressed for an hour. Check the opposite leg, which is often left hanging or in a stirrup. Measure the limb occlusion pressure after positioning and set the cuff from it ([the tourniquet](../a/a01-tourniquet.html)). Cefazolin is complete before inflation. In the pooled series nerve injury was no more frequent with non-invasive distraction than without it (2.6% against 2.2%), so the strap itself is not the concern; the unpadded holder and the duration are.

### 4. Portals and the superficial peroneal nerve
**Why:** The anteromedial portal is made first, medial to tibialis anterior. The anterolateral portal lies beside the intermediate dorsal cutaneous branch of the superficial peroneal nerve, which the surgeon marks on the skin beforehand by plantarflexing and inverting the foot to make it stand out, then enters with a skin-only incision and blunt spreading.
**Anaesthesia:** This is where the operation's signature complication is made. In the systematic review of 7,942 arthroscopies the overall complication rate was 4.09%, more than half of all complications were neurological, a third of those involved the superficial peroneal nerve, and one in five nerve injuries was permanent. A patient with a numb or burning dorsum of the foot at two weeks who also had a popliteal block will have both explanations offered. The pre-operative record of sensation, the block details and the fact that the deficit follows a single cutaneous branch at a portal are what separate them; the pathway is in [nerve injury and compartment syndrome](../a/a11-nerve-injury-compartment.html).

### 5. Diagnostic arthroscopy, synovectomy and spur resection
**Why:** The joint is inspected, the anterolateral gutter cleared of scar and synovium with a shaver, and the tibial and talar osteophytes burred back until the ankle dorsiflexes freely under direct vision.
**Anaesthesia:** Irrigation runs by gravity or a low-pressure pump; the volumes are small compared with a shoulder, but fluid can track through a capsular rent into the anterior compartment of the leg, so feel the calf and shin at the end of a long case. Movement during burring next to the neurovascular bundle is unwelcome, which is an argument for adequate depth and not for neuromuscular block.

### 6. Osteochondral lesion: debridement and microfracture
**Why:** Unstable cartilage is curetted back to a vertical, stable edge, the calcified layer removed, and the subchondral bone perforated with an awl or a fine drill every 3-4 mm so that marrow elements fill the defect and form fibrocartilage.
**Anaesthesia:** This converts a capsule operation into a bone one and adds a point to the pain for 48 h. The surgeon will often let the tourniquet down or stop the inflow briefly to see blood and fat droplets emerge from the holes; the field is meant to bleed. The patient will be non-weight-bearing for about six weeks, which changes the crutch and VTE conversation.

### 7. Posterior arthroscopy: the prone variant
**Why:** For an os trigonum, posterior impingement or flexor hallucis longus tenosynovitis, the patient is prone with the foot over the table end and two portals are made either side of the Achilles tendon; the surgeon works lateral to the flexor hallucis longus tendon, because the tibial nerve and posterior tibial vessels lie immediately medial to it.
**Anaesthesia:** A prone case for a 40-min operation: a tracheal tube or a reinforced supraglottic airway according to the patient and local practice, eyes and face checked after the turn, chest and pelvic supports, and the thigh cuff applied before turning. The complication rate was higher than for anterior arthroscopy in the pooled data (6.6% against 4.35%), with the sural and tibial nerves the ones at risk.

### 8. Lateral approach and the Brostrom repair
**Why:** A curved incision over the front of the distal fibula; the extensor retinaculum is identified and preserved, the capsule and ATFL are divided a few millimetres from the fibula, the fibular edge is roughened to bleeding bone, and two or three suture anchors are placed. The sleeve is advanced and tied with the foot held everted and at neutral, then the CFL is tightened the same way under the peroneal tendons.
**Anaesthesia:** The assistant holds the foot in a fixed position while the knots go down, and it must not move; a lightening patient who plantarflexes costs the repair its tension. The superficial peroneal nerve crosses the top of the incision and the sural nerve the bottom, both in the surgical field. This is the step that produces the pain the block is for: drilled fibula, sutured capsule, and a repair that will be sore with any inversion for weeks.

### 9. Reinforcement: retinaculum, tape or graft, and the peroneal tendons
**Why:** The Gould step sutures the extensor retinaculum to the fibula over the repair. A suture tape adds two more anchors, one in the talus. A graft reconstruction harvests gracilis from the knee or uses allograft and fixes it in bone tunnels with interference screws. If the peroneal tendons are split or subluxing they are repaired or the groove deepened through an extension of the incision behind the fibula.
**Anaesthesia:** A gracilis harvest is a second surgical site at the medial knee, outside a popliteal block, and needs its own infiltration. Tunnels and tendon work add 20-40 min of tourniquet time; call the time at 60 and 90 min. Each added element moves the pain towards the upper end of the card.

### 10. Closure, infiltration and the boot
**Why:** Layered closure, tourniquet down, portal sutures, and a backslab or boot with the ankle at neutral and slightly everted to take tension off the repair.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask for the surgeon's infiltration of the portals and the anteromedial skin, which the popliteal block does not reach. The splint goes on before emergence, because a blocked foot dropped into inversion off the trolley loads the repair that has just been tied.

### 11. Recovery and discharge
**Why:** Arthroscopy patients bear weight as tolerated in a bandage; ligament repair patients are in a boot, usually protected weight-bearing after two weeks, earlier after arthroscopic repair.
**Anaesthesia:** First oral doses before discharge. After arthroscopy alone the patient leaves with sensation intact and simple analgesia. After a popliteal block they leave with an insensate foot, crutches, and the written night plan; with intravenous dexamethasone the block lasts about 26 h in the randomised data, so it fades the next afternoon or evening. Days 1 and 2 are the sore ones and by day 3 the regular regimen is enough.

## Pain plan

The plan depends on which operation was done.

Arthroscopy alone, including spur resection: the mechanism is capsular with a little bone, the pain is short and the patient is weight-bearing. Paracetamol, an NSAID, dexamethasone 8 mg IV and the surgeon's portal and intra-articular local anaesthetic, with a small supply of oral opioid for the first night after a microfracture. No nerve block. The block menu's rule applies: for an operation that scores 1-2, a long-acting block can create a worse night than the surgery would have, and it puts a weight-bearing patient on crutches with a foot they cannot feel.

Ligament reconstruction: a capsulotomy, drilled bone and a tensioned soft-tissue repair, immobilised afterwards. The defensible default is a single-shot popliteal sciatic block with intravenous dexamethasone under general anaesthesia, regular paracetamol and an NSAID, and oral opioid for two nights. There is no trial of blocks specifically for ligament reconstruction; the reasoning borrows from the ankle fracture trials, where popliteal and saphenous blocks beat spinal anaesthesia over 27 h at the cost of rebound, and from the randomised comparison showing dexamethasone 10 mg IV extends a ropivacaine popliteal block from about 18 h to about 26 h. The rebound risk is rated moderate here and not high because the bone component is small; the same cohort that identified bone surgery, female sex and youth as risk factors applies, and this population is young. The catheter decision is no: the pain falls within 48-72 h, the patient is ambulatory, and nothing about the mechanism outlasts a dexamethasone-extended single shot. All of this is the author's judgement by analogy.

Escalation triggers: pain out of proportion in a tight boot with a tense calf or shin after a long arthroscopy is fluid extravasation or a constricting dressing, and the splint is opened before anything else is given. Calf pain and swelling at one to three weeks is a deep vein thrombosis until excluded, the commonest major complication in the pooled arthroscopy series. Burning pain on the dorsum of the foot is the superficial peroneal nerve.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The unplanned repair. When the decision is made on the table, give the block afterwards in recovery; a sciatic block placed after surgery in an awake patient who can report paraesthesia is easy and takes five minutes.
- The athlete who wants to walk out. For a debridement, protect the plan from a well-meant block.
- Posterior arthroscopy. Prone, short, and a higher complication rate; plan the airway for the position and not for the duration.
- Cavovarus foot. A patient whose instability comes from a varus heel may have a lateralising calcaneal osteotomy or a first metatarsal osteotomy added; that is bone surgery, 2 h, and the plan from [Achilles tendon repair and adult flatfoot reconstruction](achilles-tendon-flatfoot.html) or [ankle, subtalar and triple fusion](ankle-hindfoot-fusion.html) fits better than this card.
- Hypermobility syndromes. Common in this clinic population: tape or graft more likely, positioning with care for the joints, and a patient who may already be on regular analgesics.
- Hamstring graft harvest. A second site outside the block.
- Arthroscopic Brostrom. All-inside repair through the portals with anchors; in pooled comparative studies it gave slightly lower pain scores and earlier weight-bearing than the open operation, with the superficial peroneal nerve more exposed to the anchor passes.
- Thigh holder neuropathy. A common peroneal palsy from the holder looks exactly like a block that has not worn off; note the expected offset time on the chart.
- Syndesmosis and fracture work. Acute injuries are in [ankle and pilon fracture fixation](ankle-pilon-fixation.html).

## Key sources

The complication data for arthroscopy are from a large systematic review of mostly retrospective series and are probably underestimates for transient nerve symptoms. The comparison of open and arthroscopic Brostrom repair is a meta-analysis of comparative studies with high heterogeneity. There is no procedure-specific analgesic guideline and no randomised trial of regional anaesthesia for either operation; the block advice is extrapolated from ankle fracture surgery and from the general rebound literature, and the Gerbershagen ranking is the only direct evidence of how much ligament reconstruction hurts. The advice to withhold a block for arthroscopy alone is the author's judgement.
