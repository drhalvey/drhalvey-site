## What the surgeon is doing and why

This card collects the short operations at the end of a foot list. They have one thing in common: the surgeon divides or removes a small structure through a small incision, cuts no bone that matters, repairs nothing under tension, and sends the patient home walking. The anaesthetic risk is disproportion in either direction, too little for a patient lying awake with a cuff on, or far too much block for the pain that follows.

Morton's neuroma is not a tumour. The common plantar digital nerve to the third web space (less often the second) runs under the deep transverse intermetatarsal ligament between the metatarsal heads, and with tight shoes and a tethered nerve it is compressed there until it thickens and scars. The patient describes a burning pain in the ball of the foot shooting into two toes. When injections fail, the surgeon opens the web space from the dorsum, divides the ligament, follows the nerve proximally and cuts it well behind the metatarsal heads so that the stump retracts into the intrinsic muscles, away from the weight-bearing surface. Some surgeons only divide the ligament and free the nerve (neurolysis). A plantar incision gives a direct view and is preferred for revisions, at the cost of a scar on the sole. Pooled series report complete pain relief in about three quarters after neurectomy and complete satisfaction in rather fewer, and a permanently numb web space in everyone, which is the operation working.

Plantar fasciitis is a degenerative enthesopathy where the plantar fascia attaches to the heel. The few patients who fail a year of stretching, orthoses and injections are offered a partial release: the medial third to half of the fascia is divided at its origin, through a short medial heel incision or endoscopically through two small portals, leaving the lateral band to keep the arch and the lateral column stable. Many surgeons now treat the same patients by lengthening a tight calf instead. In a gastrocnemius recession the aponeurosis of the gastrocnemius is divided transversely through a 3 cm posteromedial calf incision, leaving soleus intact, which gains ankle dorsiflexion and unloads both the plantar fascia and the forefoot.

The rest are variations on small-incision soft-tissue surgery: tarsal tunnel release (the flexor retinaculum divided over the tibial nerve behind the medial malleolus), wedge resection or chemical ablation of an ingrown toenail, excision of a ganglion, a plantar fibroma, an accessory navicular, a sesamoid or a fifth metatarsal head prominence, and flexor tenotomies for flexible claw toes.

```
 incision               skin nerve              deep structure       block that fits
 dorsal web space       superficial peroneal    plantar digital n.   SPN + tibial

                        plantar branches)

 posteromedial calf     saphenous, sural        gastrocnemius        infiltration or GA
 one toe / nail         digital nerves          nail bed             digital ring block
```

The pain logic is brief. None of these cuts bone or tensions a repair; the mechanism is incisional soreness over a divided fascia or nerve, and the fact that the wound is walked on. Simple analgesia covers it, which is the surface plan in [why operations hurt](../b/b01-why-operations-hurt.html); the nerve mechanism matters only for the neurectomy and the tarsal tunnel release, and there it shows as the patient's existing neuropathic pain, not as a need for new anti-neuropathic drugs. The exceptions are known in advance: these patients have often had months or years of pain before surgery, a cut nerve occasionally forms a painful stump, and a small proportion of foot operations of any size are followed by a regional pain syndrome.

## Steps

### 1. Reading the list: where is the incision
**Why:** "Minor foot" tells you nothing; the incision site tells you the innervation, the position and whether an ankle cuff is possible.
**Anaesthesia:** Sort the case by the table above. Dorsal forefoot and sole are within an ankle block and an ankle cuff. A calf incision is not, and a gastrocnemius recession with a thigh cuff is a short general anaesthetic. Ask whether the patient is expecting to be awake; many of these are booked as "local" with or without an anaesthetist's block in mind. Note the months of pre-operative pain and what the patient takes for it.

### 2. Choosing the anaesthetic and matching the block
**Why:** The surgeon needs 20 minutes of a still, bloodless, painless foot.
**Anaesthesia:** Two good plans. Awake or lightly sedated with a selective ankle block: block only the nerves the incision uses, which reduces volume and leaves the rest of the foot normal. For a plantar fascia release or any plantar incision that is the tibial nerve alone, and it is much kinder than infiltrating the sole, where the skin is thick and injection is very painful. For a dorsal neurectomy it is the tibial nerve (the plantar digital nerve being cut) and the superficial peroneal (the skin). Choose the agent for the pain that follows, not for the longest possible block: lidocaine 1-2% gives 2-3 h and prilocaine or mepivacaine, where stocked, a little longer; ropivacaine 0.375-0.5% is reserved for the patient with an established pain problem who will benefit from a pain-free evening. The second plan is a brief general anaesthetic with a supraglottic airway and the surgeon's infiltration at the end, which suits calf incisions, anxious patients and prone positions.

### 3. Position and tourniquet
**Why:** Supine, heel at the table end. An ankle cuff gives the surgeon a dry field in which a 2 mm nerve can be seen.
**Anaesthesia:** In the randomised comparison under ankle block, an ankle cuff was tolerated better than a calf cuff at 10, 20 and 30 min with the same field. Thirty minutes is the practical limit awake ([the tourniquet](../a/a01-tourniquet.html)), which these operations fit inside; tell the patient the tight feeling is expected and tell the surgeon the time at 20 min. A digital tourniquet for nail surgery (a rolled glove finger or a silicone ring) is the item in this card that causes harm: it is small, the same colour as the dressing, and a toe left ischaemic under a bandage is lost. It goes on the count and its removal is said aloud.

### 4. Dorsal neurectomy
**Why:** A 3 cm incision over the web space, the metatarsal heads are spread, the intermetatarsal ligament is divided, and the nerve is traced back, pulled distally and cut as far proximally as it will come; the digital branches are cut beyond the bifurcation and the specimen is sent.
**Anaesthesia:** The pull on the nerve before it is cut reaches proximal to an ankle-level tibial block only rarely, but an awake patient may feel a brief deep ache in the arch; warn them. Document sensation beforehand and tell the patient again that the numb web is permanent, because a patient who had a block and wakes with numb toes will otherwise attribute it to the needle. Adrenaline is avoided in web space infiltration.

### 5. Plantar approach and revision neurectomy
**Why:** For a recurrent neuroma the stump lies in scar under the metatarsal heads and is reached through a longitudinal plantar incision between them; some surgeons use this approach for primary cases. The stump is resected more proximally and buried in muscle.
**Anaesthesia:** Often done prone or with the leg externally rotated; a prone 30-min case in a fit patient is managed as in [Achilles tendon repair](achilles-tendon-flatfoot.html). Plantar scars are tender more often than dorsal ones in pooled comparisons (16.7% against 6.2%), and the revision patient has a neuropathic pain history: continue any neuropathic agent and expect a slower recovery than the size of the wound suggests.

### 6. Plantar fascia release, open or endoscopic
**Why:** Through a medial heel incision the fascia is exposed at its calcaneal origin and the medial third to half is divided under vision; endoscopically a slotted cannula is passed across the heel beneath the fascia and a hook knife divides the medial band. The lateral band is preserved.
**Anaesthesia:** A tibial nerve block at the ankle covers everything here, including the medial calcaneal branches if it is placed proximal to the malleolus. Endoscopic series report good function with complications in about 9%, mostly recurrent or lateral foot pain, which is a mechanical consequence of the release and not a failure of analgesia. The heel is sore to stand on for one to two weeks.

### 7. Gastrocnemius recession
**Why:** A short incision on the posteromedial calf where the gastrocnemius aponeurosis joins soleus; the sural nerve and short saphenous vein are found and protected, the aponeurosis is divided transversely with the ankle dorsiflexed, and the gap opens by 1-2 cm.
**Anaesthesia:** Above the reach of an ankle block and in the territory of the saphenous and sural nerves and the tibial motor branches. The simplest plan is a brief general anaesthetic with infiltration; under infiltration alone an awake patient feels the muscle stretch as the ankle is dorsiflexed. Supine with the leg externally rotated, or prone, according to the surgeon. The sural nerve is the structure at risk. Pain afterwards is a cramp-like calf ache that scores 2 and settles in days.

### 8. Nail and single-toe procedures
**Why:** A wedge of nail and its germinal matrix is removed and the matrix ablated with phenol or excised; a flexor tendon is divided percutaneously at the base of a clawed toe; a small exostosis is trimmed.
**Anaesthesia:** A digital or metatarsal ring block with plain lidocaine or ropivacaine is sufficient and is usually the surgeon's. The anaesthetist's presence is for the patient who cannot tolerate it awake: the needle-phobic adolescent, the patient with a learning disability, the infected toe where infiltration fails. In an infected toe, block proximally at the metatarsal level away from the cellulitis. In a diabetic or vascular patient this is not minor surgery; see [diabetic foot surgery](diabetic-foot-surgery.html).

### 9. Tarsal tunnel release and other small excisions
**Why:** The flexor retinaculum is divided from above the medial malleolus to the abductor hallucis, and the tibial nerve and its plantar branches are freed. Ganglia, fibromas, an accessory navicular or a sesamoid are excised through an incision directly over them.
**Anaesthesia:** For tarsal tunnel surgery the surgeon will want to know the nerve works afterwards, and the operated nerve is the one an ankle block targets. A general anaesthetic with skin infiltration, or a short-acting block, leaves the nerve examinable in recovery and avoids a needle beside a nerve that is already symptomatic. The accessory navicular and sesamoid involve a tendon insertion and a little bone and score 2.

### 10. Dressing, shoe and discharge
**Why:** Skin sutures, a padded dressing, cuff down, a stiff-soled shoe, heel weight-bearing at once and sutures out at two weeks.
**Anaesthesia:** Paracetamol and an NSAID before leaving, with instructions to continue both for three days. An opioid is not part of the plan for a score 1 operation; a few doses of a weak opioid are kept for the variants that score 2 (gastrocnemius recession, accessory navicular, sesamoid) and for the patient with an established pain problem. A patient with a short-acting block regains sensation in the day unit or soon after, which is the intention: pain arrives gradually while they are awake and have taken something. Elevation for the first two days does more for a throbbing foot than any tablet.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a small soft-tissue wound that is walked on, with a nerve or fascia divided beneath it. Paracetamol and an NSAID are the plan, started before discharge and taken regularly for two to three days, with ice and elevation. Opioids are a rescue for the first night at most. Dexamethasone adds little when no general anaesthetic is given.

The block is chosen as an anaesthetic. The [block menu](../a/a07-block-menu.html) makes the general point that for operations scoring 1-2 a long-acting block can create a worse night than the operation would have, and this card is where it applies most directly. A long-acting ankle block lasted a mean of 14.5 h and a popliteal block 20.9 h in the randomised forefoot comparison; either outlasts the day unit, the pharmacy and the patient's waking hours after a 20-min procedure. The risk factors for rebound in the ambulatory cohort were bone surgery, female sex and younger age; without the bone the risk is low, but an abrupt offset at night is still unpleasant and still avoidable. The defensible default is therefore a selective ankle block with a short- or intermediate-acting agent or the surgeon's infiltration, with a long-acting agent only for a specific reason. This is the author's judgement; no trial has compared block durations for these procedures.

The patients who do not fit are identifiable beforehand. Chronic neuropathic pain before a neurectomy predicts a difficult recovery, and the existing gabapentinoid or antidepressant is continued. A patient who has had a pain syndrome after previous limb surgery deserves a longer block, early movement and early review. Pain that worsens after the first week with swelling, colour change and allodynia is referred promptly.

Escalation triggers: a white or blue toe under the dressing is a retained digital tourniquet or a tight bandage and is an emergency. Throbbing that eases with elevation is expected. Electric pain on pressure over the ball of the foot weeks after neurectomy is a stump neuroma and is the surgeon's to manage.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The forgotten toe tourniquet. Counted, announced and documented.
- Awake with a proximal cuff. An ankle block does not anaesthetise the tissue under any cuff: an ankle cuff is tolerated for about 30 min, a calf cuff for 20-30 min, and a thigh cuff needs a general anaesthetic; decide the cuff before the technique.
- Plantar infiltration. Painful and slow to spread in thick skin; a tibial block is the better way to numb the sole.
- Adrenaline in digits and web spaces. Avoided, particularly with vascular disease, diabetes or Raynaud phenomenon.
- Two operations at once. Bilateral neurectomies or a recession with a fasciotomy make a 45-min case and exceed awake cuff tolerance.
- The "minor" diabetic foot. A different patient and a different card.
- Intended numbness. After neurectomy, permanent; after tarsal tunnel release, the surgeon needs to examine the nerve. Both shape the choice and duration of block.
- Over-prescription. A box of strong opioid for a nail wedge resection is the commonest prescribing error on a foot list.
- Related short cases. Screw and wire removal is in [removal of metalwork](removal-of-metalwork.html); cheilectomy and lesser toe correction, which cut bone, are in [hallux valgus and forefoot surgery](hallux-valgus-forefoot.html).

## Key sources

The surgical evidence is weak: the Cochrane review of Morton's neuroma found few randomised trials, and the outcome figures for neurectomy and endoscopic fasciotomy come from pooled case series. The tourniquet trial and the block duration figures are from forefoot bone surgery and are applied here by analogy. No study has addressed anaesthetic technique or block duration for minor soft-tissue foot procedures; the advice to match a short block to a short operation follows from the pain mechanism and the general rebound literature and is the author's judgement.
