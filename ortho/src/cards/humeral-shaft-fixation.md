## What the surgeon is doing and why

The humeral shaft is a forgiving bone. It is wrapped in muscle with a good blood supply, it does not bear weight, and the shoulder and elbow compensate for 20 degrees of angulation or a few centimetres of shortening without the patient noticing. For that reason most shaft fractures have been treated in a functional brace, and the one randomised trial of plating against bracing found no meaningful difference in arm function at a year. What it also found is that 30% of the braced patients needed an operation within the year because the fracture had not healed, and an operation through an established non-union is longer and bloodier than the primary one.

So the patients who reach theatre are selected. Some have an absolute reason: an open fracture, a vascular injury, a floating elbow, a pathological fracture, bilateral fractures, or polytrauma in which a fixed arm lets the patient use crutches and be nursed. Some have a fracture that will not stay acceptably aligned in a brace, which is commoner with transverse fractures, obesity and large breasts. And some have chosen early fixation for a faster return to work over six to twelve weeks of a mobile fracture. A fourth group arrives months later with a non-union.

One structure dominates the operation. The radial nerve leaves the axilla, winds around the back of the humerus in direct contact with bone in the spiral groove, and pierces the lateral intermuscular septum about a hand's breadth above the elbow to reach the front of the arm. It is tethered at the septum, so a fracture of the middle or distal third can stretch it, bruise it or trap it between the fragments. In the pooled literature 11.8% of shaft fractures have a radial nerve palsy at presentation; 88% of those recover, most of them spontaneously, and early exploration does not improve the result. The surgeon then has to operate next to the same nerve, and each technique meets it differently:

```
  Technique                 Nerve seen    Where the nerve is at risk
  Posterior plate           yes, always   dissected off bone, plate
                                          slid beneath it
  Anterolateral plate       distally      under retractors; brachialis
                                          split protects it
  Minimally invasive plate  no            distal incision, and blind
                                          under the anterior plate
  Antegrade nail            no            closed reduction, reaming,
                                          lateral distal locking screw
```

Plating means exposing the bone. The posterior approach splits or lifts triceps, finds the radial nerve and its companion artery in the groove, mobilises them, and lays a plate on the flat posterior surface with the nerve crossing over it; it suits middle and distal-third fractures. The anterolateral approach goes between biceps and brachialis proximally and splits brachialis distally, leaving its lateral half as a cushion over the nerve; it suits proximal and middle-third fractures and a supine patient. Minimally invasive plating slides an anterior plate under brachialis through two small incisions without opening the fracture. Nailing passes a rod down the canal from the top of the humerus through a hole in the rotator cuff, or the interval beside it, and locks it with screws at each end.

The comparisons are consistent in direction and weak in quality. The posterior approach carries more iatrogenic palsy than the anterolateral (69 of 497 against 35 of 678 in the pooled studies, OR 2.72), with similar recovery. Nailing has less iatrogenic palsy, less infection, less blood loss and a shorter operating time than open plating, at the cost of fracture comminution during insertion and the shoulder complaints of a cuff entry point. Minimally invasive plating had the lowest odds of radial nerve injury in the network meta-analysis of randomised trials, on wide intervals.

The pain logic: the dominant mechanism for a plate is periosteum, a raw, incisional and deep ache along the arm from a bone exposed over most of its length through a split muscle, peaking on day 1 and settling over three days. For a nail the arm is nearly painless and the shoulder is not, because of the cuff. Two things keep this at a 3. The fracture, which hurt with every movement in a sling, is now rigid, and many patients have less pain on day 2 than they had the week before. And the bone is not loaded.

## Steps

### 1. Before the anaesthetic: the radial nerve and the reason for surgery
**Why:** The surgeon has recorded whether the patient can extend the wrist, the fingers at the knuckles and the thumb, and whether the back of the first web space feels normal. A palsy present from the injury is usually observed; a palsy that appears after a manipulation or an operation raises the question of a trapped nerve.
**Anaesthesia:** Examine the nerve yourself and write it down with the time: wrist extension, thumb extension, sensation over the first dorsal web space. It takes 20 seconds and it is the baseline against which the recovery-room examination will be read. Then find out which patient this is. The isolated fracture in a fit adult is straightforward. The polytrauma patient has a chest, a head and other fractures that set the priorities (see [polytrauma sequencing](polytrauma-sequencing.html)). The pathological fracture has a cancer, often myeloma, renal cell or breast, with anaemia, hypercalcaemia and a tumour that may bleed heavily. The non-union has had months of disuse and often months of opioids.

### 2. The anaesthetic and the block decision
**Why:** The surgeon wants to see the wrist and thumb lift in recovery. After a closed nailing or a minimally invasive plate the nerve was never seen, so a new palsy may mean it is caught in the fracture or under the plate, and many surgeons would re-explore that day. After an open plating the nerve was seen and protected, and a new palsy is more often watched.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia is the default: the positions are awkward for an awake patient and the block that would make it comfortable is the block in question. Have the conversation before induction and record its outcome. If the surgeon would act on an immediate post-operative examination, no long-acting block goes in beforehand, the surgeon infiltrates the wound, and you offer a block in recovery once the examination is documented. If a complete palsy is already present, or the surgeon says the plan would be observation whatever the examination shows, a pre-operative supraclavicular block is reasonable and gives a smoother case. A dense plexus block does not injure the radial nerve; what it does is delay a diagnosis and leave the block as a possible explanation in the notes. Dexamethasone 8 mg IV at induction either way.

### 3. Positioning by approach
**Why:** Lateral with the arm draped over a padded bolster and the forearm hanging gives the posterior approach a vertical humerus and lets the elbow flex; some surgeons prefer prone. Supine with an arm table suits anterolateral and minimally invasive plating. Beach chair, with the shoulder off the edge of the table and extended, gives the nail its entry point.
**Anaesthesia:** Lateral and prone: a tracheal tube, the dependent arm and eye protected, the neck neutral, and the fractured arm lifted onto the bolster by the surgeon with traction, because the bone ends move and bleed. Beach chair: the blood pressure that matters is at the head and not at the cuff, with the cuff on the other arm or the leg, and the same vasopressor-supported, no-deliberate-hypotension approach as for [shoulder arthroplasty](anatomic-tsa.html). A patient with rib fractures on the dependent side, or a pelvis, may not tolerate the lateral position and that changes the surgeon's approach.

### 4. Posterior approach and the radial nerve
**Why:** A midline posterior incision, triceps split between its long and lateral heads or lifted from the lateral side, and the radial nerve found with the profunda brachii artery where they cross the bone. The nerve is freed proximally and distally and held in a loop so the plate can pass under it.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon may ask for no muscle relaxant so that a nerve stimulator, or simply a twitch on touching the nerve, helps to identify it; settle that before you give a second dose. The profunda brachii is the bleeder in this approach. Handling the nerve is what produces the transient palsies, and how the nerve looked (in continuity, bruised, trapped or divided) is worth asking about and writing down, because it decides what a wrist drop in recovery means.

### 5. Anterolateral approach and minimally invasive plating
**Why:** Open: biceps is retracted medially and brachialis split along its length, which is possible because its medial half is supplied by the musculocutaneous nerve and its lateral half by the radial; the radial nerve is found distally between brachialis and brachioradialis. Minimally invasive: a proximal incision between deltoid and biceps and a distal one over the front of the lower humerus, with a long plate tunnelled between them under brachialis and the fracture reduced by traction under fluoroscopy.
**Anaesthesia:** The musculocutaneous nerve and its terminal sensory branch, the lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm, are the structures at the distal incision: numbness along the radial border of the forearm afterwards is theirs. Minimally invasive plating trades dissection for radiation and for an indirect reduction that needs a relaxed arm and an assistant holding traction. It is the gentlest version of this operation for pain.

### 6. Reduction and plating
**Why:** The fracture is cleaned, reduced and held with clamps, then compressed with a lag screw and a neutralisation plate or bridged with a long locking plate, with at least three or four screws each side. In a non-union the fibrous tissue and sclerotic bone ends are resected back to bleeding bone and the gap is packed with graft, often from the iliac crest.
**Anaesthesia:** Clamps around the humerus are placed on bone, with the nerve in view on one side and the brachial artery and median nerve across the medial septum on the other. Bleeding is steady from the bone ends and the split muscle and is 200-500 mL in a fresh fracture. A non-union doubles the time and the loss and adds a second, often more painful, wound at the iliac crest that no arm block covers: have the surgeon infiltrate it. A pathological fracture through a vascular metastasis can bleed far beyond this range; such tumours are embolised beforehand when the diagnosis is known.

### 7. Antegrade nailing
**Why:** With the shoulder extended, a short incision at the edge of the acromion, a split in the deltoid and a small incision in the supraspinatus tendon or the rotator interval give access to the top of the humeral head. A guide wire is passed across the fracture, the canal is reamed by most surgeons, and the nail is inserted and locked proximally through a jig and distally freehand.
**Anaesthesia:** Reaming and nail passage in a humerus produce far less marrow embolisation than in a femur, and oxygenation changes are unusual in an isolated injury; in bilateral or pathological fractures with a compromised chest it is no longer negligible (see [fat embolism](../a/a03-fat-embolism.html)). The proximal locking screws pass near the axillary nerve, and a lateral-to-medial distal locking screw passes near the radial nerve, which is why most surgeons lock from front to back through a small open incision. The fracture is reduced closed: if the nerve lies between the fragments nobody sees it. Blood loss is small. This is the technique with the most to gain from an immediate nerve examination.

### 8. Closure and the nerve check on waking
**Why:** Muscle falls back over the plate, the fascia is closed, the wound is infiltrated, and the arm goes into a sling. The surgeon records in the operation note where the nerve lies in relation to the plate, for whoever takes the plate out.
**Anaesthesia:** Plan the emergence around an examination: a patient who is awake, comfortable and able to follow a command within a few minutes of leaving theatre. That means short-acting agents, the wound infiltrated, paracetamol and an opioid on board, and no residual neuromuscular block. As soon as the patient can cooperate, ask for the wrist to be lifted and the thumb raised, and write the result and the time in the anaesthetic record as well as telling the surgeon.

### 9. Recovery, the block and going home
**Why:** With rigid fixation the surgeon wants the elbow and shoulder moving within days and the hand used for light tasks at once. A patient with a radial palsy gets a wrist splint and hand therapy while it recovers.
**Anaesthesia:** With the examination documented, a single-shot supraclavicular block (or infraclavicular for a mid or distal exposure) is now simply good analgesia: 15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%, on top of the dexamethasone already given. Offer it to the open plating patient with pain above 4 in recovery, to the non-union, and to anyone opioid-tolerant; the minimally invasive plate and the nail often do not need it. A block given at 4 pm wears off in the small hours, so the oral regimen starts before discharge from recovery and the patient who goes home the same day has a written night plan and a sling that stays on while the arm is numb. If a new palsy is found, no block is placed until the surgeon has decided between re-exploration and observation.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is periosteal and muscular, short-lived, in a limb that is rested, so the plan is a general anaesthetic with surgeon infiltration, intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, regular paracetamol and an NSAID from the first evening, an oral opioid for two to four days, and a single-shot plexus block timed after the nerve examination for those who need it. Surgeons vary on NSAIDs after fracture fixation; the concern is non-union, the evidence for harm from a short course is weak, and the author's practice is a course of a week or less unless the surgeon objects, and none for a non-union operation.

The block decision is the distinctive part of this card and it is a decision about information, not about nerve safety. There is no trial of regional anaesthesia in humeral shaft fixation and no evidence that a plexus block worsens a traumatic or iatrogenic radial neuropathy. The argument for waiting is the surgical one: one in nine fractures has a palsy before surgery, a further few per cent acquire one during it (most often with the posterior approach and open plating), and the immediate examination separates the two and, after a closed technique, may send the patient back to theatre. A pre-operative block removes that examination for 12 to 24 hours with dexamethasone on board. The cost of waiting is small, because the operation is done under general anaesthesia regardless and the block works as well at the end as at the beginning. The defensible default is therefore to block afterwards. Blocking beforehand is reasonable when the examination cannot change anything: a complete palsy already documented, or a surgeon who has stated that the nerve will be visualised and a new deficit observed.

Which block follows from the incision. The shaft and its periosteum are supplied by the radial, musculocutaneous and axillary nerves, with skin over the lateral and posterior arm from the axillary and radial nerves and the medial arm from the intercostobrachial; a supraclavicular block covers nearly all of it and an infraclavicular block covers the middle and distal shaft. An axillary block does not reach high enough. The shoulder pain of an antegrade nail is suprascapular and axillary territory, which a supraclavicular block reaches incompletely and an interscalene or superior trunk block reaches well.

A catheter is rarely justified: the curve falls within three days. Rebound after a single shot is moderate here, lower than after wrist or ankle surgery in the author's experience, because the fixed fracture hurts less than the unfixed one did; the rebound cohort's risk factors (bone surgery, younger age, no dexamethasone) still apply. Escalation: severe pain with a tense arm and pain on passive finger extension is a compartment syndrome of the arm or forearm, which is rare and associated with vascular injury and crush; increasing pain with a swollen, bruised arm in a patient on an anticoagulant is a haematoma, and one that presses on the radial nerve produces a palsy hours after a normal examination, which is the reason to repeat the examination before the patient leaves.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Pre-existing radial nerve palsy. Common, usually a neurapraxia, and usually observed. It removes the argument against a pre-operative block only if it is complete; a partial palsy can still worsen and still needs a post-operative examination.
- Palsy that appears after a closed manipulation or between examinations. Treated by many surgeons as a nerve in the fracture and explored; this may be the indication for the operation.
- Holstein-Lewis pattern. A spiral fracture of the distal third with the nerve tethered at the septum beside the spike: the highest-risk configuration for both injury-related and surgical palsy.
- Non-union surgery. Longer, bloodier, an iliac crest wound, a nerve stuck in scar, and a patient who may be opioid-tolerant: the one catheter indication, and a tranexamic acid case.
- Pathological fracture. A nail is the usual implant because it protects the whole bone; the anaesthetic is governed by the cancer (haemoglobin, calcium, renal function, other lesions in the cervical spine and femur) and by bleeding from vascular metastases.
- Polytrauma. A humeral shaft is fixed early to allow crutches and nursing, often at the same sitting as a femur or tibia; position and timing are set by the chest and the head, and the arm takes whichever technique fits the position the other operations need.
- Vascular injury. A brachial artery repair comes with a fasciotomy of the forearm, an anticoagulant and no plexus block until the compartments are no longer in question.
- Beach chair hypotension during nailing. The same cerebral perfusion problem as any sitting shoulder operation, in a trauma patient who may be hypovolaemic.
- The patient who must avoid general anaesthesia. A supraclavicular block with an intercostobrachial supplement and light sedation is workable for an anterolateral or minimally invasive plate in the supine position, and the delayed nerve examination is an accepted cost that the surgeon agrees to in advance.
- Removal of the plate. Rarely needed, and the hazardous part of it is the radial nerve lying over the metal: see [removal of metalwork](removal-of-metalwork.html).

## Key sources

The radial nerve figures rest on a large systematic review of injury-related palsy and several meta-analyses of iatrogenic palsy by approach and implant, which agree in direction (posterior and open plating worst, nailing and minimally invasive plating best) and are built on small, heterogeneous studies. The decision to operate at all is informed by one good randomised trial. There is no trial of any regional technique in humeral shaft fixation: the advice to block after the nerve examination is the author's judgement about the value of that examination, consistent with the approach on the [elbow fracture](elbow-fracture-fixation.html) card.
