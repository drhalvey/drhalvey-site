## What the surgeon is doing and why

Two uncommon operations share this card because they share a neighbourhood: the two ends of the shoulder girdle, one resting on the ribs and the other on the mediastinum. In both, what lies underneath the bone matters more to you than the bone.

The scapula is a thin plate wrapped in muscle, and it takes a great deal of energy to break it. Most scapular fractures are therefore markers of a chest injury (rib fractures, pneumothorax, pulmonary contusion, sometimes a clavicle fracture or a plexus injury) and most are treated in a sling, because the muscle envelope holds the fragments and the scapulothoracic joint forgives malunion. Pooled comparative data show no overall functional benefit from fixing extra-articular fractures. The surgeon operates on the minority where geometry is lost: a displaced articular step in the glenoid fossa, a glenoid neck that has angulated or medialised so far that the cuff has lost its lever arm, a double disruption of the suspensory ring (the floating shoulder: glenoid neck plus clavicle), or a displaced acromion or coracoid.

To do it the surgeon has to reach bone that is covered on every side. The useful bone is at the edges: the lateral border, the spine, the glenoid neck. The standard route is posterior (the Judet approach): an incision along the spine and down the medial border, posterior deltoid detached from the spine, and then either the whole infraspinatus lifted off its fossa on its neurovascular pedicle (classic) or windows developed between infraspinatus and teres minor and along the medial border (modified, muscle-sparing). The suprascapular nerve at the spinoglenoid notch and the circumflex scapular artery at the lateral border are the structures in the way. Small plates go along the lateral border and the base of the spine. An anterior glenoid rim fragment is a different, smaller operation through a deltopectoral approach or arthroscopically, close to a bony [shoulder stabilisation](shoulder-stabilisation.html).

The sternoclavicular joint is the only bony link between the arm and the trunk, a shallow saddle held entirely by ligaments. An anterior dislocation is a cosmetic lump and is left alone. A posterior dislocation (or, under about 25 years, a posteriorly displaced medial physeal fracture, since that physis is the last in the body to close) drives the medial clavicle into the superior mediastinum, where the brachiocephalic veins, the brachiocephalic trunk on the right, the left common carotid and subclavian arteries, the trachea and the oesophagus lie within a centimetre or two of the posterior capsule. Half of patients have symptoms of compression (dysphagia, dyspnoea, a congested arm) and half have a compressed brachiocephalic vein on CT. The surgeon's task is to lift the clavicle forward off those structures and make it stay: closed reduction if acute, and open reduction with suture or tendon-graft stabilisation if it will not reduce or will not hold. Chronic instability and arthritis are treated with a figure-of-eight tendon graft through drill holes, or with resection of the medial 1-1.5 cm of clavicle. Kirschner wires across this joint are not used because they migrate into the chest.

The pain logic. For the scapula the mechanism is muscle: a large posterior dissection the patient then lies on, over ribs that may be broken, with a bone component from plating that settles in 48 hours. It is a 3. The sternoclavicular joint is a short incision over subcutaneous bone with drill holes: a 2 in pain and a different order of risk.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment of the trauma patient with a scapular fracture
**Why:** Fixation is semi-elective, done days after injury once the chest and head are stable, because nothing about the scapula is urgent.
**Anaesthesia:** Read the trauma CT, not the shoulder films: count the rib fractures, look for contusion, a residual pneumothorax, a chest drain and its side. A patient who is saturating on room air supine may not tolerate two to four hours lateral or prone with the injured lung uppermost and positive pressure applied to a small pneumothorax. Examine and document the plexus and the axillary, suprascapular and radial nerves, because a traction injury from the accident is common and will otherwise be attributed to the operation or to your block. A [clavicle fixation](acj-reconstruction-clavicle.html) is often done first, supine, under the same anaesthetic.

### 2. Airway, lines and positioning for the posterior approach
**Why:** The surgeon needs the whole scapula from medial border to glenoid and a freely mobile arm to manipulate the glenoid fragment.
**Anaesthesia:** Reinforced tracheal tube, secured for a patient rolled forward with the face partly down. Lateral decubitus tilted forward on a bean bag, the lower shoulder and axilla free, the upper arm draped on a padded bolster. Cannulae and arterial line (for the chest-injured or the long case) in the lower arm before the roll. If a chest drain is in, keep it below the patient and unclamped. Place the erector spinae plane block after positioning, on the operative side at T2-T3, medial to the planned incision, with ropivacaine 0.375% 25-30 mL.

### 3. Posterior exposure: deltoid, infraspinatus and the lateral border
**Why:** The posterior deltoid is released from the scapular spine and the plane beneath it opened. The surgeon then either elevates infraspinatus from medial to lateral or works through the infraspinatus and teres minor interval to reach the lateral border and glenoid neck, protecting the suprascapular nerve as it rounds the base of the spine.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the blood loss: broad muscle surfaces and the circumflex scapular artery, which crosses the lateral border exactly where the plate goes and is usually ligated. Give tranexamic acid before incision. The surgeon uses muscle twitch to find the suprascapular and axillary nerves; do not top up the neuromuscular blocker after intubation unless asked.

### 4. Reduction and plating
**Why:** The lateral border is restored first because it is the thickest bone and sets length and angulation of the glenoid neck; a second plate on the spine or medial border controls rotation. Reduction is by a pin in the glenoid fragment used as a joystick and by moving the arm.
**Anaesthesia:** Fluoroscopy comes across the chest; the arm is pulled and rotated repeatedly. Ventilation with the heavy upper arm and a surgeon leaning on the posterior chest raises airway pressure; watch for a pneumothorax that was small on CT becoming significant (rising pressures, falling saturation, falling end-tidal carbon dioxide and pressure). Drills on a thin scapular body are short, and plunging through it into the chest is possible but rare.

### 5. Intra-articular glenoid fixation
**Why:** A displaced fossa fragment is reduced under direct vision through a posterior capsulotomy, or an anterior rim fragment is fixed with screws or anchors through an anterior or arthroscopic approach.
**Anaesthesia:** For the arthroscopic or anterior case the patient is in beach chair or lateral traction and the anaesthetic is that of a [stabilisation](shoulder-stabilisation.html): an interscalene or superior trunk block is the right block for that operation, because the pain is capsule and glenoid rim, both inside its territory. Irrigation fluid can track into the chest wall and neck in a long arthroscopic fracture case.

### 6. Closure and emergence after scapular fixation
**Why:** Deltoid is reattached to the spine through bone tunnels, a drain is left under the muscle, and the arm goes into a sling with early passive motion.
**Anaesthesia:** Turn supine before lightening. Extubate awake and sitting up; a chest-injured patient who has been lateral for three hours has atelectasis in the lower lung and a block that does nothing for the rib fractures on its own. Chest radiograph in recovery if airway pressures changed or a drain was not in place.

### 7. Sternoclavicular joint: what is arranged before the patient is asleep
**Why:** Reduction pulls the clavicle forward out of the mediastinum. If it has lacerated or tamponaded a vein, that is the moment the vessel bleeds; a drill or retractor behind the joint is the other moment.
**Anaesthesia:** The catastrophic injury is rare: none needed intervention in a 125-patient multicentre series, most of them open reductions. Its rarity is the reason the plan has to be explicit, because nobody in the room will have seen one. Review the CT angiogram with the surgeon. A cardiothoracic or vascular surgeon knows the case is on and can attend within minutes, a sternal saw is in theatre, and the chest is prepared to the umbilicus. Blood is grouped and available. Two large cannulae, at least one in a leg or the opposite arm on the right-sided dislocation, and for a left-sided dislocation remember that the left brachiocephalic vein crosses behind the manubrium, so fluid given into the left arm may be going towards the hole; femoral or right-arm access is the safe choice. Arterial line in the arm opposite to a compressed artery. A patient with stridor, dysphagia or dyspnoea lying flat is a mediastinal mass until the clavicle is off the trachea: keep spontaneous ventilation at induction if there is airway compression, and have the surgeon scrubbed.

### 8. Closed reduction
**Why:** Within about 48 hours, traction on the abducted, extended arm against a bolster between the scapulae levers the medial clavicle laterally and forwards; if it does not come, a towel clip through the skin grasps the clavicle and lifts it. A clunk marks reduction, which is usually stable.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with full relaxation for the attempt. Watch the arterial trace and the airway pressure as it reduces: relief of venous compression is silent, a tear is not. The arm congestion and any pulse deficit should resolve; say so aloud. If it is stable, this is a 20-minute anaesthetic.

### 9. Open reduction and stabilisation or resection
**Why:** A transverse or hockey-stick incision over the joint; the anterior capsule is opened, the clavicle delivered forward with a bone clamp, and a malleable retractor is slid behind the manubrium and clavicle before any drill is used. Stability is restored with heavy sutures through bone tunnels or a figure-of-eight tendon graft (palmaris longus, gracilis or semitendinosus, autograft or allograft), or the medial clavicle is excised while the costoclavicular ligament is preserved.
**Anaesthesia:** Sternohyoid and sternothyroid are the only tissue between the drill and the vein. Keep the patient still: relaxed, or deep, with no coughing on the tube. Keep the venous pressure unremarkable and the lungs from pushing up into the field; a brief pause in ventilation while the posterior cortex is drilled is a reasonable request. Air embolism through an open brachiocephalic vein is possible in a head-up patient. A hamstring harvest adds a leg wound; palmaris adds a forearm one. Pneumothorax from the pleural dome is the complication you are more likely to meet than a vessel injury.

### 10. Emergence and the first night after sternoclavicular surgery
**Why:** The repair is protected in a sling or figure-of-eight brace for six weeks; the surgeon wants no straining against it and an early check of pulses.
**Anaesthesia:** A smooth emergence without coughing or bucking, for the repair and for the veins behind it. Pain is modest: the superficial cervical plexus block or the surgeon's infiltration covers the skin through the supraclavicular nerves, and paracetamol with an NSAID covers the rest. Chest radiograph in recovery. Voice change, dysphagia or a swelling neck overnight is a haematoma behind the manubrium and goes back to the surgeon at once.

## Pain plan

For scapular fixation the pain is posterior chest wall muscle over thin bone, so the block has to reach the dorsal rami and the posterior skin, which an interscalene block does not. The author's practice is a single-shot erector spinae plane block at T2-T3 with intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, surgeon infiltration of the deltoid reattachment, regular paracetamol and an NSAID (renal function and the trauma team's view permitting), and oral opioid for three to five days. This is judgement supported by anatomy and case-level reports, not by a trial in scapular surgery. An interscalene or superior trunk block covers the glenoid and most of the bone through the suprascapular, axillary and subscapular nerves and is the better block for an intra-articular or arthroscopic glenoid case; for a posterior approach it leaves the incision uncovered and paralyses half the diaphragm in a patient whose problem is the lung on that side.

The catheter decision belongs to the ribs. Three or more fractures, a flail segment or an older patient earns a continuous erector spinae, paravertebral or epidural technique on chest-injury grounds (see [rib fixation](rib-fixation.html)), and the scapular surgery then rides on it. An isolated scapular fracture does not earn a catheter.

For the sternoclavicular joint, simple analgesia with wound infiltration or a superficial cervical plexus block (ropivacaine 0.375% 10 mL) is enough, with a few doses of oral opioid. Bilateral or deep cervical blocks are avoided: a phrenic or recurrent laryngeal palsy confuses the post-operative picture in the one operation where breathlessness and voice change are the warning signs.

Escalation: pain at the back of the shoulder that rises on day 1-2 with a tense wound is a haematoma under infraspinatus. Pleuritic pain or breathlessness after either operation is a pneumothorax until the film says otherwise.

## Pitfalls and variants

- The scapula as a marker. First rib and scapular fractures travel with pulmonary contusion, blunt aortic injury and plexus injury. Fixation is rarely urgent; a patient whose oxygenation is still deteriorating on day 3 is not ready.
- Prone instead of lateral. Some surgeons prefer fully prone for a medial border or complex body fracture; the airway, eye and abdominal precautions are those of any prone case, with the difference that the arm must stay mobile.
- Floating shoulder. Clavicle first, supine, then turn. Two positions, two preparations, three to four hours.
- Acromial and scapular spine stress fractures after a [reverse TSA](reverse-tsa.html). An elderly patient, a plate on poor bone, a high rate of fixation failure, and the same posterior approach in miniature.
- Suprascapular nerve. Injured by the fracture, by retraction at the spinoglenoid notch, or blocked by you. Documented infraspinatus function before surgery is the only way to tell them apart later.
- The adolescent with a medial clavicle physeal injury. The largest series of posterior sternoclavicular injuries is in patients with a mean age under 15: paediatric equipment and blood volumes, the same mediastinum.
- Delayed presentation of a posterior dislocation. After a week or two the clavicle may be adherent to the vein; closed reduction is not attempted, and the open case carries the vascular risk that the acute series did not show.
- Medial clavicle resection for infection or arthritis. Septic arthritis of this joint occurs in intravenous drug users, dialysis patients and after central lines, and can extend into the mediastinum; see [osteomyelitis and septic arthritis](osteomyelitis-septic-arthritis.html).

## Key sources

Both halves of this card rest on thin evidence. The operative indications for scapular fractures are consensus thresholds, and the one recent meta-analysis could not show a functional benefit for extra-articular fractures overall. The sternoclavicular series are reassuring about vascular injury in acute reductions but small enough that a risk of 1 in 50 would not have been excluded; the standby recommendation is a response to the size of the consequence, not to its measured frequency. No trial compares regional techniques for either operation, and the block advice here is anatomical reasoning labelled as the author's practice.
