## What the surgeon is doing and why

A fracture that has not healed has failed for one of two reasons, and the operation follows from which. In a hypertrophic nonunion the bone is alive and trying: there is abundant callus that cannot bridge because the fracture is moving. Biology is intact and mechanics are wrong, so the surgeon adds stability (a compression plate, or a larger reamed nail in exchange for the old one) and no graft is needed. In an atrophic nonunion the bone ends are thin, sclerotic and quiet, joined by fibrous tissue: the blood supply was destroyed by the injury or the first operation, or the patient smokes, is diabetic or was infected. Mechanics and biology both have to be rebuilt. An infected nonunion is its own category and is staged.

For the atrophic nonunion the surgeon reopens the old approach through scar, removes the failed metalwork (broken screws and stripped heads make this the least predictable part of the case), takes several deep samples for culture before any antibiotic is given, excises the fibrous tissue until the bone ends bleed, reopens the sealed medullary canals with a drill, and raises thin petals of cortex on either side of the fracture with a chisel so that vascular bone flaps surround it. Deformity is corrected. A new plate is applied in compression, or a nail exchanged. Then living bone is brought in: autograft, which supplies cells, growth factors and scaffold together.

The graft has to come from somewhere. The anterior iliac crest is the default because the patient is already supine: an incision behind the anterior superior iliac spine, muscle lifted subperiosteally from the inner or outer table, and cancellous bone scooped out with gouges and curettes, or a block taken with the cortex. The posterior crest yields more but requires the patient prone or lateral and then a turn. The alternative is the reamer-irrigator-aspirator: a reamer with built-in irrigation and suction is passed down the femoral canal, and the reamings are trapped in a filter. In the randomised trial it yielded as much graft as the posterior crest, in less time, with similar union and significantly less donor-site pain.

The pain logic has to account for two wounds. The nonunion site is periosteal: a long bone stripped, chiselled and plated, in a limb that has often been painful for a year, in a patient who is frequently already on opioids. The crest is muscle torn off bone at a site that cannot be rested, since the abdominal wall and the hip abductors both attach to it, so walking, coughing and rolling over all pull on it. Trials in other specialties that use crest graft make the point that donor pain is often worse than the pain of the primary operation, and it persists: in pooled comparisons chronic donor-site pain followed about 14% of crest harvests and none of the reamer harvests. Together that is a 4, with a high rebound risk because two blocks wear off on the same night.

## Steps

### 1. Work-up and the plan for samples
**Why:** The surgeon needs to know whether the nonunion is infected, because an infected nonunion is debrided and staged, not grafted. Inflammatory markers, imaging and sometimes a biopsy inform it, and at operation five or more deep samples are taken with clean instruments before antibiotics.
**Anaesthesia:** Ask at the briefing whether antibiotics are to be withheld until sampling, and say so aloud at the time-out so that the routine dose is not given by reflex. Dexamethasone is a reasonable thing to discuss in the same breath if infection is the working diagnosis; a large trial in general surgical patients found no increase in wound infection with 8 mg, but some surgeons prefer to avoid it here. The patient profile matters: smoking, diabetes, long-term opioids, vitamin D deficiency and months of immobility are all common. Establish the baseline opioid dose; see [the opioid-tolerant patient](../a/a12-opioid-tolerant.html).

### 2. Anaesthetic plan by site
**Why:** The operation is two to four hours and involves two operative fields.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia is the default for the upper limb, the long case and the reamer harvest. Lower limb nonunion with an anterior crest harvest can be done under spinal or combined spinal-epidural anaesthesia, which covers both wounds. The heparin is timed to it as set out in [venous thromboembolism prophylaxis](../a/a05-vte.html): with prophylactic enoxaparin the needle goes in at least 12 h after a dose and the first postoperative dose is given at least 12 h after the needle; an epidural catheter is removed at least 12 h after a dose and the next dose waits 4 h; a direct oral anticoagulant is not given while an epidural catheter is in place and starts no sooner than 6 h after the needle or the removal. Add the limb block that the original fracture would have had, from the relevant fracture card, and plan the harvest site separately (step 6). For the tibia, where a compartment syndrome is possible after a long reconstruction, follow the dilute-block rules on the [tibial card](tibial-nailing-plateau.html). Two cannulae; an arterial line for femoral nonunion and for any reamer harvest in a patient with limited reserve. Tranexamic acid 1 g IV at induction.

### 3. Positioning with the crest in the field
**Why:** A bump under the buttock on the harvest side lifts the crest; both fields are prepared and draped separately so that instruments used on a possibly infected nonunion never touch the donor site. For a posterior crest harvest the patient is positioned prone or lateral first, the graft taken and the wound closed, then the patient is turned and re-draped.
**Anaesthesia:** The turn for a posterior harvest costs 20-30 min and all the usual prone precautions for a short period. If a transversus abdominis plane or quadratus lumborum block is planned, do it before draping, on the correct side, which is not always the side of the nonunion. Forced-air warming has less skin to work with when the torso and a limb are both prepared, so start early and warm the fluids.

### 4. Exposure and removal of metalwork
**Why:** The old incision is reopened and the plate or nail exposed through scar. Screws that have cold-welded, stripped or broken are drilled out with trephines or burrs; a broken nail needs hooks and extraction kits.
**Anaesthesia:** This phase sets the duration. Scar bleeds more than virgin tissue and a tourniquet, where one can be used, hides the loss until it is released. Metal debris from burring out screws generates heat and a lot of irrigation. Samples are taken here, from around the implant and the fibrous tissue: give the antibiotic when the surgeon confirms the last sample is off the table.

### 5. Debridement, canal opening and decortication
**Why:** Fibrous tissue and dead bone are removed back to cortex that shows punctate bleeding. The canals are reopened to let marrow elements reach the fracture, and the cortex on each side is raised in thin attached petals. If a tourniquet is up it is released now so the bleeding test means something.
**Anaesthesia:** Decortication of a long bone is the periosteal injury that drives the pain at the limb, and is done with a chisel and mallet: stimulation rises sharply even in a blocked limb if the block is patchy. Tourniquet release brings the usual metabolic load and the first honest view of blood loss.

### 6. Anterior iliac crest harvest
**Why:** The incision is placed over the crest at least 2-3 cm behind the anterior superior iliac spine to spare the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve and to avoid weakening the spine itself. The periosteum is incised along the crest and the muscle elevated from one table; cancellous bone is harvested with gouges through a cortical window, or a full-thickness block is cut. Bone wax or a haemostatic agent is pressed into the bed and the periosteum and muscle closed tightly over it.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the wound to plan for. The skin and the muscle attachments here are supplied by the lateral cutaneous branches of the subcostal and iliohypogastric nerves and the periosteum by local branches, which is the anatomical case for a posterior transversus abdominis plane or quadratus lumborum block. The trial support comes from children and young adults, not from nonunion surgery: in 143 patients aged 6-22 years a plane block added to wound infiltration gave less pain at 24 h and better walking, and in 18 patients having alveolar cleft grafting (mean age 14 years) it gave lower donor-site pain to 12 h and less morphine over 24 h. The surgeon infiltrates the periosteum and muscle with long-acting local anaesthetic before closing, and for a large harvest leaves a fine catheter under the muscle repair. The evidence for repeat dosing is one trial of 22 patients after cervical fusion, in which six bupivacaine injections into the site gave lower pain scores, significantly so only at 3 h after the first two doses, and a mean 24 h morphine of 32 mg against 44 mg. A trial of a single subcutaneous infiltration at the incision in 30 mandibular reconstruction patients found no difference in pain or analgesic use; it did not test periosteal or muscle infiltration. The cancellous bed oozes after closure, so a haematoma, and occasionally a late haemoglobin fall, comes from here.

### 7. Posterior crest harvest (variant)
**Why:** Through a curved or vertical incision over the posterior superior iliac spine, staying within about 8 cm of it to protect the superior cluneal nerves and well above the sciatic notch, the outer table is opened and a larger volume of cancellous bone removed.
**Anaesthesia:** More graft for more pain later: in the systematic review, chronic donor-site pain and sensory disturbance were commoner after posterior than anterior harvest. The cluneal nerves are dorsal rami, so an erector spinae plane block or the surgeon's infiltration and catheter are the options, not an abdominal wall block. Deep to the notch lie the superior gluteal artery and the sciatic nerve; a sudden brisk bleed during a posterior harvest is that artery.

### 8. Reamer-irrigator-aspirator harvest (variant)
**Why:** Through a small incision over the greater trochanter, or through the knee, a guidewire is passed down the femur and a single pass of an oversized reamer head, with irrigation flowing down the shaft and suction drawing the reamings back, fills an inline filter with 30-90 mL of graft.
**Anaesthesia:** The suction does not distinguish graft from blood. In the meta-analysis the mean blood loss was about 800 mL, the mean fall in haemoglobin 3.7 g/dL, and one patient in ten was transfused. Watch the canister, subtract the irrigation, and call the volume to the surgeon every few minutes; the remedy is to stop suction whenever the reamer is not advancing. Have blood available before starting. Reaming also sends marrow emboli, although the negative pressure reduces canal pressure compared with standard reaming. An eccentric reamer can thin or perforate the cortex and the femur can fracture then or in the following weeks, which is why the patient may be told to protect weight-bearing on the donor leg.

### 9. Fixation under compression and grafting
**Why:** The plate is contoured and applied with a tensioning device or eccentric screws to squeeze the bone ends together, or a larger nail is driven after reaming. The graft is packed around and into the nonunion and under the cortical petals.
**Anaesthesia:** The quiet end of the case. Check a haemoglobin if the femur or a reamer was involved. This is the time to make sure the limb block has been done or topped up and the harvest-site plan has actually happened, because closure of the crest often occurs earlier in the case while attention is elsewhere.

### 10. Induced membrane technique (variant)
**Why:** For an infected or segmental defect the surgeon stages it. First operation: radical debridement, stabilisation and a block of antibiotic-loaded cement in the gap. Over six to eight weeks a vascular membrane forms around the cement. Second operation: the membrane is incised, the cement removed, and the cavity filled with a large volume of autograft, which is the commonest reason for a reamer harvest.
**Anaesthesia:** Two anaesthetics some weeks apart, the second with the big harvest. The cement is hand-moulded in an open wound and carries no cement implantation risk. Antibiotic choices between stages are the microbiologist's, and the patient may arrive for the second stage on a long course with altered renal function.

### 11. Closure and the first days
**Why:** Layered closure of both wounds; drains are surgeon-dependent. Weight-bearing is restricted for the limb according to the fixation.
**Anaesthesia:** The trajectory on this card is 3 on the first evening with both blocks working, 6 on day 1, 5 on day 2, 4 on day 3 and 3 at a week. Chart the catheter boluses or infusion for the harvest site if one was left. Day 1 haemoglobin for femoral cases and all reamer harvests.

## Pain plan

Write the plan as two columns. For the limb: the same block the fracture card recommends for a fixation at that site, single shot in most cases, with a catheter when the operation is a long femoral, tibial or humeral re-plating in a patient already on opioids. For the harvest site: assume a single infiltration will have gone by the first night and choose something that lasts. That assumption is the duration of plain local anaesthetic and the author's judgement; the one trial of a single injection tested subcutaneous infiltration only and found no difference in pain or analgesic use. For the anterior crest the defensible default is a posterior transversus abdominis plane or quadratus lumborum block with 20 mL of ropivacaine 0.375% before incision, plus the surgeon's infiltration at closure, and for a harvest larger than a few cubic centimetres a catheter under the muscle repair with intermittent boluses of ropivacaine 0.2% 10 mL every 6-8 h for 24-48 h, within the total daily dose once the limb block is counted. The plane block rests on two randomised trials in children and young adults, the repeat-dose catheter on one trial of 22 patients after cervical fusion, and the plan is assembled by the author; there is no trial of any component, or of the combination, in nonunion surgery.

Systemic analgesia is constrained by the surgeon. NSAIDs are the best drug for bone pain and are the drug most nonunion surgeons forbid, on the strength of observational associations between NSAID use and failure of union; in a patient whose operation exists because a bone would not heal, the author does not argue the point. That leaves paracetamol, an opioid (continuing the baseline dose and adding to it), and adjuncts: intra-operative ketamine with a low-dose infusion for 24-48 h in the opioid-tolerant patient, and dexamethasone where the surgeon agrees. Many of these patients have had neuropathic limb pain for months and are already on a gabapentinoid or an antidepressant, which continues.

Rebound is high: a limb block and a plane block recede together at 12-18 h in a patient with reduced opioid responsiveness. Escalation triggers: donor-site pain with a swelling over the crest and a falling haemoglobin is a haematoma; burning or numbness on the outer thigh is the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve and is usually temporary; groin or thigh pain on weight-bearing after a reamer harvest is a femoral fracture until imaged; and after an anterior harvest a sudden pain at the front of the pelvis on standing is an avulsion of the anterior superior iliac spine. Donor-site pain still troublesome at six weeks should be named and treated as the start of a chronic pain problem.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Small nonunions. A clavicle, forearm or scaphoid nonunion with a small local or crest graft is a shorter operation with a score nearer 3; the scaphoid is on the [wrist arthroscopy and scaphoid card](wrist-arthroscopy-scaphoid.html).
- Hypertrophic nonunion. Stability only, no graft: an exchange nailing is the fracture nailing over again, with the reaming, on the [femoral](femoral-shaft-nailing.html) or tibial card.
- Infected nonunion. Staged, with antibiotics withheld until sampling, more blood loss from inflamed tissue and repeat visits; see [osteomyelitis and septic arthritis](osteomyelitis-septic-arthritis.html).
- Alternatives to autograft. Allograft, demineralised matrix, synthetic substitutes and bone morphogenetic protein avoid the harvest and the donor pain; the choice is the surgeon's and depends on the defect and the biology. A case booked with a crest harvest sometimes proceeds without one, and the reverse.
- Reamer harvest in a small or osteoporotic femur. Higher risk of perforation and fracture; some surgeons add a prophylactic nail.
- The patient. Long-term opioids, smoking, depression and a year off work are the norm. Expect pain reports above the card's trajectory, plan with the pain service before the day, and agree a weaning plan before discharge.
- Compartment syndrome. Possible after long tibial or forearm reconstructions; keep limb blocks dilute where the surgeon is concerned, as the [compartment syndrome card](compartment-syndrome-fasciotomy.html) describes.
- Wrong-side harvest block. The crest used is the surgeon's choice on the day and may be opposite to the limb. Confirm before the block.

## Key sources

The harvest literature is better than the nonunion literature: a randomised trial and two meta-analyses compare crest and reamer harvest and agree on equal union and less donor pain with the reamer, and a large meta-analysis quantifies the reamer's blood loss. The analgesic trials for the donor site come from spinal, cleft and maxillofacial surgery, two of the four in children and young adults, and none from nonunion surgery. They point the same way without proving it: a plane block lowered early donor-site pain in both of its trials, repeated injections lowered pain scores at two time points in a trial of 22, and a single subcutaneous infiltration made no measurable difference. The NSAID restriction rests on observational data. The pain score, the trajectory and the two-column plan are the author's synthesis.
