## What the surgeon is doing and why

The hand is a set of small bones moved by long tendons that glide through tight tunnels, fed by two arteries per digit and supplied by nerves a millimetre or two across. Every structure sits within a few millimetres of every other, so an injury rarely damages one thing and a repair of one thing scars to its neighbours. That is the surgical problem in one sentence: the hand tolerates fracture and laceration well and tolerates stiffness badly. Everything the hand surgeon does is aimed at fixation or repair strong enough to let the fingers move within days, because a finger that is held still for four weeks heals solidly and never bends again.

Four operations make up the emergency hand list, and they are often combined in one patient.

1. Fixation of metacarpals and phalanges. Most hand fractures are treated in a splint. The ones that come to theatre are rotated (the finger crosses its neighbour when the patient makes a fist), shortened, intra-articular, open or multiple. The options run from closed reduction and percutaneous K-wires (quick, no dissection, wires out at four weeks, joints stiff in the meantime) through lag screws to a plate, which gives the stability for immediate movement at the price of stripping periosteum and putting metal under the extensor tendon.
2. Flexor tendon repair. A divided flexor retracts into the palm and will not heal unless its ends are brought together inside the fibrous sheath. The surgeon retrieves the proximal end, passes it under the pulleys and joins the ends with a multi-strand core suture and a running epitendinous stitch. The repair has to be strong enough for early active movement and slim enough to glide under the pulleys; too bulky and it triggers or sticks, too weak and it ruptures in the second week. Zone 2, between the distal palmar crease and the middle of the middle phalanx where two tendons share one sheath, is the unforgiving one.
3. Extensor tendon repair. Extensors are flat, superficial and easy to reach, so the repair is technically simpler; the difficulty is that they are thin, hold a suture poorly, and stick to the bone and skin on either side.
4. Nerve and vessel repair. A digital nerve is repaired under the microscope with 9-0 or 10-0 sutures and no tension; the patient gets protective sensation back over months. A digital artery is repaired the same way when the finger is pale or when both arteries are cut.

Replantation and revascularisation sit at the far end of the same spectrum. A replant is every one of the above in a fixed order: the bone is shortened and fixed so that everything else can be repaired without tension, then extensor, flexor, arteries, nerves and veins, with at least two veins for each artery where they can be found. Revascularisation is the same operation in a digit that is still attached by something. Survival depends on warm ischaemia time, the mechanism (a clean cut does well, a crush or avulsion does not) and the patency of anastomoses less than a millimetre across in the days afterwards. In one series of 157 digits, ischaemia time and length of surgery were the significant predictors.

The pain logic splits in two. For fixation, the mechanism is bone and periosteum in a hand with almost no soft tissue cover, plus the swelling of a dependent limb, and the 179-procedure cohort is a warning against dismissing it: small bony hand operations, with metacarpophalangeal arthrodesis among the worst of all, scored far higher on day 1 than their size suggested, largely because nobody planned for them. With a block and a regimen that is actually taken, a plated metacarpal behaves like a [distal radius fixation](distal-radius-orif.html): a 2, with a sharp first night. A tendon repair is a tendon under tension only in name; the tendon is small, the splint holds it slack, and the pain is the incision and the swelling. Replantation hurts less than its length suggests, because the replanted part is denervated. There the block is doing a different job, described in step 9.

## Steps

### 1. The emergency list: fasting, urgency and the choice of technique
**Why:** Most hand trauma is urgent rather than emergent. A closed fracture can wait a week, a tendon or nerve several days, and a clean open injury is washed out and closed in the emergency department on the day and repaired on a planned list. The true emergencies are the devascularised digit, the replant, the high-pressure injection injury and the infected flexor sheath or bite. The surgeon wants the short cases done in the day and the list is ordered by theatre availability, not by the clock on anyone's stomach.
**Anaesthesia:** Two fasting problems arrive on the same list and they are opposites. The first is the replant or the devascularised finger that came in an hour after lunch: a block as the sole anaesthetic, with no sedation, is the technique that makes the stomach nearly irrelevant, and it is the author's default; if a general anaesthetic is needed (a child, an eight-hour case, a patient who cannot lie still), it is a rapid sequence induction with a tube, not a wait of six hours while the ischaemia clock runs. A block does not abolish the fasting question, because a failed or outlasted block converts to general anaesthesia mid-case, and deep sedation on top of a full stomach is the worst of both options. The second problem is far more common: the fit 25-year-old with a metacarpal fracture who was told nil by mouth from midnight and reaches theatre at 5 pm, dehydrated, hypoglycaemic and angry. Clear fluids until called for is the fix, and it is an anaesthetic instruction, not a ward one. In a randomised comparison of a short-acting infraclavicular block against general anaesthesia for day-case hand and wrist surgery, 79% of the block patients bypassed the recovery unit against 25%, with less pain on arrival: on a list of eight short cases that difference is the list finishing.

### 2. Examination, documentation and the block
**Why:** The surgeon has documented which tendons work and which digital nerves feel before any local anaesthetic went in, because after the block nobody can tell. For a tendon repair some surgeons want the patient awake and able to move the finger on request.
**Anaesthesia:** Confirm that the nerve examination is written down before you place the block, and that nobody infiltrated a digit in the emergency department without recording sensation first. Choose the block by where the tourniquet goes and how long the case is. An axillary or infraclavicular block covers the hand and forearm reliably; the upper arm tourniquet pain that appears at 45-60 min is ischaemic and only partly a matter of the intercostobrachial nerve, so plan sedation or a forearm cuff for a long awake case. For a single digit distal to the palm a wrist or digital block is enough and leaves a working arm. If the surgeon wants active movement to test a repair, that is a WALANT case or a distal block with a forearm tourniquet deflated for the test, not a plexus block, which paralyses the muscles that move the tendon. Dexamethasone 8 mg IV goes in now.

### 3. Tourniquet, washout and exploration
**Why:** Hand surgery needs a bloodless field because the structures are small and blood stains everything the same colour. Open wounds are extended in zigzag (Bruner) incisions so that scars do not cross flexion creases and contract; contaminated wounds, bites and injection injuries are debrided widely first, and the definitive repair may be deferred.
**Anaesthesia:** Antibiotic in before the cuff goes up. Note the inflation time and say it aloud at 60 and 90 min. A fight bite over a metacarpophalangeal joint is a septic arthritis until washed out, and the patient may be febrile, intoxicated or both. High-pressure injection injuries look trivial at the fingertip and need a long decompression up the forearm: the booking understates the case. Under WALANT there is no tourniquet; the adrenaline needs about 25 min to give its best field, so the infiltration is done before the patient reaches theatre, and the ceiling is lidocaine 7 mg/kg with adrenaline.

### 4. Skeletal fixation
**Why:** Bone first, because every soft tissue repair is set to length against a stable skeleton. Closed reduction and K-wires for the fracture that reduces and holds; lag screws for a long spiral; a dorsal or lateral plate for a transverse or comminuted shaft, which means incising periosteum and placing metal under the extensor mechanism; shortening of the bone by several millimetres in a replant so that vessels and nerves meet without tension.
**Anaesthesia:** K-wiring is fast and repeatedly image-guided: lead on, hands out of the beam, and expect the case to finish before the block has fully set if you placed it late. Plating adds 30-45 min and is the bony, periosteal step that drives the first night's pain; this is the patient who needs the night plan most. Rotation is checked by flexing the fingers passively into the palm, which under a general anaesthetic needs a relaxed forearm, and under a block already has one.

### 5. Flexor tendon repair
**Why:** The proximal stump is retrieved from the palm, threaded beneath the pulleys and held with a needle while a four- or six-strand core suture and an epitendinous stitch are placed. The A2 and A4 pulleys are preserved or vented just enough to let the repair glide. The surgeon then pulls on the tendon or asks for active flexion to see that the repair passes the pulleys without gapping or catching.
**Anaesthesia:** A still hand for 45-90 min of fine work under loupes: a twitch as the block recedes or a cough under a light general anaesthetic matters here more than anywhere else on the list. If the case is under WALANT, the patient flexes and extends the finger on request and the surgeon revises the repair on the spot; the pooled comparative studies (five studies, 624 fingers) show no difference in rupture, tenolysis or reoperation against conventional anaesthesia and a modest advantage in range of motion, on weak data. The argument for it is the test, not the outcome figures. For the anaesthetist a WALANT flexor repair means no sedation at all, since a drowsy patient cannot cooperate, and a long time lying still on a narrow table.

### 6. Extensor tendon repair
**Why:** The flat extensor is repaired with mattress or running sutures, the joint may be pinned in extension for a mallet or central slip injury, and the wound is closed over it.
**Anaesthesia:** Short, superficial and well suited to a distal block or WALANT. The only trap is the dorsal laceration over a knuckle that turns out to be a tooth wound into the joint, which converts a 20-minute repair into a washout and an admission for intravenous antibiotics.

### 7. Digital nerve and artery repair
**Why:** The microscope comes in. Nerve ends are trimmed back to healthy fascicles and coapted without tension; a gap is bridged with a conduit or a graft. An artery is repaired if the digit is poorly perfused. The tourniquet is released to confirm flow.
**Anaesthesia:** The microscope shows the surgeon every movement magnified 10 to 20 times, including the patient's breathing transmitted down the arm and the table being bumped. Tell the room before you adjust anything. From the moment an artery is repaired the anaesthetic changes aim from analgesia to perfusion: warm patient, full circulation, no pain, no shivering, and blood pressure at the patient's normal.

### 8. Replantation: two teams, a long case and the set-up
**Why:** One team prepares the amputated part on a side table (tagging arteries, veins and nerves, debriding, shortening bone) while the other prepares the stump. The order of repair is fixed and each digit takes hours. The surgeon's priorities are the thumb, multiple digits, any level in a child, and a clean mechanism; a single crushed finger in a heavy smoker is usually not replanted.
**Anaesthesia:** Plan for 4-12 h from the start: a block with a catheter placed before the first incision, a general anaesthetic with a tube for most adults once the case is expected to exceed what anyone can lie still for, a urinary catheter, forced-air warming and warmed fluids from induction, a second cannula in a foot or the other arm, and pressure areas padded as for any all-day case. The theatre is warmed. The author's practice is an infraclavicular catheter, because it stays in place under a bulky dressing and an elevated arm better than an axillary one. The tourniquet is used for the dissection and comes down before the anastomosis; from then on blood loss is a slow, continuous ooze that is easy to underestimate over eight hours.

### 9. Microvascular anastomosis and reperfusion
**Why:** Arteries are sutured with eight or so stitches of 10-0 or 11-0 nylon, with a reversed vein graft if there is a gap; the clamps come off, the digit pinks up, and then two veins are repaired for each artery if they exist. Spasm is treated with warm saline, topical papaverine or lidocaine, and patience.
**Anaesthesia:** The folklore is that vasopressors kill replants. The evidence is thinner than the rule. No trial has tested vasopressors in digital replantation; in head and neck free flaps the pooled studies show no increase in flap failure with perioperative vasopressors. A digital artery is a smaller, spasm-prone vessel in a cold extremity, so the extrapolation is uncertain in both directions. The defensible position is to make the question rare rather than to answer it: keep the patient warm and full, run the block so that the limb is vasodilated and the anaesthetic can be light, treat hypotension first with volume and by lightening the anaesthetic, and when a vasopressor is still needed give the smallest effective dose by infusion and tell the surgeon. Hypotension is no kinder to a 1 mm anastomosis than phenylephrine. The surgeon may ask for heparin (commonly 2,500-5,000 units IV) before the clamps come off. The block is the one intervention with trial data: in a randomised trial of 71 replanted digits, a continuous ropivacaine 0.2% infusion at 5 mL/h gave warmer digits and higher arterial flow at every time point to 48 h, lower pain scores (0.5 against 1.9) and less vascular insufficiency at 48 h (8.3% against 29%) than systemic analgesia. Seven-day survival was 97% against 91%, which was not different, and an older retrospective series of 146 patients found no survival effect either. The catheter improves the conditions for survival; nobody has shown that it improves survival.

### 10. Closure, dressing and splint
**Why:** Skin is closed loosely, or left partly open or grafted over a replant because a tight closure strangles the veins. A bulky, non-constricting dressing and a splint hold the wrist and fingers in the position that protects the repair: flexed for a flexor tendon, extended for an extensor, with the fingertips visible.
**Anaesthesia:** The splint goes on before the patient is moving. For a replant, emergence is part of the operation: no coughing against the tube, no shivering, no pain and no retching, each of which is a sympathetic surge delivered straight to the anastomosis. Extubate deep or on a remifentanil tail if the airway allows, treat nausea in advance, and have the catheter bolused and running before the volatile is off.

### 11. Recovery, the first night and replant monitoring
**Why:** Day-case patients go home in a high arm sling with the hand above the heart. Tendon patients see the hand therapist within days for a thermoplastic splint and an early active movement protocol. A replant goes to a warm room for five to seven days of hourly colour, capillary refill, turgor and temperature checks; a pale digit is arterial and a blue, congested one is venous, and both go back to theatre or get leeches.
**Anaesthesia:** For the day-case patient the risks are the insensate arm and the night. The hand goes home in a sling the patient cannot take off in their sleep, with a warning about hot drinks, heaters and car doors. First doses of paracetamol and an NSAID are given before discharge and the written plan says to take the next ones at bedtime whether or not the hand is still numb. Half of ambulatory block patients describe rebound pain, with bone surgery, younger age and female sex raising the odds and intravenous dexamethasone lowering them; the young patient with a plated metacarpal has most of the risk factors. For the replant, the catheter runs for the monitoring period, the room stays warm, and the surgeon's bans on nicotine and caffeine are theirs to enforce. A catheter that is giving good analgesia also masks nothing important here, since the monitoring is by colour and temperature and not by pain.

## Pain plan

The mechanism for fixation is bone and periosteum under thin cover, short-lived and NSAID-responsive, with a rebound first night; for tendon and nerve repair it is the incision and the swelling. The plan is therefore a single-shot plexus block that doubles as the anaesthetic, intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg, paracetamol and an NSAID started before the block recedes and taken regularly for three to five days, strict elevation, and a small supply of oral opioid for the first two nights after a plate or a multi-digit injury. Closed K-wiring and soft tissue repairs often need no opioid at all. An NSAID is not withheld on bone-healing grounds for a course this short; where a surgeon objects, the objection is usually about a replant and bleeding rather than union.

Choice of local anaesthetic is a real decision on this list. A long-acting agent (ropivacaine 0.5%) with dexamethasone gives 12 to 18 hours and carries the patient through the evening, at the cost of a flail, insensate arm at home and a rebound in the small hours. A short-acting agent (lidocaine or mepivacaine with adrenaline) gives a working hand by the time the patient is home and puts the onset of pain in daylight, where the oral regimen can meet it, and it is what made the randomised day-case comparison work. The author's practice is short-acting for K-wires and soft tissue, long-acting with dexamethasone for plates and multiple digits, and to say which was used on the discharge sheet.

No catheter for any of the day-case operations: the pain does not last long enough to justify it, and this is author's judgement extrapolated from the [distal radius](distal-radius-orif.html) evidence rather than a hand-specific trial. The catheter belongs to replantation and revascularisation, where the indication is perfusion. Ropivacaine 0.2% at 5 mL/h for the monitoring period is the regimen with trial support. Systemic opioid for a replant is kept low to avoid nausea and retching.

Escalation: pain that rises steadily in a splinted hand, with tight swollen fingers, is the dressing until proven otherwise, and splitting it to skin is the first treatment. Severe pain on passive extension of the fingers after a crush, an injection injury or a revascularisation is compartment syndrome of the hand or forearm; a dense block delays the complaint, which is one more reason to use short-acting agents after crush injuries and to say so to the surgeon. Throbbing pain with fever at day 2 to 4 after a bite or a contaminated wound is infection.

## Pitfalls and variants

- WALANT. Surgeon-delivered, no tourniquet, no sedation, and often no anaesthetist. When you are asked to stand by, the job is monitoring and reassurance, and the lidocaine dose is the surgeon's infiltration plus anything you add. Adrenaline in the finger is safe in the doses used; phentolamine is the reversal agent if a digit stays white.
- The intoxicated or uncooperative patient. Hand trauma keeps company with alcohol, fights and self-harm. A block as the sole technique needs a patient who will lie still for an hour; if that is in doubt, the case waits for sobriety when it can and gets a general anaesthetic when it cannot.
- Tourniquet time in the multi-structure case. Bone, two tendons and a nerve in one finger runs past two hours. Agree a release at 120 min in advance (see [the tourniquet](../a/a01-tourniquet.html)).
- Crush, injection and compartment risk. Short-acting block or general anaesthesia with infiltration, and a surgeon who knows what was used.
- Replant failure modes. Arterial spasm in the first hours (cold, pain, hypovolaemia, hypotension), venous congestion in the first days. The return to theatre is an emergency with the catheter already in place: bolus it and the patient may not need a general anaesthetic.
- Anticoagulation after replantation. Aspirin, heparin, dextran and leeches are all used and surgeons vary. A patient on heparin with an indwelling catheter needs the catheter removal timed against the dose.
- Infection cases. A flexor sheath infection or a septic joint is a washout under tourniquet in a patient who may be bacteraemic; block or general anaesthesia both work, and an infected axilla or cellulitis tracking up the arm moves the block proximal or off the menu.

## Key sources

The hand trauma literature is surgical; the anaesthetic evidence is borrowed. The day-case case for a block rests on one small randomised trial from 2004 and two decades of practice. WALANT for flexor tendons has a meta-analysis of five mostly non-randomised studies. The replantation catheter has one recent randomised trial showing better perfusion and less vascular insufficiency without a survival difference, and an older retrospective series showing no survival effect. The vasopressor rule has no direct evidence in digits at all; the head and neck free flap data are cited to show how weak the dogma's foundations are, not to licence phenylephrine in a replant. The catheter and local anaesthetic choices for the day-case operations are the author's judgement.
