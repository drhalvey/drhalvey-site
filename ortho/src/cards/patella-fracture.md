## What the surgeon is doing and why

The patella is a sesamoid bone inside the extensor mechanism. It holds the quadriceps tendon away from the axis of the knee and so lengthens the lever arm of the quadriceps, and its thick articular cartilage takes several times body weight when a person rises from a chair. It breaks in two ways. A sudden quadriceps contraction against a flexing knee (a stumble) pulls it apart transversely and tears the retinaculum on either side, leaving a gap and a patient who cannot lift the straight leg. A direct blow (a fall onto the knee, a dashboard) shatters it into a star, often with the retinaculum intact and the fragments undisplaced, and often with the skin over it bruised or broken.

The surgeon operates when the extensor mechanism is disrupted or the joint surface is stepped. The goals are an articular surface that is smooth, an extensor mechanism that transmits force, and a fixation strong enough to let the knee move early, because a knee kept still becomes stiff and a patella stressed too early pulls apart. The classic solution uses the tension band principle. Two parallel wires or screws cross the fracture, and a wire or cable looped in a figure of eight over the front of the patella resists the distraction on the anterior surface. When the knee flexes, the patella is bent over the femur; the band on the front stops the front opening, so the force is converted into compression at the articular side. That is why the fixation gets stronger with flexion, and why it is tested by flexing the knee.

Variants follow the fracture. Cannulated screws with the wire or cable threaded through them give compression plus a tension band; in a comparative series they produced fewer implant problems and earlier walking than wires. Comminuted fractures are held with small plates, a mesh or a cerclage around the equator. An inferior pole fracture that is too small to hold a screw is excised and the patellar tendon is sutured into drill holes in the remaining patella (partial patellectomy), which makes the operation a tendon reattachment like those on the [quadriceps and patellar tendon card](quads-patellar-tendon-repair.html). A patella that cannot be reconstructed at all is removed and the tendon ends sewn together, at a permanent cost in extension strength. In every case the torn retinaculum is repaired, because it carries a share of the extensor force.

The long-term picture is modest: at a mean of 6.5 years after fixation, patients in a functional outcome series still had measurable pain, weakness and limits on sport, and implant irritation is frequent enough that a second operation to remove metalwork is common after wiring. Older patients do worse, with reoperation in about one in nine.

The pain logic is bone under thin skin, with a joint that has been open. Fixation removes the fracture motion; the splint in extension removes most of the movement-evoked pain in the first weeks. Worst pain is on days 1 and 2 and falls quickly. This is a 3. It becomes a 4 only when a tendon has been reattached to bone under tension.

## Steps

### 1. Assessment and timing
**Why:** The surgeon checks whether the patient can perform a straight leg raise (if not, the extensor mechanism is out), inspects the prepatellar skin, and reads the radiographs and often a CT for comminution and inferior pole involvement. A closed fracture is fixed within days once swelling allows; an open fracture is debrided urgently.
**Anaesthesia:** Two patient groups. The young patient with an isolated injury is a day case. The older patient who fell has the comorbidities and anticoagulants of the fragility fracture population and needs to learn to transfer in a rigid brace, so plans for one to three nights. If the mechanism was a dashboard, ask about the hip and the femur on that side; see [hip dislocation](hip-dislocation-femoral-head.html). Damaged or blistered prepatellar skin is the surgeon's reason for delay.

### 2. Anaesthetic and block
**Why:** The surgeon needs a relaxed quadriceps for the reduction and a motionless leg for 45-150 min.
**Anaesthesia:** Spinal or general anaesthesia both suit. A spinal relaxes the quadriceps completely, which helps the reduction, and is a good fit for the older patient, whose prophylactic heparin is timed to it (needle at least 12 h after a dose of enoxaparin, first postoperative dose at least 12 h after the needle, a direct oral anticoagulant no sooner than 6 h after the needle; see [venous thromboembolism prophylaxis](../a/a05-vte.html)); a general anaesthetic with a supraglottic airway suits the day case. Place an adductor canal or femoral triangle block before or after induction. The front of the knee is supplied by the saphenous nerve and its infrapatellar branch, the nerve to vastus medialis and the anterior femoral cutaneous nerves, so a block at the femoral triangle or proximal canal covers the incision and most of the bone while keeping most quadriceps power for the first walk. A retrospective series has reported the operation done under regional anaesthesia alone without anaesthetic complications; its abstract does not name the blocks. For the patient in whom both general and neuraxial techniques are unattractive, the block that can carry the operation is a femoral nerve block with sedation and the surgeon's infiltration, without a thigh tourniquet or with a brief one. An adductor canal block is an analgesic block: it does not anaesthetise the quadriceps tendon, the arthrotomy or the thigh under a cuff.

### 3. Position and tourniquet
**Why:** Supine with a buttock bump to stop the leg rolling outward. If a tourniquet is used the knee is flexed fully while the cuff is inflated, so that the quadriceps is drawn distally before it is pinned; a cuff inflated with the knee straight traps the muscle short and holds the proximal fragment out of reach.
**Anaesthesia:** Antibiotic complete before inflation. Inflating the cuff with the knee flexed is the surgeon's step, and you can prompt it. Under general anaesthesia alone expect tourniquet hypertension after 45-60 min; with a spinal or a femoral block it is blunted. See [the tourniquet](../a/a01-tourniquet.html).

### 4. Exposure and washout
**Why:** A longitudinal midline incision, full-thickness flaps, and the fracture opened like a book. Clot and small loose fragments are removed and the joint is irrigated; the retinacular tears on each side are identified and tagged.
**Anaesthesia:** Minor stimulation. The surgeon handles the skin flaps carefully because the blood supply of prepatellar skin is poor, and wound breakdown over metalwork is the complication that brings these patients back.

### 5. Reduction
**Why:** With the knee extended to slacken the quadriceps, the fragments are brought together with pointed reduction clamps. The anterior cortex is easy to see and misleading; the articular surface is what matters, and it is checked with a finger passed through the retinacular tear or with a lateral image.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the step that needs a slack quadriceps. Under general anaesthesia a small dose of neuromuscular blocker helps if the proximal fragment will not come down; under a spinal it is already slack. A proximal fragment that still will not reduce in a delayed case reflects a shortened muscle and occasionally the tourniquet.

### 6. Fixation
**Why:** Two parallel K-wires or cannulated screws are drilled across the fracture from one pole to the other, and a wire or cable is passed in a figure of eight around or through them and tightened on the anterior surface. Comminuted fractures get mini-fragment screws, a low-profile plate or mesh, or a cerclage. Wire ends are bent and buried; in the multicentre series, wires bent at one end only migrated far more often.
**Anaesthesia:** Sharp wires are passed through the tendons at each pole on large needles; this is a glove-puncture and needlestick step for the surgical team. Imaging in two planes follows. Tourniquet time is usually 45-60 min by now.

### 7. Inferior pole fixation, partial and total patellectomy (variants)
**Why:** A comminuted inferior pole is either captured with a small hook or basket plate or excised, with the patellar tendon sutured through longitudinal drill holes and tied over the superior pole. The repair is often protected by a cerclage from the patella to the tibial tubercle. Total patellectomy removes every fragment and repairs the mechanism in a tube.
**Anaesthesia:** A reattached tendon changes the pain mechanism to tendon under tension and the rehabilitation to that of a patellar tendon repair: the brace stays locked longer, flexion is restricted, and the trajectory is a point higher on days 1 to 3. Plan the block and the opioid accordingly.

### 8. Retinacular repair and the flexion test
**Why:** The medial and lateral retinaculum are sutured with the knee slightly flexed. The surgeon then flexes the knee slowly to 90 degrees while watching the fracture line; if it stays closed, the limit for early physiotherapy is set from what was seen.
**Anaesthesia:** Keep the patient deep and still for the test. The surgeon is watching for a gap of a millimetre, and a quadriceps contraction or a cough during flexion can open the fracture or cut a wire through osteoporotic bone. If the tourniquet is still up, the test is done with it inflated in some hands and deflated in others, since an inflated cuff tethers the quadriceps.

### 9. Closure and brace
**Why:** Layered closure over the metalwork, a compressive dressing and a hinged brace locked in extension. Most surgeons allow full weight-bearing in the brace at once, with flexion introduced in steps over six weeks.
**Anaesthesia:** Tourniquet release brings a brief fall in pressure and a rise in end-tidal carbon dioxide. The brace goes on before emergence, and the emergence should be smooth for the same reason as the flexion test. Surgeon's infiltration of the skin and retinaculum adds to the block; intra-articular local anaesthetic is avoided by many surgeons over an articular fracture.

### 10. Recovery and discharge
**Why:** The patient needs to walk in the brace, manage stairs on crutches and understand the flexion limit before leaving.
**Anaesthesia:** The trajectory on this card is 2 on the first evening with the block working, 5 on day 1 when it recedes, 4 on day 2, 3 on day 3 and 2 at a week. Before discharge the physiotherapist is told which block was used: a patient with any quadriceps weakness in a locked brace can still fall on a stair. Day-case criteria are a competent crutch walk, an oral regimen started in recovery and a written night plan.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is bone and bruised anterior soft tissue in the territory of the femoral nerve's terminal branches, in a leg that the surgeon wants walking in extension the same or the next day. The block that follows from that is one that covers the anterior knee and spares the quadriceps: an adductor canal or femoral triangle block with 15-20 mL of ropivacaine 0.5%, with dexamethasone 8 mg IV to extend it. There is no randomised trial of regional analgesia for patella fixation. The nearest trial evidence, for arthroscopic knee surgery, shows a modest benefit from an adductor canal block after minor arthroscopy (lower pain scores to 8 h and about 7 mg less oral morphine equivalent over 24 h) and none after ACL reconstruction, and the retrospective patella series associates regional anaesthesia with better early range of motion; the recommendation is the author's judgement from those and from the anatomy.

A femoral nerve block gives denser cover of the patella and is defensible because the leg is locked straight in a brace, but it removes the active quadriceps contraction the physiotherapist wants to see and raises the fall risk on the first stand. The author reserves it for the inpatient with a partial patellectomy. A catheter is rarely needed. No posterior knee block is needed, because nothing at the back of the knee has been touched.

Orally: regular paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor for one to two weeks where renal function and age allow, and an oral opioid for the first two to four nights. The older patient with a spinal and a block often needs very little opioid; the young day case with a comminuted fracture needs the night plan spelled out, because in the ambulatory cohort rebound pain affected half of block patients and was more likely with bone surgery and youth. Ice and elevation are effective for a subcutaneous bone.

Escalation triggers: increasing pain with a tense, warm knee in the first week is a haemarthrosis or infection, and the surgeon decides which; a sudden give with a palpable gap and loss of straight leg raise is a failed fixation; sharp, localised pain over a prominent wire end weeks later is implant irritation, which is the usual reason for removal of metalwork, covered on [that card](removal-of-metalwork.html).

## Pitfalls and variants

- Open fractures. The patella is subcutaneous, so a direct blow often breaks the skin. Urgent debridement, antibiotics on the open fracture schedule, and a higher infection risk over metalwork.
- Delayed presentation. After two to three weeks the quadriceps has shortened and the fragments are hard to approximate; more relaxation, sometimes a tendon lengthening, and a longer case.
- The older patient. More complications, less extension at six months and reoperation in about 11% in the comparative series. Osteoporotic bone holds wires poorly, so the flexion test and the emergence matter more.
- Fracture of a resurfaced patella after knee replacement. A different problem with poor bone stock and an implant; many are treated without surgery, and the operative ones belong with [revision knee arthroplasty](revision-tka.html).
- Tourniquet or not. Without one the case is 100-250 mL bloodier and the reduction is sometimes easier. With one, the cuff position and the knee flexed at inflation matter.
- Stiffness. Arthrofibrosis is the price of protecting the fixation; a manipulation under anaesthesia at 8-12 weeks is a recognised second procedure, described on the [manipulation and arthrolysis card](knee-mua-arthrolysis.html).
- Second operation. Removal of wires is frequent after tension band wiring (a quarter of patients in one series) and is a minor day case.
- Bilateral or combined injuries. A dashboard patella comes with posterior cruciate, femoral and hip injuries; the patella is the least urgent of them.

## Key sources

Patella fracture surgery is supported by retrospective series and one widely cited functional outcome study; the comparisons between wires and screws are small and non-randomised. The analgesic evidence specific to this operation is a single retrospective cohort. The block choice is an argument from anatomy and from knee surgery trials, and the pain score and trajectory are the author's calibration against the ACL reconstruction and extensor tendon repair cards.
