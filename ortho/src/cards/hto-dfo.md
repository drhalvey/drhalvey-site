## What the surgeon is doing and why

A knee that is bowed carries its load through one compartment. In a varus knee the line from the centre of the hip to the centre of the ankle passes medial to the knee, the medial cartilage takes most of the body weight, wears, and the wear increases the bow. The patient is typically 40 to 60, active, often a manual worker, with medial joint pain and a lateral compartment that is still healthy. A knee replacement would work and would wear out; a unicompartmental replacement restricts heavy use. An osteotomy keeps the patient's own joint and moves the load: the bone is cut just outside the joint and re-angled so that the weight-bearing line passes through the healthy compartment instead.

Where the cut goes depends on where the deformity is. Varus usually lives in the proximal tibia, so the standard operation is a high tibial osteotomy. Valgus usually lives in the distal femur, so the mirror-image operation is a distal femoral osteotomy. Large deformities get both.

There are two ways to change the angle of a bone:

```
 Opening wedge (medial tibia)        Closing wedge (lateral tibia)

    |        |                           |        |
    |  cut   |  lateral hinge            | wedge  |
  ==>--------o  stays intact             o--------<==  bone removed,
    | gap    |                           |        |    gap closed
    | opens  |                           |        |
   plate medial, gap may be grafted     plate or staples lateral,
                                        fibula must be released
```

The medial opening wedge is the dominant technique. Through an anteromedial incision the surgeon releases the distal superficial medial collateral ligament, passes two guide wires obliquely from the medial metaphysis towards the tip of the fibular head, and saws along them through the medial, anterior and posterior cortices, stopping about a centimetre short of the lateral cortex. That bridge of intact lateral bone is the hinge. A second, ascending cut behind the tibial tubercle leaves the patellar tendon attached to the distal fragment. The cut is then levered open slowly, over minutes, until a long rod or cable from the hip centre to the ankle centre crosses the knee at the planned point (classically 62% of the way across the plateau from the medial side), and an angle-stable plate is fixed across the gap. Gaps beyond about 10-12 mm are often filled with iliac crest autograft, allograft wedges or a synthetic substitute; smaller gaps are left to fill themselves.

The lateral closing wedge removes a measured wedge from the lateral tibia and closes the gap. It unites faster because bone meets bone, but it needs the anterior compartment muscles lifted off the tibia and the fibula released (an osteotomy of the fibula or a disruption of the proximal tibiofibular joint), which puts the common peroneal nerve and the anterior compartment at risk.

A distal femoral osteotomy is the same idea above the joint: a lateral opening wedge or a medial closing wedge of the supracondylar femur, through a subvastus approach with the vastus elevated off the septum, the femoral vessels a few centimetres away at the adductor hiatus, and a long plate.

The pain logic is the logic of a fracture that somebody then walks on. The cut crosses the full width of well-innervated metaphyseal bone; the periosteum is elevated front, medial and back to make room for the saw and the retractors; the opened gap stretches the medial soft tissues; and the hinge sometimes cracks. In 244 opening wedge osteotomies under a standard multimodal protocol, a lateral hinge fracture occurred in 95 and independently raised day 1 pain, by about half a point. Unlike the bone work of an arthroplasty, the pain generator has not been removed and the cut bone is loaded from the first days: modern locking plates allow partial weight-bearing early, and each step compresses and shears the osteotomy until it unites at 6 to 12 weeks. That is why this sits at a 4 with [total knee arthroplasty](primary-tka.html) rather than at a 3 with [ACL reconstruction](acl-reconstruction.html): a moderate bone peak, a rebound first night, and movement- and load-evoked pain for weeks. It does not reach a 5 because nothing is under tension and the trajectory falls steadily.

## Steps

### 1. Planning, the booking and the anaesthetic
**Why:** The surgeon has measured the correction on long-leg standing films and knows the opening in millimetres, whether a graft is planned and from where, and whether an arthroscopy, cartilage procedure or ligament reconstruction is being added.
**Anaesthesia:** The correction size is your pain predictor: a 6 mm opening without graft is at the bottom of this card's range, a 14 mm opening with iliac crest autograft or a double-level osteotomy at the top. Spinal anaesthesia suits a single osteotomy of 60-90 min and is what most of the trial populations had; general anaesthesia suits the combined or double-level case. Place the adductor canal block before either, at mid-thigh and clear of the cuff, and give dexamethasone 8 mg IV. Decide the posterior cover now: either the surgeon infiltrates the posterior periosteum and capsule under direct vision, or an IPACK block goes in with the adductor canal block. Document ankle and great toe dorsiflexion before any block, because the peroneal nerve is one of the structures this operation can injure. The patient is often booked as a day case on the strength of a small incision; the default should be one night.

### 2. Position, tourniquet and arthroscopy
**Why:** The whole limb must be visible on the image intensifier from the femoral head to the ankle so that alignment can be checked with a rod. Most surgeons begin with an arthroscopy to confirm that the lateral compartment is healthy enough to take the load and to deal with meniscal tears or loose bodies.
**Anaesthesia:** Lead for everyone, since the intensifier is used repeatedly from hip to ankle. Antibiotic before the cuff, tranexamic acid 1 g IV before incision. If the arthroscopy finds lateral compartment damage the operation may stop there, which is rare and worth knowing is possible. A tourniquet is used by most for the tibial osteotomy; a surgeon who works without one will want normotension and will lose a few hundred mL in the field.

### 3. Approach and medial release
**Why:** An oblique anteromedial incision over the pes anserinus. The hamstring tendons are retracted or released, and the distal fibres of the superficial medial collateral ligament are elevated off the tibia so that the medial side can open; without that release the opening wedge would compress the medial compartment it is meant to unload. A blunt retractor is passed behind the tibia, directly on bone, to protect the popliteal vessels.
**Anaesthesia:** The infrapatellar branch of the saphenous nerve crosses this incision and is often cut: a patch of numbness lateral to the scar is expected and is not a block injury. The subperiosteal elevation here and around the back of the tibia is the periosteal component of the pain and the target for the surgeon's infiltration.

### 4. Guide wires and the hinge
**Why:** Two parallel wires are driven from the medial cortex towards the tip of the fibular head under fluoroscopy. They define the plane of the cut and its end point, which must sit in the safe zone at the level of the proximal tibiofibular joint, where the surrounding soft tissue supports the lateral cortex.
**Anaesthesia:** Dead time with repeated screening. A hinge point that is too low or too medial is what later cracks into the lateral cortex or the plateau, lengthening the case and worsening the first day.

### 5. The cuts
**Why:** An oscillating saw and then osteotomes complete the transverse cut through the posterior and medial cortices up to the hinge, with the knee flexed to 90 degrees so that the popliteal artery falls back from the posterior cortex, and the ascending anterior cut frees the tubercle fragment.
**Anaesthesia:** The posterior cortex is cut within a centimetre of the popliteal artery and the tibial nerve, with only a retractor between. Arterial injury is rare, is silent under tourniquet, and declares itself at deflation as a wound that fills, a pale foot, or days later as a pulsatile swelling behind the knee. Under spinal the patient feels vibration and hears the saw: sedation or headphones. This is the peak of intra-operative nociception under general anaesthesia if the block has not set.

### 6. Opening the wedge and checking alignment
**Why:** Stacked osteotomes or a spreader open the cut gradually, over several minutes, so that the lateral hinge deforms plastically and does not snap. The knee is brought into full extension, the alignment rod is laid from hip centre to ankle centre and screened at the knee, and the opening is adjusted until the line crosses the target point. The posterior gap is kept larger than the anterior so that the tibial slope is not increased.
**Anaesthesia:** The surgeon needs the leg fully extended and still for the alignment check, with an assistant applying axial load at the heel; a patient moving under light sedation at this point costs accuracy. A crack that the room hears is the hinge. The surgeon will add a lateral screw or staple, or accept it and slow the weight-bearing, and you should move the patient up one notch on the pain plan, since a hinge fracture is an independent predictor of a worse first day.

### 7. Plate fixation
**Why:** A long medial angle-stable plate bridges the gap, with locking screws in the proximal fragment under the plateau and in the shaft. The construct is a splint that has to carry body weight across an open gap for two to three months.
**Anaesthesia:** Drilling and screw insertion; nothing physiological. The plate sits subcutaneously on the medial tibia and is a common source of late local pain, which is why many are removed after union (see [removal of metalwork](removal-of-metalwork.html)).

### 8. Filling the gap
**Why:** Small openings are left empty. Larger ones are packed with cancellous or tricortical autograft from the iliac crest, allograft wedges, or a ceramic substitute, to speed union and support the plate.
**Anaesthesia:** An iliac crest harvest is a second operation with its own periosteal pain, often the worse of the two sites on day 1 and outside any knee block. Ask the surgeon to infiltrate the crest and the periosteum generously before closing it, and count that volume against the total local anaesthetic dose with the adductor canal block, the IPACK and the knee infiltration.

### 9. Tourniquet down, haemostasis and closure
**Why:** The cuff is released before closure by surgeons who want to see the bleeding from the cut surfaces and the vessels behind the tibia; others close under tourniquet with a drain. The hamstring layer is closed over the plate and the leg goes into a hinged brace or a simple dressing by surgeon preference.
**Anaesthesia:** At deflation look at the foot with the surgeon: colour, capillary refill and a pulse. Blood from the osteotomy now tracks into the calf, which is the mechanism of the swelling, the bruising to the ankle and the occasional compartment problem. Give the antiemetic. If an adductor canal catheter is planned and the entry point was too close to the field to place earlier, it goes in now.

### 10. Recovery, the first night and weight-bearing
**Why:** The surgeon wants early knee movement, an active quadriceps, and protected weight-bearing on crutches that progresses to full weight over two to six weeks depending on the plate, the gap and the hinge.
**Anaesthesia:** Three checks before the spinal or block obscures them further and again as it recedes: foot perfusion, ankle and great toe dorsiflexion, and a soft anterior compartment. An adductor canal block leaves the deep peroneal and tibial territories intact, so pain on passive toe flexion and a tense shin still mean what they should. The first oral paracetamol and NSAID go in before the spinal recedes, the rescue opioid is charted with a dose that reflects a 4 and not a knee arthroscopy, and the patient is told that the first steps on crutches will hurt at the cut and that this is the bone being loaded, not the plate failing. Rebound pain is the expected course after a single-shot block for bone surgery in a young patient, so the night plan is written down.

## Pain plan

The mechanism is a loaded bone cut with a circumferential periosteal elevation, so the block has to cover the front and the back of the proximal tibia for the first night and the oral regimen has to carry the patient for weeks. The innervation is split: the anteromedial tibia, the incision and the medial periosteum are saphenous and femoral territory; the posterior cortex and capsule are tibial and sciatic territory. The four small trials line up with that anatomy. An adductor canal block against placebo cut early opioid use sharply (17% against 70% needing opioid in the first 12 h) without weakening the quadriceps. A femoral block and a sciatic block, compared head to head under general anaesthesia with full multimodal analgesia, gave identical morphine use at 24 h (24 mg in each group), which reads as each covering half the operation. And an IPACK block gave lower pain for the first 10 h and longer analgesia than an adductor canal block alone, which says the posterior half matters.

The defensible default follows: spinal or general anaesthesia; a single-shot adductor canal block with ropivacaine 0.5% 15-20 mL; posterior cover by surgeon infiltration of the posterior periosteum and capsule or by an IPACK block; intravenous dexamethasone 8 mg; regular paracetamol and an NSAID or COX-2 inhibitor; and an oral opioid for the first three to five days, with a modified-release component for the first two nights in most patients. On NSAIDs and bone healing, surgeons vary: the worry is non-union of an opening wedge, the human evidence for harm from a short course is weak, and the author's practice is a course limited to the first one to two weeks unless the surgeon objects.

Why not the sciatic block, which would cover the posterior tibia better than either alternative. Because the structures at risk in this operation are in its territory: the anterior compartment, which takes the bleeding from the osteotomy and is directly dissected in a closing wedge, and the common peroneal nerve at the fibular neck. A dense sciatic block makes a foot drop uninterpretable and removes the pain that announces a compartment syndrome. The adductor canal and IPACK combination covers most of the pain and leaves both examinable. This is the author's judgement; the one trial that used a sciatic block reported no harm in 25 patients, which is not a safety signal either way.

The catheter decision: an adductor canal catheter for 48 h is reasonable where the patient or the operation predicts a hard course (opioid tolerance, double-level or bilateral surgery, a large opening with crest harvest, a hinge fracture in a patient who is already struggling), and unnecessary for a standard opening wedge with good posterior cover. There is no trial of continuous techniques in osteotomy.

Escalation: pain above 7 on day 1 with a soft shin and normal toe movement is treated with the rescue opioid and a check that the regular drugs were given; a rescue adductor canal block the next morning is harmless to the quadriceps. Pain out of proportion with a tense anterior compartment, or pain on passive plantarflexion of the toes, is compartment syndrome until proven otherwise (see [compartment syndrome](compartment-syndrome-fasciotomy.html)). A sudden increase in pain on weight-bearing in the first weeks, with a new deformity or a click, is loss of fixation through a hinge fracture.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Lateral closing wedge tibial osteotomy. Anterior compartment muscles elevated, the fibula released, the peroneal nerve close to both: more compartment and nerve risk, more muscle pain, faster union. Keep the sciatic territory examinable and say so in the notes.
- Distal femoral osteotomy. The cut is in the supracondylar femur, above the reach of a distal adductor canal block: use a femoral triangle or femoral nerve block and accept some quadriceps weakness for the first day. The vastus is elevated and aches. The femoral vessels lie posteromedial to the cut. Blood loss is higher than for the tibia and a standard tourniquet does not fit.
- Double-level osteotomy. Femur and tibia in one sitting for a large deformity: two to three hours, both pain territories, more bleeding, and the clearest catheter indication on this card.
- Combined procedures. Osteotomy with ACL reconstruction, cartilage repair or meniscal transplant is common in the younger patient; the pain course is set by the osteotomy and the duration by the rest.
- Iliac crest autograft. A second pain site that no knee block reaches; infiltration at the crest is the only cover.
- Hinge fracture. Common in opening wedges, usually stable, sometimes extending into the plateau. It lengthens the case, restricts weight-bearing and raises day 1 pain modestly.
- The smoker and the patient with a high BMI. Both delay union of an opening wedge, and both are over-represented in the population offered this operation. The NSAID conversation with the surgeon matters more for them.
- Day-case pathways. Feasible in established units with a spinal, blocks, infiltration and a written night plan. The failure mode is the rebound at 2 am in a young patient with a cut tibia, and a pathway without a reliable overnight contact is not ready.
- Popliteal artery injury. Rare, and silent under tourniquet. A foot that is pale at deflation is treated as an arterial injury on the table, and a pulsatile popliteal swelling in the first weeks is a pseudoaneurysm.
- Thromboembolism. A long bone cut and reduced weight-bearing: calf swelling is common from the haematoma alone, which makes a thrombosis easy to miss.

## Key sources

There is no PROSPECT guideline for osteotomy and the analgesic evidence is four small single-centre trials: one of adductor canal block against placebo, one of femoral against sciatic block, one of IPACK against adductor canal block, and a retrospective series on hinge fractures and pain. They agree with each other and with the anatomy, which is the main reason to trust them. The tranexamic acid meta-analysis is small but consistent with the arthroplasty evidence. The compartment and peroneal nerve cautions are consensus from the surgical literature, without reliable incidence figures, and the advice against sciatic block is the author's judgement.
