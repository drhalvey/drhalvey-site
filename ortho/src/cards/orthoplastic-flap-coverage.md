## What the surgeon is doing and why

Bone will not heal, and metalwork will not stay sterile, under an open wound. After a high-energy open fracture, most often of the tibia, where the bone lies under skin with no muscle in front of it, the orthopaedic surgeon can debride and stabilise but cannot close. The plastic surgeon's task is to bring living, vascularised tissue over the bone and implant, and the two teams plan it together from the first debridement. That joint plan has a clock. In the original series, free flaps done within 72 h of injury failed in fewer than 1% and became infected in 1.5%, against 12% and 17.5% when cover was delayed to between 72 h and three months. The national standard asks for definitive cover within 72 h and for internal fixation only when cover can follow immediately, and a recent meta-analysis found about a quarter of the osteomyelitis risk with cover inside that window. Many patients miss the window for reasons of physiology or logistics, and those are the harder cases: a colonised wound, friable vessels and a patient who has had four anaesthetics in ten days.

The method is chosen by climbing a ladder until the defect is solved. A clean bed of muscle or granulation takes a split skin graft: a thin sheet shaved from the thigh with a dermatome, meshed and laid on. Exposed bone without periosteum, tendon without paratenon, or a plate will not take a graft and needs a flap, tissue that brings its own blood supply. In the upper third of the leg the medial head of gastrocnemius is detached distally and swung over the knee or proximal tibia; in the middle third, soleus. In the distal third and the foot there is no local muscle to spare, so the choice is a fasciocutaneous flap pivoted on a perforator or a free flap. For free tissue transfer the surgeon raises a block of tissue on a single artery and vein (skin and fascia from the anterolateral thigh, the gracilis muscle, or latissimus dorsi for the largest defects), divides the pedicle, moves the tissue to the leg and joins its vessels to the tibial vessels under the microscope, outside the zone of injury where the vessel wall is healthy. A skin graft then covers the muscle and often the donor site.

For those hours the flap depends on what you deliver to a pair of anastomoses 1.5-3 mm across. Flow varies with the pressure gradient and with the fourth power of the radius, and inversely with viscosity. The transferred tissue is denervated and cannot regulate its own vessels, has no lymphatic drainage and so swells with any excess fluid, and its feeding artery in the leg is fully responsive to cold, pain, hypovolaemia and catecholamines. Each item on the anaesthetic list follows from one of those facts.

The pain logic: the flap itself has no sensation, and the recipient wound is no worse than after the previous debridement. The new pain is at the donor sites, a muscle mechanism for a harvested muscle or thigh flap and exposed dermis for the skin graft donor, which patients consistently rate as the sorest part. That is a 3, worst on day 1 and down to a 2 at a week, when what remains is the skin graft donor site at dressing changes, in a patient who may be opioid-tolerant from the preceding fortnight.

## Steps

### 1. The joint plan and the patient
**Why:** Orthopaedic and plastic surgeons agree the fixation, the flap, the recipient vessels (often after CT angiography of an injured leg) and the day. Fixation and flap in one sitting is the ideal; otherwise the fixation is timed so that cover follows within days.
**Anaesthesia:** This patient has usually had several anaesthetics since the injury, as on the [open fracture card](open-fractures-damage-control.html): check what was easy and what was not. Expect anaemia, a catabolic state, opioid use for one to two weeks, and prophylactic heparin. Smoking matters to the surgeon more than to you; nicotine is a vasoconstrictor and they will have asked the patient to stop. Find out the antithrombotic plan for the flap before choosing a regional technique. Correct the haemoglobin to a level that gives a haematocrit near 30%: lower viscosity improves flow, and much lower compromises oxygen delivery.

### 2. Induction, lines and setting up for a long case
**Why:** The surgeon needs two fields (recipient and donor) available at once, sometimes with two teams, for most of a working day.
**Anaesthesia:** General anaesthesia with a tracheal tube. Arterial line, two wide cannulae placed away from both fields and from any limb that may be used as a donor, urinary catheter, core temperature probe, and a means of assessing fluid responsiveness if you have one. Central access is seldom needed; a dilute phenylephrine infusion, the default vasopressor on this card (step 8), can run peripherally under local policy. Pad every pressure point as for a ten-hour case, protect the eyes, apply calf compression to uninvolved legs, and warm from before induction: pre-warm the patient, heat the room, warm all fluids and use forced air wherever skin is available, which is very little once a leg, a thigh and a back are prepared. In the cohort of 668 free flaps, intra-operative hypothermia was associated with twice the rate of total flap loss. Maintenance by volatile or propofol infusion is a matter of preference; avoid nitrous oxide in a case of this length, for the bowel distension, the nausea and the bone marrow effects of prolonged exposure. A remifentanil infusion gives a still patient under the microscope without residual paralysis.

### 3. Regional technique
**Why:** The surgeon wants dilated recipient vessels, no spasm and a patient who does not move or shiver afterwards.
**Anaesthesia:** A popliteal sciatic catheter with a saphenous block, placed before incision, gives analgesia for the recipient site and a sympathetic block of the leg's own vessels, which is where vasospasm occurs, since the flap's vessels are denervated. In the randomised trial of lower limb free flaps, adding an epidural raised flap arterial flow velocity by half and reduced early pain and opioid use. An epidural covers thigh donor sites as well but brings hypotension to be treated and a catheter governed by the intervals in [venous thromboembolism prophylaxis](../a/a05-vte.html): it coexists only with once-daily prophylactic enoxaparin, goes in and comes out at least 12 h after a dose, and the next dose waits 4 h after removal (the first postoperative dose 12 h after the needle). Aspirin alone places no restriction, but aspirin with heparin raises the risk and the catheter comes out under the heparin rule. A direct oral anticoagulant is not given while the catheter is in and starts no sooner than 6 h after removal. A surgeon who plans an intravenous heparin bolus at clamp release, a heparin infusion or therapeutic dosing for the flap has ruled the epidural out, and the popliteal catheter, a superficial and compressible site, is the technique. By this stage the fracture is stabilised and the early compartment syndrome window has usually closed, but nobody should be relying on the limb's sensation when the block goes in, and nerve function is documented first.

### 4. Final debridement and fixation
**Why:** The wound is excised again back to healthy, bleeding tissue; temporary external fixation is exchanged for a nail or plate if that is the plan, as on the [tibial card](tibial-nailing-plateau.html). Cover must go over a clean, stable bed.
**Anaesthesia:** This is the bloody phase and the one with a tourniquet. Replace losses as they occur with blood products, not crystalloid. If nailing with reaming is part of the case, expect the embolic load of any nailing. Re-dose cefazolin every 4 h from here on.

### 5. Recipient vessel dissection
**Why:** The surgeon exposes the anterior or posterior tibial vessels proximal to the zone of injury, where the wall is soft and the vessel pulses normally, and prepares an artery and one or two veins, often choosing an end-to-side arterial anastomosis so the foot keeps its inflow.
**Anaesthesia:** Under tourniquet for part of this. On release, watch the surgeon's view: a recipient artery in spasm is treated with topical papaverine or lidocaine by the surgeon and with warmth, analgesia and volume by you. From this point on, treat blood pressure as part of the operative field and keep it at the patient's normal.

### 6. Raising the flap
**Why:** The second team, or the same team later, dissects the flap on its pedicle: the descending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery through vastus lateralis for an anterolateral thigh flap, the gracilis pedicle in the medial thigh, or the thoracodorsal vessels for latissimus dorsi. The flap stays attached and perfused until the recipient vessels are ready.
**Anaesthesia:** A latissimus dorsi harvest needs the patient lateral or tilted, with a turn back afterwards: lines, tube and the regional catheter are all at risk in the turn. The thigh donor sites are in femoral and lateral femoral cutaneous territory. Perforator dissection is slow and needs a motionless field. Keep the patient warm and filled; a vasoconstricted patient gives the surgeon small, spastic perforators that are harder to dissect.

### 7. Pedicle division and the anastomosis
**Why:** The pedicle is clipped and cut, the flap is carried to the leg, and under the microscope the vein and artery are joined with interrupted 9-0 or 10-0 sutures or a venous coupler. The flap is ischaemic from division until the clamps come off, typically 45-90 min; muscle tolerates this less well than skin.
**Anaesthesia:** Note the time of pedicle division. Complete stillness: confirm depth and analgesia before the microscope comes in, since a cough moves the field out of focus and can tear a half-finished anastomosis. Some surgeons ask for an intravenous heparin bolus before the clamps are released; give exactly what is asked and record it. Before clamp release have the patient warm, normovolaemic and at baseline blood pressure, because the surgeon judges the anastomosis by the flow in the first minutes.

### 8. Reperfusion and the haemodynamic targets
**Why:** The clamps come off, the flap pinks, its edges bleed, and the surgeon watches the vein fill and listens with a Doppler probe. The first hour is when arterial thrombosis and venous kinking declare themselves.
**Anaesthesia:** The targets, which are the author's working rules built on the physiology and the meta-analyses: mean arterial pressure within about 10% of baseline and systolic above 100 mmHg; core temperature 36.5 degrees C or above; urine output about 0.5-1 mL/kg/h; haematocrit around 30%; normocapnia. Reach the pressure with volume if the patient is fluid-responsive and with a vasopressor if not. The old teaching that vasopressors are forbidden is not supported: across 8,653 flaps their use was associated with no more, and possibly fewer, flap failures, although flap complications were higher with vasopressors in the breast reconstruction subgroup. A later meta-analysis of 8,626 flaps compared agents: flap failure was 6% with noradrenaline against 2% with phenylephrine and 2% with ephedrine, with more revisions and thrombosis with noradrenaline, from mostly observational data with high heterogeneity in which the sicker patient is more likely to have been given noradrenaline. The defensible default that follows is a phenylephrine infusion titrated to the pressure target, with ephedrine boluses when the heart rate is slow, and noradrenaline kept for the patient who needs it for another reason. What does harm the flap is the alternative, several extra litres of crystalloid: the flap has no lymphatics and swells, and in the pooled fluid studies conventional liberal regimens produced more threatened flaps and more reoperations than goal-directed therapy. A rough ceiling of 3-5 mL/kg/h of maintenance crystalloid with losses replaced separately is a reasonable frame.

### 9. Inset, skin graft and dressings
**Why:** The flap is tailored and sutured into the defect without tension on the pedicle; a split skin graft is taken from the thigh for a muscle flap and for any donor site that will not close; drains are placed and a window is left in the dressing to watch the flap.
**Anaesthesia:** The skin graft donor is the pain generator of the next week. Ask for it to be taken within the area a lateral femoral cutaneous or fascia iliaca block will cover, do that block before emergence if no epidural is running, and ask the surgeon to apply local anaesthetic to the donor site under the dressing. No circumferential dressing or tight tape across the pedicle; check that nothing you have placed (a blood pressure cuff, a line strap) compresses the limb.

### 10. Emergence
**Why:** The surgeon wants a calm, warm, comfortable patient who does not cough, strain, shiver or vomit.
**Anaesthesia:** Each of those raises venous pressure or catecholamines at the moment the anastomosis is least robust. Reverse and extubate smoothly: remifentanil continued to extubation, analgesia established through the catheter before waking, antiemetics given, and the patient at 36.5 degrees C or more before the volatile is turned off, since shivering multiplies oxygen consumption and vasoconstricts. Transfer on oxygen, with the limb elevated on pillows and free of pressure.

### 11. The first 48 hours and the take-back
**Why:** Nurses check the flap hourly for colour, capillary refill, turgor, temperature and Doppler signal. A pale, cool flap with no signal is an arterial problem; a blue, swollen flap with brisk refill is venous. Either goes back to theatre at once, because salvage depends on time.
**Anaesthesia:** Continue the intra-operative targets on the ward: a warm room, the pressure and urine output goals written down, analgesia that prevents vasoconstriction, and a fluid prescription that is neither dry nor generous. The trajectory on this card is 3 on the first evening, 5 on day 1, 4 on day 2, 3 on day 3 and 2 at a week. A take-back is an emergency anaesthetic in a patient whose stomach empties slowly on opioids and may hold a recent meal, on heparin, with a regional catheter in place: keep it simple, keep the pressure up, and do not delay for fasting.

## Pain plan

Analgesia here does two jobs: comfort, and removal of the sympathetic drive that narrows the recipient artery. That double purpose is why a continuous regional technique on the recipient limb is the author's default for a free flap in the leg, with the randomised epidural trial as the supporting evidence for an effect on flap flow and no trial showing an effect on flap survival. A popliteal sciatic catheter (ropivacaine 0.2% at 5-8 mL/h) with a single-shot saphenous block covers the leg and foot; it does not cover a thigh donor site, which needs a fascia iliaca or lateral femoral cutaneous block. A lumbar epidural covers both when donor and recipient are in the lower limbs and is the stronger choice physiologically, at the cost of hypotension that must be treated promptly with a vasopressor and of a deep catheter in a patient who may receive heparin and aspirin. Decide between them on the surgeon's antithrombotic protocol.

Around the block: regular paracetamol; an NSAID only with the surgeon's agreement, since some avoid them for bleeding under the flap and for fracture healing; an opioid by patient-controlled analgesia for 24-48 h, with the pre-operative dose continued in the opioid-tolerant patient; and low-dose ketamine for that group. For a skin graft alone or a local muscle flap, a single-shot block of the recipient limb plus a lateral femoral cutaneous nerve block for the donor thigh and simple oral analgesia is enough.

The skin graft donor site deserves its own line on the plan. It hurts more than the grafted wound, burns for the first two or three days, is provoked again at the first dressing change, and responds to local measures: local anaesthetic applied under an adherent dressing, a block of the lateral thigh, and leaving the dressing undisturbed. Rebound is moderate. Escalation triggers: rising pain in the recipient limb with a tight, swollen flap is a haematoma or venous congestion and goes to the surgeon, not to the opioid; pain in a donor thigh with a tense anterior compartment after a large anterolateral thigh harvest closed under tension is a compartment problem; calf pain in the other leg after ten hours on the table is a positioning injury or a thrombosis.

## Pitfalls and variants

- Skin graft only. A 30-60 minute operation under any anaesthetic, with a negative pressure dressing or a tie-over. Score 2; the donor site is the pain.
- Gastrocnemius and soleus flaps. Supine or prone, under tourniquet, 90-180 min, no microsurgery. The muscle is taken from the calf of the injured leg, so the popliteal sciatic block covers donor and recipient together.
- Propeller and other perforator flaps. No anastomosis, but a single perforator twisted through up to 180 degrees is as sensitive to pressure, cold and swelling as a free flap; apply the same targets.
- Late reconstruction. As the days pass the wound is colonised and the vessels become fibrotic. The original series drew its line at 72 h: for flaps done between 72 h and three months, failure was 12% against 0.75% and infection 17.5% against 1.5%. Longer, bloodier and more likely to return.
- The vasopressor argument in theatre. Have the conversation before the case. The evidence supports treating hypotension with a vasopressor; some surgeons still prefer one agent over another, and the 2026 meta-analysis gives them some grounds for preferring phenylephrine or ephedrine to noradrenaline, which is why phenylephrine is the default here.
- Dextran and other rheological agents. Largely abandoned; anaphylaxis and pulmonary oedema outweighed any benefit.
- Upper limb and hand defects. The same principles with a brachial plexus catheter, which gives an excellent sympathetic block; see [hand trauma](hand-trauma.html).
- The failing limb. When cover fails or the limb is not salvageable the conversation turns to [amputation](amputation.html), and the patient has by then had weeks of opioids.
- Positioning injury. Ten hours supine or lateral with two teams leaning on the patient: check heels, sacrum, occiput, ulnar nerves and the eyes, and move what can be moved every two hours.

## Key sources

The timing evidence is a landmark retrospective series, a national standard built on it and a meta-analysis of observational comparisons. The vasopressor evidence is three meta-analyses of mostly retrospective data, largely from head and neck and breast reconstruction, which is applied here to the limb by extension. The effect of an epidural on flap flow comes from one randomised trial in lower limb reconstruction, with a surrogate outcome. Fluid and temperature evidence is observational or from small trials in other regions. The numerical targets in step 8 are the author's working rules and are not trial-derived; the pain score and trajectory are the author's calibration, with the donor site as the main driver.
