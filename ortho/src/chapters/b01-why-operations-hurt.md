## The claim

Pain after an orthopaedic operation is not a property of the operation's name. It is a property of what was done to which tissue, what that tissue is now doing, and who it was done to. A surgeon who says "this is the least painful operation I do" is reporting the first of those four, filtered through the patients who complained to him. A registrar who says "cuff repairs are agony" is reporting the same thing from the other end of the corridor. Both are right about the days they saw and wrong about the days they did not.

The model in this chapter has four questions. Ask them in order and the block, the catheter decision and the oral regimen follow.

```
  1. TISSUE      what was cut, reamed, stripped or detached
                 (bone, tendon, capsule, muscle, periosteum, nerve, skin and portals)
                        |
  2. TENSION     what was left pulled tight, lengthened or re-tensioned
                 (a repaired tendon, a lengthened arm, a balanced knee, a corrected osteotomy)
                        |
  3. LOAD        what will be loaded in the days after, and how soon
                 (weight-bearing, hand use, physiotherapy, rolling over in bed)
                        |
  4. PATIENT     who it was done to
                 (age, sex, pre-operative pain, opioid exposure, catastrophising, what they expect)
                        |
                        v
            pain SCORE, pain SHAPE over day 0-7, and the PLAN that fits the shape
```

## Tissue: what each kind of cut feels like

Nociceptors are not evenly distributed. Periosteum is dense with them; cancellous bone marrow is sensitive to pressure and inflammation; tendon and ligament carry mechanoreceptors that fire with stretch; synovium and capsule are richly innervated; muscle belly is comparatively quiet unless it is torn or ischaemic; cartilage has no nerves at all, which is why a bone-on-bone joint hurts and a cartilage-covered one does not. The surgeon's choice of what to cut therefore sets the baseline.

| Mechanism tag | What the surgeon did | How it hurts | How long |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| bone | osteotomy, reaming, broaching, drilling for anchors, decorticating a footprint, cement interface | hard, deep, inflammatory; responds to NSAIDs; worse with the first loading | peaks day 1-2, falls fast once nothing is loaded through it |
| tendon-tension | a tendon or ligament repaired or reattached under tension, or a graft fixed in a tunnel | sharp with every movement that loads it; night pain because the patient cannot find a position; aching between | plateau for one to two weeks, then slow decline over the healing period |
| retension | the joint re-tensioned or the limb lengthened: reverse shoulder, knee balancing, osteotomy correction, leg length change at the hip | deep ache and stiffness pain, worse with range-of-motion exercises, a feeling of the limb being "too tight" | weeks, improving as the soft tissues creep |
| capsule | capsular release, labral repair, synovectomy, joint distension | sharp with movement in the released direction, settles once the joint is moved regularly | days |
| muscle | a muscle split, released, lengthened or widely dissected | aching, position-dependent, worse on contraction | days to a week |
| periosteum | subperiosteal stripping or plating along a long bone | raw and incisional along the whole length of the plate, worse than the fracture it fixed | days, then the bone takes over |
| nerve | nerve handling, traction, decompression or transposition | burning, dysaesthetic, in the territory rather than the wound | unpredictable; may be the longest |
| tourniquet | ischaemia and reperfusion plus cuff-site pressure | dull thigh or arm ache after a long tourniquet, separate from the wound | 24-48 h |
| surface | arthroscopic portals and irrigation only | minimal; swelling more than pain | hours |
| visceral | a retroperitoneal or anterior approach to the spine or pelvis | abdominal wall and visceral, ileus, referred pain | days |

Two of these tags do most of the work in the atlas. Bone explains why an [anatomic shoulder replacement](../c/anatomic-tsa.html), a [distal radius plate](../c/distal-radius-orif.html) and a [primary hip](../c/tha-posterior.html) hurt hard for two days and then stop. Tendon-tension explains why a [rotator cuff repair](../c/rotator-cuff-repair.html), a [distal biceps repair](../c/distal-biceps-ulnar-nerve.html) and an [MPFL reconstruction](../c/patellofemoral-mpfl-tto.html) hurt for two weeks, at night, every time the patient moves.

## Tension: what was left pulled tight

The surgeon's second decision is what the tissues are left doing. A replaced humeral head sized to the one that was removed leaves the cuff and capsule at their original length: nothing pulls, so after the bone settles there is nothing left to hurt. A reverse shoulder deliberately lengthens the arm and tensions the deltoid so that it can lift the arm without a cuff; the deltoid and the capsule spend weeks creeping to the new length and the patient feels every centimetre. A knee replacement is balanced by releasing tight ligaments and choosing insert thickness until the gaps match; the collateral ligaments and the posterior capsule are then held at a new tension through every flexion exercise. An osteotomy that corrects an alignment deformity leaves the soft tissues on the lengthened side stretched. A repaired tendon is the extreme case: by design it is pulled back to bone under enough tension to hold, and the tension is then tested by every movement until it heals.

This is the single most useful question to ask a surgeon who says an operation is painless or painful: what have you left under tension? The answer predicts the shape of the curve better than anything else they can tell you.

## Load: what the patient will do to it

A cut bone that is not loaded is quiet by day 3. The same cut bone loaded by weight-bearing is not, which is why the Gerbershagen cohort, 50,523 patients ranked by their worst pain on the first day after 179 different operations, put open reduction of a calcaneal fracture at the top of the whole list, above every major abdominal and thoracic operation, and put foot arthrodesis, metacarpophalangeal arthrodesis and hand resection arthroplasty in the top eleven. Small bones that have to bear the body's weight or do the hand's work from day 1 hurt out of all proportion to the size of the incision. Twenty-two of the forty most painful procedure groups in that cohort were orthopaedic or trauma operations on the limbs. Spinal fusion and scoliosis surgery held ranks 2, 3 and 6, with no epidural analgesia used.

The same cohort delivered the finding that should keep every anaesthetist honest: appendicectomy, cholecystectomy, haemorrhoidectomy and tonsillectomy all ranked in the 25 most painful procedures, and laparoscopic appendicectomy patients reported pain equal to knee replacement and sternotomy patients, while receiving almost no opioid because nobody expected them to need it. Expectation sets the regimen, and the regimen sets the pain. "Minor" is a description of the surgeon's effort, not the patient's.

Load also explains the gap between pain at rest and pain on movement, which most of the published trials never measured: an updated systematic review of 944 post-surgical analgesic trials found that 53% did not assess movement-evoked pain at all. Blocks and catheters earn their keep on movement-evoked pain, which is the pain that stops physiotherapy, sleep and discharge; a trial that reports only rest pain understates what a continuous technique does for a tendon repair and overstates what it does for a bone cut.

## The shape of the curve, not the height of the peak

Put tissue, tension and load together and each operation has a characteristic trajectory. The cards record it as the expected worst pain (0-10) on the evening of surgery, day 1, day 2, day 3 and day 7, with the card's recommended plan in place.


The folk wisdom that an anatomic shoulder replacement is painless and a cuff repair is agony turns out to be wrong about the peak and right about the shape. In the only prospective comparison, 102 patients having either an arthroscopic cuff repair or a short-stay shoulder arthroplasty under long-acting interscalene blocks peaked on the same day (day 2) at the same height (mean 4.4 and 5.1, not different) and tracked together for 14 days; what separated them was that the cuff repair patients were still reaching for opioid at a week. The peak is set by the block wearing off and by the bone work both operations share. The tail is set by the tendon.

Two consequences for planning. First, the day 1-2 peak is where the single-shot block ends and the oral regimen has to be already running; it is the same problem for every card with a bone tag, and intravenous dexamethasone plus a pre-emptive oral dose is the answer for all of them. Second, the catheter question is a question about the tail. A catheter that comes out on day 2 has done its job for a bone operation and has barely started for a tendon repair, which is why the atlas marks the catheter as selective for most bone work and gives it its strongest claim for the tendon-tension and retension cards.

## Rebound pain: the predictable collapse

When a single-shot block recedes, the pain does not return to where it would have been without the block; for a few hours it is worse. In 972 ambulatory patients who had a peripheral nerve block, half met the definition of rebound pain (well-controlled, 3 or less, while the block worked, then 7 or more within 24 h of placement). The odds were higher after bone surgery (OR 1.82), in women (OR 1.52) and in the young, and lower when intravenous dexamethasone had been given (its absence carried an OR of 1.78). Satisfaction with the block was still 83%, which is the measure of how much patients value the hours it buys.

The practical rule set that follows:

1. Give intravenous dexamethasone with the block unless there is a reason not to. It prolongs the block and reduces the rebound.
2. Start the oral regimen before the block recedes, in recovery, not when the patient rings.
3. Tell the patient what will happen and when, in words. A rebound that was predicted is a symptom; one that was not is an emergency.
4. For a bone-tagged operation in a young woman going home the same day, assume rebound will happen and write the night plan accordingly.
5. For a tendon-tagged or retension-tagged operation, the rebound is the start of the plateau, not a blip; that is the catheter conversation.

## The patient

Who it was done to modifies everything above. Pre-operative pain, pre-operative opioid use, anxiety and catastrophising raise the whole curve and lengthen the tail; the opioid-tolerant patient needs a separate chapter ([A12](../a/a12-opioid-tolerant.html)). Age lowers reported pain and raises the cost of treating it. Sex shifts the rebound odds. Expectation works in both directions: the patient told to expect a painless operation who wakes in pain has lost confidence in the plan; the one told to expect a hard first night who has one takes the next dose and goes back to sleep. The patient-facing line on every card exists for this reason.

## The scale the atlas uses

Each card carries a score from 1 to 5. It describes the pain the operation produces with the card's own recommended plan in place, not the pain of the untreated operation, so a score of 3 for a replacement means "with a working block and a regimen, moderate", and a score of 5 means "even with a good plan, severe and prolonged". The scale is deliberately built on what the plan has to be rather than on a number on day 1, because the number on day 1 is the same for a 3 and a 5.

| Score | Label | What the plan has to be | Anchors |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Minimal | Simple analgesia. Day case. A block, if used, is for the anaesthetic, not for pain. | carpal tunnel release, knee arthroscopy and meniscectomy, minor removal of metalwork |
| 2 | Mild to moderate | Regular paracetamol and NSAID with a few opioid doses on day 0-1; a single-shot block helps; a catheter is not needed. | subacromial decompression, distal radius ORIF, hip arthroscopy, primary THA with spinal and infiltration, lumbar microdiscectomy |
| 3 | Moderate | A block or neuraxial technique is expected; opioids for two days; worst pain on day 1-2, then a quick fall because the mechanism is bone. | anatomic TSA, ACL reconstruction, hip hemiarthroplasty, ankle ORIF, ACDF, elbow fracture fixation |
| 4 | Severe | A catheter or a robust multimodal plan; opioids to day 3; rebound after a single-shot block is a real problem; movement-evoked pain limits physiotherapy. | primary TKA, reverse TSA, Latarjet, high tibial osteotomy, posterior lumbar fusion |
| 5 | Severe and prolonged | The catheter has its strongest claim; pain persists beyond 72 h and is re-provoked by movement for weeks; the mechanism is tissue under tension or a bone that keeps being loaded. The early peak need not be higher than a 3 or a 4. | rotator cuff repair, multiligament knee reconstruction, periacetabular osteotomy, scoliosis correction, amputation |

Two cautions. The evidence for continuous techniques is weaker than the mechanism argument suggests, because most trials measured rest pain over the first 48 hours, which is exactly the window where a dexamethasone-prolonged single shot and a catheter look alike; the trial that added an infusion on top of full multimodal analgesia in shoulder surgery found no difference in morphine use or pain scores at any point to 48 h. And a catheter that falls out, leaks or is never topped up is a single shot with extra steps; the logistics decide more outcomes than the pharmacology.

## Why the book is organised this way

PROSPECT, the procedure-specific pain management collaboration, exists because generic guidelines fail: the same drug list applied to every operation treats a cholecystectomy and a cuff repair alike, and the Gerbershagen data show what that costs. Their method is to review the evidence operation by operation and recommend an analgesic skeleton that fits the mechanism; the rotator cuff repair guideline is the worked example that the shoulder cards lean on. This book takes the same step one further back: the mechanism itself is written down for every card, so that when there is no PROSPECT guideline, which is most of orthopaedics, the plan can still be derived rather than guessed. Where a guideline exists, the card cites it. Where it does not, the card says which tags apply and what that implies, and marks the catheter call as author's judgement. That is the limit of what the model can honestly claim: it predicts the shape of the curve from first principles, and it tells you where to look when the evidence is thin.

## Using it at seven in the morning

Five questions, in order, for an operation you have not seen before:

1. What tissue is being cut, and is any of it bone? If yes, plan for a peak on day 1-2 and for rebound.
2. What is being left under tension? A repaired tendon, a lengthened limb, a balanced joint, a corrected osteotomy: plan for a tail, and have the catheter conversation.
3. When will it be loaded? Weight-bearing or hand use from day 1 raises the score by one. A sling, a brace or bed rest lowers it.
4. Who is the patient? Young, female, anxious, opioid-exposed, or burned by a previous block wearing off: raise the plan, not the score.
5. What can the system deliver? A day-case patient with nobody at home and a unit that cannot run an ambulatory catheter gets the single shot, the dexamethasone, the written night plan and a phone call, and that is a complete plan rather than a compromise.

Then open the card, and see whether the atlas agrees.
