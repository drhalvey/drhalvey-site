## What the cuff does to the limb

A pneumatic tourniquet does two separate things, and the complications sort themselves by which one caused them. Under the cuff it compresses tissue: skin, fat, muscle and, most importantly, nerve. Distal to the cuff it stops blood flow: muscle, nerve and endothelium go without oxygen for as long as the surgeon needs, and then get it all back at once. The separation is worth keeping, because pressure drives the first and time drives the second.

Compression injury is a pressure-gradient injury. Nerve fibres under the middle of the cuff are squeezed evenly; fibres at the proximal and distal edges are sheared, because tissue a centimetre away is at a different pressure. The classic histology is displacement of myelin at the nodes of Ranvier under the cuff edge, and the clinical picture is a conduction block that usually recovers over weeks to months. The two levers that shrink the gradient are a lower cuff pressure and a wider cuff, which is the whole argument for setting the pressure from the limb occlusion pressure rather than from a number printed on the machine. The radial nerve is the most vulnerable in the arm, where it lies against the humerus under the cuff; in the leg the sciatic nerve, and particularly its peroneal division, carries the risk. Skin is the second compression casualty: friction blisters from an unpadded cuff and chemical burns from skin preparation that has pooled beneath it and is pressed into the dermis for two hours.

Ischaemia distal to the cuff follows a timetable. Nerve conduction fails first, within about half an hour. Muscle depletes its high-energy phosphate, switches to anaerobic metabolism, acidifies, and leaks potassium and lactate into the trapped venous blood; the limb cools. By two hours there are ultrastructural changes in muscle, and the longer the cuff stays up beyond that, the more of the change is irreversible. Endothelium is the quiet victim: ischaemic endothelium becomes leaky and adhesive, which is the substrate for the reperfusion phase.

Reperfusion is the third injury and the one the patient notices for weeks. Oxygen returning to ischaemic tissue generates free radicals, primed neutrophils adhere to the activated endothelium, capillaries leak, and the limb swells. The result is post-tourniquet syndrome: a swollen, stiff, weak, dysaesthetic limb for days to weeks, worse after longer inflations. The pain mechanism tagged "tourniquet" in the Part B model is partly this reperfusion oedema and partly the cuff-site injury; both scale with time, and the cuff-site component scales with pressure too.

## Pressure: limb occlusion pressure and the margin

The pressure that occludes arterial flow in a given limb depends on the patient's systolic pressure, the circumference of the limb, the width and shape of the cuff, and the thickness of the soft tissue under it. A fixed pressure of 250 mmHg for the thigh and 200 mmHg for the arm, or systolic plus 100 mmHg and systolic plus 50 mmHg, overshoots in most patients and undershoots in a few. The limb occlusion pressure (LOP) is the minimum cuff pressure at which the distal arterial signal disappears, measured with a Doppler or a plethysmographic probe at the time of application; most modern tourniquet systems measure it automatically in under a minute. A safety margin is then added to cover the rise in blood pressure with surgical stimulation and the change in limb position during the case. The tiered margins that have become standard, originally published by AORN, are 40 mmHg for an LOP below 130 mmHg, 60 mmHg for 131-190 mmHg and 80 mmHg above 190 mmHg.

The randomised trials consistently show that the LOP method gives a lower pressure with an equally dry field and, in most of them, less pain. In 164 knee replacements the LOP group ran at a significantly lower pressure with no loss of field quality; pain was not different, but 40 of the 47 wound complications at discharge had occurred with cuff pressures above 225 mmHg, and there were no infections at or below that pressure. In 311 knee replacements the LOP method gave pressures of about 224-240 mmHg against 262-265 mmHg for systolic plus 150, with less thigh pain and better early range of motion. In 60 ACL reconstructions the LOP group ran at a mean of 180 mmHg against 275 mmHg and had less tourniquet-site pain for the first five days and lower opioid use. None of these trials was large enough to show a difference in nerve injury, which is too rare to study this way, but the mechanism points the same way: in a neurophysiological series of 20 knee replacements at a mean cuff pressure of 237 mmHg, the one patient with electromyographic denervation had been at 294 mmHg.

Two practical points follow. A wider, contoured cuff placed at the greatest circumference of the limb occludes at a lower pressure, so cuff choice is part of pressure choice. And when the surgeon asks for more pressure because the field is wet, the usual reasons are venous (incomplete exsanguination, a cuff that has slipped, a limb that has been lowered), not arterial; a brief deflation and re-exsanguination is often the better answer. Where the LOP cannot be measured, the old defaults are the fallback: about 200 mmHg for a normotensive arm and 250 mmHg for a normotensive thigh, higher in obesity, and never more than is needed.

## Time: the two-hour convention and what sits behind it

There is no proven safe duration. The convention of two hours for a continuous inflation comes from the ischaemic timetable of muscle and nerve and from the complication series, not from a trial. In the Norwegian national survey of 63,484 operations under a tourniquet there were 26 possible tourniquet complications, 1 in 2,442 operations, of which 15 were neurological; two were permanent, which is 1 in 31,742. The lower limb rate was 1 in 3,752 and the upper limb 1 in 6,155, and two of the complications followed inflations of three hours.

Beyond two hours the risk climbs steeply. In 1,166 knee replacements with tourniquet times above 120 min, neurological complications (peroneal, tibial or both) occurred in 7.7% of patients, with an odds ratio of 2.8 for every additional 30 min; younger patients and those with a flexion contracture over 20 degrees were at higher risk, and in the subset with tourniquet times of 180 min or more, a longer deflation interval reduced the risk only modestly. In a register series of 641 knee arthroplasties, tourniquet time over 100 min carried an adjusted odds ratio of 2.4 for complications of any kind.

The rules in practice are a mixture of that evidence and consensus:

1. Plan the case so that the cuff comes down at or before 120 min. Tell the surgeon the inflation time at 60 and 90 min without being asked.
2. If the surgeon needs longer, deflate for 10-15 min with the limb level, then re-inflate. The reperfusion interval restores some high-energy phosphate and washes out metabolites, but it is a partial measure; the total time still counts, and a second inflation of 60 min is about the most that is defensible.
3. Treat three hours of total inflation as a hard ceiling, and expect a post-tourniquet syndrome of weeks after anything near it.
4. Record inflation and deflation times; the tourniquet clock is one of the few numbers in orthopaedic anaesthesia that will be read in a courtroom.

## Inflation and the drugs that are already in the limb

Exsanguination by elevation or by an elastic bandage returns a few hundred millilitres of blood to the circulation, enough to matter in a patient with a poor ventricle, especially for bilateral lower limb cases. Avoid the elastic bandage where it could dislodge something: infection, tumour, a known deep vein thrombosis, a fracture haematoma. Elevation alone exsanguinates adequately for most knee and ankle surgery.

Anything you want in the limb has to be there before the cuff goes up. The antibiotic is the important one: the infusion should be complete, with a few minutes for tissue distribution, before inflation, and for a cefazolin dose given on the way into theatre that usually means waiting rather than inflating. The reverse also applies: drug sequestered in the limb at inflation returns at deflation. In a pilot pharmacokinetic study, plasma sufentanil rose 30-60 min after the cuff came down in most patients, an argument against a large opioid dose in the minutes before inflation and for watching the patient in recovery after a long case.

Tourniquet hypertension is the other inflation phenomenon. Thirty to sixty minutes into an inflation under general anaesthesia the blood pressure and heart rate climb, usually by 20-30% and sometimes much more, and do not respond well to deepening the anaesthetic. It is the autonomic expression of tourniquet pain (see below) and it resolves with deflation. In a randomised trial of knee arthroscopy under general anaesthesia it occurred in 68.8% of patients given saline and 37.5% of those given lidocaine 1.5 mg/kg IV ten minutes before inflation. A working block prevents it, small doses of a short-acting vasodilator or beta blocker contain it, and chasing it with volatile agent produces a hypotensive patient at deflation.

## Deflation: what arrives at the right heart

Deflation is the most predictable physiological event in orthopaedic anaesthesia, and the frail patient and the patient with a poor right ventricle get into trouble in a window you can see coming.

```
 t = 0        cuff down
 0-30 s       embolic shower to the right heart (marrow fat, debris)
              pulmonary artery pressure rises 25-40%
 1 min        end-tidal CO2 peaks (CO2 production up ~80%)
 2-4 min      acidosis worst; lactate and potassium peak;
              cerebral blood flow velocity up ~50%
 0-15 min     MAP and CVP fall as the limb refills
 10 min       end-tidal CO2 back to baseline
 10-30 min    metabolic washout complete; core temperature dips
 to 30 min    transient hypocoagulable state (antithrombin release)
 30-60 min    sequestered drug returns to plasma
```

The embolic load is real and visible. In 40 knee replacements monitored with transoesophageal echocardiography, the embolic signal peaked 30 s after release, pulmonary artery pressure rose by 25-40%, the emboli were marrow in origin, and the load was greater in older, heavier patients with fattier marrow. For most patients it passes unnoticed. For the patient with pulmonary hypertension, a dilated right ventricle or a patent foramen ovale, it is the moment of risk, and bilateral simultaneous deflation doubles it.

The metabolic washout follows. Oxygen consumption rises by about 55% and carbon dioxide production by about 80% at 2 min, end-tidal CO2 peaks at 1 min and is back to baseline by about 10 min, potassium rises by a mean of 0.28 mmol/L after an inflation of 60-120 min, lactate peaks about 2 mmol/L above baseline and the acidosis is worst at around 4 min and gone by 30 min. The CO2 raises middle cerebral artery flow velocity by about 50% at 2-4 min, which matters in the patient with a tight intracranial compartment. The numbers scale with the muscle mass that was ischaemic and the time it was ischaemic: a thigh for two hours produces several times the load of a forearm for forty minutes.

The haemodynamic fall is the one you treat. Blood pressure and central venous pressure fall for about 15 min as the limb's vascular bed, dilated by ischaemic metabolites, refills with a few hundred millilitres of blood. Under a spinal with an already reduced venous capacitance, or in a hypovolaemic patient, the fall can be steep.

The rule set for deflation:

- Know the time; be at the patient, not at the chart.
- Have the patient normovolaemic before the cuff comes down, with a vasopressor drawn up and, in the frail or cardiac patient, running.
- Increase minute ventilation by about half for five minutes around deflation in the ventilated patient, which blunts the CO2 peak and the cerebral hyperaemia; in the spontaneously breathing patient expect a brief rise in respiratory rate.
- Deflate one limb at a time for bilateral cases, with 10-15 min between them.
- Treat a potassium problem only in the patient who arrived with one: 0.3 mmol/L on top of 5.5 mmol/L in the renal patient is a different proposition from the same rise in a fit patient.
- Expect cooling and keep the forced-air warmer on through deflation.
- Watch the field: the transient hypocoagulable state and the reactive hyperaemia mean the wound bleeds more in the first half hour than it did during the operation. Tranexamic acid given before incision (see [blood management](a04-blood-management-txa.html)) is the counter.

## Tourniquet pain under regional anaesthesia and why it happens

Tourniquet pain is a dull, deep, poorly localised ache that starts 30-45 min into an inflation, builds steadily, is relieved only by deflation, and appears in patients whose surgical block is working perfectly. It is the characteristic failure mode of a spinal or an epidural for a long lower limb case, and the mechanism explains both why the block does not cover it and why the usual rescues do not either.

Three things combine. First, the pain is generated under the cuff and in the ischaemic muscle, by compression and by the metabolites of ischaemia, at a dermatomal level (L1-L3 for a thigh cuff, T2 and C5-T1 for an arm cuff) that a block aimed at the operation may only just reach. Second, the fibres that carry it are predominantly unmyelinated C fibres. A-delta conduction under the cuff is stopped by the compression itself at about 30 min, which removes the sharp, localised component and leaves the slow, burning, C-fibre component unopposed; the intrathecal morphine study that showed partial protection against a standardised thigh tourniquet was built on this C-fibre hypothesis. Third, as a spinal or epidural regresses, C-fibre transmission from the cuff site returns before pinprick sensation at the same level has returned, so a block that still tests as adequate can be transmitting tourniquet pain. Under general anaesthesia the same input shows as tourniquet hypertension and tachycardia; central sensitisation through NMDA receptor activation by the ischaemic barrage is the proposed reason it is so hard to suppress once established.

The comparative evidence favours a dense block over a thin one. In 60 patients with lower limb tourniquets of 60 min or more, tourniquet pain was less frequent under spinal than under epidural anaesthesia to the same sensory level, and an alkalinised epidural solution removed the difference, which fits a density explanation. Intrathecal morphine 0.3 mg gave partial prophylaxis at the cost of urinary retention and vomiting, and intrathecal clonidine 150 mcg abolished it in a small trial.

What to do, in order of usefulness:

1. Plan the block for the tourniquet, not the incision. For a thigh tourniquet of an hour or more under a spinal, use a full dose of hyperbaric bupivacaine with an intrathecal opioid rather than a low-dose, short-acting technique, and accept the longer recovery.
2. Add sedation that acts on the central component: small doses of ketamine or an alpha-2 agonist work better than more propofol.
3. Under general anaesthesia, treat the hypertension as sympathetic output rather than as light anaesthesia.
4. Ask the surgeon how long the cuff really needs to be up. Deflation is the only complete treatment, and many surgeons will deflate once the bony work is done and close in a bloody field.
5. In the awake patient with a peripheral block and a thigh tourniquet, the plan was wrong from the start; the block covers the operation and the cuff site is outside it. A forearm or calf tourniquet is tolerated for 20-30 min by most patients; a thigh tourniquet beyond that needs a neuraxial block or a general anaesthetic.

## The total knee arthroplasty tourniquet debate

A tourniquet is used in most knee replacements because it gives the surgeon a dry field for the bone cuts and dry cancellous bone for cement interdigitation. The question is whether that convenience costs the patient anything, and the randomised evidence now says it does.

The Cochrane review pooled 41 trials and 2,819 patients. With a tourniquet, serious adverse events (thromboembolism, infection, reoperation and the like) were more frequent: a risk ratio of 1.73 (95% CI 1.10-2.73), from 29 to 59 per 1,000, with moderate certainty. Pain on day one was 1.25 points higher on a 10-point scale (0.32-2.19). Intra-operative blood loss was 147 mL lower but post-operative loss 58 mL higher, and total blood loss and transfusion (RR 1.20, 0.86-1.67) were not different. Surgery was 3.7 min shorter. Hospital stay was 0.34 days longer. Function at 12 months was probably not different. The feasibility trial that followed, randomising 53 patients to an inflated or a non-inflated cuff, saw the same trend to more thigh pain and more adverse events with the inflated cuff and one new ischaemic brain lesion on post-operative MRI.

The honest reading is that the tourniquet's benefit is to the surgeon's view and the operating time, that the cement-interdigitation argument has not been shown to translate into implant survival in the trials that exist, and that the costs land on the patient as pain, reperfusion injury to the thigh and a higher complication rate. Surgeons vary: some have abandoned the tourniquet with tranexamic acid doing the work, some inflate only for cementation (which keeps the time under 30 min and most of the benefit), and some use it throughout. The anaesthetic position is to make the no-tourniquet or short-tourniquet knee easy for the surgeon: tranexamic acid before incision, a normotensive but not hypertensive patient, and a working block so that the field is not fogged by a sympathetic surge. The [primary TKA](../c/primary-tka.html) and [unicompartmental](../c/unicompartmental-knee.html) cards carry the per-operation detail.

## Contraindications and the patients who need a different plan

There are no absolute contraindications in the sense that a tourniquet can be justified in almost anyone for a short time, but there is a list of patients in whom the default should be no cuff, or a short one, with the surgeon told in advance.

- Severe peripheral vascular disease, calcified vessels on the radiograph, or absent distal pulses: the cuff can crack plaque and thrombose a vessel, and reperfusion of an already marginal limb is poorly tolerated. Previous vascular surgery or a bypass graft in the limb is a contraindication to a cuff over it.
- An arteriovenous fistula in the limb.
- Known deep vein thrombosis in the limb, or a high clinical suspicion of one: exsanguination and inflation can dislodge it.
- Sickle cell disease: the stagnant, acidotic, hypoxic limb is a sickling chamber. If a tourniquet is unavoidable, exsanguinate completely, keep the inflation short, keep the patient warm, oxygenated and hydrated, and expect to treat a crisis.
- Severe crush injury, heavily contaminated open fracture, local infection or tumour: a cuff can be used, but exsanguinate by elevation rather than by bandage.
- Fragile skin (long-term steroids, rheumatoid disease, the very elderly) and the paediatric limb: skin and compression injury risk is higher, and padding and pressure matter more.
- Poor cardiac reserve for bilateral cases: the exsanguination volume at inflation and the embolic and metabolic load at deflation are both doubled; stage the deflations and consider whether the second side should be done at all.
- Raised intracranial pressure: the CO2 and cerebral hyperaemia at deflation are an avoidable insult.
- Severe uncontrolled hypertension: the LOP will be high and the margin pushes the set pressure into the range where wound and nerve complications rise.

## The rule set

1. Decide before the case whether a tourniquet is needed at all, and for how long; the knee replacement evidence is against routine use.
2. Widest contoured cuff, smooth padding beyond both edges, sealed distal edge, placed at the greatest circumference.
3. Antibiotic complete before inflation.
4. Measure the LOP after positioning and set the pressure to LOP plus the tiered margin (40, 60 or 80 mmHg); record both.
5. Exsanguinate by elevation where there is any question of clot, infection or tumour.
6. Call the time at 60 and 90 min; plan for deflation at 120 min; a reperfusion interval of 10-15 min buys a limited second inflation; three hours is the ceiling.
7. Under neuraxial anaesthesia, plan the density and duration of the block for the cuff, not the incision, and add an intrathecal opioid for a long thigh tourniquet.
8. Before deflation: normovolaemia, vasopressor ready, warming on, ventilation up, one limb at a time.
9. After deflation: expect 15 min of hypotension, a CO2 and potassium peak, a cold patient and a wet wound; stay at the bedside.
10. Document inflation and deflation times, pressure, LOP, cuff site and padding, and any pre-existing neurological deficit in the limb before the cuff went on.

The cards that carry a tourniquet tag follow these rules without repeating them; where the operation changes the rule (a bilateral knee, a calf tourniquet for foot surgery, a forearm cuff for hand surgery) the card says so.
