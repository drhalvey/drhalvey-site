## How to read the menu

A block is chosen by laying three maps over each other.

The first map is the innervation of what the surgeon cuts. Skin is the least of it. Joints are supplied by articular branches of every nerve that crosses them, bone by its periosteal and endosteal nerves, and the deep structures follow sclerotomes, not dermatomes: the shoulder joint is C5-6 whatever the skin over the incision says, and the back of the knee capsule is tibial and obturator even though the incision is anterior.

The second map is what the block reaches. Each block has a reliable core and an unreliable edge, and the edge is where a "failed" block is usually a correctly working block that was never going to cover that structure.

The third map is what else the block takes. Motor block and collateral nerve block are the price: the phrenic nerve with an interscalene injection, the quadriceps with a femoral block, the foot with a sciatic block, and in an injured limb the ability to feel ischaemic muscle.

The operation sets the first map and the patient sets the tolerance for the third. The [pain model](../b/b01-why-operations-hurt.html) sets how long the block needs to last.

## Shoulder

The joint, the cuff, the subacromial bursa and most of the capsule are supplied by the suprascapular nerve (posterior and superior, about two thirds of the joint) and the axillary nerve (anterior and inferior), with contributions from the lateral pectoral and subscapular nerves. All come from C5-6 through the superior trunk. The skin over the top of the shoulder is C3-4 (supraclavicular nerves) and the axilla and medial arm are T2.

| Block | Covers | Spares or misses | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Interscalene | Whole joint, C4 cape by spread | Posterior portal and axilla (T2); ulnar side of hand | Phrenic paresis near-universal at conventional volumes; Horner, hoarseness |
| Superior trunk | As interscalene for the joint | Supraclavicular nerves (add a superficial cervical plexus block for clavicle and superior incisions) | Phrenic paresis less complete |
| Suprascapular plus axillary | Most of the joint | Anterior capsule (lateral pectoral, subscapular); skin | No phrenic block; two injections; not a surgical block |
| Supraclavicular or infraclavicular plus suprascapular | Joint by a different route | Variable | Lower phrenic risk; less evidence |

The phrenic nerve lies on the anterior scalene a few millimetres from the C5 root. With the volumes once used, hemidiaphragmatic paresis occurred in every patient. Low volume, dilute solution and extrafascial injection reduce it, but no single measure brings it below about 20%. Moving distally to the superior trunk gave non-inferior analgesia at 24 hours with paresis in 76% against 98% and, more usefully, complete paresis in 5% against 73%. Suprascapular block avoids the phrenic nerve altogether: across 16 trials it matched interscalene block for 24-hour opioid use and for pain at every time after the recovery room, with fewer respiratory complications. PROSPECT accepts either for [rotator cuff repair](../c/rotator-cuff-repair.html).

So the menu reads: interscalene or superior trunk block for [arthroplasty](../c/anatomic-tsa.html), [reverse arthroplasty](../c/reverse-tsa.html), cuff repair and [open stabilisation](../c/shoulder-stabilisation.html) in a patient with normal lungs; superior trunk in the patient with moderate respiratory disease or obesity; suprascapular plus axillary under general anaesthesia when a 25% fall in vital capacity cannot be afforded (severe COPD, contralateral phrenic palsy or pneumonectomy); and for [subacromial decompression](../c/subacromial-decompression-acj.html), [diagnostic arthroscopy](../c/shoulder-arthroscopy-debridement.html) and [capsular release](../c/frozen-shoulder.html), a single shot or a suprascapular block is enough. [Clavicle](../c/acj-reconstruction-clavicle.html) surgery needs C3-4 as well as C5-6. After a [proximal humerus fracture](../c/proximal-humerus-fracture.html) or dislocation, test and record axillary nerve function before any block.

## Elbow

All four terminal nerves cross the elbow and all supply the joint, and the medial skin is supplied by the medial cutaneous nerves of arm and forearm, so the block must be at the plexus: supraclavicular, infraclavicular or axillary with a separate musculocutaneous injection. Infraclavicular is the catheter site of choice because the pectoral muscles hold the catheter and the site is away from the field. Supraclavicular injection blocks the phrenic nerve in a substantial minority.

What the block hides matters more here than anywhere else in the upper limb. After [distal humerus or olecranon fixation](../c/elbow-fracture-fixation.html) and [ulnar nerve transposition](../c/distal-biceps-ulnar-nerve.html) the surgeon wants to know that the ulnar nerve works; after [distal biceps repair](../c/distal-biceps-ulnar-nerve.html) the posterior interosseous and lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerves are the ones at risk; after [humeral shaft fixation](../c/humeral-shaft-fixation.html) it is the radial nerve. Three workable answers: block after the surgeon has examined the patient in recovery; use a short-acting agent for surgery and accept oral analgesia afterwards; or place a catheter, leave it unloaded until the examination is done, then start it. For [elbow arthroplasty](../c/total-elbow-radial-head.html) and [arthrolysis](../c/elbow-arthroscopy-release.html), where continuous passive motion is the point of the operation, a catheter has a strong claim.

## Wrist and hand

Below the elbow the choice is between a plexus block that gives a numb, paralysed arm with tourniquet tolerance, and distal blocks that keep the elbow and shoulder working.

| Operation | Defensible default | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Distal radius ORIF](../c/distal-radius-orif.html), [wrist fusion](../c/trapeziectomy-wrist-fusion.html), [scaphoid](../c/wrist-arthroscopy-scaphoid.html) | Axillary, infraclavicular or supraclavicular block, long-acting | Bone surgery with an upper arm tourniquet; rebound pain is the trap |
| [Carpal tunnel, trigger finger](../c/carpal-tunnel-minor-hand.html) | Surgeon's local infiltration | Pain score 1; a plexus block is a worse experience than the operation |
| [Dupuytren's](../c/dupuytrens-fasciectomy.html), [trapeziectomy](../c/trapeziectomy-wrist-fusion.html) | Plexus block, or short-acting plexus block plus long-acting forearm blocks | Anaesthesia from the first, analgesia from the second, without a flail arm overnight |
| [Tendon repair](../c/hand-trauma.html) where active movement is tested | Wide-awake local with adrenaline, no tourniquet | The patient moves the repair on request |

A forearm tourniquet is tolerated for about 20 minutes with distal blocks alone. The combination of a short-acting proximal block for surgery and long-acting blocks of the median, ulnar and radial nerves in the forearm gives 12 hours of hand analgesia with a shoulder and elbow that work by the time the patient is home, which is the author's preference for day-case hand surgery.

## Hip

The anterior capsule, which carries most of the nociceptors, is supplied by articular branches of the femoral, obturator and accessory obturator nerves; the posterior capsule by the sciatic nerve, the nerve to quadratus femoris and the superior gluteal nerve. The skin of the lateral and posterior approaches is lateral femoral cutaneous, subcostal and cluneal.

| Block | Covers | Spares or misses | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fascia iliaca (suprainguinal) | Femoral, lateral femoral cutaneous, variable obturator | Posterior capsule | Quadriceps weakness |
| Femoral | Anterior capsule in part, anterior thigh | Obturator, lateral skin, posterior capsule | Quadriceps weakness, falls |
| PENG | Anterior capsule articular branches | Skin, posterior capsule | Motor-sparing in principle; femoral spread with larger volumes |
| Lumbar plexus | Femoral, obturator, lateral femoral cutaneous | Posterior capsule | Deep block: neuraxial anticoagulant rules, epidural spread |
| Spinal | Everything | Nothing, for 2-4 h | No analgesia after it wears off unless an opioid is added |

PROSPECT for [total hip arthroplasty](../c/tha-posterior.html) recommends a fascia iliaca block or local infiltration analgesia on top of paracetamol, an NSAID and dexamethasone, and recommends against femoral block, lumbar plexus block and epidural because the weakness, falls and hypotension outweigh the analgesia in an operation whose pain is moderate and short. That fits the mechanism: THA is a pain score 2-3 bone operation and the patient needs to walk the same day. The defensible default is a spinal, surgeon infiltration, and no motor-blocking peripheral block; fascia iliaca or PENG is added for the patient in whom basic analgesics are contraindicated or pain is expected to be high.

For [hip fracture](../c/neck-of-femur.html) the block is given on arrival, not in theatre: femoral or fascia iliaca block reduces pain on movement and opioid use while the patient waits, and quadriceps weakness matters less before fixation. The PENG block was described for exactly this purpose and is an alternative. For [hip arthroscopy](../c/hip-arthroscopy.html) the pain is capsular and fluid-related and short-lived; a fascia iliaca block adds quadriceps weakness for a modest gain, and intra-articular or portal local anaesthetic is usually enough. [Periacetabular osteotomy](../c/periacetabular-osteotomy.html) and [revision arthroplasty](../c/revision-tha.html) are where an epidural, a lumbar plexus catheter or intrathecal morphine still have a place.

## Knee

The anterior knee is supplied by the saphenous nerve and the nerves to the vasti (all in or near the adductor canal) with the anterior obturator; the posterior capsule by the tibial nerve and the posterior obturator through the popliteal plexus; the lateral side by genicular branches of the common peroneal nerve.

The quadriceps question is the centre of the knee menu. A femoral block gives excellent anterior analgesia and takes the quadriceps with it: in a randomised comparison of continuous blocks after knee replacement, quadriceps strength was 18% of baseline with a femoral catheter and 52% with an adductor canal catheter, with no difference in pain or morphine use. A patient who cannot lock the knee cannot walk safely on day 0, and a buckling knee is how inpatient falls after arthroplasty happen. PROSPECT for [primary TKA](../c/primary-tka.html) therefore recommends a single-shot adductor canal block with surgeon periarticular infiltration and intravenous dexamethasone.

The adductor canal block misses the back of the knee. The options are surgeon infiltration of the posterior capsule under direct vision, an IPACK injection (between the popliteal artery and the capsule), or a sciatic block. Sciatic block gives the best posterior analgesia and has two costs: a foot drop that delays walking, and a foot drop that hides a common peroneal palsy, which is the nerve injury of knee replacement in a valgus or flexed knee and of [high tibial osteotomy](../c/hto-dfo.html). The defensible default is infiltration or IPACK, with sciatic block reserved for [revision](../c/revision-tka.html) and for patients with severe posterior pain after surgery.

[ACL reconstruction](../c/acl-reconstruction.html): adductor canal block; the hamstring harvest site is posterior and medial and is not covered, and a femoral block is avoided because early quadriceps activation is the rehabilitation. [Multiligament reconstruction](../c/multiligament-knee.html) and [quadriceps or patellar tendon repair](../c/quads-patellar-tendon-repair.html): pain score 4-5, catheter territory; for multiligament reconstruction an adductor canal catheter, because the braced knee still needs a quadriceps and the sciatic territory is kept examinable; for extensor mechanism repair the limb is braced in extension so quadriceps weakness costs less. [Knee arthroscopy](../c/knee-arthroscopy.html): portal infiltration only. [Tibial plateau fixation and tibial nailing](../c/tibial-nailing-plateau.html): the compartment syndrome question decides, below.

## Ankle and foot

Everything below the knee is sciatic except a medial strip of skin to the medial malleolus and sometimes the medial midfoot, which is saphenous.

| Operation | Block | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Forefoot surgery | Ankle block | Ankle motor function kept; walks out in a stiff shoe; calf or ankle tourniquet tolerated for short cases |
| [Ankle fracture fixation](../c/ankle-pilon-fixation.html), Achilles repair | Popliteal sciatic plus saphenous, single shot | Bone surgery in the young: the highest rebound risk |
| Hindfoot fusion, calcaneal fixation, ankle replacement, [pilon](../c/ankle-pilon-fixation.html) | Popliteal catheter | Among the most painful operations in the 179-procedure cohort; pain lasts beyond any single shot |
| [Amputation](../c/amputation.html) | Sciatic catheter, plus femoral or adductor canal for a below-knee stump | Surgeon-placed perineural catheters are an alternative |

A popliteal block does not cover a thigh tourniquet, so the anaesthetic is a spinal or general anaesthetic with the block for analgesia, or the surgeon uses a calf cuff. The blocked foot is insensate and the patient is on crutches: non-weight-bearing instructions, a protected heel and a warning about burns and pressure are part of the block.

## Spine

Regional techniques for spinal surgery are fascial plane blocks (erector spinae, thoracolumbar interfascial plane), surgeon wound infiltration and intrathecal morphine placed by the surgeon or anaesthetist. The plane blocks reduce early opioid use in small trials and their effect is short; for fusion the stronger options are intrathecal morphine and a multimodal intravenous regimen, covered in [spine essentials](a14-spine-essentials.html). No block should obscure the first postoperative neurological examination.

## Single shot or catheter

A single shot of ropivacaine or bupivacaine lasts 10-16 hours and, with dexamethasone, 20-24 hours. A catheter is justified when the operation's pain is severe for longer than that and movement-evoked (pain score 4-5 in this book's scale), when the patient is opioid-tolerant, or when early forced movement is the purpose of the surgery.

The evidence is weaker than the logic. In the shoulder, a randomised trial of 60 arthroplasty and cuff repair patients who all received dexamethasone and multimodal analgesia found that adding a ropivacaine infusion to day 2 changed neither morphine use nor pain scores. The catheter's advantage has shrunk as the single shot has lengthened, and the default in most operations has moved to a single shot with intravenous dexamethasone and a planned oral regimen, with catheters selective.

Ambulatory catheter logistics, where a service offers them: an elastomeric pump with ropivacaine 0.2% at 5-8 mL/h with or without a patient bolus; written and verbal instructions; a named 24-hour contact; a daily telephone call; a carer at home for the first night; catheter removal by the patient or carer at 48-72 hours with the tip checked. Large prospective series report few serious complications. The failure modes are leakage, dislodgement and secondary block failure, and for interscalene infusions continuous phrenic paresis, which excludes patients with limited respiratory reserve. Exclude patients who live alone, cannot be reached by telephone, or have significant renal or hepatic impairment.

## Adjuncts

Dexamethasone. Intravenous and perineural routes were equivalent in a placebo-controlled interscalene trial: median analgesia 757 minutes with ropivacaine alone, 1,405 minutes with perineural and 1,275 minutes with intravenous dexamethasone 10 mg. Perineural dosing shows a ceiling at 4 mg and is off-label. Because the intravenous route also treats nausea, is recommended by every PROSPECT guideline cited here and is associated with less rebound pain, dexamethasone 8-10 mg IV at induction is the default and perineural use adds little. Expect a transient rise in glucose in diabetic patients.

Dexmedetomidine. Perineural dexmedetomidine 50-60 micrograms prolongs sensory block and analgesia by more than half, prolongs motor block by the same proportion, and causes bradycardia and hypotension. It is useful when dexamethasone is not wanted; the motor prolongation is a cost in day surgery.

Adrenaline marks intravascular injection and adds little duration to ropivacaine. Clonidine adds a modest prolongation with sedation and hypotension.

Liposomal bupivacaine. The plain reading of the evidence is that it does not deliver what the formulation promises. In nerve blocks, the pooled benefit over plain bupivacaine at 24-72 hours was 1.0 cm.h of area under the pain curve, clinically unimportant, and it vanished when the one industry-sponsored trial was removed. Infiltrated, it beat plain local anaesthetic in 4 of 36 trials, and a nerve block with plain bupivacaine beat infiltrated liposomal bupivacaine in 11 of 12. Positive results clustered in trials at high risk of bias. It is not a substitute for a catheter.

## Rebound pain

Rebound pain is the abrupt arrival of severe pain when a dense block resolves, typically 12-24 hours after surgery, at night, at home, in a patient who has taken no analgesia because nothing hurt. In 972 ambulatory patients it occurred in 49.6%. The independent associations were bone surgery, female sex, younger age and the absence of intravenous dexamethasone.

The plan that follows: give dexamethasone 8-10 mg IV; start regular paracetamol and an NSAID before the block wears off, not after; tell the patient the hour at which the block is expected to fade and to take the first opioid dose at the first tingling or at bedtime, whichever comes first; and for bone surgery in a young patient ([ankle fixation](../c/ankle-pilon-fixation.html), [distal radius ORIF](../c/distal-radius-orif.html)) consider whether a shorter block that fades in daylight is kinder than a long one that fades at 2 am. For operations with a pain score of 1-2 a long-acting block can create a worse night than the operation would have.

## Local anaesthetic dose and LAST

Conventional maximum doses, by lean body weight: bupivacaine and levobupivacaine 2 mg/kg, ropivacaine 3 mg/kg, lidocaine 3 mg/kg plain and 7 mg/kg with adrenaline. The bupivacaine figure is the adult plain-solution figure; children are dosed from [the child having orthopaedic surgery](a15-the-child.html), where the ceilings differ by age and route. Reduce in the frail and elderly, in hepatic or cardiac failure and in pregnancy. The limits are crude and the site matters more than the number, but they are the budget, and in arthroplasty the budget is shared: surgeon infiltration of ropivacaine 300 mg in a knee plus an adductor canal block of 100 mg exceeds 3 mg/kg in any patient under 130 kg. Agree the total before the first syringe is drawn up.

For toxicity the ASRA checklist governs: stop injecting, call for help and the lipid, manage the airway and stop seizures with a benzodiazepine, then lipid emulsion 20%. Over 70 kg: 100 mL over 2-3 minutes then 200-250 mL over 15-20 minutes. Under 70 kg: 1.5 mL/kg then 0.25 mL/kg/min. Repeat the bolus or double the infusion if the circulation remains unstable, to a ceiling of about 12 mL/kg. Adrenaline in small boluses of 1 microgram/kg or less; avoid vasopressin, calcium channel blockers, beta blockers and further local anaesthetic; avoid propofol when the circulation is unstable. Observe for at least two hours after a seizure and four to six hours after cardiovascular toxicity.

## Before the needle: neurology and the compartment question

Record the limb's neurology before the block: pre-existing numbness or weakness, the specific nerve at risk from the injury or operation, and the time and expected duration of the block so that a deficit persisting beyond it triggers action. The reasoning and the pathway are in [nerve injury and compartment syndrome](a11-nerve-injury-compartment.html), as is the argument about blocking a limb at risk of compartment syndrome. The short version for the menu: in a tibial fracture, a high-energy forearm fracture or a crush injury, a dense, long block that outlasts surgery by many hours is avoided; a low-concentration block or catheter with structured surveillance is acceptable; and the decision is made with the surgeon and documented.
