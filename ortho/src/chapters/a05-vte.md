## Why orthopaedic operations clot

Virchow's triad is delivered in full by a hip or knee replacement. Stasis: the femoral vein is kinked for minutes at a time when the hip is dislocated, flexed and rotated for femoral preparation, a thigh tourniquet stops venous return completely for an hour, and the calf pump is idle during and after surgery. Endothelial injury: the same vein is stretched and twisted, and a segment of it sits against a retractor. Hypercoagulability: reaming and broaching a medullary canal pushes marrow fat and tissue thromboplastin into the venous blood, and the surgical stress response adds fibrinogen and platelet activation for a week or more. The link between marrow embolism and thrombosis is direct: in a randomised trial of cemented hip replacement, suction venting of the canal cut embolic cascades on echocardiography from 91% to 15% and day-4 deep vein thrombosis from 18% to 3%. The same embolic load is the subject of [bone cement implantation syndrome](a02-cement-bcis.html) and [fat embolism](a03-fat-embolism.html).

Hip fracture adds the days of immobility and dehydration before the operation, so thrombus may already be present on arrival. Pelvic and acetabular fracture adds direct venous injury in the pelvis. At the other end of the scale, an arthroscopy or a hand operation injures no large vein, opens no canal and immobilises nobody, and the baseline risk is that of the patient.

The risk continues after discharge. The hypercoagulable state outlasts the hospital stay, which is why symptomatic events after arthroplasty cluster in the second and third weeks, and why every guideline's duration (10-14 days at minimum, up to 35 days after hip replacement) is longer than the admission.

Patient factors move a patient up a tier: previous thromboembolism (the strongest), active cancer, known thrombophilia, oestrogen therapy, pregnancy and the puerperium, severe obesity, age, and anything that will keep the patient in bed. In the Danish fast-track cohort the independent predictors of thrombosis after hip and knee replacement were age over 85 and a BMI above 35, with odds ratios of 2.5 to 3.7.

For the low tier the trials are clear. In POT-KAST, eight days of low-molecular-weight heparin after knee arthroscopy gave symptomatic thromboembolism in 0.7% against 0.4% with nothing. In POT-CAST, heparin for the whole period of a lower-leg cast gave 1.4% against 1.8%. Routine anticoagulation does not work in unselected patients because the baseline risk is too low for it to show; that does not mean the patient with a previous embolus having an arthroscopy should go without.

## The agent after arthroplasty

Four regimens are in common use and each has a trial behind it.

Low-molecular-weight heparin (enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily) is the reference agent against which everything else has been tested. It is the suggested first choice of the 2012 ACCP guideline, it has predictable timing around a neuraxial block, and it requires an injection every day for two to five weeks, which is the reason patients and surgeons look for alternatives.

Direct oral anticoagulants (rivaroxaban 10 mg daily, apixaban 2.5 mg twice daily) are oral, at least as effective as enoxaparin against all thrombosis, and are the agents the 2019 American Society of Hematology guideline conditionally prefers when an anticoagulant is chosen. The surgical objection is wound bleeding and haematoma, and they cannot be given while an epidural or deep catheter is in place.

Aspirin (100 mg daily in Australia) is cheap, oral, already taken by many of the patients and places no restriction on neuraxial technique. Both the ACCP and ASH guidelines list it as an acceptable option. CRISTAL is the direct test of aspirin as sole chemoprophylaxis and it is an Australian trial, nested in the national joint registry: 31 hospitals were randomised to give aspirin 100 mg or enoxaparin 40 mg daily for 35 days after hip replacement and 14 days after knee replacement, and the trial stopped early at 9,711 patients. Symptomatic thromboembolism within 90 days occurred in 3.45% with aspirin and 1.82% with enoxaparin. Aspirin failed the non-inferiority test and enoxaparin was superior. No secondary outcome differed, including death and major bleeding.

The plain reading has two halves. Aspirin alone prevents fewer clots than enoxaparin, and that question is closed. But of the 256 events, 174 were below-knee deep vein thromboses, 18 were above-knee and 79 were pulmonary emboli, and the trial was not powered to show a difference in embolism or death. How much a symptomatic calf thrombosis matters (it means weeks of therapeutic anticoagulation in a patient with a fresh joint, and some propagate) against the convenience and wound profile of aspirin is a value judgement, and surgeons continue to make it differently.

The hybrid regimen answers part of the objection. In EPCAT II every patient received rivaroxaban 10 mg daily for five days and was then randomised to aspirin 81 mg or continued rivaroxaban, for a total of 14 days after knee and 35 days after hip replacement. Symptomatic thromboembolism was 0.64% with the switch to aspirin and 0.70% with continued rivaroxaban, with bleeding no different. An anticoagulant for the first days, when the risk is highest and the patient is in or near hospital, followed by aspirin for the tail, is the structure NICE recommends for hip replacement (low-molecular-weight heparin for 10 days then aspirin for 28 days) alongside the alternatives of 28 days of heparin with stockings or 35 days of rivaroxaban; for knee replacement NICE accepts 14 days of aspirin alone, heparin with stockings, or rivaroxaban.

```
  Regimen                     Clots prevented    Wound/bleeding    Neuraxial timing
  --------------------------  -----------------  ----------------  -----------------
  Enoxaparin 40 mg daily      reference          reference         12 h rule
  DOAC (rivaroxaban 10 mg)    at least as good   surgeon's worry   no catheter in situ
  Aspirin 100 mg alone        fewer (CRISTAL)    least             no restriction
  Anticoagulant 5-10 days,    as good as DOAC    middle            as for the first
    then aspirin              (EPCAT II)                           agent
```

The Australian position is a framework, not a prescription. The national Venous Thromboembolism Prevention Clinical Care Standard requires that every admitted patient has thrombotic risk and bleeding risk assessed and documented, is offered prophylaxis appropriate to both, is told what it is for, is reassessed when the clinical picture changes, and leaves with a plan that states the agent and the stop date. It does not name an agent for arthroplasty. Practice among Australian arthroplasty surgeons has varied in agent and duration, and since CRISTAL the defensible default for a standard-risk patient is an anticoagulant in hospital with a switch to aspirin, or enoxaparin throughout, with aspirin alone reserved for the low-risk, rapidly mobilising patient after an informed conversation. That sentence is author's judgement from the trials above.

For fracture surgery in younger patients the evidence points the same way with a different emphasis. PREVENT CLOT randomised 12,211 patients with operatively treated limb fractures or pelvic and acetabular fractures (mean age 45) to aspirin 81 mg twice daily or enoxaparin 30 mg twice daily. Death at 90 days was 0.78% against 0.73%, pulmonary embolism was 1.49% in each group, and deep vein thrombosis was 2.51% against 1.71%. Aspirin is a legitimate choice after isolated limb fracture fixation in a patient who will be up quickly. It was not a trial of the elderly hip fracture patient, for whom low-molecular-weight heparin from admission, timed around surgery and continued for about a month, remains the standard, and for whom the ACCP guideline specifically does not list the direct oral anticoagulants.

## Mechanical prophylaxis and movement

Intermittent pneumatic compression empties the calf veins, replaces the idle muscle pump and stimulates endothelial fibrinolysis, with no bleeding cost. The ACCP guideline accepts a compression device as sole prophylaxis in a patient at high bleeding risk and suggests adding one to drug prophylaxis during the hospital stay; the ASH guideline prefers pneumatic compression to graduated stockings. The practical rules: put the sleeve on the non-operated leg before induction and on both legs in recovery, keep it running whenever the patient is in bed (a device hanging on the bed end has no effect), and leave it off a limb with critical ischaemia, a fresh skin graft or fragile, ulcerated skin. Stockings add little to a working compression device and injure skin in arterial disease and oedematous limbs.

Early walking is the most powerful mechanical prophylaxis and the reason the modern event rates are a fraction of the historical ones. In the Danish fast-track cohort of 17,582 hip and knee replacements with a median stay of two days and prophylaxis during the admission only, 90-day symptomatic thromboembolism was 0.40% and pulmonary embolism 0.16%. The anaesthetic contributes directly: a technique that has the patient standing within hours of surgery (short-acting spinal, motor-sparing blocks, no urinary catheter, treated nausea) is thromboprophylaxis. See [enhanced recovery and day-case arthroplasty](a09-eras-day-case.html).

## Timing around neuraxial anaesthesia and catheters

The full interval table is in [blood management](a04-blood-management-txa.html). The rules that govern the postoperative chart are these.

```
  Enoxaparin 40 mg daily and a single-shot spinal

  needle                       first dose
    |------------ 12 h -----------|-------- 24 h --------| next dose
  09:00                         21:00                   21:00

  With an epidural or deep catheter in place

    dose ------ 12 h ------ remove catheter --- 4 h --- next dose
```

Prophylactic enoxaparin: the needle goes in at least 12 hours after a dose and the first postoperative dose is given at least 12 hours after the needle. An evening dosing time achieves both without anyone doing arithmetic: a morning or afternoon spinal is followed by a dose at 20:00-22:00, and a pre-operative dose for a hip fracture patient given the evening before leaves a clear 12 hours by the morning list. A catheter is removed at least 12 hours after a dose and the next dose waits four hours. Twice-daily and therapeutic regimens do not coexist with a neuraxial catheter. In renal impairment (creatinine clearance below 30 mL/min) the dose is halved and the intervals lengthen.

Direct oral anticoagulants: the first prophylactic dose is given no sooner than six hours after the needle or after catheter removal, and not while an epidural or deep plexus catheter is in place. A patient who needs a catheter for two days has enoxaparin for those two days and switches when the catheter is out.

Aspirin: no restriction at any dose, alone. The risk rises when it is combined with another agent, which is exactly the hybrid regimen, so the catheter comes out under the anticoagulant's rule.

Bloody or difficult tap: delay the first dose to 24 hours and write it on the chart, because the person giving the evening dose was not in theatre.

Peripheral catheters: for superficial, compressible sites ([adductor canal](a07-block-menu.html), femoral, popliteal, interscalene) prophylactic anticoagulation is not a barrier and the catheter is removed without special timing, though removal at the trough is sensible. Deep sites follow the neuraxial rule.

## The day-case and short-stay patient

The patient who goes home the same day has shown, by doing so, the mobility that protects against thrombosis, and has also left the building where the injections and the compression device were. The plan has to work at home.

For day-case hip and knee replacement, selection has already removed most high-risk patients. The usual regimens are an oral agent for the guideline duration: a direct oral anticoagulant, or an anticoagulant for the first days followed by aspirin, with aspirin alone for the lowest-risk. Enoxaparin requires teaching the injection before the day of surgery, not in recovery. The discharge document states the agent, the dose, the stop date and the symptoms that should bring the patient back (calf swelling or pain, breathlessness, pleuritic pain, and a wound that is leaking more on day five than on day two).

For arthroscopy, upper limb surgery and minor foot surgery, nothing beyond walking unless the patient carries a risk factor. For a lower-limb operation that ends in a cast or boot and weeks of non-weight-bearing, risk-stratify: POT-CAST showed no benefit in unselected patients, and the patient with a previous clot, cancer, oestrogen therapy or an Achilles rupture is the one who is given low-molecular-weight heparin or a direct oral anticoagulant for the period of immobilisation.

## Edge cases

Already anticoagulated. The patient on a therapeutic direct oral anticoagulant or warfarin for atrial fibrillation or previous thrombosis needs prophylaxis from the evening of surgery (prophylactic enoxaparin, or the prophylactic dose of their own agent) and returns to the treatment dose when the surgeon is content with the wound, typically at 48-72 hours after arthroplasty. Restarting a full dose on the first evening is how a haematoma becomes a [periprosthetic infection](../c/periprosthetic-joint-infection.html). Bridging with therapeutic heparin is reserved for mechanical valves and very recent thrombosis.

Recent thromboembolism. Elective surgery within three months of a deep vein thrombosis or embolus should wait. When a fracture cannot wait and anticoagulation must stop, a retrievable vena cava filter is considered for a proximal thrombosis within the previous month; as primary prevention filters are recommended against by both ACCP and ASH.

Spine. The bleeding complication is an epidural haematoma with cord compression, so the balance tips toward mechanical prophylaxis alone for decompressions and short fusions, with low-molecular-weight heparin started 24-48 hours after surgery for long fusions, tumour, trauma with neurological deficit and combined anterior-posterior procedures. This is consensus practice, not trial evidence.

Polytrauma and pelvic fracture. Start low-molecular-weight heparin as soon as bleeding is controlled, usually within 24-48 hours, and time the doses around repeat trips to theatre. Compression devices go on any limb that can take one.

Obesity and extremes of weight. Fixed-dose enoxaparin under-doses at 140 kg and over-doses at 45 kg. Weight-banded dosing (40 mg twice daily or about 0.5 mg/kg above 120 kg, 20 mg below 50 kg) is widely used and rests on anti-Xa levels, not outcome trials.

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia. Any heparin is contraindicated, including flushes. A direct oral anticoagulant or fondaparinux replaces it, and fondaparinux brings its own neuraxial restriction.

Tranexamic acid. It does not replace or interfere with thromboprophylaxis, and across the randomised trials it has not increased thrombosis; give both.
