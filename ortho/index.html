    Part A. Principles

    1. The tourniquet

      A tourniquet is a controlled crush injury over a controlled ischaemic limb, and both halves of that sentence produce complications. Nerve injury tracks cuff pressure and the pressure gradient at the cuff edge, so the pressure should be set from the measured limb occlusion pressure plus a margin rather than from habit. Tissue injury tracks time, with no proven safe limit and a steep rise in neurological complications beyond two hours. Deflation delivers a bolus of cold, acidotic, potassium-rich, embolus-laden blood to the right heart over about ten minutes, and the frail patient feels it. For knee replacement, the randomised evidence shows a tourniquet saves about four minutes of operating time at the cost of more pain on day one and roughly double the rate of serious adverse events.

    2. Neuraxial versus general anaesthesia for arthroplasty

      The large registry associations favour neuraxial anaesthesia for hip and knee arthroplasty, and the two big randomised hip fracture trials (REGAIN and RAGA) found no difference in survival, walking or delirium. Both are true and they answer different questions. Neuraxial reliably changes blood loss, the early pain curve, urinary retention and the shape of recovery on the day; it does not reliably change mortality or delirium once a modern general anaesthetic is the comparator. The author's default is a low-dose spinal for arthroplasty and for hip fracture in a patient who can lie still, a general anaesthetic for the patient who cannot, and a refusal to let either technique be a reason to delay a hip fracture.

    3. Enhanced recovery and day-case arthroplasty

      Enhanced recovery for arthroplasty is a short list of things that each remove one reason a patient stays in bed: anaemia corrected, fasting shortened, a spinal dosed for the operation rather than the tradition, a motor-sparing block and infiltration, dexamethasone, tranexamic acid, no drain, no catheter, and a physiotherapist at the bedside on the afternoon of surgery. Same-day discharge is the same pathway with the patient, the home and the discharge criteria selected in advance; in pooled data it carries no excess readmission or complication, but the cohorts were chosen to succeed. The failures are predictable (orthostatic intolerance, pain when a short block recedes, nausea, urinary retention) and each has a specific prevention.

    4. The orthopaedic patient: frailty, hip fracture pathways, rheumatoid and ankylosing spondylitis, obesity

      Five patient phenotypes recur across the orthopaedic list and each breaks the standard plan in a predictable place. The frail older patient dies or loses independence according to frailty, delirium and time to theatre, not anaesthetic technique, so the pathway (block in the emergency department, surgery within 36 hours, orthogeriatric care, day 1 mobilisation) is the treatment. Rheumatoid arthritis is an airway and a cervical spine problem before it is a joint problem. Ankylosing spondylitis is a fixed neck and a difficult neuraxial space. Obesity changes positioning, dosing, block technique and the day-case question. The young trauma patient is the one in whom compartment syndrome and fat embolism are real.

    Part B. Why operations hurt

    1. Why operations hurt: a first-principles pain model

      Post-operative pain is predictable from four things: which tissue was cut, what was left under tension, what will be loaded afterwards, and who the patient is. Bone hurts hard and briefly; a tendon pulled onto bone under tension hurts for as long as it is loaded; a joint that has been re-tensioned aches for weeks; arthroscopic surface work barely registers. The early peak is a poor guide, because the shape of the curve after day 2 is what decides whether a single-shot block, a catheter or nothing is the right plan. Every card in Part C carries a 1-5 score and a day 0 to 7 trajectory built on this model, and this chapter defines them.
