    Part C. The procedure atlas

    Part A. Principles

    1. The tourniquet

      A tourniquet is a controlled crush injury over a controlled ischaemic limb, and both halves of that sentence produce complications. Nerve injury tracks cuff pressure and the pressure gradient at the cuff edge, so the pressure should be set from the measured limb occlusion pressure plus a margin rather than from habit. Tissue injury tracks time, with no proven safe limit and a steep rise in neurological complications beyond two hours. Deflation delivers a bolus of cold, acidotic, potassium-rich, embolus-laden blood to the right heart over about ten minutes, and the frail patient feels it. For knee replacement, the randomised evidence shows a tourniquet saves about four minutes of operating time at the cost of more pain on day one and roughly double the rate of serious adverse events.

    2. Bone cement and bone cement implantation syndrome

      Bone cement implantation syndrome is an embolic event with a mediator tail: pressurising cement into a long bone forces marrow fat, air and debris into the femoral veins, the right heart receives it over a minute or two, and the pulmonary circulation answers with vasoconstriction, right ventricular dilatation and a fall in left-sided filling. Hypoxia, hypotension and loss of consciousness around cementation define it, and the Donaldson grading (1: SpO2 below 94% or systolic fall of 20-40%; 2: SpO2 below 88% or systolic fall over 40% or unexpected loss of consciousness; 3: collapse needing CPR) is what the cohorts are built on. Something scoreable happens in about a quarter of cemented arthroplasties and in a third of cemented hemiarthroplasties for hip fracture, severe grades carry a three- to sixteen-fold rise in early death, and the patients at risk are the old, the ASA III-IV, the renal and the pulmonary hypertensive, having a cemented stem for a fracture. The defence is a bundle, not a drug: canal lavage, a dry vented canal, a cement gun with vacuum-mixed cement and restrained pressurisation on the surgical side; a named moment, a normovolaemic patient, a vasopressor already running, oxygen up and the ventilator watched on the anaesthetic side. Cement still wins for hip fracture: the randomised and registry evidence shows a day-one mortality excess that is repaid within a week by fewer periprosthetic fractures and better function.

    3. Fat embolism

      Fat embolism is marrow fat in the venous blood and it happens in almost every long bone fracture and in almost every reamed nail; fat embolism syndrome is the small fraction of patients in whom that fat produces lung injury, brain injury and a petechial rash 12-72 h later. Two mechanisms run in series: a mechanical obstruction of the pulmonary bed by fat and marrow at the moment of fracture or reaming, then a chemical injury as lipase turns the trapped neutral fat into free fatty acids that strip the capillary endothelium. The diagnosis is clinical, the Gurd, Schonfeld and Lindeque criteria are three unvalidated ways of saying so, and the treatment is supportive. The one intervention with consistent evidence is early fixation of the femur, within 24 h where the patient's physiology allows it, with external fixation as the damage-control alternative when it does not. The collapse during nailing is a different animal from the syndrome on the ward: it is acute right heart failure from an embolic shower, and the differential at that moment is blood loss, tension pneumothorax, anaphylaxis and a paradoxical embolus through a foramen ovale.

    4. Blood management and tranexamic acid

      Orthopaedic bleeding comes from cut bone that cannot constrict and from fibrinolysis switched on by the surgery, so it continues for hours after the wound is closed and much of it never reaches the suction. The plan has three parts: arrive with enough red cells (find and treat anaemia and iron deficiency weeks before elective surgery), lose fewer (tranexamic acid for almost everyone, with position, temperature, pressure and cell salvage where the operation is big enough), and tolerate a lower haemoglobin afterwards (a restrictive threshold of about 80 g/L, or symptoms, is as safe as 100 g/L even in elderly hip fracture patients with cardiovascular disease). Tranexamic acid cuts transfusion by about two thirds in arthroplasty and by a third to a half in hip fracture and spine surgery, works by any route, and has not been shown to raise thrombotic risk in more than 125,000 randomised patients. The anticoagulant timing rules for neuraxial and deep blocks are the other half of the same subject, because the drug that prevents the clot is the drug that causes the haematoma.

    5. Venous thromboembolism prophylaxis

      Hip and knee replacement, hip fracture and pelvic trauma combine all three limbs of Virchow's triad in one operation, which is why they sit at the top of every risk table; arthroscopy, upper limb and most day surgery sit at the bottom and need nothing beyond early walking unless the patient brings the risk. The agent question after arthroplasty is no longer open-ended: in the Australian CRISTAL trial aspirin alone gave more symptomatic thromboembolism than enoxaparin (3.45% versus 1.82%), almost all of the difference in below-knee thrombosis, with no difference in death or major bleeding, while a short course of rivaroxaban followed by aspirin matched continued rivaroxaban in EPCAT II. Guidelines therefore accept several regimens and differ mainly in how much weight they give to a calf thrombosis against a wound haematoma. The anaesthetist's part is timing: the first dose after a spinal, the dose before a catheter comes out, and the hip fracture patient whose evening enoxaparin must not sit inside the morning's needle. Mechanical prophylaxis and walking on the day of surgery are the constants under every regimen.

    6. Positioning: beach chair, lateral, prone and the traction table

      Each orthopaedic position buys the surgeon access and charges the patient somewhere else, and the charge is predictable from the geometry. The beach chair lifts the brain 20-30 cm above the cuff, so the brain sees 15-25 mmHg less than the number on the screen (Cullen quotes 20-40 mmHg at the brainstem, depending on height and angle), cerebral desaturation is the rule when that is ignored, and the rare strokes in this position have followed uncorrected or deliberate hypotension. Lateral and prone positions move the risk to the brachial plexus, the dependent eye and the abdomen: arm position changes account for most upper limb evoked potential changes in spine surgery, and a compressed abdomen turns epidural veins into the surgeon's bleeding problem. The traction table trades a perineal post against the pudendal nerve in proportion to force and time, and a raised well leg against its own calf perfusion. The defence in every case is the same: refer the pressure to the organ at risk, pad and align so that no nerve is stretched or pressed, limit the time, and write down what was done.

    7. The block menu: regional anaesthesia keyed to the operation

      Choosing a block is matching three maps: which nerves supply the tissue the surgeon will cut, which of those the block reaches, and which nerves it paralyses that the patient needs afterwards. The useful blocks are therefore defined as much by what they spare as by what they cover: a superior trunk or suprascapular block trades a little early analgesia for a working diaphragm, an adductor canal block trades the back of the knee for quadriceps that still lift the leg, and a block chosen for an injured tibia is the one that leaves ischaemic pain reportable. A single shot with dexamethasone 8-10 mg IV is the default for most operations; a catheter earns its logistics only where pain is severe beyond 24 hours, and in the shoulder a randomised trial found no added benefit once dexamethasone and multimodal analgesia were in place. Rebound pain affects about half of ambulatory block patients and is reduced by intravenous dexamethasone and by oral analgesia started before the block fades; liposomal bupivacaine does not solve it. Dose the local anaesthetic as one budget shared with the surgeon's infiltration, know where the lipid is, and record the limb's neurology before the needle goes in.

    8. Neuraxial versus general anaesthesia for arthroplasty

      The large registry associations favour neuraxial anaesthesia for hip and knee arthroplasty, and the two big randomised hip fracture trials (REGAIN and RAGA) found no difference in survival, walking or delirium. Both are true and they answer different questions. Neuraxial reliably changes blood loss, the early pain curve, urinary retention and the shape of recovery on the day; it does not reliably change mortality or delirium once a modern general anaesthetic is the comparator. The author's default is a low-dose spinal for arthroplasty and for hip fracture in a patient who can lie still, a general anaesthetic for the patient who cannot, and a refusal to let either technique be a reason to delay a hip fracture.

    9. Enhanced recovery and day-case arthroplasty

      Enhanced recovery for arthroplasty is a short list of things that each remove one reason a patient stays in bed: anaemia corrected, fasting shortened, a spinal dosed for the operation rather than the tradition, a motor-sparing block and infiltration, dexamethasone, tranexamic acid, no drain, no catheter, and a physiotherapist at the bedside on the afternoon of surgery. Same-day discharge is the same pathway with the patient, the home and the discharge criteria selected in advance; the pooled reviews agree on no excess readmission and disagree on complications, and the cohorts were chosen to succeed. The failures are predictable (orthostatic intolerance, pain when a short block recedes, nausea, urinary retention) and each has a specific prevention.

    10. The orthopaedic patient: frailty, hip fracture pathways, rheumatoid and ankylosing spondylitis, obesity

      Five patient phenotypes recur across the orthopaedic list and each breaks the standard plan in a predictable place. The frail older patient dies or loses independence according to frailty, delirium and time to theatre, not anaesthetic technique, so the pathway (block in the emergency department, surgery within 36 hours, orthogeriatric care, day 1 mobilisation) is the treatment. Rheumatoid arthritis is an airway and a cervical spine problem before it is a joint problem. Ankylosing spondylitis is a fixed neck and a difficult neuraxial space. Obesity changes positioning, dosing, block technique and the day-case question. The young trauma patient is the one in whom compartment syndrome and fat embolism are real.

    11. Nerve injury and compartment syndrome under regional anaesthesia

      A new deficit after orthopaedic surgery is far more often caused by the operation, the tourniquet or the position than by the block: nerve injury follows about 0.7-0.8% of hip and knee replacements and 2.2% of shoulder replacements whether or not a block was used, while block-related injury in a prospective Australasian audit was 0.4 per 1,000. The block's real hazard is different: it can hide a deficit that has a time-critical remedy, so the rule is that any deficit outside the block's territory or outlasting its expected duration is surgical or compressive until shown otherwise. Compartment syndrome is the sharpest version of the same problem, and the evidence, all case reports, does not show that regional analgesia delays the diagnosis when surveillance is adequate; ischaemic pain broke through a working epidural in most reported cases. What the consensus guidance asks for is a block that is low in concentration, free of duration-prolonging adjuncts and no longer than it needs to be, a patient watched on a schedule by people who know what they are looking for, and a decision shared with the surgeon and written down.

    12. Opioid-tolerant and chronic pain patients

      The opioid-tolerant patient has three separate problems that are easy to merge into one: a baseline requirement that must be supplied or withdrawal follows, a shifted dose-response so that acute doses need to be larger, and a sensitised nervous system that makes the same operation hurt more. The plan is built before admission with the pain service and the usual prescriber: verify and continue the baseline including methadone and buprenorphine, put the weight of the analgesia on techniques that do not act at the opioid receptor (a block or catheter, ketamine, an NSAID), size the rescue dose from the patient's own daily total, and watch sedation, because tolerance to respiratory depression is less dependable than tolerance to analgesia. Buprenorphine should not be routinely stopped. About a quarter of arthroplasty patients take opioids beforehand and do worse in absolute terms, but they still benefit from the operation, and those who taper before surgery do as well as patients who never took them. The discharge plan names a date for return to baseline and a single prescriber; pain catastrophising identifies who will struggle but has not been shown to be fixed by pre-operative coping-skills training.

    13. Antibiotic prophylaxis and periprosthetic infection

      An implant lets a handful of skin bacteria establish a biofilm that no later antibiotic will clear, so prophylaxis is a race to have a bactericidal tissue level in place from incision to closure: cefazolin 2 g IV (3 g at 120 kg or more) finished within 60 minutes before the knife and before any tourniquet goes up, repeated at four hours or after 1.5 L of blood loss, and stopped within 24 hours. A penicillin allergy label should almost never displace cefazolin: its side chain is shared with no penicillin, the measured rate of dual allergy is under 1%, and the usual substitute, vancomycin, was associated with nearly three times the rate of methicillin-sensitive staphylococcal infection in Australian surveillance data and added nothing but harm when given with cefazolin in the ASAP trial. Vancomycin, when it is indicated, needs an hour or more to infuse and so has to be started before the patient reaches theatre. In established periprosthetic infection a single prophylactic dose does not spoil the cultures and should not be withheld. For open fractures the antibiotic matters most in the first hour after injury, long before the anaesthetist meets the patient, so the job at induction is to find out what was given and when.

    14. Spine essentials: neuromonitoring, prone physiology, blood loss and vision loss

      Spinal surgery adds four things to an orthopaedic anaesthetic that the limb operations do not have: a patient turned face down for hours, a cord whose function is being watched electrically, a bleeding surface that scales with the number of levels, and an airway that the operation itself can swell. Prone positioning lowers cardiac index through reduced venous return and the size of the fall depends on the frame; a free abdomen is both haemodynamic and haemostatic. Ischaemic optic neuropathy is the visual loss of long, bloody prone operations, not of eye pressure: in the registry 96% of cases had six hours of anaesthesia or a litre of blood loss, and the independent risk factors are male sex, obesity, the Wilson frame, duration, blood loss and a low proportion of colloid. Motor evoked potentials require a propofol-opioid anaesthetic with no relaxant after intubation, and a signal change is managed by a rehearsed checklist that starts with pressure, haemoglobin, temperature and the anaesthetic before it reaches the implants. Tranexamic acid reduces blood loss in multilevel surgery; after anterior cervical surgery the commoner airway failure is pharyngeal oedema at about 36 hours, with haematoma the early one; and intrathecal morphine buys the first 24 hours after fusion at the price of itch and monitoring.

    Part B. Why operations hurt

    1. Why operations hurt: a first-principles pain model

      Post-operative pain is predictable from four things: which tissue was cut, what was left under tension, what will be loaded afterwards, and who the patient is. Bone hurts hard and briefly; a tendon pulled onto bone under tension hurts for as long as it is loaded; a joint that has been re-tensioned aches for weeks; arthroscopic surface work barely registers. The early peak is a poor guide, because the shape of the curve after day 2 is what decides whether a single-shot block, a catheter or nothing is the right plan. Every card in Part C carries a 1-5 score and a day 0 to 7 trajectory built on this model, and this chapter defines them.
