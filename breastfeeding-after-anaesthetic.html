Breastfeeding after an anaesthetic

This page is for anyone who is breastfeeding and having an anaesthetic, including for a caesarean. In most cases you can feed your baby as soon as you are awake and alert.

Perth, Western Australia
The usual answer

Feed as soon as you are awake, alert and able to hold your baby.

Your milk

There is no need to express and throw milk away after an anaesthetic.

Medicines to avoid

Codeine is not recommended while breastfeeding. Some others need care.

Watch your baby

Get advice if your baby is unusually sleepy, floppy or not feeding.

On this page
General information only. This page is not personal medical advice. Always follow the instructions from Dr Halvey, your surgeon and the hospital, including the times your hospital gives you.

The short answer

Anaesthetic medicines and most pain medicines pass into breast milk in only very small amounts. For almost all medicines used around surgery, there is no evidence that these amounts harm a breastfed baby.

Current guidance from the Association of Anaesthetists (UK) is that you can breastfeed as soon as you are awake, alert and able to feed or hold your baby. You do not need to express and throw away (“pump and dump”) your milk after an anaesthetic.

Before your anaesthetic

  • Tell Dr Halvey and the hospital team that you are breastfeeding, and how old your baby is. This helps with choosing medicines.
  • Feed your baby, or express, just before you come in if you can. This keeps you comfortable.
  • If you use a breast pump, bring it with you. Ask the hospital whether your baby and a support person can stay with you.
  • Fasting rules apply to you, not your baby. Your baby can feed as normal.
  • If you can, arrange for another adult to help care for your baby for the rest of the day after your anaesthetic.

After a general anaesthetic

Once you are awake and alert, you can usually feed your baby. If you feel too drowsy to hold your baby safely, wait until you feel more awake, or ask someone to help. Expressed milk from before or after the anaesthetic can be kept and used.

Where it suits the operation, local anaesthetic, a nerve block or a spinal can reduce the need for strong pain medicines. Dr Halvey will discuss this with you.

After a caesarean

Most caesareans are done with a spinal or epidural, so you are awake. The medicines used reach your milk in tiny amounts. You can usually hold your baby skin to skin and start feeding in theatre or the recovery area, if you and your baby are well.

After a general anaesthetic for a caesarean, you can feed as soon as you are awake and able to hold your baby.

Pain medicines and breastfeeding

Good pain relief helps you move, rest and feed your baby. The table gives general guidance. Your team will choose medicines and doses for you.

MedicineWhat to know
ParacetamolConsidered safe while breastfeeding. The amount in milk is much less than a baby's own dose.
Anti-inflammatories such as ibuprofenVery little passes into milk. Breastfeeding can continue as normal.
Strong opioids such as oxycodone or morphineUsed for short periods at the lowest dose that controls your pain. Watch your baby for sleepiness or poor feeding. Extra care is needed for newborns, premature babies and babies who are unwell.
TramadolCan be used with caution. Watch your baby for extra sleepiness or changes in breathing.
CodeineNot recommended while breastfeeding. Some women turn codeine into morphine very quickly, which can make a baby dangerously drowsy. This includes codeine in combination products you can buy from a pharmacy.
Sleeping tablets and sedativesA single dose around surgery is usually acceptable. Repeated doses need more care. Ask before taking any at home.
Anti-sickness medicinesThe medicines commonly used around surgery are usually compatible with breastfeeding.

Check with a doctor, midwife or pharmacist before you take any new medicine at home, including products from a pharmacy or supermarket.

Watch your baby

If you are taking strong pain medicines, watch for changes in your baby. Stop breastfeeding and get advice the same day if your baby:

  • is much sleepier than usual or hard to wake
  • is not feeding well, or is too sleepy to feed
  • seems floppy.
Call 000 straight away if your baby has pauses in breathing, is breathing very slowly, turns blue or pale, or you cannot wake them.

Sleep and safety at home

Anaesthetic and pain medicines can make you less responsive than usual, even when you feel fine.

  • Take care with bed-sharing, and with feeding in a chair or on a sofa where you might fall asleep with your baby.
  • Have another adult with you, especially on the first night.
  • Avoid alcohol while you are taking strong pain medicines.

Who to ask
