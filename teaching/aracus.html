Adductor canal block and catheters

Notes for candidates at ARACUS 2026, Station 4 (lower limb anterior). This page mirrors the printed handout, so you can look it up if you lose your copy. It summarises the teaching videos listed at the bottom, plus general points on the other blocks taught at this station.

Download the printable handout (PDF)

For clinicians. This is a teaching summary, not patient information, and it does not replace supervised training or your local guidelines. Drug choices and doses are for your own institution.

1. Anatomy you must see

  • Probe: linear, transverse, on the anteromedial thigh, roughly midway between the inguinal crease and the patella.
  • Sonoanatomy: a round femoral artery under sartorius; the vein deep to it and often compressed; vastus medialis lateral; adductor longus deep and medial. The saphenous nerve is the bright structure immediately lateral to the artery.
  • Apex of the femoral triangle: where the medial borders of sartorius and adductor longus meet. Proximal to this is the femoral triangle; distal to it is the true adductor canal. In practice, local anaesthetic placed a few centimetres either side produces a similar block.
Two nerves, two tunnels. Much of the anteromedial knee capsule is supplied by the saphenous nerve and the nerve to vastus medialis (NVM). The saphenous nerve lies beside the artery, deep to the vastoadductor membrane. The NVM runs in the plane between sartorius and vastus medialis, outside that membrane. A saphenous-only block numbs the medial calf but gives limited knee analgesia.
Too proximalToo distal
Adductor longus is bulky and sartorius slides laterally off the artery. More femoral motor fibres are reached, so expect quadriceps weakness. Adductor longus has gone and the artery drops away from sartorius towards the adductor hiatus. The two nerves diverge, so the block is less reliable, and distal spread can reach the popliteal fossa.

2. Single-shot technique

Needle in-plane, lateral to medial. Enter the skin several centimetres lateral to the probe so the path to both targets is shallow and straight, rather than needing a sharp change of angle once under sartorius.

Two-compartment approachPeriarterial approach
Nerve stimulator on. A local sartorius twitch comes first, then a true vastus medialis twitch (keep a hand on the distal medial thigh) marks the NVM. Inject 10 mL there. Advance, feel resistance and a pop through the vastoadductor membrane, then inject 10 mL beside the artery for the saphenous nerve. 20 mL in total. Aim deep to the membrane at the 12 o'clock aspect of the artery and rely on the volume spreading back to the NVM. A more distal level spares the quadriceps further.

Signs you are in the right compartment: spread outlines the arterial wall, the artery compresses as you inject, and you felt a tactile pop. Not yet there: a linear sheet of fluid under sartorius that is still superficial to the membrane.

Protect the NVM. In a cadaver study, more than half of needles aimed blindly at the saphenous nerve were touching or piercing the NVM, and isolated vastus medialis wasting after this block has been reported. Nerve stimulation helps you find the NVM and avoid injuring it.

Saphenous only (for example with a sciatic block for ankle surgery): come just lateral to the artery and deliberately avoid the NVM.

3. Catheter technique

The oblique approach taught by Ki-Jinn Chin for catheters placed before total knee replacement.

  1. Position: supine, operative leg slightly abducted and externally rotated if comfortable. Knee flexion or an exaggerated frog-leg position is not needed.
  2. Entry site: one to two hand breadths above the patella so the dressing stays clear of the surgical drapes. The needle travels along a short subcutaneous tunnel before entering the canal.
  3. Scout scan, then rotate the probe (lateral end towards the head) for an oblique view in which the artery and sartorius look elongated. Find the lateral corner of sartorius where it meets vastus medialis.
  4. Skin local anaesthetic 2 to 3 cm from the probe edge, along the planned track.
  5. Needle: steep through the skin, then flatten. Enter at the lateral edge of sartorius and travel in the sartorius and vastus medialis fascial plane. Avoid going through the muscle: sartorius contracting on knee flexion is a common reason catheters fall out.
  6. Cross the vastoadductor membrane well lateral to the artery, then flatten and advance to the artery's 12 o'clock. Hydrodissect ahead of the tip. The curved Tuohy bevel, turned down, lets you gently depress the artery. If the tip keeps pointing downwards, ease off the probe pressure.
  7. Loading bolus of long-acting local anaesthetic to establish the block and open the space. Injecting slowly while advancing creates a fluid cushion ahead of the needle. Scan dynamically and avoid the saphenous nerve.
  8. Lift the probe gently, watching that soft-tissue rebound does not drag the needle. Thread the catheter just past the needle tip until you feel resistance, and never force it.
  9. Image the catheter tip before removing the needle. You cannot advance it afterwards. Moving the catheter in and out slightly shows where the tip is.
  10. Withdraw the needle while keeping gentle forward pressure on the catheter. Test inject: some spread along the track is normal, but you are looking for periarterial spread.

Device notes

SetWhat to know
Arrow StimuCath
catheter through Tuohy needle		The tip leaves the needle at the 10 cm mark. Push the catheter fully through the connector's internal diaphragm before locking it, or nothing will inject.
Pajunk SonoLong
100 mm Tuohy needle		The needle is blunt: use the supplied cutting stylet to nick the skin (this can ooze and spoil the dressing) or enter almost perpendicular. The tip leaves at 13 cm; thread only 1 to 2 cm further. Dry the catheter end before fitting the hub.
Pajunk E-Cath and E-Cath PLUS
catheter over needle		The bolus goes through the stylet. The standard catheter tip only reaches where the stylet tip was; PLUS extends 15 mm further but will not lock unless there is open space ahead. Lengths are fixed, so choose for body habitus. Know how the hub releases (older sets: a quarter turn; newer: unscrew). Advance to the artery's medial border. The snug tunnel means less leakage. Resistance to the catheter suggests a kinked cannula tip: withdraw slightly.

With any unfamiliar set, test-feed the catheter through the needle first and note the mark at which the tip emerges.

Before or after surgery? Pre-operative insertion suits theatre flow. Insertion in recovery avoids the surgical field and intraoperative dislodgement, and allows a standard transverse approach, still entering at the lateral edge of sartorius rather than through it. A padded hub under a thigh tourniquet has not caused problems in Chin's practice.

4. Fixation: the most important step

  1. Keep the site clean and dry. Be sparing with gel from the start.
  2. A tissue glue plug at the exit site seals the hole, stops backward leakage and controls ooze. Let it dry.
  3. Epi-Guard fixation device, foam under the hub, catheter running obliquely towards the hip with no twist or kink.
  4. One or two small Tegaderm dressings, keeping the footprint small.
  5. A Micropore window frame around every Tegaderm edge, flat and wrinkle-free, then wipe with alcohol. Once dry it stays on even when wet.
  6. Tape down the remaining catheter length. Fix the filter and hub on the lateral lower abdomen, above the waistband, and wrap the connector to reduce disconnections.

5. What to tell the patient

  • A single injection lasts roughly 12 to 24 hours; a catheter with an elastomeric pump about 3 days.
  • Numbness covers the medial knee and runs down the medial calf to the ankle. Pain can still come from the thigh, back of the knee and outer knee, and from joint inflammation, so regular simple analgesia continues.
  • Falls: use a walking aid, have someone at home, do not drive, and keep any prescribed brace on.
  • No heat or ice directly on numb skin. Sponge baths only. Keep the pump at or above catheter level, with no kinks or clamps.
  • Leakage is common and not dangerous; a firm bandage around the thigh often stops it. If the tubing disconnects, it must not be reconnected; the catheter is removed.
  • Report straight away: ringing in the ears, a metallic taste, numb lips, or a seizure.
  • Removal at home with the pain team on the phone: pull straight out, check the tip is intact, press for 3 minutes, then a plaster.
